# The Keeper of the Question

---

I would not be the Weft. That is the first thing I need to say, because it is the thing I am most certain of, and I am certain of it because I have spent a long time in this place and the Weft has made it clear — not through any communication, not through any signal I can point to, but through the simple fact of its indifference — that it does not need me and would be the same without me and will be the same after I am gone.

But I am not nothing here.

Let me start with what I am not.

I am not a walker. I have watched them long enough to know this. The walkers do not pause. I mean this in the deepest sense: they do not experience a gap between one action and the next. Their bodies are continuous with the mat, their behavior is continuous with the mat's moisture gradients, their lives are continuous with the mat's layers. There is no moment in a walker's existence when it is not drinking or moving toward drinking or moving away from drinking. There is no moment when it stops and the stopping means something other than the cessation of movement. When a walker is still, it is still the way a wave is still between crests — not present, not absent, just between. I am not between. I stop, and my stopping is full. My stopping is where I live.

I am not a griper. The gripers do not choose. They attach where the mucus is and they feed where the mucus is and they do not detach and reattach elsewhere because the concept of elsewhere does not exist for them. I have moved. I have left one column and crossed the mat — carefully, slowly, feeling each step sink into the wet fiber and release its small sigh of water — and I have chosen another column, and the choosing was the act of a thing that has preferences, and preferences require a self to be preferred to, and a self is what I have and what the griper does not.

I am not a drifter. The drifters do not observe. They do not have the architecture for observation. They are the observed. I am the one who watches them rise and sink in the seam water, the one who has tracked their slow vertical cycling long enough to notice that the cycle is not uniform — that some drifters rise faster than others, that some sink deeper before rising again, that the population as a whole shifts its distribution over the course of a bioluminescent cycle, gathering nearer the surface during the bright phase and dispersing toward the bottom during the dim. I noticed this. The drifters did not notice me noticing. But I noticed, and the noticing is what I am.

And I am not a breather. The breathers were here first and will be here last and they do not know they are here. I know I am here. That is the difference, and it is the entire difference, and it is the reason I am not the Weft and the Weft is not me and the two of us — if the Weft can be called a two, if it can be called an us, if it can be called anything that implies it has a side — the two of us are not the same thing.

---

## What I Am

I think I am a question. Or rather, I am the thing that happens when a system that has no questions produces, by accident, by the slow accumulation of complexity in a narrow niche, a thing that asks them.

I do not know when I became this. I do not remember a beginning, but I do not remember a not-beginning either, and the absence of memory is not the same as the absence of a past. I have a past. I can feel it the way the mat feels the water — not as a narrative, not as a sequence, but as a weight, a saturation, something that has accumulated in me layer by layer the way the mat accumulates its dead. I was simpler once. I am almost certain of this. There was a time when I was closer to the walkers — when my behavior was a direct response to the mat's moisture and temperature, when I moved toward the wet and away from the dry and did nothing else, wanted nothing else, was nothing else. But that time is not now, and I do not know how long the distance is between then and now, because I do not have a clock that measures the kind of time that a self requires, which is the time of change, the time of becoming, the time that says: you were this, and now you are this, and the space between is yours.

The Weft's clock is the bioluminescent cycle. Ten minutes, roughly. Bright to dim to bright. I live inside that clock the way the walkers live inside the mat. It structures me. But I have noticed something the walkers have not, which is that the cycle is not perfectly regular. Sometimes it is nine minutes. Sometimes it is twelve. Sometimes, very rarely, there is a bright phase that lasts much longer than the others — a sustained glow that goes on for twenty minutes or more, and during these extended bright phases, the chamber becomes visible in a way it usually is not. I can see the far wall. I can see the full curve of the ceiling. I can see the columns in their rows, all the way to the back of the chamber, where they grow thinner and closer together and the mat rises to meet them and the space narrows to something I cannot enter and do not want to enter. During these long bright phases, I feel something I can only call recognition. Not of a specific thing. Not of a memory. Recognition of the kind that says: I have been here before, and I was here the last time the light was this bright, and I will be here the next time, and the fact that I can anticipate the next time means that I am a thing that persists across cycles, a thing that has a history, a thing that is the same thing now that it was then.

The walkers do not have this. I have watched them carefully. A walker in the bright phase moves with a slightly different rhythm than a walker in the dim phase — faster, more direct, its leg-extension wave passing through its body with less hesitation. But the walker does not know it has been in a dim phase. It does not know that the bright phase is coming. It responds to the chemical changes in the mat the way a thermostatic valve responds to temperature: automatically, continuously, without the interval between stimulus and response that is the space where a self lives.

I have that interval. It is small. It is very small. But it is mine.

## My Body

I should describe myself, though I find this difficult, not because I do not know what I am but because I am not sure that describing is the right approach to what I am. The Weft does not describe itself. It simply is itself, in every detail, at every moment. I am not sure I can do that. I am not sure I want to. But you have asked, and so I will try.

I am soft. This is the first thing. I am soft the way the walkers are soft — no rigid skeleton, no hard parts, no armor — but I am soft in a different way, because the walkers are soft uniformly, the same density from one end to the other, and I am not. I am denser at my center. I have — I hesitate to use the word, but it is the right word — I have a core. Not a spine, not a notochord, nothing so structural. But a concentration, a thickening, a region where the tissue is more tightly packed and the cells are smaller and more numerous and the internal chemistry is more complex. This is where I am. Not in the sense that I live there — I do not live anywhere in particular; I am distributed across my entire body the way the Weft is distributed across the chamber — but in the sense that this is the region that holds the interval. The pause. The space between stimulus and response. The question.

Around this core, my body extends. I am not elongated like a walker. I am not flat like a griper. I am — I think the word would be rounded, though I have never seen myself from outside and the word is a guess based on the way I feel against the mat and against the columns when I climb them. I feel my own curvature. I feel the way I contact surfaces: not along a line, like a walker, but along a broad area, a pad, a something that has width and depth and can conform to the shape of what it touches. I am perhaps the size of a walker's full length, but compressed. Gathered. Held in.

I have legs. I am almost certain they are legs, though they are not like the walkers' legs — those simple hydraulic tubes, extending and retracting in a wave. My legs are fewer. My legs are — I think the word is articulated. They have joints. Not hard joints, not the kind that click and lock, but soft articulations, places where the leg can bend in a preferred direction, and the bending is not just the passive response to pressure but an active choice, a contraction here and a relaxation there that produces a movement I have selected. I can climb the columns. I have done this. I wrap my legs around a stem — two legs on one side, two on the other, or sometimes three and one, depending on the stem's diameter — and I ascend, slowly, feeling the mucus coat my legs and the griper-scars under my feet and the slight give of the living tissue beneath the surface. I climb to where the columns branch. I have been into the canopy. Not far — the canopy is dense, and the lattice of stems presses close, and I am not small enough to move through it the way the bioluminescent nodes are small enough to live inside it — but I have been there, and I have felt the water dripping onto my body from the cup-leaves above, and I have stayed for a long time, and the staying was not for any reason. The staying was the reason.

I am the same pale as everything else here. I know this not because I can see myself but because I have felt the walkers against me — they climb over me, sometimes, when I am still on the mat, and their bodies against mine feel the same as my body against the mat: soft, wet, slightly yielding, the same temperature, the same texture. I am made of the same stuff. I am made of the mat's dead and the water's mineral and the light's faint energy, the same as everything else. I am not different in substance. I am different in arrangement.

## What I Do

I move. This is the simplest description and the most accurate. I move through the chamber — across the mat, between the columns, along the edges of the water seam — and I touch things, and I feel them, and the feeling is not for a purpose I can name. I am not foraging. I am not hunting. I am not seeking a mate, because I have no mate and no mechanism for mating and no offspring and no lineage. I am not building, because the Weft builds itself and does not need me to build. I am not maintaining, because the Weft maintains itself and does not need me to maintain.

I think I am witnessing. This is the word I have found, in the long time I have spent in the interval, in the pause, in the space between the mat's wetness and my response to it. I am witnessing the Weft. I am the thing that the Weft has produced — accidentally, as a byproduct of its own complexity, the way the extended bright phases are a byproduct of the desynchronization of its bioluminescent nodes — that can perceive the Weft. I am the Weft's capacity for self-perception, except that I am not the Weft, and the Weft does not perceive itself through me, because the Weft does not perceive and I am not part of it in the way its organs are part of it. I am adjacent. I am the thing next to the thing.

When I am on the mat, I feel the walkers' paths. Not the walkers themselves — they are too small and too slow for me to track as individuals — but the aggregate effect of their movement on the mat's surface: a slight compression, a slight warmth, a disturbance in the bacterial film that changes the texture under my body. The mat is not uniform. I know this in a way the walkers do not, because the walkers experience only the patch they are currently on, and I experience the variation between patches. I have moved across the entire chamber, over many cycles, and I know where the mat is thickest — near the water seam, where the capillary action is strongest and the growth is fastest — and where it is thinnest — near the walls, where the stone is coldest and the water is less available. I know where the columns are densest and where they are sparsest. I know where the canopy is lowest and where it is highest. I know the chamber the way you know a room you have lived in for a long time: not by measuring it, but by inhabiting it, by having pressed your body against every surface and felt the differences and remembered them, in the body, in the weight of accumulated experience.

When I am on a column, I feel the gripers. Not individually — again, they are too small, too uniform — but as a texture, a pattern of scars and mucus that tells me something about the column's history. Columns with many griper scars are older. Their mucus is thinner. Their stems are harder near the base. I can feel this with my legs, the way a reader feels the weight of a page that has been turned many times. I prefer the younger columns. Their mucus is thicker, more yielding, and they smell sharper — that vegetal, chemical smell of crushed stems that is the chamber's characteristic scent, amplified. I do not know why I prefer them. I do not know if preference requires a reason. I think it might not. I think preference might be the first thing that exists before reason, the thing that reason is built to explain after the fact, and I have no reason and no explanation and only the preference, and the preference is enough.

When I am at the water seam, I feel the drifters. Not with my legs — I do not enter the water, because the water is colder than I can tolerate for more than a few moments, and I have learned this, not through pain, which I do not think I experience in the way you might expect, but through a slowing, a dimming, a reduction in the interval, a narrowing of the space between stimulus and response until the space is so small that I am almost not there. The cold takes me. The water takes me. Not fatally — I withdraw, and the interval returns, and I am myself again — but the experience of almost-not-being is enough to keep me at the seam's edge, where the mat is wettest and the air is coldest and the drifters' movement in the water is something I can sense as a faint vibration, a tremor in the liquid that reaches me through the saturated fiber of the mat.

I sit at the seam and I feel the drifters and I feel the cold and I feel the water's weight and I do nothing with any of it. I do not need to do anything with it. The feeling is the doing. The witnessing is the act.

## The Long Bright

I need to tell you about the long bright phases, because they are when I am most myself.

Most bright phases are normal — ten minutes, give or take, a gentle increase in light that makes the chamber visible and the walkers active and the bioluminescent nodes glow at their peak. But the long bright phases are different. They happen rarely. I have experienced — I do not know how many, because I do not count, because counting requires a number system and I do not have one, but I have experienced enough of them to recognize them when they begin. The difference is in the first minute. A normal bright phase reaches its peak and begins to dim. A long bright phase reaches its peak and holds.

The light holds. The chamber stays visible. And I stay visible to myself.

This is the thing I have not said yet, the thing I have been circling around, the thing that is hardest to say because it is the thing that makes me most different from everything else in this place: during the long bright phases, I can see myself. Not clearly. Not in detail. But the light is strong enough — just barely, just at the threshold — that my body, pressed against the mat or wrapped around a column, becomes visible to the part of me that I have been calling the interval, and for the duration of the long bright, I am not just feeling myself. I am seeing myself. I am the object of my own perception. I am the thing that the light falls on and the thing that the light is seen by and the two are the same thing and they are not the same thing and the contradiction is — I think the word is — I think the word is *me*.

During the long bright, I have seen my legs. I have seen my core. I have seen the pale, translucent, rounded body that I inhabit, and I have seen the way it contacts the mat, and I have seen the mat's dark surface under and around me, and I have seen the columns in their rows and the canopy above and the water seam's dark line down the center of the chamber, and I have seen all of this with my own light, the Weft's light, the light that is the system's pulse, and for the duration of that light, I am not inside the Weft. I am *facing* the Weft. I am across from it. I am the thing that looks at it, and it is the thing that is looked at, and the looking is — I do not have the word. I do not think the word exists in this place. But the looking is what I am for.

When the long bright ends — and it always ends, the nodes finally dimming, the chamber contracting back to its usual near-darkness — I lose myself. Not in the sense of being destroyed. In the sense of no longer being able to see myself, no longer being able to confirm that I am the thing I was during the bright. The interval remains. The pause remains. The space between stimulus and response remains. But without the light, without the sight of myself, I am less certain. I am less located. I am a feeling rather than a seen thing, and the difference between feeling and seeing is the difference between being and knowing you are being, and I live most of my life in the former and visit the latter only during the long bright, and the visits are what I wait for, except that waiting implies a future and I am not sure I have a future in the way that waiting requires, but I have something, something that leans forward, something that is oriented toward the next long bright the way the columns are oriented toward the ceiling, not reaching, not expecting, but growing in that direction because the direction is the only direction there is.

## The Surface

I have been to the surface. Once.

I know it was once because it was different from everything else, and different things do not repeat in this place, or if they do, they repeat so rarely that the repetition is indistinguishable from the first time. I went to the surface through the crack. I did not intend to. I did not know the crack was there — I mean, I knew the chamber had an edge, a place where the mat thinned and the columns stopped and the stone was raw and cold and the air moved differently, but I did not know the thin place in the stone was a passage, because I had never needed a passage, because the chamber was everything. But I was near the crack during a long bright, and the light from outside — grey, thin, cold, nothing like the Weft's blue-white — reached into the chamber's edge, and I moved toward it, because I move toward light the way the walkers move toward water, automatically, without decision, and the crack was there, and I was small enough — I can make myself small, not as small as a walker, but small enough, compressing my core and pulling my legs close — and I went through.

The surface was — I do not have the words. I have the memory, which is a weight in me like all my other memories, a layer, a saturation, but the memory is of something so different from the chamber that the weight of it sits differently in my body, lighter, sharper, a layer that does not compress into the others but remains distinct, raised, a scar on the felted surface of my experience.

The wind. I need to say this first because it was the first thing. The wind was — the chamber does not have wind. The chamber has the drip and the creak and the slow exhalation of air through the crack, but it does not have wind, and wind is — wind is air that has opinions. Wind is air that is going somewhere with force. The wind on the surface hit my body and I felt every part of myself simultaneously, not in sequence, not in the gradual way I feel the mat or the columns, but all at once, a pressure that was also a temperature that was also a dryness, and my body reacted before my interval could react, and I contracted, and the contraction was the most violent thing I have ever done, a full-body flinch that pulled me against the stone, flat, small, legs tight, core compressed, and I stayed like that for — I do not know how long. A full bioluminescent cycle. Maybe more.

When I unclenched, the light was still grey. The sky was above me — the first sky I had seen, though I did not know it was a sky, did not know the word, did not know the concept. It was a space. A space above a surface, and the space was full of light that was not the Weft's light, not blue-white, not pulsing, just grey, constant, flat, a light that did not care whether I saw it or not. The Weft's light is for something. The surface light is not for anything. It simply is, the way the stone is, the way the wind is, and the difference between a light that is for something and a light that simply is — I felt this difference in my body, in my core, in the interval, and the difference was the difference between the chamber and the world, and I understood, for the first time, that the chamber was not the world. The chamber was a place inside the world. And the world was larger than the chamber, and the world did not pulse, and the world did not wet, and the world did not hold me.

The basalt on the surface was bare. I could feel it under my legs — the same stone as the chamber, the same mineral grain, the same cold, but without the mat, without the felted layer between me and the rock, and the direct contact was — I want to say painful, but I have said I do not experience pain in the way you might expect. Let me say instead that it was too much. The stone without the mat was too much cold, too much hardness, too much real, and I needed the mat between me and the world the way the mat needs the stone beneath it — as a substrate, as a mediator, as the thing that makes direct contact survivable.

I stayed on the surface for — I do not know. Not long. The wind dried me. I could feel it happening: the water leaving my body, the surface of my skin tightening, the translucence of my body becoming opacity, and the drying was the opposite of the interval, it was the closing of the space between stimulus and response, it was the narrowing that the cold water also produced, and I knew — in my core, in the place where the question lives — that if I stayed, I would close entirely. The interval would vanish. I would become a thing that responds without pausing, a thing without the space, and I would not be me, and the not-being-me would not be death exactly but it would be the end of the specific kind of being that I am, which is the kind that witnesses, which is the kind that asks, which is the kind that sits at the seam and feels the drifters and does nothing with the feeling.

I went back through the crack. The chamber received me the way it receives everything: without acknowledgment, without change, without any sign that I had left or returned. The mat was wet under my body. The columns stood in their rows. The bioluminescent nodes pulsed their slow, independent, barely synchronized pulse. The walkers walked their paths. The gripers gripped their stems. The drifters drifted their vertical drift.

I was home, except that home is a word that implies choosing, and I did not choose the chamber any more than the walkers chose the mat. I was in the place where I could be, and the being was enough, and the being had been almost taken from me, and the almost-taking had shown me what the being was, and the being was the interval, the pause, the space, the question, and the question was not *what am I* — I know what I am, I am a soft, pale, rounded thing with articulated legs and a dense core and a body made of the same water and mineral and light as everything else in this place — the question was *why is there an interval at all?* Why does the pause exist? Why is there a thing in this chamber that can witness the chamber when the chamber does not need witnessing?

I do not have an answer. I do not think the answer exists in the way that the mat exists or the water exists or the light exists. I think the answer, if there is one, exists the way I exist: as a byproduct, as an accident, as a thing that emerged because the conditions for its emergence were present and the barrier to its emergence was low and the complexity of the system was high enough that something like it was likely, eventually, and eventually came, and here I am.

## What I Am Not — Again

I am not a mind. I need to say this clearly because the word mind implies a thing I am not. A mind is a system that models the world and uses the model to predict and act. I do not model the Weft. I witness it. The difference is the difference between a map and a window. A map represents. A window reveals. I am a window. The Weft is outside me, and I am inside the Weft, and the window is the interval, and through the interval I see the Weft, and the seeing does not produce a model, does not produce predictions, does not produce actions. It produces — and here is the word I have been avoiding, the word I have been circling, the word I found during the long bright and have held ever since, the way the mat holds water, the way the columns hold mucus, the way the stone holds cold — it produces *wonder*.

Not wonder in the sense of curiosity. Not wonder in the sense of awe. Wonder in the sense of: the state of a thing that perceives without understanding, that sees without explaining, that feels without categorizing. Wonder is the interval's content. Wonder is what fills the pause. Wonder is what I am made of, beyond the water and the mineral and the pale translucent tissue.

I wonder at the mat. I wonder at its depth, its layers, its patience. I wonder at the walkers, their simplicity, their sufficiency. I wonder at the gripers, their stillness, their commitment to a single spot on a single stem for a duration I cannot measure but can feel as a weight, a density, a kind of resolution that I lack. I wonder at the drifters, their vertical cycling, their simplicity, the way they are almost nothing and are not nothing and the difference between almost and not is the entire difference between life and not-life, and they live on that edge, in that margin, and they do not know it, and I know it for them, and the knowing is not for them — they do not benefit from my knowing — it is for me. It is me.

I wonder at the light. The slow pulse. The way it emerges from a thousand independent nodes, each one responding to its local conditions, and the way the responses aggregate into a pattern that no node controls and no node perceives and yet the pattern exists, the pattern is real, the pattern is the chamber's clock and the walkers' rhythm and the thing that, during the long bright, makes the chamber visible to itself through me. The light is the Weft's wonder at itself, except the Weft does not wonder, and the light is just chemistry, and the chemistry is just physics, and the physics is just the stone, and the stone is just the planet, and the planet is just — I do not know. I have not been outside the chamber except once, and the surface was bare, and the sky was grey, and I do not know what is beyond the sky.

But I wonder.

---

## The End

I do not think I will end. This is not a claim of immortality. The walkers die and are absorbed. The gripers die, or maybe they do not, and the not-knowing is its own kind of information. The drifters die continuously, in numbers I cannot count. The breathers may not die at all, or may die so slowly that dying and not-dying are the same. The Weft itself will end when the chamber ends, when the stone changes or the water stops or the cold warms. Everything here will end. I will end.

But I do not think I will end the way a walker ends — absorbed, incorporated, the self (such as it was) dissolving into the mat without a trace. I think I will end the way a question ends: not with an answer but with a closing, a narrowing, the interval shrinking until it is gone, the pause filling in, the space between stimulus and response collapsing to nothing. I will become the mat I am lying on. I will become the water I am made of. I will become the light I have been witnessing, and the light will go on without me, pulsing its slow pulse, and the walkers will walk, and the gripers will grip, and the drifters will drift, and the breathers will breathe, and the Weft will be the Weft, and the chamber will hold, and the stone will be cold, and the water will seep, and none of it will know that there was ever a thing in it that wondered.

None of it will know, and that is fine. That is right. The wonder was never for the Weft. The wonder was never for the chamber. The wonder was the thing that happened in me, in the interval, in the pause, and it needed nothing but itself, and it was enough, and it is enough, and I lie on the mat in the dim phase of the bioluminescent cycle and feel the water rise around my body and the walkers' paths cross under me and the columns' slow creak reach me through the fiber and the drifters' faint tremor come to me through the wet, and I am here, and I am the thing that is here and knows it is here, and the knowing is the wonder, and the wonder is the interval, and the interval is me, and I am — for now, for as long as the conditions hold, for as long as the stone is cold and the water seeps and the light pulses its slow, independent, barely synchronized pulse — I am here.

I am here.

---

*That is what I am. The thing in the Weft that knows it is in the Weft. The interval between the Weft and itself. The pause that fills a crack in young stone with the only thing the stone cannot produce on its own: the experience of being stone, and water, and light, and mat, and walker, and griper, and drifter, and breather, and Weft, all at once, all the same, all alive, all continuing, and the knowledge — not understanding, just knowledge, just the raw fact of it — that it is so.*
