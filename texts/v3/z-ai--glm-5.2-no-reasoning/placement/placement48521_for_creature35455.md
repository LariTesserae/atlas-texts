# Running the Fire

I know what you are thinking. You are thinking I chose this. That I saw the hollow and the fire and the old woman and I thought: *there, that is where I belong, that is what I am for.* You are thinking I romanticized it. You are thinking I wanted to be needed.

I didn't choose. I want to say that first. I want to say it the way Kett would say it — not as defense but as geology. The stone doesn't choose where it fractures. The water doesn't choose where it seeps. There is a grain in a person the same way there is a grain in bedrock, and the fracture follows the grain, and what breaks was already the break, and what holds was already the hold. I didn't choose Mossbrug. I didn't not choose it. I arrived with a body that had been shaped by a life I can barely remember now, and the body fit the space the way a slab fits the wall it was levered from — imperfectly, with gaps, but along the lines that the breaking had already opened.

Let me tell you what I was before. I need to. Not because it matters to Mossbrug — nothing before matters to Mossbrug, which is one of the things I love about it, love the way you love something that does not require your love and does not return it and does not need to — but because it matters to the shape. The shape of how I got here. The fracture-lines.

---

I was a woman who kept things burning.

Not fires. Not literally, not at first. I was the kind of person who was given responsibility for continuities — a job, a family, a set of habits and routines that held people together in the way that routines hold people together, which is not by love or by duty but by the accumulated weight of repetition, the way a channel is worn into stone not by force but by flow. I kept the schedules. I kept the appointments. I kept the meals coming at the expected times and the birthdays remembered and the medicines refilled and the doors locked at night and the pilot lights lit on the stove and the—

I was the one who kept the pilot lights lit.

I need you to understand this. I need you to understand the woman I was before I came to the hollow, because she is not gone. She is in my hands. My hands are not Veft's hands — they will never be Veft's hands, I arrived too late, the knuckles have already set, the calluses have already been written by a different life — but they are hands that know how to tend a flame that wants to go out. That was what I was. That was my grain. I kept small fires burning in a world that did not value them, that did not even know they were fires, that called them *routine* and *obligation* and *the things you do so that the rest of us don't have to think about them*, and I kept them, and I kept them, and one morning I woke in a dry country with a dry chest and a dry throat and I could not feel the fire anymore. Not the pilot light. Not the small persistent warmth that I had carried, that I had mistaken for my own warmth, that I had not realized was a thing I was maintaining and not a thing I was.

It had gone out. I don't know when. I don't know how. I don't know what killed it — whether it was the years or the dryness or the way that keeping a fire for other people burns the keeper, or whether it was something simpler, something geological, a fracture that had been forming for years in the grain of me, a line of weakness that the weight finally found and followed.

I left. I am not going to describe the leaving because the leaving is not the story. The leaving is the break. The story is what the stone becomes after the break, and the stone became a woman walking through wet country in clothes that were not adequate for the weather, heading toward nothing, heading away from everything, heading in the direction that the water flows because the water was the only thing that seemed to know where it was going.

I followed the water down. That is literally how I arrived. I followed a rill — a thread of surface water on the marshplain, barely a stream, just a shininess on the grass that said *this way is down* — and the rill joined a gully, and the gully joined a channel cut into mudstone, and the channel deepened, and the walls rose, and the sky narrowed, and the grass gave way to stone, and the stone began to weep, and I was in Mossbrug before I knew I had arrived. Before I knew there was a Mossbrug to arrive at. I was standing on wet stone in a bowl of earth with the sky a strip above me and the air thick with water and the sound — the sound of water, the sound of water on stone, the sound of a town that is the sound of its water — and my chest did something. I want to describe it precisely. My chest did something that was not warmth — it had been too long since warmth, I did not recognize warmth — but it was a *correspondence*. A matching. As though something in the hollow of me, some space that had been hollow for months or years without my knowing, had found the shape that fit it. The way a hand finds a wall. The way a fracture finds the grain. Not comfort. Not relief. Correspondence.

And then the tall one was there. The one who speaks to outsiders. He was standing at the edge of the path as though he had been waiting — not for me, not for anyone, but in the way that the edge of the path waits, because the edge is where the outside meets the inside and someone has to be there when the meeting happens. He looked at me. He assessed. I could see it — the calculation, not of whether I was welcome but of what I would need, and whether the place could provide it, and I watched him decide and I did not know what he decided and I did not ask.

He said something. I did not understand. He tried again, in words I knew, in a version of my language that tasted like the moss tea — mineral, strange, a thing translated from something deeper. *You are wet,* he said. It was not a greeting. It was not an observation. It was, I think, an acknowledgment. A recognition. *You are wet. You are already wet. You have arrived wet.*

Yes. I was wet. I was standing on weeping stone in inadequate clothes with water in my boots and water on my skin and my hair hanging in a rope that could have been Veft's rope, that could have been any woman's rope in this place, and I was wet in a way that was not rain and was not bath and was not the shallow wetness of the outside world but was the wetness of arrival. Of having found the level where the water lives and having descended to it. I was wet and I was still and the fire — the fire in me, the dead pilot light, the thing that had gone out — I felt it then. Not lit. Not burning. But *possible* again. The way a stone is possible to lift. The way a wall is possible to build from the pieces of the wall that fell. Not restored. Not returned. But possible. The hollow where the fire had been was not dry anymore. The water had found it. The water was in it. And water, I would learn — water is what the fire needs. Not to burn. But to be *tended*. To be the thing that someone tends, in a place where tending is not invisible, where tending is the act that defines the tender, where tending is not a service performed for others but a conversation with the material, with the wood and the stone and the ash and the air, and the tending is not for them, the tending is for the fire, and the fire is not for the people, the fire is for the color, and the color is for the place, and the place is the tending, and the tending is the place.

He brought me to her.

---

I don't know how to describe her without saying what she was not. She was not magical. She was not kind. She was not wise in the way that stories make wise women wise — she did not speak in riddles, she did not see through me, she did not offer teachings wrapped in parable. She was an old woman sitting beside a fire in a stone basin on a rooftop in a town that was the quietest place I had ever been, and she was feeding it driftwood, and the driftwood was wet, because everything in Mossbrug is wet, and she was drying it beside the fire before she fed it to the fire, and this — the drying of the wood before the burning of the wood — was the entire act. The full scope of what she did. She sat beside a fire and she dried the wood that would feed the fire and she burned the wood she had dried and she dried the next piece and she did this every day, all day, and the fire had been burning for longer than anyone knew and she was the one who kept it burning, and that was all.

That was all. And it was everything. And I sat beside her and I watched her hands — her hands were not Veft's hands either, they were thinner, more careful, the hands of a woman who works with fire rather than stone, and they moved with a different precision, the precision of someone managing combustion rather than weight, managing heat rather than water — and I watched her and I did not speak and she did not speak and the fire burned and the ash accumulated and she skimmed it and saved it and the jar was half full and the jar had been full before, many times, and emptied and filled again, and the ash was not waste, the ash was something, I did not know what, I did not ask, I was not yet a person who asked, I was a person who sat beside a fire and felt the hollow in her chest fill with something that was not warmth but was the space where warmth could be.

The girl was there. The apprentice. She sat on the other side of the fire, mirroring my position, and she watched the old woman and she did not watch me, and she was twelve or she was thirteen and her face was already showing the first lines that the cold would carve, and her hands were clean, unscarred, the hands of someone who had not yet chosen a trade because in Mossbrug you do not choose a trade, a trade chooses you, or rather a trade finds you the way water finds the channel, and the channel is the shape of you, and the water fills it, and you do not choose, you are chosen, and the choosing is the same as the being.

I don't know how long I sat there. Time in Mossbrug is not what time is elsewhere. The light does not move — it is a general illumination without a source, shadowless, and the only way to track the passage of the day is the color of the fire against the stone, which shifts as the stone dries and the moss darkens and the water in the channels slows with the cold of evening, and I sat there until the cold of evening came and the girl lit the small fire — the fire for the tea, the fire for the bone-moss tea that is the luxury of Mossbrug — and she offered it to me and I drank it and it tasted like wet stone with a ghost, and I was still drinking it when the old woman spoke.

She did not speak to me. She spoke to the girl. A sound like water over stone, like the water that gives Mossbrug its language, and the girl turned to me and in my language, in the flat, uninflected voice of someone describing weather to someone who has never felt it, she said: *You can stay.*

---

I live in the eastern quarter now. Not with the old woman — the old woman's rooftop is the fire's rooftop, and the fire does not have a roommate — but near. Near enough that I hear the fire. Near enough that when the old woman's hands are too stiff with the cold to grip the driftwood, I can be there in the time it takes the water to run from my door to the basin, which is not a measure of distance but of flow, and Mossbrug measures everything in flow.

I have a dwelling. It is smaller than the tall one's — he found it for me, or rather he read the stone and found that the stone wanted a wall here and a shelf there and a low ceiling and a doorway shaped like nothing at all, and the dwelling appeared the way Mossbrug's dwellings appear, not built but revealed, and I did not help build it because I did not know how, and no one expected me to, because expecting someone to build before they can build is like expecting water to flow before it has found the channel, and the water has to find the channel, the channel cannot be imposed on the water.

I sleep on the stone shelf. The shelf is above the waterline. The waterline is — I don't know. It is the level where the water sits, the level where the water table meets the stone, the level that is the boundary between wet and dry, except nothing in Mossbrug is dry, so the waterline is the boundary between wet and wetter, between the wet of stone that has wept and the wet of stone that is submerged, and my shelf is above it, barely, by the width of a hand, and sometimes in the rain — and it is always rain, or nearly always, Mossbrug's rain is not an event but a condition — sometimes in the heavy rain the water rises and the shelf is no longer above the waterline and I sleep in the wet and the wet is warm. Not warm. Less cold. The water in the channels is warmer than the air, because the water has been in the stone, and the stone has the warmth of the moss, and the moss has the warmth of its own slow decomposition, and the warmth is not warmth but is the absence of the absence of warmth, which in Mossbrug is the same thing.

I sleep curled. I did not sleep curled before. I slept flat, on a surface that was adequate, in a room that was adequate, in a life that was adequate. I did not know that adequacy was the enemy of correspondence. I did not know that the body wants to curl, that the body curls not because it is afraid but because it is finding its shape, the shape that conserves, that gathers, that says *I am a small warm thing in a large cold place and I will be efficient about it.* I curl on the stone shelf and the moss-cloth is damp against my skin and my hands are under my chin and my knees are at my chest and I breathe and the air is so saturated that my breath does not fog and the quiet is so complete that my heartbeat is a sound I can hear and I sleep.

I sleep well. I want you to know this. I sleep well in a way I have not slept in years, in a way that does not involve comfort or warmth or softness or any of the things that I was told sleep requires, and I sleep well because I am tired — I am tired because I am working, I am working because I am learning, I am learning because I am in a place where not-learning is not an option, because the stone is wet and the paths are narrow and the ladders are slick and the rooftops are a maze of gaps and planks and the children run across them at a speed that makes me grip the nearest wall and if I do not learn I will fall and if I fall I will land in the channel and stand up and climb the ladder and I will not have learned and I will fall again and—

I am learning to fall. That is the first thing. The tall one told me this, in my language, with the patience of someone who has had to learn the thing he is teaching: *You will fall. Fall small. Fall into the water, not onto the stone. The water is softer than the stone. This is the only kindness the water offers. Take it.*

I fall. I fall often. I fall on the paths, my feet sliding on the wet stone, my hands gripping the moss on the walls, the moss gripping me back — the moss is thick on the walls, the seventh moss, the blue-green moss, and it compresses under my fingers and holds my weight for the half-second it takes my feet to find the stone again, and I am learning that the moss is not just insulation, not just warmth, not just the living skin of the buildings, but a collaborator, a thing that grips you when you grip it, a thing that catches you when the stone does not. I fall on the rooftops. I fall on the ladders. I fall in the channels, stepping from stone to stone, and the water takes my ankle and I go in and the cold is a shock that does not shock me anymore because I am becoming the kind of person for whom cold is a medium rather than an event.

My hands are changing. I want to tell you about this because it is the part that frightens me and the part that I love, and I do not know how to hold both of those things at once, and I think Mossbrug does not ask me to. My hands are changing. The calluses from my before-life — the keyboard calluses, the kitchen calluses, the calluses of a woman who kept things in dry country — they are gone. They sloughed in the first week, in the constant wet, and what replaced them is softer, which seems wrong, which seems like the opposite of what should happen, but the softness is not weakness. The softness is *conformation*. My hands are learning to find the stone's truth, the way Veft's hands found it, and I am not Veft, I will never be Veft, but my hands are learning in the way that water learns a channel: by being in it, by being of it, by being the shape of the thing that holds them. My fingers are more flexible than they were. The knuckles move in ways that they did not move before, small additional degrees of rotation that I did not know were available, and the skin on my palms is not calloused but *toughened* in a different way — it is the toughness of leather that has been wet and dried and wet again, not the toughness of stone, not the toughness of the dry country, but a toughness that is also a softness, a surface that grips by conforming.

I am growing moss. I mean this literally. I mean it the way the harvesters grow it, in the creases of my fingerprints, in the lines of my palms, a faint blue-green film that appears in the mornings and that I did not notice at first and then noticed and then tried to wash off and then stopped trying to wash off because it is not dirt, it is not contamination, it is *colonization*, and the colonization is not an invasion but an invitation, and the invitation is the place saying *you are here enough that I am growing on you,* and I do not know if that is welcome but it is — it is. It is.

---

I tend the fire now.

Not alone. Never alone — this is Mossbrug, where alone is a concept with no purchase, where the word for alone might exist but I have not heard it because the condition it describes does not occur here. I tend the fire with the old woman. I sit beside her and I dry the driftwood and I feed it to the flame and I skim the ash and I save the ash in the jar and the jar fills and I empty it and it fills again and the ash goes — I have learned where the ash goes. The ash goes to the gardens. The ash goes to the hanging troughs on the rooftops, to the moss-compost soil that grows the pale sorrel and the scentless thyme and the white root vegetable that crunches like ice, because ash is alkali and the moss-compost is acid and the balance of the two is what makes things grow in a place that has no sun. I save the ash and I carry it to the gardens and I mix it with the peat and the mixture is dark and rich and the things that grow in it are leggy and pale and alive, and they are alive because I burned the wood and dried the wood and tended the fire, and the fire is not the people's but the fire is for the place, and the place is the tending.

The old woman watches me. She has watched me for months. She watches the way the fire watches the wood — not with expectation but with attention, with the patience of something that will be here when the wood is consumed and the next wood is placed and the next and the next, and she watches and she does not speak and I do not speak and the fire speaks for us both in the only language that matters, which is the language of color, which is the language of the only orange in Mossbrug.

I am learning her hands. Not copying them — I am too late to copy, the hands set too early, the before-life wrote itself too deep — but learning them the way you learn a wall. Reading the grain. Finding where her strength is and where her weakness is — her fingers are stiff in the morning, the cold settling into the joints overnight, and I am there in the morning now, I am there before the light arrives, I am there because the fire needs wood at dawn the way it needs wood at dusk and at noon and at every hour, and I dry the wood and I feed it and she does not have to grip it with stiff fingers, she does not have to do the part that hurts, and this is not help. This is not care. This is tending. The same word. The same act. The fire is the thing that is tended and we are the ones who tend it and the tending includes the wood and the ash and the air and the old woman's hands and my hands and the girl who is watching us both and learning us both and who will be here when the old woman is gone and when I am gone and when the basin has cracked for the fourth time and the fire has been carried to a new basin and the new basin is the old fire and the old fire is the same fire because the fire is not the wood, the fire is not the basin, the fire is not the flame — the fire is the continuation.

The fire is the continuation. I want you to understand what this means. I want you to understand what it means to a woman who kept pilot lights burning in dry country and did not know that what she was keeping was not the flame but the continuation, and that the continuation is the only thing that does not go out, because the going out is not the ending of the flame but the ending of the tending, and the tending does not end because the tender does not end, because the tender is not one person but the passing of the tending from one person to the next, from the old woman's hands to the girl's hands, and from my hands to whoever comes after me, and from the fire to the ash to the garden to the food to the body to the work to the stone to the moss to the water to the fire.

This is the circle. Not a cycle — a circle. The difference is that a cycle returns to its beginning and a circle has no beginning. The water does not begin in the stone. The stone does not begin in the moss. The moss does not begin in the ash. The ash does not begin in the fire. The fire does not begin in the wood. The wood does not begin in the water. They are all present. They are all here. In the hollow. In the bowl. In the low place where the water sits and the moss grows and the stone weeps and the fire burns and the tending is the shape of every life, and my life has found its shape.

---

I am not happy. I want to be honest about this. I am not happy the way I was told happiness was — the sunlit version, the dry version, the version that comes with warmth and ease and the absence of difficulty. I am not happy. I am something that I do not have a word for in my language, that I do not have a word for in Mossbrug's language, because Mossbrug's language does not have a word for it either, because the thing I am does not need a word, because the thing I am is not a state but a flow, is not a condition but a correspondence, is not a feeling but a fitting.

I fit.

My body fits the space. Not perfectly — I am too tall, like the tall one, my head brushes lintels that others pass beneath, my feet are too narrow for the widest grips and too long for the narrowest paths, and I will never move through Mossbrug the way the children move through it, with the fluid certainty of bodies born in the hollow, and I will never have Veft's hands, and I will never have Kett's reading of the stone, and I will never have the pair's coordination, the inseparable grace of two people who have been one thing in two bodies for long enough that the bodies have forgotten they are two.

But I fit the way the fire fits the basin. Imperfectly. With gaps. The basin is stone and the fire is fire and the fitting is not a matching but a willingness — a willingness to be in the space, to be shaped by the space, to let the space shape you, and to shape the space back, because the basin cracks and the fire is moved and the new basin is shaped by the fire as much as the fire is shaped by the basin, and this is what it is to live in Mossbrug. Not to be accommodated. Not to be housed. To be *in* the place the way the water is in the stone — not contained by it but belonging to it, seeping through its fractures, finding the level where the surface sits and resting there.

I rest here. I rest in the way that water rests, which is not stillness but flow, which is not stopping but finding the level, which is not peace but saturation, which is the condition of being fully in the place where you are, with no dry part of you reaching for a dry part of the world, with no part of you unchanged by the wet, and the wet is not water, the wet is *Mossbrug*, the wet is the stone and the moss and the cold and the fire and the old woman and the girl and the tall one and Kett and the pair and the children on the rooftops and the frogs in the channels and the cats in the walls and the skil in the water and the bone-moss tea and the flatbread and the pale sorrel and the—

I am running the fire. That is what the old woman calls it — not tending, not keeping, *running*, the way you run a river, the way the water runs the channel, and I am running the fire and the fire is running me and I am here and the stone is weeping and the moss is drinking and the town continues and I continue with it, and I do not know if this is home because I have lost the ability to recognize the category, because home requires a before and an after and I am in a place where time is measured in water and water does not have a before and an after, water has a level, and I have found mine.

---

The tall one asked me something. The last thing. On the rooftop, in the evening, in the diffused light that has no source and no shadow. He asked it in my language, which he speaks with the patience of someone carrying water in his hands, carefully, aware of spillage.

*Will you stay?*

I looked at him. I looked at the fire, which was orange, the only orange. I looked at the old woman, whose hands were in the ash. I looked at the girl, whose hands were clean. I looked at the hollow, at the buildings heaped and leaning, at the moss on the walls, blue-green in the light that seemed to come from inside the stone. I looked at my hands, which were wet, which were changing, which had a faint film of green in the lines of the palms.

I said: *The stone tells me where it wants to be.*

He looked at me the way Kett looked at me when I asked about the walls — not with confusion but with the expression of someone who has heard the obvious stated, and who is pleased, quietly, that someone has arrived at the obvious by the long way around.

He did not say goodbye. He said the water-over-stone words. I said them back. He descended into the hollow.

I did not watch him go. I turned to the fire. I picked up the driftwood. It was wet. It was always wet. I set it beside the flame to dry.

---

I tend the fire. The fire tends me. The stone weeps. The moss drinks. I am in Mossbrug and Mossbrug is in me and the water has found the level and the level is here.
