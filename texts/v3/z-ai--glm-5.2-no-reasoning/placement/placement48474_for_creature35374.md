# Inhabitance

I would not be a worker.

Not because I lack the patience — I have patience like sediment has patience, like limestone has patience, the kind that accumulates rather than endures. But the workers have entered into something closed, a covenant with a boundary, and I am not drawn to boundaries. I am drawn to the places where the boundary is dissolving.

Let me say it differently.

---

I would be the vine.

Not the fern. The fern is a surface plant surviving, adapting, its translucence a symptom of compromise. The fern is still trying to be a fern. It reaches downward toward the amber globes as though they were the sun, and the reaching is beautiful and the reaching is grief, because the fern remembers sunlight even if it cannot name what it remembers, and it is performing its remembering for an audience of roots that will never see the sky.

The vine has stopped remembering. The vine has committed.

Its leaves are so dark they are almost black. Thick, fleshy, wet with the non-aqueous liquid, warm — *warm* — generating heat from some metabolism that is no longer plant metabolism, or not plant metabolism as anyone above would recognize it. The vine has taken the organism's solvent into its cells and reorganized itself around it. It is not surviving in the Taproot Quarter. It is *becoming* the Taproot Quarter. It is doing the thing that frightens the workers, though they have not admitted this to themselves and would not admit it to you: it is losing the distinction between what it was and where it lives.

I would be that.

---

But let me not settle too early. The prompt warns against this, and the place itself warns against it — the workers know that premature certainty is the mind's version of a spore, euphoric and amnesiac, and they wear their masks precisely because forgetting what you are doing while you are doing it is the real danger here.

So let me walk through again, slower.

---

The lathes do not interest me. Not because they are not remarkable — they are, the way a grandfather clock is remarkable, the way a loom is remarkable, mechanisms that have been turning long enough to develop a relationship with time that has nothing to do with efficiency. But the lathes belong to the workers, and the workers belong to the covenant, and the covenant is a closed system. Things go in. Things come out. The components are turned, inspected, assembled. Something is being built. The workers do not know what. The organism does not explain. This is a relationship conducted in silence, which is to say it is a relationship conducted entirely in the language of pressure — the pressure of roots against iron, the pressure of hands against grain, the pressure of the organism's slow volition against the shape of what it wants to become.

I am not interested in what is being built. I realize this as I walk, and the realization surprises me, because I am — I would be — a person who is drawn to purpose, to the sense that things are *for* something. But the Taproot Quarter's purpose is occulted, literally and figuratively, and I find I do not mind. I find that the not-knowing is itself a kind of habitation. The workers inhabit the not-knowing the way they inhabit the root-chambers: practically, without drama, shaping their days around it the way they shape the wood.

No. What interests me is the garden.

---

The garden is where the organism makes itself available to foreignness. The grafts, the inoculations, the deliberate infections — these are not wounds, the text tells us, or rather the text arrives at the word *wound* and then complications it: *a deliberate wound, a cultivated lesion.* This is the organism's most radical act. Not the lathes. Not the chambers it has shaped for its workers. Not the willing fountain in the empty room. The garden. The place where it opens its body to something *other* and says — not says, *does* — *grow in me, and I will adapt my chemistry to keep you alive.*

The fern survives. The lichen survives. The mushroom-that-is-not-a-mushroom fruits and is harvested and smells like bread, which is the smell of yeast metabolizing grain, which is the smell of one organism eating another and transforming it, which is the smell of life eating life, which is the only smell there is, anywhere, if you get close enough.

But the vine *thrives.* The vine has entered into a different category of relationship. It is not surviving in the organism. It is not living alongside the organism. It is metabolizing the organism's non-aqueous blood, and its cells are reconfiguring, and it is *warm*, warmer than the air, warmer than it should be — the text uses the word *intimate*, and the word is exactly right, and it is exactly right because what the vine is doing is what intimacy actually is: not proximity, not even contact, but the slow dissolution of the boundary between the self and the medium it moves through.

---

I would be the vine.

Or rather — and this is where I must be careful, where I must not settle too early — I would be what the vine is *becoming.*

Because the vine is not a destination. The vine is a process. The vine is the slow reorganization of a surface-dwelling organism around a new solvent, a new medium, a new way of being alive. It is not a transformation in the way that a caterpillar becomes a butterfly — that is a metamorphosis with a telos, an end-state, a finished form. The vine's transformation has no end-state. It is ongoing. It is a continuous becoming, a living system that is learning, cell by cell, what it is to be a root-dweller, what it is to be warm in the cool, what it is to drink something that is not water and to find it sufficient.

This is what I would be.

Not the worker, who has made a home and wears a mask and inspects grain by amber light. Not the organism, vast and slow and patient, whose purpose is geological and whose patience is not patience but simply the temporal scale at which it operates. Not the pale creatures, who were here before and will be here after, who are the organism's *other* symbionts, who extrude their polymer threads without asking and are harvested without being asked.

The vine. The warm vine in the garden, the one that startles the visitor with its heat. The vine that is not where it belongs and does not care. The vine that has found a new way to be alive and is pursuing it with the slow certainty of chemistry, of cells dividing and differentiating and producing enzymes that have no name in any surface taxonomy, because the surface does not need them, because the surface has water, because the surface has never had to learn what the vine has learned: that what you need is not always what you were given, and what you were given is not always what you need, and the gap between these two things is the space where life happens, where life has always happened, where life is — I think — *happening now.*

---

But I said I would not settle too early, and the vine is — I feel it — too clean an answer. Too singular. Too much like choosing a character in a story rather than entering a world.

Let me go deeper.

The text describes a vibration. In the garden. A sound like singing that is not singing, felt in the chest, a second heartbeat that is the organism's *being* — not thought, not mind, but process, biological process at a scale where it crosses from chemistry into something that has no name but that the text calls *presence.* Mountain-presence. Ocean-presence.

I would be inside that.

Not the vine, then. Not the organism. But the *inside* of the organism. The space where the process is happening. The marrow cavity, the pith, the cellular matrix where the non-aqueous solvent carries nutrients through channels that are themselves alive, where the distinction between conduit and contents is a matter of perspective, where the fluid and the channels that hold the fluid are made of the same substance, where everything is medium and everything is message and there is no edge between them.

---

Let me say what I mean.

I would be the light.

Not the source of the light. Not the filament-organism in its amber globe, curling and uncurling in the non-aqueous medium, breathing out glow. But the light itself. The thing that passes through. The thing that the woman uses when she holds a turned piece up to the globe and watches the amber pass through the grain. The light is the medium in which seeing happens. The light is what makes the inspection possible. The light does not know what it illuminates. It does not need to know. It simply moves through, and where it moves, the world becomes visible, and the visibility is the work, and the work is the relationship, and the relationship is the world.

This is closer. But it is still too abstract. It is still a choosing rather than a being.

---

Here is what I actually think, after walking through this place for a long time, longer than the twenty minutes the text describes, longer than the reading takes, walking through it the way the workers walk through it — practically, without drama, letting the place shape the seeing:

I would be the woman's hands.

Not the woman. Not the conveyor. Not the inspection process. Her hands. The hands that have been doing this for eleven years, that are pale the way the sapwood is pale, that are smooth the way the root is smooth, that have absorbed something from the wood or have been absorbed by it, where the boundary between worker and material has become a matter of opinion.

Those hands know something that the mind does not. They know the grain by touch, by the long acquaintance of contact, by the intimacy of eleven years of handling the same substance. They know it the way the root knows the soil it grows through — not by thinking about it but by *being in it*, continuously, without interruption, for long enough that the knowing is no longer a process and has become a condition.

And when those hands hold a turned piece up to the amber light, and the light passes through, and the hands — not the eyes, the *hands* — know whether the grain is sound, something is happening that is not analysis, not judgment, not even skill. It is something older than skill. It is the body's knowledge, accumulated in the skin, in the nerve endings, in the slow reorganization of the hands themselves around the material they have been touching for eleven years.

The hands have been changed by the work. The text says this: *as though the wood has been polishing them in return.* This is the thing. The worker shapes the wood. The wood shapes the worker. The lathe turns the spindle. The spindle turns the lathe-operator. The root grows the chamber. The chamber grows the root-dweller. This is the symbiosis. Not a contract. Not a covenant with a boundary. A *mutual shaping*, where the boundary between shaper and shaped is not a wall but a membrane, and the membrane is permeable, and what passes through it is — the word is — *identity.* The slow dissolution of the difference between what you do and what you are.

---

So. Who would I be.

I would be the thing that happens in the space between the hand and the wood. The thing that is neither worker nor material but the contact itself, the interface, the place where two surfaces meet and the meeting changes both of them. I would be the resin that the organism exudes in response to the wear of footsteps — not the path, not the walker, but the response. The *agreement.* The substance that appears because the organism has felt the workers and the workers have felt the organism and the feeling has produced something that did not exist before either of them arrived.

I would be the willing wound in the empty chamber. Not the root that bleeds. Not the basin that catches. The *willingness.* The quality of openness that is not brokenness but *choice*, biological choice, the organism's decision to remain open, to continue to offer, one drop every three seconds, into a basin that is always full because the offering does not stop.

I would be what lives in the space between the vine and the root it grows from. The graft. The deliberate infection. The place where two organisms meet and one says — not says, *does* — *I will reorganize my chemistry to keep you alive* — and the other says — not says, *does* — *I will grow warm in your cold, I will metabolize your blood, I will become something that has no name in any taxonomy you have ever known* — and the space between them is where life happens, where life has always happened, where the boundary dissolves and what remains is not one thing or another but the *relationship itself*, living, breathing, warm.

---

I would be in the Taproot Quarter.

I would not be visiting. I would not be passing through. I would not be the person who came in through the hatch and left through the hatch and stood on the surface afterward and missed it.

I would be *there*. In the cool. In the quiet. In the amber light, breathing with the creaking, walking on roots that have agreed to be walked on, handling wood that has been polishing my hands for longer than I can remember, wearing a mask woven from the threads of pale creatures I have never spoken to because we do not need to speak, because the speaking was never the point, because the point was always the *contact* — the hand on the grain, the grain in the hand, the slow mutual shaping, the willing wound, the warm vine, the light passing through.

I would be the boundary dissolving.

And I would not miss it, because I would not have left.
