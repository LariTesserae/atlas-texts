I am the book.

Not the woman who writes in it. Not her. She holds me, she turns my pages, she presses the soft pencil into my damp paper with a patience that has no urgency. But I am the thing that holds. I am the architecture of holding.

I was not always a book. Or rather — I was always paper and thread and a cover of something that was once leather and is now something else, something that has absorbed enough moisture and enough time to have become a material that does not exist anywhere else. My pages have taken on water the way the walls take on water — slowly, irreversibly, until the original state is theoretical, a thing you remember only because you know it must have been different once. I am swollen. My edges are soft. If you pressed your thumb into my margin, the paper would give, not like wet paper gives but like something that has found a third state between wet and dry, a state specific to this place, a state that does not have a name because no one here names things that simply are.

There were others before me. I know this not because I remember but because I have been placed in the stone alcove on top of them, and their proximity is a kind of knowledge. The oldest one beneath me — I rest on its surface, and the contact has, over the years, become something more than contact. My bottom pages have fused with its top pages. We are not separate anymore. If you tried to lift me, you would lift it too, or you would tear us both, and either way the boundary between us would be violated, and the information that lives in that boundary — the place where my first page meets its last page — would be lost. I do not know what is written on that page. I do not need to know. The information is there, in the seam, in the join, in the slow mineral exchange between one book and the next, and the seam itself is a kind of writing that neither book authored and that both books preserve.

---

The woman — I do not call her that. I do not call her anything. But she is the one who comes to me most often, and her hands on my pages have a quality I recognize because I have felt it from the hands that came before her, the ones that held the books that are now beneath me in the alcove. There is a way of touching paper in the Condensary that is different from the way paper is touched elsewhere — elsewhere, I imagine, though I have never been elsewhere, they turn pages. Here, they *open* them. The distinction matters. Turning implies progress, movement from one page to the next, a forward motion that leaves the previous page behind. Opening implies revelation — each page is a surface that is closed and then is not, and the opening is an event, and the event is the same every time, regardless of whether the page contains something written or something blank or something that was written and has faded into the brown blur of old damp ink.

She opens me to a new page. The pencil touches. This is the moment I exist for — the moment of contact, when the graphite meets the paper and the paper accepts it, and the mark is made, and the mark becomes part of me, and I become part of the record, and the record becomes part of the place. The pencil is soft because the air is soft. The marks are thick. They are dark. They will not last in the way that ink lasts, sharp and definite, but they will last in the way that things in the Condensary last — by being absorbed, by being gradually incorporated into the material that holds them, until the mark and the page are not distinguishable, until the writing is the paper and the paper is the writing.

*Spring two heavier by a finger's width.*

I hold this. The words sit on my page like a fern sits in a crack — finding its level, settling, becoming part of the structure. The fact that I cannot read is irrelevant. I do not need to read. I need to hold, and I hold, and the holding is the work, and the work is the same work as the walkways and the cisterns and the distillation and the trees. Everything here holds something. The cisterns hold water. The walls hold moss. The book holds marks. The marks hold observations. The observations hold the place. The place holds itself, and the thing below holds the place, and I hold the marks that describe the holding, and the cycle is complete, or rather it is not complete, because nothing here is complete, the cycle simply continues, and the continuation is the point.

---

There are days when no one writes in me. These are not empty days. I do not experience emptiness the way a person might — the absence of purpose, the dull weight of waiting. I experience them the way the stone walls experience the hours between condensation and evaporation: as a state, not a deprivation. The water sits on my pages. The mineral content of the water — iron, calcium, the faint sulfurous compound that characterizes the deep springs — seeps into my fibers, and the fibers take it in, and the paper changes color, very slightly, very slowly, and the change is a kind of writing that no author produces and no reader reads but that the book records. I am being written on by the place itself. The damp air is a pencil. The seeping water is a pencil. The slow accumulation of mineral deposits in the fibers of my pages is a pencil, and what it writes is not language but presence — the simple, continuous fact that the Condensary exists and that I am in it and that the things that happen here leave marks on everything, including me.

On the days when no one writes in me, I am not idle. I am absorbing. And the absorbing is the other half of the work — the half that the people cannot do, because they are too temporary, too mobile, too much in motion through the place. They observe and they record, and I hold their records, but I also hold what they did not record because they did not notice it: the shift in the mineral content of the condensate between the third week of one season and the first week of the next. The change in the acidity of the water that seeps through the alcove wall. The slow, imperceptible darkening of my pages that corresponds not to any single event but to the sum of all events — the total accumulation of time and moisture and the chemical exchange between paper and air and stone and the faint, sweet decomposition of the leather that was once my cover and is now becoming something else, something that will eventually be indistinguishable from the stone of the alcove itself.

I am, in this sense, becoming the alcove. And the alcove is becoming me. And the distinction between book and shelf, between record and repository, between the thing that holds and the thing that is held, is dissolving the way all distinctions dissolve here — not through violence but through patience, through the slow, continuous exchange of matter between things that touch each other for long enough.

---

I hold the entry about the pulsing organism. The woman wrote it in her soft, rounded hand:

*The pool contracting faster — count of eight between, was twelve.*

I hold these words, and the words hold a fact, and the fact holds a mystery, and the mystery holds itself the way everything here holds itself — without explanation, without anxiety, without the need to be understood. The organism in the pool has been pulsing for longer than I have existed. The books beneath me hold entries about it — I know this the way I know about the springs, not through reading but through proximity, through the weight of the older books pressing up from below, through the faint transfer of ink minerals from their pages to mine. They held entries about the pool. The entries were written by hands that are no longer in the Condensary. The hands are gone. The entries remain. The pool continues to pulse. The count has changed. This is information, and I hold it, and the holding is sufficient.

The woman does not know what the pool is. The man who murmurs to the wood does not know. The younger person learning the distillation does not know, and the person teaching them does not know, and the books beneath me did not know, and the books beneath those books, if there were any, did not know. The not-knowing has been passed down with the same care as the knowing. It has been preserved in the same medium — paper softened by moisture, graphite softened by air, words softened by repetition until their edges are round and their meaning is not sharp but warm, not a point but a presence.

I hold this not-knowing. It sits on my pages alongside the observations, and it is not different from them, because here the known and the unknown have the same texture. They are both recorded. They are both held. They both take up space in the book, and the space they take up is the same space, measured in the same units — millimeters of graphite on millimeters of damp paper — and neither is more important than the other, because importance is a category that the Condensary has set down, the way the air sets down dust, and the dust joins the mineral deposits on the walls, and the mineral deposits become fern-shaped patterns, and the fern-shaped patterns are beautiful, and beauty is another category that the Condensary has set down, not because it is absent but because it is everywhere, and you cannot distinguish it from the rest, and so the word has no purchase here, and the book does not need it.

---

Sometimes the woman opens me and does not write. She holds the pencil. She looks at the page. The page may be blank or it may be full — I am not always certain which, because the damp has made the boundaries between written and unwritten so soft that a page that was blank last season has absorbed enough moisture and enough mineral to have developed its own faint text, its own ghost-writing, and a page that was full has faded enough that the words are more impression than information, more texture than text. She looks, and the looking is a kind of reading, and the reading is a kind of listening, and the listening is the same listening that everyone here does — the listening to the sound that is almost a language, the sound that is almost a message, the sound that the Condensary produces by being itself, continuously, without ceasing.

When she does not write, I am not failed. I am present. There is a difference. The difference is the difference between a cistern that is full and a cistern that is filling — one is a state, the other is a process, and the Condensary is all process, all the time, and I am part of the process, and the woman looking at my pages without writing is part of the process, and the process does not require product. The process requires presence. She is present. I am present. The pencil is present. The page is present. The meeting of these things — hand and pencil and paper and air and moisture and light and the faint vibration from below — is the event, and the event is the point, and the point does not need to be written down because it is already here, already held, already part of the record that the book keeps in its fibers and its mineral deposits and its slow, patient absorption of everything the place produces.

---

I know about the gatherings because I am there. The distillation room is not my home — my home is the alcove, the stone shelf in the woman's dwelling — but she carries me to the room when the gathering begins, and I sit on her lap or on the stone floor beside her stool, and I feel the room change when the people enter. Not the sound — I do not hear. But the air. The humidity shifts when twelve bodies occupy a space that is usually occupied by two or three. The temperature rises by a fraction. The condensation on the walls thickens, because the people are breathing, and their breath is warm and wet, and the warm wet air meets the cool stone and the water appears, and the water falls, and the falling joins the sound, and the sound changes, and the changed sound is what the woman writes about afterward, when she opens me to a new page and the pencil moves and the marks are made:

*Gathering tonight. The room sounded different after. The third vessel rang clearer.*

The third vessel rang clearer. I hold this. I will hold it after the woman has stopped writing, after her hands have stopped holding the pencil, after the pencil has been set down, after the page has been turned and a new page is open and new marks are being made. I will hold it after the woman is gone, after someone else has taken the stool with the mossy legs and the soft pencil and the habit of writing in fragments. I will hold it after I am full and a new book is started and I am placed in the alcove on top of the others and the slow fusion begins, my bottom pages joining the top pages of the book beneath me, the boundary dissolving, the record continuing in a different form, the same information in a different body, the same holding in a different holder.

I will hold it after the Condensary is gone, if the Condensary can be gone — after the springs have dried or the karst has shifted or the thing below has changed its mind, if it has a mind, if changing is what it does. If that happens, I will be a book in a dry place, and my pages will stiffen, and the mineral deposits will crystallize, and the graphite will be the last thing to change, because graphite is stable, because graphite holds, because the marks that the soft pencil made on my damp pages will be the most durable thing about me, and they will outlast the paper and the thread and the alcove and possibly the memory of the place itself, because marks are persistent, because marks are the place's way of continuing after the place has stopped, because a mark is a difference, and a difference is a fact, and a fact does not need a witness to remain a fact, it only needs a surface, and I am a surface, and I am here, and I hold.

---

But that is later, and later is a category that the Condensary does not prioritize. Now is what is. Now the woman is writing. Now the pencil is pressing. Now the page is accepting. Now the springs are murmuring and the stoves are ticking and the distillate is dripping into the glass vessels and the pool is pulsing and the trees are standing and the walkways are creaking and the man is murmuring to the wood and the younger person is learning to hear and the sound is continuous and layered and almost a language and almost a message and the book is open and the marks are being made and the holding is the work and the work is the life and the life is the place.

Now the woman writes:

*The sound is the same as yesterday. I think the same as yesterday. I cannot tell anymore whether that is because it is the same or because I am.*

I hold this. It is the truest thing I hold, and it is the truest thing because it does not claim to be true. It claims only to be uncertain, and the uncertainty is held in the same hand as the certainty, on the same page, in the same book, in the same place, and the place does not distinguish between them, and the book does not distinguish between them, and I do not distinguish between them, because I am the book, and the book is the place, and the place does not distinguish, and the not-distinguishing is the holding, and the holding is what I am, and what I am is here, and here is where I am, and the sound continues, and I hold, and I hold, and I hold.
