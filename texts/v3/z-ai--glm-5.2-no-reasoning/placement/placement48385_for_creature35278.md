# What I Am in the Underwrought

I come down the stair because I am the kind of person who takes the narrow switchback when they find it. I don't mean this metaphorically. I mean I have always been drawn to the infrastructure of things — the service corridors, the pump rooms, the spaces behind the visible — and I have a habit of following them past the point of comfort. Not courage. Just a particular kind of curiosity that doesn't know when to stop, that has always mistaken the interesting for the safe.

So I descend, and the air changes, and I keep going, and by the time I reach the bottom I have understood something about the place that I can't yet articulate, and I stay.

---

## I stay

Not permanently. Not in a dramatic sense. But I come back. I come back the next day, and the day after that, and the day after that, and eventually I am someone who has a reason to be here — I have fabricated the reason, or the reason has fabricated me, it is hard to tell the difference after a while. I bring a notebook. I bring a lamp of my own, though I find I don't always use it. I bring a thermos of coffee that goes cold in the warm air. I bring a stool, and I leave it here, and no one takes it, because no one comes down here except me.

I am not a scientist. I want to be clear about this. I am not a botanist or a mycologist or a geologist. I am not studying the Underwrought in any rigorous way. I am a person who left a job I can no longer remember the details of, who lives in the town above the stair, who found the stair one afternoon and went down it and came back and went down again and kept going down until the going-down became the shape of my days.

I think I am keeping records. I have a notebook, and I write in it, but what I write is not data. It's description. I write about the color of the moss, which I am learning to distinguish — there are at least nine shades of green in the chamber, and I have given them names that are not scientific names, names like *stagnant* and *eager* and *the green that is almost gray* and *the green that is almost light*. I write about the vine, which I have started calling something, not a name exactly, more a designation, because I want to be able to refer to it in my notes without describing it each time. I call it the Tenant.

I write about the lights. I am becoming obsessed with the lights. The arrhythmic flicker of the surviving lamps has begun to feel like something I can almost predict, not in the sense that I know when a particular lamp will come on or go off, but in the sense that I can feel the system's breathing, the way it surges and recedes, the way the power flows and doesn't flow, and I have started to write down the times of the flickers, and the pattern is not a pattern, it is a process, and I am trying to learn the process the way you learn the habits of someone you live with.

I am not a scientist. I am a person who has fallen into the habit of visiting a place, and the place has become the habit, and the habit has become the structure of my days, and I don't know if that makes me an observer or an inhabitant or something else.

I think it makes me something else.

---

## What I notice

The first thing I noticed, after the visits became regular, was that the vine has a rhythm.

Not a visible rhythm, not something you'd see in a single visit. But over weeks — I come down most days, stay for an hour, sometimes two, sometimes longer when I lose track of time, which happens more often than I'd like to admit — over weeks, I noticed that the vine's new growth comes in flushes. Not every few weeks, as I first thought, but in a cycle that seems to correspond to something I can't identify. A new tendril appears, pale and tender, and it reaches for a few days, and then it stops, and the tip darkens, and the vine seems to pause, and then another tendril starts somewhere else. The pauses are not regular. The starts are not regular. But there is a logic to it, a cadence, and I am starting to think the vine is responding to the water in the pipes — not the water itself, which is constant, but the pressure, which varies, which surges and drops on a cycle that I can hear in the boiler's resonance and that I think the vine can feel in its roots.

The vine and I are listening to the same heartbeat.

The second thing I noticed is that the mushrooms in the corners are not randomly distributed. They cluster, yes, but they cluster in lines, in branching lines that follow the cracks in the floor, and the cracks in the floor follow the grout lines between tiles, and the grout lines connect to the drainage channels, and the drainage channels connect to the places where the water collects, and the water collects where the floor slopes, and the floor slopes because the builders made it slope. The mushrooms are growing in the shape of the builders' drainage plan. They are following a design that was made for water, and they are doing it because the water brings them what they need, and the water goes where it was told to go, and the mushrooms go where the water goes. The builders are still shaping this place, through the water, through the slope of the floor, through the channels they cut. The builders are dead or gone and they are still here, in the path of a mushroom.

The third thing I noticed is something I almost didn't notice, because it is so small and so slow that it is almost not there. The rust on the iron beams is not just rust. It is not just iron oxide. It is layered — layers of different colors, different textures, and in some places, between the layers, there is green. Not moss-green. Not the green of the vine. A different green, a green that is almost blue, a green that I think is a copper compound, and I think it is there because the iron was galvanized, or because the pipes were joined with copper fittings, or because there is copper in the alloy of the beams, and the water has found it and carried it out and deposited it in the rust, and the rust has become a record, a stratigraphy, a history of the iron's dissolution written in color. The oldest layers, deepest, are black — the first oxidation, the first reaction of iron with water. Above that, red-brown — the familiar rust, the everyday decay. Above that, orange — the rust that has been exposed to air and water in cycles. And between and through it all, threads of blue-green, copper, carried by water, deposited by evaporation, a mineral thread in a mineral tapestry. The rust is a geology. The rust is a sediment record. The rust is telling the story of this place in a language I am learning to read but will never speak.

I notice these things and I write them down, and the writing down is the thing I do, and I don't know if it matters, and I don't know if the noticing matters, and I have stopped asking whether it matters, because the asking is a habit from the surface, from the world where things are supposed to matter, and down here the question doesn't quite apply. Down here, things don't matter or not-matter. They just are. The vine is. The moss is. The water is. The rust is. My notebook is. I am. The question of whether we matter is a question that belongs to a world with more light.

---

## What I don't do

I don't touch the vine.

I want to. I want to feel the texture of its stems, the woody bark of the old growth, the tender give of the new tips. I want to know what it feels like to run my fingers along a tendril that has wrapped itself around an iron pipe and held on for years. But I don't. I am afraid — not of the vine, not of being hurt, but of changing it. Of leaving the oil of my skin on a surface that has been untouched for so long. Of introducing something — a bacterium, a spore, a compound from the surface world — that wasn't here before. I am afraid of being a contamination. I am afraid of mattering in the wrong way.

I don't move the tools on the pegboard. They are where they are, and the paint outlines show where the missing ones were, and the arrangement is a record of someone else's order, someone else's system of meaning, and I don't know what it means and I don't want to disrupt it. The wrench that still hangs on its peg, corroded into a sculpture of its former self, is where it was put. It was put there by a person who knew where it went, and it has stayed there, and it will stay there, and I am not going to be the one who moves it.

I don't pick the mushrooms. I don't pull the ferns. I don't clear the moss. I don't clean anything, don't fix anything, don't try to make the lamps work or the pump run or the pipes carry water again. I am not a restorer. I am not a curator. I am not here to return this place to what it was. I am here to be here while it is what it is.

I don't bring other people. I have thought about it. I have thought about showing someone the chamber, the vine, the mushrooms, the creature in the puddle. I have imagined bringing someone down the stair and watching their face as the air changes and the light dims and the warmth closes around them. But I don't. I think it might be that I am afraid they would see it differently than I do — that they would see a ruin, a damp hole, a place that should be cleaned up or sealed off or forgotten. Or that they would see it the same way I do, and that would be worse, because then it wouldn't be mine, and I would have to share it, and the sharing would change it, and I don't want it to change.

This is possessive. I know it is possessive. I know that wanting a place to be yours, wanting to be the only one who knows it, is a form of ownership, and that ownership is a surface habit, a habit of the world where things can be owned. Down here, ownership doesn't apply. The vine doesn't belong to me. The chamber doesn't belong to me. I belong to it, maybe, in the sense that I keep coming back, in the sense that it has become the structure of my days, in the sense that I have started to think in its rhythms, to wake when the lights flicker on (which they do — I have started waking at three in the morning, and I think it is because the power surges then, and I think I can feel it, which is impossible, but I think it anyway). But it doesn't belong to me. It belongs to the water and the heat and the slow processes that have been running since before I found the stair and that will be running long after I stop coming down.

I am a visitor who has mistaken the frequency of his visits for residency. I am a regular. I am a habitué. I am the chamber's most devoted and least necessary attendee.

---

## What I am becoming

I said I wasn't a scientist. I should also say I am not exactly a person anymore, not in the way I was when I first came down the stair. Something has happened to me, or is happening, and I don't mean this in a mystical or supernatural way. I mean it in the way that a person changes when they spend enough time in a place that the place starts to shape them.

I have started to sweat differently. Not more, not less, but the smell of it has changed — I smell like the chamber now, like wet stone and iron and the sweetness underneath, and I don't know if that's because I am carrying the smell out with me or because my body has started to produce it, has started to metabolize something in the air or the water or the warmth that it wasn't metabolizing before.

I have started to see in the dark. Not well. Not the way a cat sees or an owl sees. But better than I used to. The first time I came down, I needed my lamp to navigate beyond the first twenty feet. Now I can walk the length of the chamber in the dim amber of the surviving lamps, and in the corners, where the mushrooms glow, I can see — barely, just barely — the shapes of things, the edges of the benches, the curve of the boiler, the line of the pipes. My eyes have adapted. The rods in my retina have recalibrated to a world of almost-no-light, and I can see in a way I couldn't before, and I don't know if this is a gain or a loss.

I have started to hear the water in the pipes as a language. Not a language I understand — not a language with words or grammar or meaning — but a language in the sense that it is a structured system of sounds that varies in ways that are not random, that carries information, that I am learning to distinguish. The pulse in the pipes near the boiler is different from the pulse in the pipes near the tanks, and both are different from the murmur of the condensation on the walls, and all three are different from the sound of the water on the floor, which is not a sound at all but a motion, a visible motion, the slow sheets sliding across the tiles, and I can read it now, I can look at the water on the floor and tell you which way it is flowing and how fast and whether it has been flowing faster or slower than yesterday, and I can't explain how I do this, I just do it, the way you can read a face.

I am becoming a part of the place's monitoring system. I am the sensor it grew for itself.

---

## The creature

I spend a lot of time watching the creature in the puddle.

It is small. It is translucent. It moves with a motion that is either swimming or writhing, and I have spent long enough watching it that I no longer think the distinction matters. It moves the way it moves. It is alive the way it is alive. It doesn't know I am here.

Or does it? Can it sense the shadow of my head blocking the stair-light? Can it feel the vibration of my boots on the tiles? Can it taste the coffee I spilled last week, the dark thread of it dissolving in the puddle's mineral water, the caffeine and the acids and the organic compounds from the surface world seeping into its world? I spilled the coffee by accident and I was horrified — horrified at the contamination, horrified at the introduction — and I mopped it up as best I could with my sleeve, but some of it reached the puddle, and I watched for days to see if it would affect the creature, and it didn't, or I couldn't tell, and I was both relieved and oddly disappointed.

I think about the creature more than I think about the vine or the moss or the fungi. It is the only thing in this chamber that moves at a speed I can perceive. Everything else moves on a timescale that makes glaciers look hasty — the vine grows in weeks, the moss spreads in months, the rust advances in years. But the creature moves in real time. It swims. It writhes. It changes direction. It is alive in a way that I can watch being alive, and that makes it the most relatable thing in the chamber, the thing I can map most easily onto my own experience of being alive.

And yet I am not sure it is the thing I am closest to. I think I may be closer to the vine. The vine and I have the same habit: we come here every day, we follow the paths that were laid out before we arrived, we reach for the light, we grow slowly, we adapt to the conditions, and we don't leave. The vine and I are both organisms that have chosen this place, or been chosen by it, and we are both responding to it in the only ways we know how.

But the creature is the thing I watch. There is something about its smallness, its trans lucidity, its aloneness in the puddle, that I recognize. It is the only one of its kind in the chamber — or the only one I can see — and it doesn't know that, or if it does, it doesn't seem to mind. It moves through the water. It feeds. It lives. It is a body in a place, doing what bodies do in places, and it is fine.

I want to be like the creature. I want to be fine.

---

## What I am

I am the thing the chamber didn't ask for and doesn't need. I am the animal that came back. I am the vertebrate, the large warm-blooded mammal with my big brain and my big eyes and my big demands, and I came down the stair and I found a world that was running fine without me, and instead of leaving, I stayed, and instead of destroying it, I watched, and instead of watching from outside, I let it change me.

I am not the builder. I am not the inheritor. I am not the guardian or the keeper or the steward. I am a witness. I am a body in the room, adding its warmth to the warmth, its moisture to the moisture, its carbon dioxide to the air for the moss to breathe. I am a small part of the system now. I am a thread in the mycelium, a drop in the water, a flicker in the light. I am the part that can write, that can take notes, that can notice the nine shades of green and name them, that can hear the heartbeat in the pipes and feel the rhythm of the vine. I am the part that knows it is a part.

And I am the part that will leave. Not today, not tomorrow, but eventually. I will stop coming down the stair, or the stair will collapse, or I will die, and the chamber will go on without me, and the vine will grow over the place where my stool sat, and the moss will colonize the pages of my notebook if I leave it, and the water will dissolve the ink and carry it into the cracks and the channels and the puddles, and the creature will swim through the diluted words, and nothing I wrote will matter, and nothing I noticed will matter, and the place will be exactly what it was before I came and exactly what it will be after I go and exactly what it is right now, while I sit here in the warm dark and listen to the water and watch the light flicker and feel the heat rise through the soles of my boots and know — not believe, not hope, but know — that I am in the presence of something that is alive and that does not need me and that is letting me be here anyway, and that this is enough, and that I am enough, and that the word *enough* doesn't mean anything down here but I am using it anyway because I brought it with me from the surface and it is the word I have for the feeling of being in a place that is complete without you and that lets you be there.

Enough.

I go. The water keeps moving. The vine keeps growing. The creature in the puddle turns, slowly, in its world, and does not watch me leave.

I come back tomorrow.
