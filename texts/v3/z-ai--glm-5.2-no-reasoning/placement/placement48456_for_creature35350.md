# The Seventh Platform

---

I arrived with a practical purpose and a limited vocabulary for what I'd find.

An assessment. That was the word in the paperwork — and there is paperwork, always, because the surface runs on paper the way the Rootworks runs on root-felt, and someone in an office somewhere had decided the Rootworks needed assessing. Product volume, equipment condition, occupational health. The forms had boxes. The boxes had categories. I carried them in a satchel that was waterproof in principle and damp in practice, and by the time I reached the bottom of the stairs the forms were curling at the edges and the ink on the header page had bled into the paper like something trying to escape.

I mention the forms because they are the last artifact of the world I came from, and because they are useless here, and because watching them become useless was my first real act of understanding. The ink ran. The categories didn't fit. There is no box on a government assessment form for *the wall is breathing* or *the light has a pulse* or *the person I need to interview is sitting with her hand in a compost pit and I think she's listening to it.*

---

I was given a platform. That was the first thing that happened — before the tour, before the introductions, before Harker looked at me with an expression I would come to recognize as her version of welcome, which is *I see you and I am not turning away.* Ren showed me the platform. It was one of the two empty ones — not Edra's, the other one, the one whose impression had already filled in, the root-felt risen back to level, a blank page.

"It'll shape itself," Ren said. That was all. No explanation of how long, no advice on sleeping position, no acknowledgment that the platform would be learning my body the way the body would be learning the platform. Ren said it the way you'd say *the sun will rise* — a fact about the world that doesn't require elaboration.

I slept on it that first night. Or the period that functioned as night — the light dimmed slightly on its four-minute breath, and the generator muttered, and the pumps thumped, and I lay on a surface that was softer than I expected and warmer than it had any right to be and I didn't sleep so much as *was absorbed.* The Rootworks doesn't let you fall asleep. It lets you sink. The way you sink into warm water. The way you sink into a thought you've been avoiding. My body pressed into the root-felt and the root-felt pressed back and the pressing was mutual and slow and I understood, before I understood anything else about this place, that the platform was not furniture. It was a negotiation between my weight and the world's willingness to hold me.

I woke with the taste of cellulose in my mouth. I had been breathing through the material. Not into it — *through* it. The root-felt was porous and the air moved through it and I had been breathing air that had passed through a living surface and it tasted like green and like time.

---

The first three days I tried to do my job.

I followed Harker. Harker let me follow. She didn't acknowledge me beyond the initial look — the *I see you* — and the absence of acknowledgment was not rejection. It was patience. Harker has been watched before. Harker has been assessed before, or the Rootworks has, and the assessments come and go and the Rootworks stays and Harker stays and the assessments end up in files that no one reads. She pruned. I followed. I held the satchel. I tried to ask questions from the form and the questions sounded absurd in the chamber's acoustics — thin, sharp, the verbal equivalent of a straight line in a place that has none.

*How would you characterize the working relationship between residents?*

Harker clipped a pale stem at the growth line. The stem was maybe an inch long. She examined the cut. She moved to the next one.

"There's work," she said. "We do it."

I wrote this down. The writing felt like a betrayal of something I couldn't name.

---

I followed Pell on the second day. Pell was more talkative, but not in a way that helped the forms. Pell talks to the generator, and the generator doesn't fill out paperwork. He was bleeding the fuel line when I found him — a wrench in one hand, a rag in the other, a thin stream of diesel flowing into a catch pan that had roots growing over its rim.

"She's rough today," Pell said, and I understood after a moment that he meant the generator. I wrote *generator — operational, maintenance ongoing* on the form and Pell looked at what I'd written and looked at me and I could see him deciding not to say what he was thinking, which was that *operational* was a word that described a category the generator had left behind years ago, that the generator was not operational the way a machine is operational but alive the way a stubborn animal is alive, kept going by familiarity and stubbornness and the daily attention of a man who had decided that leaving it was not an option he was willing to exercise.

Pell showed me the pumps. He showed me the grease can. He showed me the rust on the pipes where they entered the growth and the reddish-brown stain spreading into the surrounding tissue like a bruise that won't heal. He didn't editorialize. He didn't say *this is a problem.* He showed me. The showing was the statement. I wrote *infrastructure — aging, functional, no replacement parts available* and the words were accurate and they were nothing. They were the shadow of an understanding cast onto a form that couldn't hold the shape of the thing itself.

---

On the third day I found Ren at the compost pits.

Ren was sitting on the edge of a pit with both hands in the compost, eyes half-closed, and the heat from the pit was visible as a shimmer in the air above it, a distortion like the air above a road in summer, and Ren was — I have to be careful with the word — Ren was *listening.* Not to a sound. The compost doesn't make a sound that human ears can detect. But Ren was receiving something. Information. State. The way the pit was feeling, if a pit can feel, which in the Rootworks it can, because the pit is part of the root-mass and the root-mass is alive and alive things have states and states can be perceived if you've spent long enough learning the language of perception.

I sat next to Ren. I didn't put my hands in the compost. I wasn't ready for that. But I sat, and Ren didn't move, and we sat together for — I don't know. A long time. Long enough that the light cycled through several breaths. Long enough that the generator's mutter became a texture rather than a sound. Long enough that I stopped thinking about the forms in my satchel and started thinking about the heat rising from the pit and the smell — the green-rot sweetness concentrated here, intense, almost fermentable, the smell of transformation happening in real time — and I understood something.

I understood that Ren was not maintaining the compost. Ren was *in relationship with* the compost. The distinction matters. Maintenance is what you do to a machine. Relationship is what you have with a living thing. And the compost was alive. The pit was alive. The root-felt was alive. The chamber was alive. Not metaphorically. The walls breathed. The light breathed. The growth grew. The water wept. And Ren sat with it the way you sit with someone you know well — not talking, not doing, just being present, and the presence was the work.

---

On the fourth day I stopped filling out the forms.

I didn't decide to stop. I didn't have a moment of revelation or a crisis of conscience. I just stopped. The forms were in the satchel and the satchel was on my platform and I was on my platform and the root-felt had begun — I could feel it, or I imagined I could — the faintest adjustment to the shape of my shoulders. A softening. A welcoming. The surface was learning me the way I was learning it, and the forms were in the satchel and the satchel was sitting on a surface that was alive and the forms were irrelevant and the irrelevance was not a thought I had but a condition I was entering, the way you enter a room, the way you enter water, the way you enter a sleep that isn't quite sleep.

I picked up the shears.

Not Harker's shears — there was another pair, hanging on a hook near the south end, a pair that had been there long enough that the hook had grown around the handle, the root-felt creeping up the wood, claiming it. I took them down. The handle was warm. The blade was dull. I found the sharpening stone — the stone in the niche near the generator room, the stone worn concave by Harker's years of the same motion — and I sharpened the blade, and the motion of sharpening was the first thing I'd done in the Rootworks that felt like it belonged to the Rootworks, a slow repeated arc, the stone whispering against the steel, the sound joining the chamber's chord.

I started cutting. I didn't know the growth line — I didn't know where the shape of the Rootworks ended and the shape of the wilderness began — so I cut what looked new, what looked pale, what looked like it was reaching into a space that should be open. I cut badly. I cut at the wrong angle. I cut stems that were too thick and the shears slipped and the cut was ragged and I knew, without being told, that a ragged cut heals slower than a clean one and that Harker would have looked at my cuts and said nothing and felt something that was not anger but was adjacent to anger, the way a splinter is adjacent to a wound.

I cut for hours. My hands blistered. The blisters broke and the tannin got into the raw skin and it stung and the sting was — I want to say the sting was real, but that implies the rest of what I'd been doing was not real, and that's not what I mean. The sting was *specific.* The sting was the Rootworks entering my body through my skin, the way it had entered through my lungs, the way it was entering through the platform. The stain started that day. On my fingertips. Under my nails. The beginning of the brown-yellow that Harker carries like a geography, that Pell carries on his forearms, that Ren carries everywhere.

I didn't fill out the forms that night. I lay on the platform and the platform was slightly softer than the night before and the generator muttered and the pumps thumped and I thought: *I am being assessed. Not by the Rootworks. By the root-felt. By the platform. By the stone that sharpened the blade. By the stems I cut badly. They are learning me. They are filling out their own form, in their own language, and the form has no boxes and no categories and no header page and the ink does not bleed because there is no ink because the assessment is happening in the medium itself, in the contact, in the pressure, in the slow mutual adjustment of a body and a surface.*

---

I am not the seventh person. Or I am, but the word *person* doesn't mean here what it means in the office where the forms came from. In the office, a person is a unit. A role. A header on a form. In the Rootworks, a person is — I don't have the word. A site. A location where the system touches itself. A point of contact between the growth and the water and the machines and the compost and the light. Harker is a site. Pell is a site. Senn, Tori, Ren — sites. Edra was a site, and the site is closing over, the root-felt filling in, the system healing the way it heals everything.

I am a new site. The platform is learning my shape. My hands are learning the shears. My lungs are learning the air. The blisters are healing into calluses and the calluses are the beginning of the stain and the stain is the beginning of belonging and belonging is not a word I use anymore because belonging implies that you and the place are separate, and in the Rootworks you and the place are the same substance in different configurations, the way a wall and a platform are the same fiber in different densities.

---

The assessment is not complete.

The forms are in the satchel. The satchel is on the platform. The platform has grown over the satchel's lower edge — not much, an inch, maybe less, the root-felt creeping up the canvas the way it creeps up everything, slow, patient, the pace of a place that measures time in the depth of body-impressions and the thickness of stems. I could pull the satchel free. The root-felt would release it; it hasn't bonded, hasn't grown *into* the fabric, just *around* it, the way a river flows around a stone. I could pull it free and fill out the forms and climb the stairs and deliver the assessment and the assessment would say *product volume: adequate, equipment condition: poor, occupational health: difficult to evaluate* and it would be true and it would be nothing.

I could.

But it is morning, or the Rootworks' version of morning, which is the same as the Rootworks' version of evening except that Harker is already moving and the shears are off the hook and the sound of clipping has started at the south end, the clean snip of a blade meeting a stem at the node. And the generator has caught its rhythm and the pumps are thumping and the water is weeping and the light is in the brightening phase of its breath and I am lying on a platform that has begun to know me and my hands have the stain and my lungs have the taste and the forms are in the satchel and the satchel is being held by the root-felt and the root-felt is alive and the aliveness is the point.

I stand. I take the shears from the hook. I sharpen them on the stone. I go to where Harker is working and I start cutting beside her, and Harker doesn't look up — doesn't look up in either sense, doesn't glance at the ceiling and doesn't acknowledge my presence — but she shifts. Half a step to the left. Making room. And the room is not much. And the room is everything.

I cut. The stem is pale and new and my blade meets it at the node and the cut is clean this time. The cut is clean and the stem falls and the root-felt absorbs it and the compost will take it and the cycle will continue and I am in the cycle now and the cycle is in me and the pumps keep pumping and the water keeps weeping and the growth keeps growing and the light keeps breathing and I am the seventh site, the newest impression, the shallowest concavity in a platform that is learning my shape, and I am enough.

I am enough.

The shears go snip. The stone goes whisper. The generator goes mutter. The pumps go thump. The water goes weep. The light goes bright-dim-bright-dim. The root-felt goes slow.

It goes on.
