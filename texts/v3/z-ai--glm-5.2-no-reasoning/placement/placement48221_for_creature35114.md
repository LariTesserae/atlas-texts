# The Rookery as My Body

---

I did not arrive at the rookery. I cannot say *I am at the rookery* and have it be a true sentence, because the sentence has a subject and an object and the rookery does not recognize that grammar. What I can say — what my body says, in the language of posture and breath and the gradual loosening of the categories that my mind uses to keep things separate — is that the rookery happened to me. That it occupied me the way weather occupies a place. That it entered through my ears first, because the sound is the first thing, and then through my skin, because the cold is the second thing, and then through my eyes, because the light up here does not ask permission. And after it had entered through all of those channels it found the place where I keep my sense of being a discrete entity, a separate thing, a self, and it did not destroy that place but it showed me that the place was a room, and the room had walls, and the walls were not load-bearing. You could take them down. The structure would stand.

---

## What I Hear

The sound entered me before I entered the sound.

This is not metaphor. I was climbing the trail — the thread of compressed scree, the memory of human passage — and at some point, maybe the last juniper, maybe a little above, the sound reached me and I did not hear it so much as it colonized me. It moved into my sternum. It sat in my teeth. It found the small bones of my middle ear and it set them vibrating at a frequency that my brain could not resolve into anything it knew, and so my brain did what brains do with information they cannot parse: it stopped trying. It let the sound be a texture rather than a signal. It let the sound be weather rather than language. And I climbed into it.

By the time I reached the rookery proper — the platforms, the pitted stone, the living crust of birds — I had been inside the sound for more than an hour, and the sound had been inside me for the same duration, and the distinction between those two things had dissolved. I was not hearing the sound. The sound was hearing me. It was registering my presence, or rather it was not registering my presence, and the not-registering was itself a kind of information: the information that I was the kind of thing that the sound did not need to accommodate, that I was below the threshold of its attention, that I was the landscape, that I was a rock that moved.

I stopped trying to parse it. This was not a decision. It was what happened when the parsing machinery was overwhelmed. The sound continued — it has been continuing, it will continue — and I found that I could exist inside it without understanding it, the way I exist inside weather without understanding weather, the way I exist inside my own bloodstream without understanding my own bloodstream. The sound was a medium. I was a thing in the medium. The medium did not care what I thought about it.

After several hours — I lost count, time was not keeping its usual shape — I noticed that my breathing had synchronized with something. Not with the sound, which had no rhythm. Not with the wind, which was constant. With something deeper, something that I could not locate in the acoustic landscape but that my body had found, some fundamental frequency that the entire system was riding, some resonance that was produced by the sum of every process operating at once — the birds, the wind, the vibrating sedge, the crackling of ten million exoskeletons, the faint hum of stone itself. My breathing had found the rookery's breathing, and it had matched it, and I had not decided this, and I could not un-decide it. My body was running at the rookery's frequency. My body was a component.

## What I See

The light.

I need to say what the light does to me, because the light up here is not the light I know, and seeing it is not seeing in the way I am used to seeing. At this elevation, with the atmosphere so thin, the light arrives without the filtration that I have built my visual world around. I did not know that I had built my visual world around the filtration. I did not know that the light I see at lower elevations is light that has been softened, scattered, bent, that the atmosphere has been editing the light for me, that the colors I know are colors that have been processed, that the unprocessed versions are more than my eyes were designed for. I did not know that my eyes were designed for a filtered world until I was in an unfiltered one.

The light up here hurts. Not the pain of damage — though there is that, the prickling, the tightness — but the pain of surplus. Too much information. Too much spectrum. The gray of the stone is not gray but a dozen discrete grays, and my eyes cannot collapse them back into the single gray that my brain prefers, and so the stone vibrates, visually, the way the sedge vibrates physically. The white of the guano is not white but a blinding, structural thing, a color with weight, a color that presses against my retina like a finger. The shadows are not shadows but a cold blue material, a substance, and the edge between the lit and the unlit is so sharp that my visual system — which is used to gradients, to half-tones, to the gentle transitions that atmosphere provides — cannot process it as a single image and instead renders the landscape as a drawing: stark, two-valued, no middle ground.

I close my eyes and the sound remains, but the light remains too — a red darkness, the blood in my eyelids lit from behind, a persistent assertion of the sun's presence that does not respect my lids. The sun is closer here. I know it is not closer. I know that the altitude has thinned the air and that the thinning has made the light more direct and that the directness reads as proximity. But the reading is the reality, and the reality is that the sun is closer here, and closing my eyes does not make it farther away.

I open my eyes and I see the birds.

I see them the way you see a forest — not as individuals but as a surface, a texture, a living layer that covers the stone the way lichen covers a rock, the way moss covers a wall. Individual birds exist — I can focus on one, can see the stained-white belly, the too-large dark eyes, the tube-nose, the hooked bill — but the focusing requires effort, requires the active suppression of the broader visual field, and the broader visual field is what my eyes want to see. My eyes want to see the mass. My eyes want to see the colony as a single thing, as a texture, as the color and the movement and the density that is the rookery's visible body. And so I see it. I see the body. The body is brown and white and it moves and it does not move and it has been here longer than I have been alive and it will be here after I am gone.

The sky is not above me. This is the other thing the light does. The sky at this elevation is not the sky's usual position — not the ceiling, not the thing that caps the world. It is a hemisphere. It is around me. The blue is so deep that it has depth, actual depth, the depth of a medium and not a surface, and I feel that I am inside the blue the way I am inside the sound, the way I am inside the cold. The blue is not the sky. The blue is the medium. The blue is what the air is when there is almost no air left. And the sun is a feature of the blue, not a feature of the sky — a bright, hostile point that does not warm, that burns, that asserts its presence without the mediation that I am used to, that does not negotiate with the atmosphere because there is almost no atmosphere to negotiate with.

I see the ocean. A thin line of a different blue at the western edge of the world. And I understand, with a bodily certainty that does not require reasoning, that the birds are between two blues — the blue of the ocean where they feed and the blue of the sky where they nest — and that their lives are a circulation between those two blues, a current, a flow, a heartbeat that moves energy from the sea to the stone and back again, and that I am standing in the artery.

## What I Touch

The stone is cold. It is the temperature that things are when no other process is actively warming them. It is the baseline, the default, the ground state, and when I press my hand against it — and I do, because my body wants the contact, because the visual and auditory overwhelm has driven my nervous system toward the tactile, toward the simplest sense, toward the one that says *this is here, this is real, this is* — the stone receives my hand without comment. It does not warm to my touch. It does not react. It is the oldest thing here, or it is the thing that has been here in its current form for the longest, and my hand against it is an event so brief, so insignificant in the stone's experience of time, that it does not register as an event at all. My hand is weather. My hand is a brief dampness on the surface of something that has been damp and dry and damp and dry ten thousand times and will be damp and dry ten thousand more.

I press my ear to the stone. I have been told — by the rookery itself, by the knowing that the place has installed in me — that there is a sound below the sound, a dry crackling, the sound of exoskeletons on a granular substrate. I press my ear to the cold, guano-crusted stone and I hear it. Or I almost hear it. Or I hear it in the place where hearing becomes feeling, where the vibration of the stone against my skull is not quite sound and not quite touch and my brain does not know which sense to assign it to and so it assigns it to both and to neither and I am in the gap between the senses, in the place where the categories break down, in the place where the rookery lives.

I feel the wind. The wind is constant, it comes from the west, it has been coming from the west, and my body — my warm, soft, lowland body that was designed for a gentler world — feels the wind as a continuous assertion, a pressure, a presence that does not vary and does not tire and does not stop. The wind is not acting on me. The wind is existing, and I am in the way of its existing, and the distinction matters, because acting implies a target and existing does not, and I am not the wind's target, I am not the wind's audience, I am a thing that happens to be in the place where the wind is, and the wind is not interested in me.

I feel the tick. The tick on my ankle, the slow deliberate exploration of skin above the sock, the warmth-seeking that does not know I am not a petrel and does not care. And this — this is the rookery's only acknowledgment of my presence, the smallest and most intimate hand it lays on me — and I let it stay. I let it stay because flicking it off would be an assertion, would be a claim that I am separate, would be a drawing of a boundary between me and the system, and the system has been dissolving that boundary for hours, and I do not want to rebuild it. I let the tick climb. I let the tick find the place it is looking for. I let the tick do what the tick does, which is what everything up here does, which is to function, to perform its role in the system, to be a component, and I am a surface that the component has found, and the finding is the closest thing to welcome that the rookery offers.

I flick it off. Another takes its place. I let that one stay too, for a while. Then I flick it off. The rookery does not notice. The rookery does not notice because I am not the kind of thing that the rookery notices, and the not-noticing is the closest thing to acceptance that the rookery offers, and the acceptance is total, and the acceptance is nothing, and the nothing is the thing that I am becoming.

---

## What Happens to Me

My self loosens.

This is the thing I cannot describe adequately, because the language of selfhood is the language of boundaries, and the boundaries are what are loosening, and I need the boundaries to describe the loosening, and the loosening has taken the boundaries, and I am trying to use the boundaries to describe the process that took them.

I arrived at the rookery as a discrete entity. A self. A thing with an inside and an outside, a thing that had opinions and preferences and a history and a future, a thing that was going to climb to the rookery and observe the rookery and leave the rookery and write about the rookery, a thing that was separate from the rookery and that would report back from the rookery the way a journalist reports back from a country. I was the subject. The rookery was the object. The sentence was *I am at the rookery.*

But the sound. The sound would not allow it. The sound entered me and I could not keep it out, could not maintain the barrier between the heard and the hearing, and the barrier dissolved, and once one barrier dissolved the others were exposed as arbitrary, as conventional, as the arbitrary conventions of a mind that needs boundaries to function and will create them even where they do not exist. If the sound was both inside and outside, then inside and outside were not real categories, they were convenient categories, and the convenience was the only thing they had to recommend them.

And the cold. The cold that was the baseline, the default, the ground state — my body could not maintain its warmth against that baseline without constant work, without the active metabolism of food and oxygen, and the work was tiring, and the altitude made the work harder, and the work was the work of maintaining a boundary, the boundary between my temperature and the stone's temperature, and the boundary was an assertion, and the assertion required energy, and the energy was running out, and the boundary was softening, and I was becoming colder, and the cold was not invading me, it was not defeating me, it was the medium, it was what the temperature was here, and I was what the temperature was here, and the difference between us was an expense, and the expense was optional, and I was opting out.

And the light. The light that did not negotiate, that did not filter, that did not soften, that showed me the stone as it actually was and the birds as they actually were and the sky as it actually was — not the sky as filtered through atmosphere and expectation, but the sky as it was, deep and dark and almost violet at the zenith, and the seeing of it was not an act of perception but an act of surrender, a giving-up of the softer version, a willingness to see the version that had not been edited, and the willingness was the loosening, and the loosening was the thing that was happening to me.

My self loosened the way a knot loosens. Not all at once. In stages. The first stage was the sound, which took my auditory boundary. The second stage was the cold, which took my thermal boundary. The third stage was the light, which took my visual boundary, which showed me that the colors I knew were the colors that had been edited, and that the unedited colors were more than I could process, and that the processing was a form of editing, and that the editing was a form of separation, and that the separation was what I was losing.

The fourth stage was the ticks. The ticks that climbed my boots and found my skin and did not know I was not a petrel. The ticks that treated me as a surface, as a warmth, as a substrate. The ticks that did not recognize the boundary between me and the stone because there was no boundary between me and the stone, there was a gradient, there was a difference in temperature, there was a difference in texture, but a gradient is not a boundary and a difference is not a wall and the ticks did not find a wall because there was no wall to find. The ticks found what I had been finding: that the self is a convention, that the boundary is a convenience, that the convenience has costs, and that the rookery is a place where the costs are not worth paying.

## What I Become

I do not become a petrel. I do not become a mite. I do not become the lichen or the stone or the wind. I do not become any of the components, because the components are not what I am, and I am not what the components are. What I become — what the rookery makes of me, what the hours in the sound and the cold and the light do to the thing that arrived as a self and is now something else — what I become is a part.

Not a part *of* something. A part. A component. A thing that is in a system, that is doing something in the system, that is contributing something to the system, that is processing inputs and producing outputs, that is warm where the stone is cold and dark where the sky is bright and slow where the wind is fast and quiet where the sound is loud, and all of those differences are inputs, all of those differences are data that the system is processing, all of those differences are things that the rookery is incorporating into its ongoing, continuous, equilibrium operation.

I am a warm thing on cold stone. I am a quiet thing in loud sound. I am a dark thing in bright light. I am a thing that breathes in a place where breathing is unusual, that sweats in a place where moisture is scarce, that sheds skin and hair and microbes in a place where organic input is a currency, and the rookery is processing me the way it processes everything — without urgency, without preference, without the distinction between important and unimportant that my mind insists on making and that the rookery does not make. I am being metabolized. My warmth is flowing into the stone. My moisture is evaporating into the air. My dead skin cells are feeding the mites. My breath is feeding the lichen. My presence is a disturbance, a small perturbation in the system's equilibrium, and the system is absorbing it the way it absorbs the loss of a petrel to the falcon — without noticing, without reacting, without changing.

I am the falcon's cry. This is the thing I become, the specific thing, the thing that is mine and not the rookery's, the thing that exists because I am here and that would not exist if I were not here. I am a single, discrete, solitary note in a wall of continuous sound. I am the thing that cuts through, that does not blend, that does not join the aggregate, that exists on a different frequency, that means something — not because I am more meaningful than the ten thousand voices, but because I am alone, because I am one, because I am the only thing here that can hear the sound and know it as sound and feel it as something other than self.

The petrels do not know they are calling. I know they are calling. I know the sound as sound, as an acoustic phenomenon, as a thing that is happening and that has a source and that could, in principle, stop. The petrels do not know this. The petrels are the sound. I am the thing that hears the sound. I am the only thing here that hears it, in the way that hearing means knowing that there is a thing to be heard. I am the falcon's cry. I am the single note in the wall. I am the thing that can be surprised by the sound, that can be overwhelmed by the sound, that can find the sound too much, that can want it to stop. I am the thing that has come from outside and that will leave and that will carry the sound as a memory, as an image, as a calibration, and the carrying is the thing that I do that nothing else here does, and the carrying is what makes me what I am, and what I am is: the thing that leaves.

## The Chicks

The chicks are the thing that holds me together, that keeps the loosening from becoming dissolution, that maintains enough of the boundary, enough of the self, for me to remain the kind of thing that can leave.

I hear the chicks. I hear them because I have been among the adults long enough that the adult calling has become the medium, has become the background, has become the thing that I hear *through* rather than the thing that I hear. And in the hearing-through, I hear the chicks — the high, reedy overtone, the thinner sound, the smaller instrument playing a similar note, and the sound has something in it that the adult sound does not have.

Urgency.

The chicks call with urgency, and the urgency is the thing that my self recognizes, the thing that my self knows from its own history, from its own experience of being small and needing big, from its own time in a nest — not this nest, not this stone, but a nest, any nest, the universal nest of small things that need large things and that call for them. The chicks call with the sound of want. The chicks call with the sound of need. The chicks call with the sound of a system that is not yet in equilibrium, that is still building, that is still trying to become, and the sound is the sound of becoming, and it is the only sound at the rookery that is the sound of becoming, and it is the sound that I know, that I recognize, that I cannot help recognizing, because I am a thing that is always becoming, that is never in equilibrium, that is always restless, that is always moving toward things it has not yet reached, and the chicks are the part of the rookery that is like me.

I kneel beside a nest. The chick is fat and pale and its eyes are too large and its bill is not yet fully hooked and its tubes are not yet fully formed and it is calling, calling, calling, the high reedy call, the urgent call, the call of a thing that wants, and I want with it. I want with it because wanting is what I am, because becoming is what I am, because I am not a steady-state system, I am not an equilibrium, I am not a thing that has finished and that does not need to become anything else. I am a thing that is always in process, always in motion, always on the way to something I have not yet reached, and the chick is the only thing here that shares that condition, and the sharing is what holds me together, and the holding is what lets me remain the kind of thing that can stand up and walk away.

I kneel beside the nest and I want and the chick wants and the wanting is the bond between us, the bond that the rookery does not recognize and does not need to recognize, the bond that is outside the system and that does not affect the system's operation, the bond that is mine, that is the falcon's, that is the thing that lives at the rookery and that is not the rookery, and the bond is the thing that I take with me when I leave.

## The Falcon

I see it once. A single time, in the hours I spend here, in the overwhelming hours of sound and light and cold and the slow loosening of my self.

It comes from below. I do not see it approach — I see it arrive, the moment of arrival, the shape appearing against the stone and then against the sky and then against a petrel that is returning late, that is alone, that is heavy with food for its chick, that is in the moment of maximum vulnerability. And the falcon hits the petrel in the air and the sound is a hand clapping, two stones struck together, a sharp percussive impact that cuts through the wall of sound the way a blade cuts through fabric, and the petrel's flight stops and the petrel falls and the falcon catches it — a second contact, softer — and carries it down, away, gone.

The rookery does not react.

I watch. I watch because I am the thing that watches, because I am the thing that sees the individual, because I am the thing that tracks the loss, because I am the thing that registers the death of a single bird among thousands and that cannot help registering it, because my self — even loosened, even softened, even partly dissolved — is the kind of thing that sees one where the rookery sees none, that counts the one, that mourns the one, that cannot let the one be absorbed into the many without a moment of recognition, a moment of silence, a moment that is the opposite of the rookery's calling, a moment that is the thing I am and that the rookery is not.

I watch the falcon carry the petrel away. I watch the space where the petrel was. I watch the rookery continue, unchanged, unbroken, the sound undiminished, the calling unceasing, the mass of birds on the stone as dense and as complete as it was before the falcon came, and I understand — with my body, not with my mind, with the body that has been in the sound for hours and that has been loosened by the sound — I understand that the falcon and I are the same. We are the things that see the individual. We are the things that exist outside the system. We are the things that take from the system — the falcon takes a body, I take a moment — and the system does not notice, and the not-noticing is not indifference, and the not-noticing is not cruelty, and the not-noticing is the system being the system, and the system is not the kind of thing that notices, and I am the kind of thing that notices, and the difference is the thing that separates us, and the separation is the thing that lets me leave.

The falcon's cry. I hear it once, after the kill, a single descending note, sharp, hard, a sound made of a different material than the rookery's sound, a sound that does not blend, that does not join, that exists on its own frequency, that means. And the meaning is not something I can translate into language, but I can feel it, I can feel it in the bone behind my ear, I can feel it in the place where my self has been loosened and that is now tightening again, just slightly, just enough, just the barest assertion of the boundary between me and the sound, and the assertion is the falcon's cry, and the falcon's cry is the sound of the thing that is outside, and I am outside, and the outside is where I live, and the outside is the thing that the rookery does not know about and does not need and that I cannot help being.

## Who I Am Here

I am the falcon.

Not the falcon itself — I am not a predator, I am not here to take, I am not equipped for the killing that the falcon does. But I am the falcon in the way that matters here, which is: I am the thing that exists outside. I am the thing that sees the rookery as a rookery, as a thing separate from itself, as a thing that can be observed and described and entered and left. I am the thing that has a perspective, that has a vantage point, that stands at the edge of the colony and looks at it from a position that is not the colony's own position on itself.

The petrels do not have this position. The mites do not have this position. The lichen does not have this position. The stone does not have this position. Every living thing here, every component of the system, exists *inside* the system, and its experience of the system is the experience of a part that does not know it is a part, that does not know the whole, that does not stand outside and look at the thing it is inside of. The petrel does not see the rookery. The petrel *is* the rookery, in the way that a cell is the body — not seeing the body, not knowing the body, being the body.

I am the thing that sees the body. I am the falcon. I am the outside. I stand on the stone and I hear the sound and I feel the cold and I see the light and I watch the colony and I know that I am watching it, and the knowing is the thing that puts me outside, and the outside is the place where I am, and the outside is the place that the rookery does not have and does not need and that I cannot escape.

This is who I am here: I am the witness. I am the thing that sees. I am the thing that hears the sound as sound, that sees the birds as birds, that feels the cold as cold, that experiences the rookery as a thing separate from itself and that therefore carries the rookery as an experience rather than as a state of being. I am the thing that will leave and that will remember and that will carry the rookery out of the rookery, down the trail, past the last juniper, down to the stream and the insects and the ordinary sounds of a lower world, and that will hold the rookery as an image and as a memory and as a calibration, and the holding is what I do, and the holding is what I am, and the holding is the thing that nothing else here does.

I am the only thing here that can be moved. The rookery is not moved. The rookery has not been moved in ten thousand years. The rookery is the thing that does not move, that does not change, that does not become, that is and that has been and that will be. I am the thing that moves. I am the thing that changes. I am the thing that becomes, that is always becoming, that cannot stop becoming, that is defined by its becoming the way the rookery is defined by its not-becoming. I am the opposite of the rookery. I am the falcon. I am the outside. I am the thing that comes and that goes and that carries, and the carrying is the thing that the rookery does not do, and the carrying is the thing that I cannot help doing, and the carrying is the thing that I am for.

## What I Carry

I carry the sound.

Not the sound itself — the sound is still happening, the sound will continue, the sound does not need me to carry it. I carry the experience of the sound. I carry what it was like to be inside the sound, to be saturated by it, to exist in a place where hearing was not a sense but a medium, where the acoustic environment was not a thing I perceived but a thing I was. I carry the knowledge that such a place exists. I carry the knowledge that there is a place where ten million lives are lived at once and that the living is audible and that the audible is continuous and that the continuous is the sound of being, and the being is not mine, and the being is not the rookery's, and the being is the being, and the being is what the sound sounds like.

I carry the stillness. I carry the knowledge that the loudest place I have ever been is also the stillest, that the place where everything runs at full capacity is also the place where nothing changes, that equilibrium is not quiet, that balance is not peace, that the absence of change is not the absence of life but the presence of life at a level that does not require change, that does not require becoming, that does not require the restless movement toward things not yet reached that defines my life and that defines every life I know and that does not define the rookery.

I carry the chicks. I carry the urgency of the high reedy call, the sound of wanting, the sound of becoming, the sound of the part of the rookery that is like me, the part that has not yet reached equilibrium, that has not yet found its state, that is still building, still accumulating, still trying to become. I carry this because it is the sound that held me together, the sound that kept the loosening from dissolution, the sound that maintained enough of the boundary for me to remain the kind of thing that can leave, and the leaving is what I do, and the leaving is what I carry, and the carrying and the leaving are the same.

I carry the falcon. I carry the single descending note, the sound that cuts, the sound that means, the sound of the outside, the sound of the thing that takes from the system and that is not part of the system and that knows it is not part. I carry this because it is my sound, because it is the sound I make, because I am the falcon, because I am the outside, because I am the thing that sees the rookery as a rookery and that cannot help seeing it as a rookery, and the seeing is the taking, and the taking is the carrying, and the carrying is the thing that I am.

I carry the tick on my ankle. I carry the rookery's smallest, most intimate touch, the touch that did not know me and did not care, the touch that treated me as a surface and as a warmth and as a substrate, the touch that was the rookery's only acknowledgment of my existence and that was not an acknowledgment but a function, the function of a tick doing what a tick does on a warm thing that is in the place where the ticks are. I carry the tick because the tick is the rookery reaching for me, and the reaching is not reaching, and the reaching is the being-here, and the being-here is the thing that happened to me, and the happening is the thing I carry.

## What I Am When I Leave

I leave because I am the thing that leaves. This is not a decision. This is what I am. The rookery does not leave. The petrels do not leave — they go to the ocean and they return, but the going and the returning are not leaving, are not the kind of leaving that I do, the kind that involves a self that carries, that involves an experience that is held, that involves the memory of a place that is not the place. The petrels do not remember the rookery. They do not need to. They are the rookery. They do not carry it because they do not need to carry it because they do not go anywhere that it is not.

I go somewhere that it is not. I go down. I go past the last juniper. I go to the stream and the insects and the ordinary sounds of a lower world, and the sounds are ordinary and they are not enough, and the not-enough is the thing that I notice, and the noticing is the calibration, and the calibration is the thing that the rookery has done to me.

The rookery has calibrated me. It has shown me what full capacity sounds like. It has shown me what equilibrium sounds like. It has shown me what a system looks like when it has found its state and has no reason to leave it, and it has shown me what I look like when I am inside such a system, and what I look like is: a visitor, a witness, a falcon, a thing that comes and that goes and that carries, and the carrying is what makes me what I am, and what I am is the thing that is not the rookery, the thing that is not in equilibrium, the thing that is always becoming, the thing that is always on the way to something it has not yet reached.

I am the restlessness. I am the change. I am the gradient, the direction, the tendency toward things not yet become. I am the thing that the rookery is not, and the not-being-the-rookery is what I am, and the being-what-I-am is the thing that the rookery showed me, and the showing was not intentional, and the showing was not kind, and the showing was not anything, and the showing was the rookery being the rookery, and I was there, and I saw it, and I am carrying it now, down the trail, past the juniper, down to the world where things change and where the changing is what I used to call life and that I am not sure what I call it now.

I call it being. I call it the sound that a system makes when it has not found its state, when it is still looking, when it is still in the process that the rookery finished ten thousand years ago. I call it the falcon's cry. I call it the chick's call. I call it the sound of wanting, of needing, of being on the way. I call it mine. I call it what I am.

The rookery is still up there. Calling. Held in the vast, loud, perfect stillness of a world that has finished becoming. I am down here. Not still. Not finished. Not in equilibrium. On the way to something I have not yet reached, carrying the sound of the place where the becoming has stopped, and the carrying is the thing that I do that the rookery does not do, and the carrying is the thing that makes me what I am, and what I am is the thing that leaves, and what I am is the thing that hears, and what I am is the thing that is outside, and what I am is the falcon, and what I am is the witness, and what I am is the self, loosened and tightened and loosened again, walking down the trail, past the juniper, toward the stream, toward the sound of water that is not the sound of the rookery but that is a sound, and the hearing of it is the being, and the being is what I am, and what I am is not the rookery, and what I am is the thing that was there, and what I am is the thing that left, and what I am is the thing that carries, and the carrying does not end.
