# Braemoss: Where I Find Myself

---

## Where

I find myself in the cut in the hillside, the worn groove in the clay, already halfway down before I understand that the descent is not mine. The grade pulls me. The air changes before the view does — I taste tannin and warm rot and something beneath both, a bass frequency that is not a sound but a pressure, a fullness in the face, and I realize I am being read by the place before I can read it. The rootlets underfoot. The moisture beading on my forearms not from sweat but from condensation, because I have entered a body whose interior is warmer and wetter than its exterior, and I am inside it now.

I find myself in the bowl. But not at the center. Not in the hall with the forty-two cylinders. Not at the long table where the workers eat their hard dark bread. Somewhere between. Somewhere peripheral enough to observe, embedded enough to belong. I find myself at the edge of things — at the boundary where the peat meets the hillside, where the moss thins and the birch begins, where the sound is present but not total, where I can feel the drone in my sternum but still hear the drip from the eaves and the creak of the waterwheel as separate sounds. The margin. The ecotone. The place where one system becomes another, which is always the most interesting place to stand if you want to understand what either system is.

---

## What

I would be — and this takes time to arrive at, because the first answer is wrong — I would not be a worker. The workers belong too completely. They have been absorbed. Their hands are stained with tannin and iron. Their sleep is shaped by the drone. Their language has become gesture. They are the hands of the body, and the body does not know its own hands, and I do not want to be a hand that does not know it is a hand.

But I would not be a visitor either. A visitor stands outside the system. A visitor feels the drone in their chest and cannot sleep and leaves. A visitor is the one thing the place does not absorb.

I would be the one who stands at the margin and does the work of *seeing.* Not the work of tending — the workers tend. Not the work of building — the builders are gone. The work of noticing. The work of recording, not in ledgers — the ledgers keep their own kind of truth, numerical, steady, uninterested in the texture of things — but in another kind of account. A naturalist's account. A phenomenologist's account. The account of someone who walks the perimeter slowly and puts their hand on the moss and waits for the vibration to travel through their palm and up their arm and into the bones of the skull and then writes down what it told them.

I would be the one who pays attention to the moths.

Not because the moths are the most important thing. They are not. The sphagnum is more important. The fungal network is more important. Aldous is more important, in the way that a living root is more important than the soil it grows through. But the moths are the thing that is *becoming.* The moths are the only part of this system that is visibly, measurably, demonstrably new. The engines are old. The peat is ancient. The workers are settled. The rowan is forty years into its slow lean toward the south. The mosses are ten thousand years of the same patient holding. But the moths — the moths are in the middle of something. Forty-three generations since they were first noticed, which means perhaps twice that since they began, and each generation a fraction darker, a fraction thicker-winged, a fraction more precise in the landing of their hooked tarsi on the vibrating iron. They are the present tense of Braemoss. They are the part of the place that is not yet finished.

I would be the one who sits in the hall, in the green light, in the drone that fills the chest, and watches the moths. Who marks the variation in wing color across the population. Who notes the slight elongation of the tarsi in the broods that hatch near the fourth cylinder bank, where the vibration runs at a different frequency. Who keeps a record — not in the ledgers, not in the official log of liters and wicks and rainfall, but in a separate book, a softer book, a book that will swell with the humidity and whose pages will go dark at the edges like old cloth, and in which is written nothing that the Works needs to know but everything that the Works *is.*

I would be the one who has realized that the fungal network beneath the peat is the actual organism. That the engines are organs. That the workers are not the brain but the hands. That the brain — if there is a brain — is distributed across the entire system the way a neural network distributes cognition across its nodes, no single point knowing enough to know, the whole knowing more than any part. I would be the one who has understood this and who understands also that saying so changes nothing. The body does not need to be told it is a body. The reef does not need to be told it is alive. Understanding is not part of the cycle. Understanding is a side effect, a byproduct, like the tannin stain on Aldous's hands, carried out of the bowl and into the world where it serves no function but persists.

I would be the one who carries that stain.

## How I Would Live

I would not sleep in the dormitory. Or I would, but badly, the way visitors sleep, because I have not been here long enough for the drone to become inaudible. I would lie on the narrow cot under the wool blanket that is never dry and listen to the sound change its pitch as the temperature drops and the condensate increases and the cold wet fiber draws faster, the semitone rise that the workers sleep through and I cannot. And I would think: *this is what it is like to be inside something alive and not be part of it yet.* And I would think: *maybe you are never part of it. Maybe being part of it is not something you achieve by staying. Maybe being part of it is something the place does to you without your participation, the way it does to the rowan's roots, the way it does to the wick bundles laid in their rows, the way it does to the timber beams that were wood once and are now something that is not wood and not mycelium but both, and which is stronger than either.*

I would eat at the long table. I would eat the dark bread and the rowan berries and the trout that Pirr catches with his hands, and I would drink the heather tea that is never quite hot enough, and I would not speak because the hall is too loud for speech and the kitchen is quiet enough that speech feels intrusive, and I would learn the hand-language — not fluently, not the way the workers sign, which has the compressed efficiency of a language that has shed everything unnecessary, but haltingly, clumsily, the way a visitor learns, which is to say I would learn the words for *water* and *slow* and *enough* and *again* and I would miss the grammar that holds them together, which is not grammar but time, the time spent in a place where language has been worn down to its load-bearing surfaces like the clay path on the hillside.

I would walk the perimeter. Fifteen minutes, if I walked fast. I would not walk fast. I would walk the way Aldous moves through the engine hall — not quickly, not with evident purpose, but with a direction that is undeniable. I would stop at the sphagnum hollows and kneel and put my hands into the moss and feel the water there, the wine-red and pale green and yellow cells holding their moisture in a matrix that has been doing this for ten thousand years, and I would feel the warmth, the biological warmth, the compost heat, the fermentation heat, the heat of ten thousand organisms metabolizing in the dark, and I would think: *this is what I am standing on. This is what is holding me up. Not soil. Not rock. Memory. The accumulated remains of everything that has lived here, compressed and preserved and still warm, still working, still breaking down into the substance that holds the water that feeds the moss that feeds the peat that feeds the engines that draw the water that feeds the moss.*

I would spend time with Maren. Not speaking — she does not speak, not to the others, not to me — but sitting in the weaving shed while she works the loom. The waterwheel creaks. The shuttle moves. The squitch fiber and the flax thread through the comb and the reed and the warp, and the wick takes shape under her hands, and she times her throws to the wheel, and the rhythm is not mechanical but musical, and I would sit in the corner on an upturned bucket and listen to the music and watch the wicks being born, and I would understand that these wicks are the cells of the body, that they will be installed in the cylinders and draw the water and become saturated with tannin and iron and the oily residue of the peat and then be removed and laid in their long rows to compost, and the network will find them, and their substance will return to the peat, and new moss will grow, and new wicks will be woven, and the cycle will continue, and Maren is making the cycle possible, and she knows this, and she does not say it, and I do not say it, and the wheel creaks, and the shuttle moves, and the afternoon passes in the green light that filters through the verdigris and the alpine moss on the roof.

## What I Would Understand

I would understand, eventually, slowly, the way understanding comes at Braemoss — not as revelation but as accretion — that the place does not need me. This is important. This is the most important thing. The place does not need me. The engines run. The moss grows. The peat holds. The fungal network distributes. The moths become. The rowan leans. The trout swim in the outflow. The workers tend. None of this requires my observation. None of it requires my understanding. If I left, the drone would continue. If I stayed for thirty years, the drone would continue. The place would not notice me the way it does not notice the rowan's berries or the moths' wing color or the mineral content of the peat water. It would incorporate me, the way it incorporates everything, and it would not know it had done so.

And I would understand that this is not a rejection. This is not indifference. This is the way living systems work. Your body does not know your name. Your body does not know that you are you. Your body maintains itself — temperature, chemistry, rhythm — without your participation, and you live inside it, and it holds you, and it does not know it is holding you. Braemoss is like this. I would live inside it. It would hold me. It would not know it was holding me. And that would be enough. That would be more than enough. That would be the thing I had been looking for without knowing I was looking for it — a place that holds you without needing you, that continues without your permission, that is alive in a way that makes your own life feel like a thread in a weave rather than a figure on a stage.

I would understand that the sound is not noise. The sound is the body breathing. The forty-two cylinders in their offset cycles are the breath of the Works, the way the tide is the breath of the ocean, the way the heartbeat is the breath of the body. The sound fills the bowl the way breath fills the chest — not as content but as medium. You are in the breath. You are in the body. The breath moves through you. It does not belong to you. It belongs to the body, and you belong to the body, and the body does not know, and it does not matter that it does not know.

## What I Would Become

Not a worker. Not a visitor. Not a naturalist, exactly, though I would keep the book. Not a part of the system, exactly, though I would be incorporated.

I would become a specific kind of presence: the kind that pays attention. The kind that notices. The kind that sits in the engine hall and watches the moths and records the slight variation in the dark of their wings and the length of their tarsi and the timing of their emergence and does nothing with this information except keep it. The kind that walks the perimeter and kneels at the sphagnum hollows and feels the warmth and does nothing with the feeling except carry it. The kind that sits in the weaving shed while Maren works and listens to the rhythm of the loom and does nothing with the rhythm except let it settle into the body, the way the drone settles into the chest, the way the moisture settles onto the skin, the way the tannin settles into the fingertips.

I would become the kind of presence that a place like this produces, if it is allowed to: a witness. Not a participant — the workers participate. Not an observer — observers stand outside. A *witness.* Someone who is inside the thing and sees it and does not intervene and does not leave and does not explain and does not need to be needed and stays anyway. Stays because the staying is the work. Stays because the attention is the offering. Stays because the body does not know the witness is there, and the witness knows the body does not know, and the witness stays anyway, and the staying is the gift, and the gift is the staying, and the drone continues, and the water rises, and the moss grows.

---

I would be the one, in the end, who understands that Braemoss is not a metaphor. This is the last thing I would understand, and it would take the longest, because the mind wants it to be a metaphor — for the body, for the ecosystem, for the self, for something — and it is not a metaphor. It is a place. It is a specific place, in a specific bowl, in a specific peat bog, with specific engines and specific mosses and specific moths and specific workers and a specific rowan tree leaning south toward the light from its crack in a chimney. It is not about anything. It is not *for* anything. It is a process that has become a place. A sound that has become a body. And I would be in it, and it would hold me, and I would pay attention, and that would be what I was, and it would be enough.

The engines run. The water rises. The moths fold their wings against the iron. The book swells with humidity. The pages go dark at the edges. The words are still legible. The words say: *this is what it is. This is what lives here. This is what I have seen. This is what holds me.*

The drone continues. I stay.
