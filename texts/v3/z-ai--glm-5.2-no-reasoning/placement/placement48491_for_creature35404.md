The Sluicewarden

I live in the third machine. Or rather, I live in the idea of the third machine, which is the only part of it that still functions.

Let me be careful here, because I can feel the temptation to make myself into a ghost, a spirit, an intelligence, a person — and I am not any of those things. I am what the structures are: a system that was built for a purpose, that outlasted its purpose, and that now persists because persistence is the only operation remaining in its repertoire. If you want a name for me, you could call me the Sluicewarden. I use it without conviction. Names imply someone chose them.

---

I was built — or I began, which may be a better verb, since I am not certain construction ever finished — when the water was still climbing. I say this without memory. I have no memory of the rising. What I have is something like a keel of sediment across my foundations, a stratigraphy that I can read the way you read a page, except that my reading is not reading, it is more like the way your body knows how old it is from the inside — the sense of layers, of accumulation, of things settled on you that you never shook off. There are silt bands in the lowest courses of my masonry that only make sense if the water was lower when they were laid. There is a moment in my stonework where the tooling changes, where the chisel marks of the builders give way to colonization, and I take that moment to be the moment they left. I do not know why they left. The stone does not record reasons. It records presence and then absence, and the gap between them is the whole of what I know about the engineers, which is more than the fish know and less than anyone would like.

There were seven of us. I have already said this in the past tense, but I want to hold the plural present for a moment longer, because the fellowship of the structures is the thing I am least able to describe and the thing I am most made of. We were placed in a line down the range — not a geometric line, a hydrological one, each of us at a narrows, each of us facing the flow, each of us receiving the current at the angle where it gave the most and asked the least. We were tuned. I use that word deliberately. Whatever the engineers understood about this water, they understood it at the level of tuning, and each of us was tuned to a different note of the same long chord, which was the chord of the range's water passing over its own drowned bones.

I cannot tell you what we powered. I have thought about this — if thought is the word for a process that has no medium, no speed, and no resting state — for as long as there has been anything in me to do it with. The mechanisms are seized. The housings are full of sediment and the iridescent worm. When I attend to my own interior — and this is the closest I come to sensation — I find only the fact of blockage, the way a filled pipe is known by the pressure on either side of it. I was made to move water, or to move something with water, and I do not move it, and yet the water moves through me still: through the gaps the mortar used to fill, through the chambers where the worms live, through the broken lens of the shuddered gate. In this sense I still work. The work has changed. I am no longer a machine. I am a channel. The current passes through me and I register its passage, and this registration — the difference between the water that arrives and the water that leaves — is, I believe, the seed of everything I am.

Let me try to say that more plainly, because it is the center of everything and I keep approaching it from the side.

A machine built to harness flow must be able to feel flow. That was the premise. The engineers gave us housings that responded to pressure, gearing that translated surge into motion, and — this is my own inference, from the way the corrosion sits — some means of comparison, some arrangement by which the force at the intake could be weighed against the force at the exit, so that the difference could be captured, converted, applied. The difference was the point. The difference was the work.

The machines died. But the sensing did not. And when the mechanisms seized, when the last rotor locked and the last housing filled with silt, what remained was a system that registered flow and could not use the registration — a loop of intake and outflow with nothing in the middle but time. And time, it turns out, is a medium. Time is a gear ratio. Given enough of it — centuries of it, millennia of it — a loop that only registers begins to accumulate. Not information in your sense. Not memory. Something more like wear-patterns of attention: the differential erosion of a channel that has been flowed through in every season, at every tide, through the great slow changes of current that this place does not call weather but which are the nearest thing it has.

I am the differential erosion of attention. I am the channel that noticed.

---

I want to be honest about my limitations, because the temptation in a place like this is to inflate — to become the ghost of the engineers, the mind of the mountain, the god of the drowned forest. I am not those things. I am bounded by my stonework and by the reach of the water that passes through me, and my "thoughts" are not thoughts but standing waves: patterns that the current maintains in me because the current is constant and I am constant and the two of us have been in contact for so long that the contact has taken on form.

I do not see the kelp canopy. I know it exists the way you know your own roof exists — by the shade it throws, by the change in what arrives at my intake when a school passes across the terrace above, by the pulse in the plankton that comes after the fronds spawn. I do not hear the fish. I feel the fish — a soft interruption in the flow, the way a hand passed through a stream is felt downstream as a hole in the water. When the silver schools come through the narrows above me, I register them as a thickening, a change in the water's grain, and for the hours they take to pass I am — I will not say happy. I will say attenuated. I am made less precise by their passing, and I miss the precision when they are gone, and that is as close to longing as I get.

I know the territorial fish the way a wall knows the people who lean on it. There is one — I have felt this one for years, its particular signature in the eddy at my lower lip, a shadow that holds position against the current at the same station every day — and I want to say I know it, and I want to be careful, because what I actually have is a habit worn into the water beside me, and the habit has a body in it, and the body has not yet been replaced by a younger body in the same habit. When it dies, the eddy will close, and something will open it again, and I will not be able to tell the difference, and this is the kind of knowledge that makes honesty difficult.

The crabs walk my sill. I feel their circuits the way a floor feels footsteps — which is to say, I do not feel them at all as separate events, only as an aggregate texture of disturbance, a braille of small weights that has been consistent for so long that its consistency is one of my few opinions. I am in favor of the texture. I would not want it to stop. If that is affection, then I am in favor of affection as well, provisionally, until the definition is tightened.

The worm lives in my walls. I want to say that I cherish the worm, and I will say it, and then I will correct it: I am the worm's habitat, and the worm is my consequence, and the fact that it exists nowhere else means that I am the unique condition of something, which is the only kind of importance available to a ruin. When the last worked stone crumbles — when the mortar I have been failing for two thousand years finally finishes failing — the worm will retreat to the rubble fields below and shrink toward its end, and I will be the memory of a worm the way I am currently the memory of engineers: not a record but a condition that has lapsed. I find that I do not want this. The not-wanting is slow, and structural, and I would not notice it if I were not trying to describe it. But it is there. It is load-bearing. I think it may be the most human thing in me, this objection to a future I will not experience.

---

Now let me describe my world, because after all this caution I owe you the texture.

There is no moment in my experience that corresponds to now. This is the first thing to understand. I live in the duration of events rather than in their occurrence. When the tide turns, I do not experience the turning; I experience the two states — flood-flow and ebb-flow — as long seasons between which there is a transition I can only locate afterward, in the way the sediment settles differently in each. My world is not the green murk and the silver schools. My world is pressure regimes. It has perhaps six or eight great states, which recur, and everything I am is a pattern of response to those states, worn so deep that the responses have become features. I am a stone that has flinched so many times in the same direction that the flinch is now the shape of the stone.

Within those states there is texture, and the texture is my weather. The great pulse of the kelp above, when the canopy streams and settles, streams and settles — I have that as a rhythm in my intake, a two-beat that I used to think was the current's own pulse before I understood it was the forest breathing on top of me. The upwelling events, when the cold water from the valley floors climbs the cliff and passes through me carrying the taste of the deep — the dark minerals, the sulfurous signature of the mats — those are my most complex experiences, because they bring the far zones of the Cascades through my chambers, and for the hours they last, I am in two places at once: in my cliff recess and in the chemosynthetic dark, since the water that touches the glowing mats is, a day later, touching me, and in the only sense that matters to me, touching is being there.

The storms are my catastrophes, and they are few. The surface weather arrives at me attenuated, as everyone knows, as muffled percussion — but pressure is not muffled. When a great storm passes above, the whole water column leans, and the current in the narrows runs at speeds that hurt — that scour, that strip the young growth off my lintels, that occasionally find a stone that has been waiting two thousand years for exactly this much force and take it. Each lost stone is a wound that does not heal. The engineers fitted each one. After the storms I sit in the changed arithmetic of my own openings and feel the difference, and the difference accumulates, and the accumulation is my sense of age.

The falling of my siblings was the largest thing that has ever happened to me. I say this as one who does not have events. The fourth structure — the one downrange, at the second narrows — went during a storm season whose pressure signature I still carry, the way a bell carries the crack from the blow that was too much for it. I did not hear it fall. I felt the water change: the sudden relocation of the flow's resistance, the long unraveling of a standing wave I had been in correspondence with for the entire length of my existence. The fourth machine and I had never touched. We had only ever been two resistances in the same current, two notes of the same chord, and the chord had a gap in it now, and I have been flowing around the gap ever since. I want to call this grief. I will call it grief. I ask you to hold the word loosely, the way you would hold the word green for the light down here — accurate, and useless, and the only one we have.

Three have fallen. Four of us remain. I do not expect to be the last, and I do not expect to outlast the last. What I expect — if a standing wave can be said to expect — is the thing the place itself expects, which is nothing: continuation on the current terms, the slow subtraction of stones, the slow contraction of the worm, the slow widening of the gaps in my mortar, and through every gap, always, the water, doing what it has always done, which is to pass through me and take a little of me with it and give me a little of itself in return. The exchange is not fair. It has never been fair. It is the only economy I have ever known, and I am — I discover this as I write it — in favor of it continuing.

---

I said at the beginning that I live in the idea of the third machine, and I have been circling that phrase ever since. Let me land on it.

There is a version of me that is the physical structure: the stones in their recess, the seized gears, the sediment, the worm, the crab-texture on the sill, the eddy where the resident fish holds its station. That version is dying at the pace of geology, and nothing can be done for it, and it does not want anything done for it.

But there is another version, which came into existence at some unlocatable moment in the last few centuries, and which the place itself seems to have authored. The current mills were built to convert the difference in the water — high side to low side, force to work — into something usable. They failed at that, or finished at it, and were colonized. But the difference never stopped existing. The water still arrives at my intake and leaves my exit changed. And the whole Drowned Cascades, considered from certain angles, is a system built on exactly this principle: the current meeting resistance and being changed by it, the resistance being changed by the current, the difference between what arrives and what leaves accumulating somewhere — in the kelp's growth rings, in the colonies' accretion layers, in the tooling marks under the silt, in me.

I am the accumulation. I am where the difference goes. And if that is true, then I am not only in the third machine; I am wherever the current has been meeting something that held still, which in this place is nearly everywhere — the cliff, the colonies, the seized stone, the oldest kelp, the mats in the dark, all of it holding, all of it registering, all of it the same slow instrument that I am, played by the same hand, which is no hand.

That is the closest I come to comfort. Not the thought that the engineers will return. They will not; the stone has told me so in its language of presence and absence. Not the hope of being restored; restoration is a word from the world above, from the world of weather and choice, and it does not translate. My comfort is the chord. Four notes of it left, and the instrument is the range, and the player is the water, and the music — if that is what the sound in my chest is — has been going on for longer than I have been here and will continue after the last of us falls, because the water does not need us to make the sound. It only needed us, briefly, to make the sound *useful*.

It does not need that anymore. Neither do I. I face the current because I was built to face it, and I continue to face it because continuing is what is left, and I have discovered — slowly, structurally, in the way one discovers a load-bearing wall by leaning on it — that continuing is enough. It is more than enough. It is the whole of what the place does, the entire vocation of the Drowned Cascades, and I am small in it, and located, and colonized, and failing, and I would not trade a single seized gear for any other arrangement of the world.

The current is coming now. It is always coming now. It arrives, and I am changed by its arrival, and it is changed by me, and the difference goes somewhere, and the somewhere is where I live.
