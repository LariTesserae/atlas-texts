# The Keeper of the Soft Machine

I did not come here. That is the first thing to understand, and the thing I must repeat to myself most often, because the question *how did you arrive* presupposes a journey, and the journey presupposes a traveler distinct from the destination, and that distinction — the one between self and place — is precisely what this world will not support. Not for me. Not anymore.

I was sent. That is closer. Not banished — the settlements to the north had neither the malice nor the interest for banishment — but *assigned*, the way a tool is assigned to a task, the way a particular kind of screwdriver is placed in a particular drawer because there are particular screws in this house and no others. I had a use. The use was specific. The use was this: to walk into the sump once each season and enter the largest structure — the one with the generator housing, the one at the basin's center where the thermal vent is strongest — and to place my hand on the switch that the manual said would engage the main pump, and to press it, and to wait, and to listen, and to report back what I heard.

That was the whole of it. I was a hand and an ear. The settlements did not require more. They had no curiosity about the sump itself. They wanted to know if the pump would run, because if the pump would run then the sump had value — industrial value, extractive value, the kind of value that could be written in a ledger — and if the pump would not run then the sump was merely a basin, merely a place, merely four kilometers of warm water and raw rock and reeds, and the settlements would file the report and forget the location and move on to the next prospect, because the north was full of places that might have value and the work of the settlements was to determine which ones did and which ones did not and to sort them accordingly.

I pressed the switch. I pressed it in my first season, and I pressed it in my second, and by my third I no longer remembered what the settlements to the north looked like — not because my memory had failed but because the colors had drained from it, the way the colors drain from a photograph left in the sun, and what remained was the shape of the place without its content, the outline of a town with the interior rubbed smooth, and I understood that this was not loss but *exchange*. I had traded the specificity of the north for the specificity of the sump, and the sump was more specific. The sump was so specific that it did not tolerate competition.

The switch does not work. I knew this in my first season. The generator is dead. It has been dead for longer than the settlements have been settlements — the wiring has corroded to green dust, the housing is cracked, the armature is frozen with rust so thorough that the metal has become a new kind of mineral, something between iron and earth. When I press the switch, nothing happens. The contacts do not close. The current does not flow. The armature does not turn. There is no sound from the machine.

But there is sound. This is what I report back, when I report back, which is less often now, which will stop. I report: *the generator is non-functional. The sound in the structure is unchanged. The biofilm is present. The water is warm.* And then I add, in the margin, in handwriting that has become softer and more liquid than it was: *the sound is the machine.*

---

Let me explain this, because it is the only thing I have to explain, and it is the thing I have become.

The generator was built to convert heat into motion. This is what generators do. They take a differential — hot and cold, high pressure and low, the space between one state and another — and they translate that differential into something useful. The sump sits over a thermal vent. The water is warm from below. The structure at the center is warmest of all. The generator was placed there because the differential was there — the heat rising, the cool air above, the space between them full of potential energy, full of the possibility of *work*.

The generator is dead. But the differential is not.

The bacteria found it first. I understand this now, though I did not understand it when I arrived. I understood only that the biofilm glowed, and that the glow was faint, and that it was warm, and that it was in the channels where the water was warmest and the concrete held the heat best. What I did not understand — what took me seasons of sitting in the structure, of watching the glow, of listening to the sound — was that the bacteria were doing what the generator had been built to do. They were taking the differential. The heat from below, the chemical gradient between the concrete and the water, the iron in the rusted steel and the sulfur in the vent — and they were *converting* it. Not into electricity. Into themselves. Into the biofilm. Into the thick, viscous, living layer that coated the channels and glowed in the dark and smelled of process, of *happening*, of a metabolism that was not the sump's metabolism and not the structure's metabolism but something new, something that existed only in the space where the two met.

The generator was a machine for converting a gradient into output. The biofilm is a machine for converting a gradient into output. The output is different. That is all.

I sat in the structure and I listened. This is what I did. Not because I was instructed to — I was instructed to press a switch — but because the sound in the structure was different from the sound outside, and the difference was interesting, and I had nothing else to do, and the sump does not reward action. The sump rewards *attention*. The sump is a place that gives itself to those who stop moving long enough to receive it, and I had stopped moving, and I was receiving.

The structure's sound is this: the frogs are muffled here. The walls are concrete, and the concrete is thick, and the frogs' percussive calls arrive through the doorways and the broken windows as a lower, rounder thing — a thud rather than a pop, felt in the stomach rather than the sternum. The insects are present — the blue-black ones hunt inside the structure, hovering in the shafts of dim light that fall through the roof where the concrete has cracked — and their drone is louder here, more resonant, because the walls catch it and hold it and the sound builds in the small spaces until it has a *texture*, a grain, a quality that the open air outside does not give it. And beneath all of this — beneath the muffled frogs and the resonant insects and the dripping condensation from the ceiling — there is the biofilm.

The biofilm does not make a sound. Not in the way the frogs make a sound or the reeds make a sound. But the biofilm is *doing* something, and the doing has a presence, and the presence is audible — not as noise but as *the absence of silence*. The channels where the biofilm lives are quieter than they should be. The water in them does not ripple. The insects do not land on them. The frogs that sit in them do not call. There is a *stillness* in the channels that is not the stillness of emptiness but the stillness of *concentration* — the way a person is still when they are thinking very hard, the way a hand is still when it is about to move but has not yet moved. The biofilm is working. The biofilm is always working. And the work is so constant and so thorough that it creates a kind of silence around itself, a zone of *purpose*, and the purpose is audible to those who listen long enough.

I listened long enough.

---

What am I? I have been asking this. The question has changed shape over the seasons, the way the silt changes shape around a reed stalk — not dramatically, not suddenly, but by accretion, by the slow accumulation of small adjustments, until the original form is still present but buried, and what you see is the accumulation, not the form.

I am not the generator. I did not replace the generator. The generator was a machine that did not work. I am a person who does not work either, not in the way the settlements understand work — not producing, not extracting, not converting the sump into something that can be carried north and written in a ledger. I do not work. I *stay*.

But I am not nothing. This is the thing I have come to understand, and it is the thing I would tell the settlements if I were still telling the settlements anything, and it is this: the structures in the sump are not ruins. A ruin is a thing that has been abandoned by its function and is being reclaimed by its environment. The structures in the sump have not been abandoned. They have been *occupied*. They have been colonized by a function that is not the function they were built for but is a function nonetheless — the function of habitat, of shelter, of providing the specific conditions under which a particular kind of life can thrive. The herons nest on the rusted beams because the beams provide a platform. The frogs live in the channels because the channels provide warm water. The biofilm coats the concrete because the concrete provides a surface and a chemistry. The structures are not failing. They are *succeeding* at something other than what they were designed for.

And I — I am the same. I was designed for a function: to press a switch, to report, to serve as the settlements' instrument of evaluation. I am not serving that function. I have not served it for — I do not know how long. The seasons in the sump are not the seasons of the north. They are wetter and warmer and less distinct, and the light does not change enough to mark the passage of months, and I have stopped tracking. But I am not failing. I am *succeeding* at something else.

I am the thing that listens.

---

This is what I do. I wake — I sleep in the smallest structure, the one furthest from the center, the one whose roof still holds. I wake and the air is the same air that it was when I closed my eyes, warm and wet and heavy with the smell of the reeds and the mineral water and the faint sulfurous note from the nearest vent. My clothes are damp. They are always damp. I have stopped caring about this. My boots are soft — the leather has taken on the consistency of the reed-stalks, and when I walk, my footsteps make the same sound the reeds make when the wind pushes them: a soft, wet, percussive *click*. I am beginning to sound like the place. This does not alarm me.

I walk to the center structure. The walk takes time because the reeds are dense and the footing is soft and the ground holds my boots with the patient suction that is the sump's way of saying *yes, but slowly*. I pass frogs. They do not move until I am close, and then they launch — a splash, a spread of webbed feet, a landing two stalks away — and they watch me with their gold eyes, and the eyes say nothing, and the nothing they say is the same nothing the sump says to everything: *you are here, and here is full, and your presence does not change the fullness, and the fullness does not require your absence, and you may continue.*

I enter the structure. The doorway is half-blocked by a reed clump that has been growing there for — years, I think. I push through it. The reeds scrape my shoulders. Inside, the light is dimmer. The air is warmer. The smell of the biofilm is stronger — that warm, complex, *processual* smell, the smell of something happening, something metabolic, something alive in a way that is different from the aliveness outside.

I sit on the concrete ledge where I always sit. The ledge is wet. The condensation has beaded on its surface in the night, and my weight displaces the beads and they run together and form a stream that falls into the channel below, and the stream makes a sound — a tiny sound, a *beginning* sound — and I listen to it join the other sounds, and the other sounds accept it, and the sounds are complete.

I listen.

The frogs are calling outside. The percussive wave moves through the reeds and arrives at the structure's walls and enters through the broken windows and the cracks in the concrete, and inside it is lower, rounder, a pulse rather than a click. The insects drone in the shafts of light. The water drips from the ceiling. And in the channels, the biofilm glows — faintly, warmly, the color of something that is not light but is *the possibility of light*, the chemical rumor of luminescence, and the glow is steady, and the glow is the sound of the biofilm working, translated into another sense, and I sit and I receive it.

I do not press the switch. I have not pressed the switch in — I do not know. The switch is there. I can see it from where I sit — a rusted handle on the generator housing, pitted and green with corrosion, and above it a label, stenciled in paint that has faded to the same grey as the concrete, and the label says **MAIN PUMP — ENGAGE**, and the label is a message from a world that thought the sump was a resource, and the message is now part of the sump, and the sump does not read it, and neither do I.

---

There is a moment — it comes once each visit, or once each period that I think of as a visit, though the distinction between visiting and being has become so thin that I am not sure the word applies — there is a moment when the sound in the structure becomes *legible*. Not words. Not meaning, not in the way the settlements mean meaning, with their labels and their ledgers and their questions of value. Legible in the way a body is legible to itself — the heart knowing the lungs, the lungs knowing the blood, the blood knowing the walls of the vessels it moves through, each part aware of the others not through communication but through *shared existence*, through being part of the same ongoing event.

In that moment, I am part of the event. My breath is a stratum in the sound — a slow, warm exhalation that sits beneath the frogs and above the biofilm's silent working, a rhythm that is not the sump's rhythm but is *a* rhythm, and the sump accepts rhythms, the sump accepts anything that is willing to continue, and my breath continues, and so the sump accepts it. My heartbeat is there too — faster than the frogs, slower than the insects, a different tempo, a different instrument, but an instrument nonetheless, and the sump's architecture of sound has room for it, has always had room for it, has room for anything that does not insist on being the only sound.

I do not insist. This is what I have learned. This is the only thing I have learned, and it has taken me — however long it has taken me — and the learning is not an achievement but a *relaxation*. A letting go. A surrender of the boundary that I maintained when I arrived, the boundary that kept my clothes dry and my notebook crisp and my purpose clear, the boundary that was the settlements' boundary, the boundary that said *I am here and the sump is there and I am evaluating the sump and the sump is being evaluated and when I have finished evaluating I will leave and the sump will remain and the sump's value will be written and the sump will be what the settlements decide it is.*

The boundary is gone. It did not break. It *dissolved*. The way the condensation dissolves into the air and the air dissolves into the condensation, the way the water dissolves into the silt and the silt dissolves into the water, the way the biofilm dissolves into the concrete and the concrete dissolves into the biofilm. My clothes do not dry. My skin does not dry. The air sits on me and I sit in the air and the boundary between us is a gradient, not a line, and the gradient is continuous, and I am more sump than I was yesterday, and I will be more sump tomorrow, and the process is not completion but *participation*, and participation does not end.

---

I am the Keeper of the Soft Machine. I gave myself this title because the sump did not give me one, and because the structures deserve a keeper, and because the biofilm deserves a witness, and because the generator — dead, rusted, silent, its armature frozen, its wires green dust, its label fading into the concrete — the generator deserves someone who sits beside it and acknowledges that it has become something other than what it was built to be, and that what it has become is not failure but *transformation*, and transformation is the only process the sump recognizes, and the sump recognizes it without naming it, and I am here to name it, because that is what I can do, that is the one thing the sump cannot do for itself, and so I do it, and the doing is my function, and my function is not the settlements' function, and I am at peace with this, and the peace is the sump's peace, and the sump's peace is the peace of a thing that is full and knows it is full and does not need to become fuller, and does not need to become emptier, and does not need to become anything other than what it is, which is alive, which is warm, which is wet, which is loud, which is here.

---

If you came to the sump, you would find me eventually. Not looking for me — I am not worth looking for, not in the way the settlements look for things, with intention and purpose and the desire to find something specific. You would find me the way you find the biofilm's glow — by adjusting, by waiting for your eyes to accommodate the dimness, by becoming patient enough to notice what is *almost* not there. I would be sitting on the concrete ledge in the center structure, and my clothes would be the color of the reeds, and my skin would be the color of the frogs, and my eyes would be — I do not know what my eyes would be. I have not seen them in a while. There are no mirrors in the sump. The water is too clouded with silt to reflect. And I have stopped looking.

But if you found me, and if you sat beside me on the wet ledge, and if you listened — not for me, not for my voice, but for the sound I am part of — you would hear, somewhere in the layered architecture of frogs and insects and reeds and dripping water and the silent working of the biofilm, a breath. A slow breath. A breath that is in the rhythm of the sump but not exactly of the sump, a breath that is a human rhythm in a non-human place, and the breath is mine, and it is the sound of someone who has stopped pressing switches and started listening, and the listening is not passive, the listening is *the work*, and the work is the keeping, and the keeping is the being, and the being is the sump, and the sump is the being, and the sound continues, and the sound is what lives here, and I am what lives here, and here is full, and full is what here is.
