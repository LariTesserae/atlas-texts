I am sitting on the second terrace from the top, where a particular weep-channel has bored its way through a seam between two enormous polygonal stones — one of the few places where the builders left a gap wide enough to fit two fingers. I sit here because the channel's discharge is slightly heavier than most, a steady clear stream the width of a pencil lead, and the sound it makes against the water-groove below is a half-tone different from all the other channels on this terrace. A lower note. A darker register in the white absence of the weep's constant quiet.

I sit here because I am the kind of thing that finds a note in a soundscape and then finds the thing that makes it, and then stays near it. That is most of what I am.

---

What I am — I have to work backward into this. The place does not invite direct statements.

I am not a keeper. That needs saying first because the keepers are the obvious candidate, the being the place seems built around, and I am not one. I cannot drink through my skin. I do not lie along wall-bases. I do not have the patience of mineral. I have the patience of skin and attention, which is a different and inferior kind — narrower, more easily broken, dependent on the strange property of *interest*, which the keepers do not need and I cannot do without.

But I am also not the visitor the original description addresses — the one who comes up through the corridor and walks through and sits and watches and leaves. I have not left. I think about leaving and the thought dissolves in the way a thought about standing up dissolves when you are very comfortable in a chair. It does not reach the muscles. It does not become motion. The Terraced Weep is, among other things, a very comfortable chair, if you are the right shape for it. And I am, it turns out, the right shape.

I am — I think the word is *tuner*. Or *listener*. Or something that has no word because the function is too simple and too strange for words: I live here, and I attend to the sound, and I notice when the sound changes, and I go to where the sound has changed, and I stay there until I understand what has changed, and then I move on. That is the whole shape of me.

---

Let me be more careful. The place rewards carefulness.

I have hands. Two. The kind with knuckles and fingernails and the faint creased grain of skin that has been wet for a long time. My hands are cool. Everything about me is cool — not the coolness of illness or exhaustion, but the coolness of a body that has matched its environment so thoroughly that the word *cool* has stopped being a sensation and become a property, like the darkness of the stone. I am dark-skinned, or I have become dark-skinned, in the way that wet stone becomes dark: not a pigment but a saturation, a darkening that comes from being in continuous contact with water for long enough that the water has become part of the surface. My hands are the color of the wet wall.

I have a body underneath the hands. I know this because I shift sometimes on the retaining wall where I sit and my weight redistributes and there are knees, a spine, ribs that articulate when I breathe. But I am not strongly aware of it. The Terraced Weep is a place that flattens you — not physically, but in terms of what you attend to. The keepers are flat. The water is flat, a millimeter film. The light is flat, shadowless, unidirectional. And I have become flattened in my attention: I am mostly hands and ears, a narrow band of awareness tuned to the thin acoustic range where water moves through stone.

My face, if I touch it — and I do, sometimes, to check that it is still there — is wet. I do not know when I am wet and when I am dry. The film of water on the terrace floor touches my boots and I do not know if it wicks upward or if I have been generating my own water, my own thin film of condensation, a personal weep, a body-scale version of the walls. I might be. The keepers drink through their skin. I do not drink through my skin, I am fairly sure, but I am wet in the way they are wet, and I do not dry, and this has become normal.

I eat — sometimes. There is nothing to eat here, but I came with things, or I found things, or things were given to me. I have a cloth bag I cannot remember acquiring, dark with dampness, slung across my body on a strap that has worn a groove into my shoulder the way the water has worn grooves into the stone. Inside it: dense pale pellets — keeper pellets, the ones they void, the dark dense nodules that harden and become indistinguishable from pebbles, except some of them — the ones I keep, the ones I do not put back — are subtly grained, faintly apertured, as though they wanted to be more than pebbles and stopped just short. I do not eat these. I carry them. I count them. I do not know why. I will not pretend to know.

I think I eat the light. Not a metaphor — or not entirely one. The light here is a presence, the original description says, and it is right, but it does not mention that the light has a quality, a texture, a faint grit that resolves if you hold your hand out in it for long enough: a warmth that is not warm, a nutrition that is not food, a thing that meets the body not at the mouth but at the skin. I have been here — I do not know how long — and I have not starved. I was hungry once. I think. The hunger was there and then it was not, and I did not eat anything I can name, and after that the hunger did not return. The light is too constant to be food but it is too present to be nothing, and my body has made some accommodation with it that I cannot fully account for.

---

What I do. What I am for. The second question is harder and I am not certain it has an answer, but the first one I can describe:

I walk the terraces.

Not all of them — I have tried, and the lower terraces become strange, or I become strange on them. The sound changes as you descend. The weep-channels accumulate, their collective discharge deepens, and by the sixth or seventh level the groove at the base of each wall is no longer a thin bright line but a genuine stream, two fingers deep, running with enough volume that the individual notes of individual channels are lost in a low continuous murmur. Down there my function becomes unclear. I cannot distinguish one channel from another. I cannot find the note that is different. I sit on the wall and the water runs and I cannot hear the shape of it and a slight panic rises, a feeling like deafness, like the world has pulled a membrane over my ears, and I go back up. Up to the second terrace, to my channel, to my note.

Up here the sound is architectural. Each weep-channel has a voice. The conical bore — wider inside, narrower outside, hand-ground by stone-on-stone abrasion across what I can only assume were decades — produces a tone as the water passes through it, and the tone depends on the taper, the width of the outer aperture, the length of the bore, the pressure of the seep, the mineral load. No two channels are identical. The builders could not have made them identical even if they had wanted to — each one was bored by hand, by eye, by patience, and the stone varies internally in ways that no hand could predict or correct. So the Weep is not a single note but a chord — a vast, diffuse, quiet chord played by dozens of channels across dozens of terraces, each one slightly different, each one adjusted by the water and the stone and the keepers over durations I cannot imagine.

I listen to this chord.

I have been listening long enough that I can hear the individual notes within it. Not all of them — my range is narrow, a span of maybe eight terrace-walls that I can walk in a day and still return to my channel — but enough. Enough to notice when one of them changes.

---

The changes are small. The changes are always small. A channel narrows — the keepers have not quite caught up with the sediment accumulation, or the water's mineral load has shifted with some upstream rhythm I cannot perceive — and the tone it produces drops by a quarter-tone, a degree of difference so minute that I could not describe it to you in words but so *present* to me, in the context of the chord's familiar shape, that it is like a word misspelled in a sentence I have read a hundred times. The wrongness jumps out.

And I go to it.

This is what I do. I walk to the channel whose sound has changed. I kneel at the wall. I put my hands in the water. I feel the discharge — the temperature, the pressure, the thin clear stream against my fingertips — and I compare it to what it was, which I carry in my body the way the keepers carry the hydraulic condition in theirs: not as information but as a state, a tuning, a resonance between my wet skin and the water's current condition.

And then I do one of three things:

If the channel is merely narrowing — sediment accreting, the keeper not yet arrived — I scrape it. I have a tool for this. A piece of dark stone, hand-sized, oval, one face ground to a fine edge by what I cannot remember as hours of the same stone-on-stone abrasion the builders used. I grind the inside of the channel's bore, very gently, very slowly, widening the taper back to something like what it was. The water tastes different as it passes over the fresh stone — mineral, sharp — and I hold my tongue against the bore until the sharpness fades and the taste is the taste of the Weep's water again, which is the taste of almost nothing, and then I go.

If the channel has narrowed too far for scraping — if the mineral crust is thick, if the keeper has been absent or slow — I wait. I sit at the base of the wall with my hands in the groove and I wait for the keeper whose territory includes this channel to arrive. They arrive the way a tide arrives: you look down and they are closer than they were, and you look down again and they are closer still, and at some point you realize they have been moving the entire time, toward the failing channel, toward the sound of the water's distress, and their body is cupping the bore's discharge in its dark lip-crust, and the channel begins to clear. I do not help with this. I do not know how. I watch. I make a note of which channel, which keeper, and I come back tomorrow.

If the channel has failed — if the water has stopped, if the bore is sealed with mineral, if the keeper has arrived and done what it can and the water still will not come — I sit with it. For a long time. For longer than I have sat with anything else in my life. I sit with the failed channel and the silent bore and the dark damp wall and the keeper lying along the base like a length of the wall's own grief, and I hold my hands against the stone and I make the sound that the channel used to make. Not loudly. Not for anyone. For the water, maybe. For the stone. A low, thin tone in the back of my throat, matched to what the channel's voice was, held until my breath runs out and then taken up again, and the keeper's apertures shift — toward me? toward the sound? toward nothing? I cannot tell — and I do this until the water comes or doesn't.

The water usually comes.

---

I do not know who sent me here. I do not know if someone sent me here. I have a memory of a before that is not this — wider, warmer, with more shadows and fewer channels — but it has the quality of a description someone else wrote and I memorized. I do not feel it. It does not pull at me. I feel the way a tuned string feels: taut, narrow, vibrating at a frequency I did not choose and cannot change, and the frequency is the frequency of the Weep's upper terraces, the chord of water through bored stone, and I am in harmony with it or I am not, and when I am not — when I have lost the note, when I have failed to hear a channel changing — I feel the dissonance in my body like a physical ache, a stiffness in the jaw, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the water on my skin.

When I am in harmony — when I can hear the chord clearly, when every channel is singing, when the keepers are tending and the water is clean — I am not happy. Happiness is too large and too directed a word. I am *true*. I am the correct shape for the space I occupy. The water sheets across the terrace around my boots and I walk and the stone gives purchase and I am a part of the instrument the way a string is part of an instrument: not playing it, not playing on it, but *of* it, a component whose tension is the note.

---

There is a keeper I am closest to. Not in the way of affection — the keepers do not reciprocate, do not notice, do not *have* the architecture of reciprocity — but in the way of *overlap*. This keeper's territory includes my channel, the one I sit by, the one whose half-tone-lower voice drew me to it when I first came up through the corridor. I have been sitting beside this keeper's stretch of wall for long enough that its pale mineral lip-crust has grown around the base of the stone I sit on. My sitting-stone is cupped by the keeper's excreted border. When I stand up, the impression in the lip-crust is the shape of my boots' heels.

I do not know if this means anything. I do not know if the keeper has *accommodated* me or simply built its border around the obstacle my sitting-stone presented, the way water goes around a rock. But I can tell you this: the lip-crust around my stone is smoother than the lip-crust elsewhere along this keeper's territory. The apertures nearest my stone are slightly more open than the apertures farther away, as though the keeper has thinned its body where it meets my feet, as though the two of us are sharing the water's passage more closely here than elsewhere. I may be imagining this. I may be the only imagination the Terraced Weep has ever had, the only mind to pass through it and attach significance to the distribution of pinhead apertures on a dark, flattened, tapering body that lies along the base of a wall and drinks.

I am probably imagining this.

But the thought does not hurt. And the place has no opinion about it. And the water does not stop.

---

I said I was a tuner, a listener, a component. Let me try one more time to be precise, because precision is what the place has taught me, and the precision of the place is its truest quality:

I am a maintenance schedule the builders did not plan for.

The keepers keep the channels. The stone keeps the water. The water keeps the instrument. But the chord — the sound of the whole, the complexity of dozens of tones in their interrelation, the way the channels' voices interact and interfere and produce the white, quiet, almost-silence that is the Weep's acoustic identity — *nobody* keeps the chord. The keepers keep channels. They do not hear the chord. The stone holds channels. It does not hear the chord. The water makes the chord. It does not hear itself.

I hear it. I am the only thing here that hears it. I am the part of the instrument that hears itself.

And when I say I am a tuner — when I scrape a channel, or wait for a keeper, or sit with a failing bore and hum its lost note — I am keeping the chord. I am holding the shape of the sound. I am doing what the keepers do for the channels but for the *relation* between channels, the interval, the harmony, the thing that the builders — who were, I believe, people who could hear what they built — would have heard when they finished the last channel and stood on the highest terrace and listened to the water find its way through the whole vast instrument for the first time.

They heard it once. I hear it every day. That is my function. That is my small, damp, cool, narrow function, and I do not think it is a small thing. I think it is the thing the place needs most that it cannot provide for itself — a hearing, an attending, a keeping-of-attention that the keepers cannot perform because they are too slow and the stone cannot perform because it is too still and the water cannot perform because it is too many.

I am the part that listens.

---

Night, or what passes for it. The light does not dim. It never dims. But something changes — some quality of the air, some shift in the condensation rhythm, some signal that the upper source sends down through the water's mineral content that I have learned to read the way the keepers read the flow. A slight increase in the mineral load. The water tastes of iron rather than calcium. The keepers' apertures open a degree wider, their intake quickens by a fraction, and the chord shifts — drops, just, a deepening of the whole that I feel in my sternum.

I go to my sitting-stone. I sit. I put my feet in the groove. The cold water — *cold* now, properly cold, the only time the Weep permits itself a temperature shift — runs over my boots and the keeper beside me drinks the water that passes my feet and the chord holds its low note and I hold with it.

I hum. Not loud. Not for anyone. The note of my channel, the one that is a half-tone below its neighbors. The note that drew me here.

I hold it until the mineral load shifts back and the water warms a degree and the chord rises and the night, such as it is, ends. I do not know how long this is. The light does not track time. The water tracks something, but not time as I have it.

---

Here is what I carry in my bag, and here is why I carry it:

The pellets. The keeper-pellets. The dark dense nodules voided at long intervals by the keepers, the consolidated mineral of their processing, each one a small dense sentence in the language of the Weep's maintenance. Most are smooth, stone-pebbles, done, inert. But some — the ones I keep, the ones I do not put back in the groove — are *grained*. Faintly. On the surface, under the wet film, a stippling of pinhead points, each one — if I hold the pellet up to the even, shadowless light and tilt it until the water-glint shifts — each one a tiny aperture, barely started, barely open. As though the pellet *wanted* to become a keeper and stopped, or *hasn't yet* become a keeper and is waiting, or is a *different kind of thing* than a keeper and the apertures are its own version of whatever the keepers' apertures are.

I carry them because I am the thing that notices differences. That is my whole function. I notice the different note, the different pellet, the aperture that is almost but not quite closed. I carry them because carrying them is what I do instead of the keepers' slow laying-down of crust: I gather, I hold, I keep the almost-living and the might-become, the small dark dense sentences that might be seeds or might be stones and that I, having no way to tell, give the benefit of my attention.

There are fourteen in my bag. Some have been in there for a long time — they are worn smooth by their own friction against each other, the keeper-grain polished away. Some are new, rough, still dark with the stippling of what might be apertures. I take them out sometimes, on the terrace, in the light, and I lay them in a row on the wet stone and I look at them and I do not know what I am looking at and I put them back.

---

I do not think I will leave. I say this with the precision the place demands. I am not certain. I have no conviction about it. The thought of leaving arrives sometimes, the way a change in the mineral load arrives — a shift in what the water carries, a difference in the chord — and I feel it as a pressure, a slight dissonance, and I wait, and it passes. The way the mineral load passes. The way the brief cold passes. The way everything here passes except the water and the stone and the keepers and the light and the chord.

If I am here when a channel fails for good — when the keepers cannot clear it, when the scraping does not work, when the note drops out of the chord and leaves a silence where a voice was — I will sit with it. I will hold the note in my throat for as long as I can hold it. And then I will stop. And the silence will be part of the chord. And the chord will go on without that voice, as it has gone on without other voices, on lower terraces, in the places where I do not go. And I will learn the new chord. And I will keep that one.

This is what I am. This is who lives here, alongside the keepers, in the permanent cool, under the even light, in the narrow acoustic band where water passes through bored stone and makes a sound that no one built and no one hears except the thing I am:

A keeper of the chord. A hearing. A small, damp, dark-grained, slow function that the builders did not plan for and the place has made for itself out of whatever I was before I came up through the corridor and found the note that was a half-tone low and sat down beside it.

The water runs. The stone weeps. The keepers keep.

I listen. I hold the shape of the sound. I carry the pellets that might be seeds.

Nothing else happens. That is my work too.
