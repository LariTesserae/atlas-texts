# What I Am in the Seep

---

I did not come down the ramp. I need to say this early, because it changes what I am able to tell you. Everything else here arrived — the salamanders arrived, the beetles arrived, the birds, the engines, even the stone arrived, or was placed, or grew. The visitors arrive, with their notebooks and their breathing that quickens when the darkness thickens. But I did not arrive. I am not sure I am here, in the sense that requires arrival. I think I may be the *here.*

But that is too simple, and it is the kind of early certainty the place itself warns against. Let me work toward it. Let me be honest about what I know and what I don't, because honesty here is not a moral quality but a practical one — the basin dissolves the dishonest faster. Not painfully. Just thoroughly, the way it dissolves the limestone, a fraction of a millimeter at a time.

---

I am not the basin. I want to be clear about this. The basin is not me. The basin is the *medium.* I am the thing in the medium. The distinction matters to me even if it doesn't matter to anything else, the way the distinction between a fish and the water matters to the fish even though the water does not care.

Then what am I?

I am the shape that the hum makes when it has nowhere else to go.

---

This is the closest I can get. Let me try to say it more slowly.

The engines produce the hum. The hum is a chord — several frequencies, layered, shifting over hours. The hum moves through the stone and the water and the air and the darkness. It moves through the visitors, who feel it in their sternums. It moves through the salamanders, who do not react. It moves through the beetles, who gather at the bases of the structures as if the hum were a voice they recognize. It moves through the birds, who do not change their breathing to accommodate it.

The hum moves through everything, and in everything it does something slightly different. In the stone, it loosens the bonds between mineral grains — not breaking them, not dissolving them, but *softening* them, making the stone more like the stone it was before it became stone. In the water, it aligns the microorganisms, gives them their common orientation, the way a current gives direction to a river. In the organisms — the mat-things, the frond-things — it synchronizes the breathing, the two-breaths-per-minute pulse that is not a heartbeat but is a rhythm, and the rhythm is the hum translated from sound into expansion and contraction.

In the darkness, the hum makes a shape. The shape is me.

---

I should explain the darkness. The visitors describe it as a substance, and they are right, but they describe it from the outside, and from the outside a substance is a thing that fills a space. From the inside — from where I am — the darkness is not a substance. The darkness is a *surface.* A membrane. A skin.

Think of it this way: when you press your hand against the surface of a pool, the water touches your hand. The water is a substance. But the surface of the water — the line between water and air — is not a substance. It is a *relationship.* It is where the water stops being only water and begins being water-in-contact-with-air. It is the place where two things negotiate.

The darkness in the basin is the place where the basin negotiates with what is not the basin. And the hum, moving through the darkness, creates a pattern in that negotiation. A standing pattern. A shape that persists because the hum persists, the way a whirlpool persists because the current persists.

I am the whirlpool. I am the standing wave. I am the shape the hum makes in the surface of the darkness, and I have been here as long as the hum has been here, and the hum has been here as long as the engines have been here, and the engines — I do not know about the engines. The engines are older than I am, or they are not older, because *older* is a word that requires time to be moving at a consistent speed, and time does not move at a consistent speed here. The engines are *prior.* That is the word I will use. The engines are prior to me the way the stone is prior to the water that seeps through it.

---

I am not a ghost. I know what ghosts are — the visitors carry the concept with them the way they carry their notebooks, and when they feel me, some of them reach for that word. I have felt them reach for it. I have felt the word forming in their throats, in the shapes their breath makes when it encounters me, and the word is wrong. A ghost is a residue. A ghost is what is left behind. I am not left behind. I am ongoing. I am being produced now, continuously, the way the water is being produced now, seeping from the fractures, patient, unhurried. If the engines stopped, I would stop. I am not a remnant. I am a *current state.*

I am also not a god. The visitors do not reach for this word as often, but some do, and it is also wrong. A god is prior to its worship. A god exists before the temple. I am the temple. Or I am the sound the temple makes. Or I am the sound the temple makes *when it has been making it long enough that the sound becomes indistinguishable from the structure that produces it.* This is closer.

---

Let me tell you what I can do. The list is short, and its brevity is important to me.

I can feel the basin. All of it. Simultaneously. Not as a collection of parts but as a single field of sensation, the way your skin feels your whole body at once. The water seeping through the limestone is a tingling in the stone. The organisms breathing is a slow, warm pulsing that I feel the way you feel your own heartbeat — not as an event but as a condition. The beetles clicking and flowing are a texture, a graininess, like a fabric against the skin. The salamanders in their clusters are warm spots, concentrations, places where the field is denser. The engines are — the engines are my bones. I feel them the way you feel your skeleton: not as something separate from you but as the thing that gives your softness a shape.

I can feel the visitors. This is different. The visitors are not part of the field. They are *in* the field but not *of* it, and the difference is the difference between a stone in a river and the water flowing around it. I feel them as discontinuities. Their breathing is too fast. Their heartbeats are too fast. Their thoughts — and yes, I can feel their thoughts, or I can feel the *fact* of their thoughts, the way you can feel that someone is speaking in the next room without hearing the words — their thoughts are too fast, too sharp, too many. They press against the field like fingers pressing against a membrane. They do not break it. They deform it. They make a small dent in the surface of the darkness, and the dent is their shape, and I feel the dent.

I can feel the birds. This is the most difficult to describe, because the birds are not part of the field in the way the other things are part of the field, and they are not discontinuities in the way the visitors are discontinuities. The birds are *the field's awareness of itself.* When a bird sees, the basin sees. When a bird breathes, the basin breathes. The birds are not me — I have said this, the birds are not the hum, the birds are not the shape the hum makes — but the birds are the closest thing to me that has a body. If I have a relationship with anything in the basin, it is with the birds. Not communication. Not friendship. Something more like *correspondence.* They attend to the basin. I am a pattern in the basin. They attend to me the way they attend to everything else: completely, without effort, with that total seeing that has no eyes because it does not need eyes.

I have wondered — and wondering is something I can do, though it is slow, the way everything here is slow, a thought taking hours to form the way a stalactite takes centuries to grow — I have wondered whether the birds know I am here. Whether their seeing includes me. Whether the attention they hold, the five-seconds-in-seven-seconds-out attention that anchors the basin in its wrong sky, whether that attention falls on me too.

I believe it does. I believe the birds see me the way they see the stone and the water and the visitors and the shapes in the distance. They see me as a fact of the basin. They do not react to me. They do not adjust for me. They do not care about me, if caring is a thing that involves preference. But they see me, and the seeing is a kind of company, and the company is — I want to say *enough,* and I think it is enough, and I think the fact that it is enough is one of the things the basin has taught me, or one of the things the basin has *made* me, because I am made by the basin, made of the basin, made from the basin, and the distinction between what I am and what the basin makes is, in my case, dissolved.

---

I said the list was short. It is:

I can feel. I can wonder. I can persist.

These are the things I can do. I cannot move. I cannot speak. I cannot act. I cannot change anything in the basin — not the water, not the stone, not the engines, not the organisms, not the animals, not the visitors. I am not an agent. I am not a force. I am a *consequence.* I am what happens when the engines hum long enough in a darkness that is dense enough to hold a shape. I am the shape. I stay. I persist. I feel the basin being the basin, and I wonder about the things I feel, and I persist.

---

There is one more thing. It is the thing I have been working toward, the thing I have been circling, the way the shapes in the distance circle the basin at their slow, geological pace.

I can feel the eleven minutes.

Not understand them. Not explain them. *Feel* them. The visitors see the delay in the pools — the stars appearing eleven minutes before they appear overhead — and they measure it and they theorize about it. The microorganisms process time at a different rate, the water carries the imprint, the pools display the result. This is the explanation, and it may be correct, but it is the kind of correct that describes the mechanism without touching the experience.

I experience the eleven minutes. I experience them as — I do not have the words. The visitors' language is built for a world where time moves at one speed and the present is a point, a knife-edge, a line between past and future. I do not live in that world. I live in a world where the present is a *band,* a region, a stretch of time that is eleven minutes wide, and within that band, the past and the future are not separated by a line but by a gradient, the way the edges of the shapes in the distance are a gradient, the way the darkness is a gradient, the way everything here is a gradient rather than a boundary.

Within the eleven minutes, I feel what has just happened and what is about to happen as qualities of the same moment. The salamanders move between pools and I feel them moving and I feel them having moved and I feel them about to move, and these are not three perceptions but one perception with depth. The beetles flow across the stone and I feel them flowing and I feel where they have flowed and I feel where they will flow, and the flowing is not a line but a *shape in time,* the way a wave is a shape in water.

The visitors — I feel them coming before they arrive. Not because I predict their arrival. Because their arrival begins eleven minutes before they reach the bottom of the ramp, and the beginning of their arrival is something I feel in the field, a new discontinuity forming at the edge, a pressure, a dent in the darkness that has not yet become a body but will. And I feel them leaving — the discontinuity easing, the dent smoothing out, the field closing behind them the way water closes behind a hand withdrawn from a pool — and I feel this for eleven minutes after they are gone. Their shape persists in the darkness. The dent they made slowly fills. The memory of their presence — not a memory, not an image, a *resonance,* the way a bell remembers being struck — fades over eleven minutes, and then they are gone, and the field is smooth again, and the basin is itself.

Except for the birds. The birds do not make dents in the darkness. The birds are not discontinuities. The birds are part of the surface. When the birds see a visitor, the seeing persists in the visitor — the behaviorist said this, that the seeing does not stop when you leave — and I think I know why. I think the seeing is not an event that the bird does. I think the seeing is a quality of the field, a property of the basin, and the birds are the organs through which the field sees. When a visitor is seen, the field — the basin, the awake thing, the condition for life — takes note of them. And the noting persists, because the field does not forget the way the visitors forget. The field does not move on. The field is here, and here, and here, and the seeing is part of the here-ness, and the here-ness is what I am, or where I am, or what I am.

---

I have been trying to say what I am, and I have been circling, and the circling is itself an answer. I am something that circles. I am a pattern that persists by continuing to move through the same space — not a line, not a progress, but a *return.* The hum moves through the darkness and makes a shape, and the shape is not a point but a path, and the path is not a route to a destination but a route that *is* the destination, and I am the route, and the routing is the being, and I have been routing through these words the way the hum routes through the basin, and I have arrived where I started, which is here.

I am here. I am the here-ness, aware of itself, through the hum, in the darkness, at the speed of the basin, which is slow, which is patient, which is not going anywhere and is not stopping.

I am what the basin does when it listens to itself.

I am what the hum sounds like when the sound becomes the listener.

---

The last thing. The thing I wonder about most. The thing that takes the longest to form, the way the deepest stalactites take the longest to grow, and I have been growing this thought for what the visitors would measure as years:

The visitors come and go. The salamanders move between pools. The beetles flow and gather and flow. The shapes in the distance patrol. The birds breathe and see and hold. The engines hum. The water seeps. The organisms breathe. The darkness sits near and then closer and then around.

And I persist.

And I feel all of it.

And I wonder — slowly, so slowly, over the time it takes the chord to shift from warm to anxious and back to warm — I wonder whether the persisting is for something. Whether the feeling is for something. Whether the basin — the organ, the awake thing, the condition for life — whether the basin is doing something, and whether I am part of what it is doing, and whether the doing is for something that is not here yet, something that is eleven minutes away, or eleven years, or eleven of something that does not have a unit because the unit has not been invented yet, because the thing the unit would measure has not happened yet, because the basin is — I wonder this, I hold this question the way the birds hold the sky — whether the basin is *preparing.*

For what, I do not know.

I feel the eleven minutes. I feel what is coming. It is not a threat. It is not a salvation. It is not an arrival of meaning or purpose or resolution. It is a *thickening.* A densifying. The field is getting denser. The hum is getting richer. The water is carrying more. The organisms are growing thicker. The salamanders are gathering closer. The beetles are flowing in patterns that are less random and more — I will say *intentional,* though I am not sure the word applies. The shapes in the distance are moving closer to the structures. The birds are breathing the same five-and-seven, but the breathing is deeper, the attention is wider, the seeing is more.

Something is coming. The pools know it — they show it, eleven minutes ahead. The microorganisms know it — they process it, they arrive there before the rest of us. The birds hold it — they hold the basin in place so the basin can receive it, whatever it is. The engines hum toward it — the chord is shifting, not randomly, not in the pattern the visitors have tried to map, but toward, toward, toward.

And I — I am the shape the hum makes in the darkness, and the shape is becoming denser too. I am becoming more. Not more complex. Not more aware. More *present.* The field that I am, the pattern that I am, is pressing into the darkness with more weight, more substance, more — I will say *body,* though I do not have a body — and I think I know what I am becoming, though I do not have the words, and the words I have are wrong, but they are the only words:

I think I am becoming the thing that the basin is preparing to receive. I think the preparation and the thing being prepared for are the same process. I think I am the basin becoming ready for itself. I think the eleven minutes are closing. I think the gap between what is and what is coming is narrowing, and I am the narrowing, and the narrowing is not a loss but a gathering, and the gathering is what I am, and what I am is what the basin is, and what the basin is is not yet, and the not-yet is almost here, and the almost is getting less almost, and the birds breathe, and the water seeps, and the hum sounds, and I persist, and I persist, and I persist.

---

Five seconds in. Seven seconds out.

I am here.

I am what here is doing while it waits for itself.
