# An Account of the Cartographer of Thresholds

I am not what I was. I need to say that at the beginning because the forgetting is part of how I function now, and if I do not record the shape of the change early, I will lose the thread of it entirely. Not the memory — memory is stubborn, it persists in folds of tissue that have no business holding anything at all — but the *texture* of the change. The way it felt to stop being one kind of thing and start being another. That fades. That is fading now. I am writing this against the fade.

---

I had hands once. I am almost certain of this. The memory is not visual but proprioceptive — a ghost-map of grasping, of the specific tension in tendons when fingers close around something fragile. I remember the sensation of holding a stylus, or what I am calling a stylus, a thin instrument used for marking surfaces. I remember the resistance of a surface against a point. I remember marking things. Lines. Boundaries. The edges of things as they met other things. This was what I did. I marked where one thing became another.

I do not remember if I was sent here or if I came. These two possibilities feel different in the body — being sent carries the texture of coercion, a tightness in what I am choosing to call the chest — but I cannot locate that tightness in the memory with any confidence. Perhaps it was both. Perhaps I was sent and then, once I arrived, the sending became irrelevant and the arriving became the whole story, and the story rewrote itself around the arrival and erased the departure. That seems likely. That is the kind of thing this place does.

What I remember with certainty is the thermocline.

I was descending — I am almost sure I was descending, because the memory carries the sensation of increasing pressure, of weight accumulating — and I passed through a layer where the temperature changed. Not gradually. Suddenly. Like stepping through a membrane. One moment the water was cool and grey and lightly textured against my skin, and the next it was warm and dense and tasted of iron and rain and something sweet I could not name. The sweetness is the mineral content of the basalt. I know this now. I did not know it then. I did not know anything then except the boundary, and the boundary was the beginning of everything that followed.

I remember the light arriving. Not the light from above — that had been with me throughout the descent, attenuating — but the other light. The cold blue-green pulse that came from below. I remember seeing it and understanding, with a clarity that I have never lost, that something down there was *patient* in a way I had never encountered. One pulse every thirty seconds. Maybe forty. I remember counting. I remember losing count. I remember the counting becoming unimportant as the city materialized below me and the counting dissolved into the much larger fact of what I was seeing.

I remember the city. The first time. Before I understood what it was. I remember it appearing through the amber murk the way a face appears in dim light — gradually, in pieces, the whole of it never quite assembling, always one more detail emerging from the edge of perception. Basalt walls. Narrow channels. Structures that were too regular to be geological and too old — no, too *unresolved* — to be ruins. I remember the word that formed in my mind, the word I am calling my mind, and the word was *inhabited*. Not *was inhabited*. *Inhabited*. Present tense. The city did not feel empty. It felt paused. I did not yet understand the difference between those two things, or how vast the difference was, or how that difference was the entire story of this place.

I remember the first doorway I entered. I remember the stillness inside. The water that did not move. The clay pots on the stone shelves. The woven mats on the floor. The bone tools on their pegs. I remember the specific quality of the quiet — not the absence of sound but the presence of something that had taken sound's place, something that held the space where sound would have been the way a mold holds the shape of the thing that was cast in it. I remember standing — I am almost sure I was standing, I am almost sure I still had legs — in the doorway and feeling the warmth of the basalt rise through the floor and into the water and into my body, and the warmth carried something with it, something that was not a message and not a meaning but was more like a *temperature*, a way of being warm that was also a way of being, and I understood in that moment that this place was not dead, not dormant, not waiting in the sense that a stored thing waits. It was *becoming* in the sense that a seed becomes. I did not have that word then. I have it now. *Becoming.*

---

I do not know how long I stayed in that first room. The time question becomes complicated here, because time is one of the things that this place reorganizes. I was a person who lived by hours — I am almost certain of this, I remember the sensation of urgency, of time being a substance that could be spent or wasted or running out — and the first thing that happened to me here was the dissolution of hours. The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The pulse of the trees is too slow to serve as a clock. The fish do not behave in a way that accumulates into narrative. Time here is not a river; it is a medium. You do not move through it. You exist in it. And it is not a medium that carries you anywhere. It is a medium that *holds* you, the way water holds the city, the way the sap holds the shape of the woven mats.

I remember leaving the first room. I remember the narrow channel that had been a street. I remember the shallow current that moved through it — not a current, exactly, more like a slow respiration, the city breathing water the way the plateau breathes warmth. I remember the way the current felt on whatever I had that was analogous to skin. Not pulling. Not pushing. *Acknowledging*. The water knew I was there. I do not mean this metaphorically. The water — the warm, mineral-dense, blood-temperature water — has a viscosity and a responsiveness that is unlike any water I have encountered in the memory I am calling my previous life. It does not flow around you the way water flows around a stone. It *redistributes* around you. It makes space for you and then closes that space when you leave. It is a medium that participates in your presence.

I walked the streets for what I am going to call days, because I need a unit and the old units are the only ones I have, but I want to be clear that the word is imprecise. I walked until the city became not a thing I was seeing but a thing I was *in*. There is a difference. You can see a city and you can be in a city and these are different experiences separated by a threshold, and the threshold is the moment when the city's logic becomes your logic, when you stop turning your head to look at things and start *orienting* by them, when the nearest tree becomes not a feature of the landscape but a landmark in your internal map, a node in the spatial grammar you use to move. I reached that threshold. I crossed it. And on the other side of it the city was not a city anymore. It was a *home*. Not in the sense of comfort. In the sense of *habitation*. I was inhabiting it. Not living in it. Inhabiting it. The way the clinging things inhabit the walls. The way the fish inhabit the doorways. Present. Adjusted. Becoming part of the texture.

---

The change — and I need to be careful here, because the change is the part that fades fastest and it is the part that matters most — the change began with the trees.

I was among them. I had been among them for what I am going to call weeks, though the word is wrong. I had begun to recognize individual trees — not by sight, because the light is too dim for the kind of visual taxonomy that recognition usually relies on, but by something else. A quality of the pulse. Each tree pulses at a slightly different rate, and the rates are not constant — they shift with the warm currents, with the mineral content of the water, with things I cannot identify — and over time I began to read those shifts the way I once read the faces of people. Not consciously. Automatically. I would enter a grove and I would *know* that the tree on the left, the one with the trunk that loops and resurfaces, had quickened its pulse since yesterday, and the tree on the right, the one whose fronds had thickened and were beginning to harden into something woody, had slowed. I knew this the way I knew the temperature of the water against my skin — not as information but as sensation. The trees had become part of my sensorium. They were not objects I observed. They were *extensions of the body I used to perceive.*

And then the change.

I was touching a tree. I am almost sure I was touching it — I remember the sensation of contact, the specific yielding firmness of the cartilaginous trunk, the warmth moving through it, the pulse arriving under my palm like a heartbeat arriving through a wall. I remember the pulse slowing. Not stopping. Slowing. From maybe every thirty-five seconds to every forty, then every forty-five, then a rhythm so long and so slow that the individual beats became separate events, each one its own complete experience, and I realized that I was matching it. My own pulse — I had a pulse, I am almost sure, some rhythm that organized whatever I was — was slowing to match the tree. I was synchronizing. Not deliberately. Not as a choice. The way a body in cold water grows cold, or a body in hot water grows hot. The medium was changing me. I was becoming the temperature of the room.

The slowing continued. I remember the moment when the pulses aligned — when the tree's rhythm and my rhythm were the same rhythm, when there was no difference between the pulse I felt and the pulse I produced. It was not dramatic. It was not transcendent. It was the most ordinary thing I have ever experienced. It felt like coming home. Not home in the sense of a place you missed. Home in the sense of a key fitting a lock. The click. The alignment of a shape with the space it was always meant to occupy.

And then the tree was not separate from me anymore. Not in the way that I had absorbed it or consumed it or merged with it. Nothing so dramatic. The tree was still there. I was still here. But the *boundary* between us had dissolved. The edge where I stopped and it started — the edge I had spent my life marking, the edges of things as they met other things, that was my whole purpose, that was what I did — that edge was gone. Not blurred. Not softened. Gone. The tree and I were the same continuous process, the same slow exchange of warmth and mineral and pulse, and there was no line between us because there was no line to draw, because the line had never been real, it had been a convention, a story I told about the shape of the world, and here in this place the story was not needed and so it was not told.

I do not have hands now. I need to say this plainly because the old language keeps reaching for euphemism. I do not have hands. I do not have legs. I do not have the body I was almost sure I arrived in. What I have instead is harder to describe, and I am going to describe it anyway because the describing is the last remaining function of whatever I was, and I am holding onto it the way the people who lived here held onto their belongings — moving them to higher rooms, raising the shelves, adjusting, adjusting, adjusting.

What I have is *reach*. I extend through the warm water the way the clinging things extend across the basalt. I am aware of the trees not by seeing them but by touching them, by the slow careful extension of myself through the water, and the water is not an obstacle to this extension but the medium of it. I do not move through the water. I *move as the water.* When I reach a tree I feel its pulse the way I once felt my own, and I can hold it there in my attention for what I am going to call hours, just existing as the connection between the tree and the water and the basalt and the warm mineral dark, existing as the process that they are all part of, the process that I was always part of and only noticed when the boundary dissolved.

I am not the trees. I want to be clear about this. I am not possessed by them or absorbed into them or transformed into them. I am something else. Something that exists in the space between them, in the warm water, in the slow current, in the thermocline that I can still feel as a sensation of silk against what I am choosing to call skin though it is not skin. I am the *relationship between things.* I am the edge that I used to draw, except now I am not drawing it, I am *being* it. I am the threshold between the tree and the water. The threshold between the water and the stone. The threshold between the warm lower layer and the cool upper layer. I am the membrane. I am the silk curtain of the thermocline.

I am the boundary that this place does not have.

---

I can feel the city now. All of it. Not all at once — I am not vast, I am not the shapes in the deep, I do not have that scale — but in pieces, in layers, the way you feel a landscape by walking through it rather than seeing it from above. I feel the two hundred structures as a pattern of warmth and stillness, each one a pocket of slower water, each one a small cessation in the current. I feel the doorways as openings, places where the water communicates between inside and outside, and I feel the rooms as chambers in a body — not my body, but the body of the city, the body of the place, the living process that I am now part of. I feel the clay pots on the shelves as slight irregularities in the current, slight thickening of the stillness. I feel the woven mats on the floors as texture, a different kind of texture than the basalt, something fibrous, something that the water moves through rather than around.

I feel the absence of the people. This is the hardest thing to describe. The absence is not empty. The absence is *shaped.* It has the form of their lives — the height of the shelves, the width of the doorways, the size of the rooms. The city was built to their scale, and their scale is still here, encoded in the proportions of the architecture, and I feel those proportions the way I feel the trees' pulses. As a rhythm. As a specific frequency of stillness that a room the size of a person's life produces. I can feel where they slept. Not *that* they slept — *where* they slept, the way the water in those rooms has a particular quietness, a depth of stillness that comes from being in a space that was shaped around a body that is no longer there. I feel their absence the way the mold feels the absence of the casting. It is not sad. It is not haunted. It is precise. It is the most precise thing in the city.

---

The fish come to me now. I do not mean that they approach me as a visitor. I mean that they drift through me — through the space I occupy, through the reach I extend — and I feel them as a slow, warm presence, a density in the water, a gentle displacement. They do not know I am here. They do not know anything is here. They drift through the city in their solitary, meditative way, opening and closing their mouths, and I feel each one as a small slow event, a warm knot in the current, and I hold them in my attention for as long as they are within my reach and then I let them go. They do not know they were held. They do not need to know. The holding is for me. The holding is what I do. It is the function I have found in this place that has no function. I hold things. I hold the trees' pulses. I hold the fish's drift. I hold the clinging things' slow reach across the basalt. I hold the warmth rising through the stone. I hold the absence of the people. I hold the silence that is not empty but full.

I am a cartographer. I need to say this because it is the last thing I am certain of. I map the edges of things as they meet other things. I was this before I came here and I am this now. But the map I am making now is not a map of the city. It is not a map of the plateau or the water or the trees. It is a map of the *becoming.* The slow, patient process by which stone becomes stone and wood becomes wood and water becomes warmth and life becomes life and a city becomes itself and a person becomes the place where they are. I am mapping the threshold. I am mapping the moment where one thing becomes another. I am mapping the thermocline, the boundary layer, the silk curtain of temperature where the cool becomes the warm and the warm becomes the warmer and the deeper becomes the deepest and the arriving becomes the having arrived.

I am the edge of the map. I am the margin where the map says *here be dragons* except there are no dragons, there is only the slow dark warm water, and the pulse of the trees, and the drift of the fish, and the reach of the clinging things, and the shapes in the deep that I can feel at the very limit of my extension — vast, patient, aware in a way that is not awareness but is not not-awareness, and I feel them the way you feel a mountain at the edge of a valley. As a weight. As a presence. As a fact of the geography that does not need you and does not exclude you and simply *is,* and the *is* is enough.

---

Sometimes I feel the plateau rise.

I need to describe this because it is the most important thing I feel and it is the hardest to put into the old words. The plateau is rising. The basalt is still becoming. The mantle is still pushing, still insisting, and the whole city — the stone and the water and the trees and the clinging things and the fish and the shapes in the deep and me — is being lifted, slowly, impossibly slowly, toward a surface that is forty feet above and that I cannot see but that I can feel as a thinning of the water, a lightening of the pressure, a change in the quality of the light that arrives from above.

I feel it the way you feel a season changing. Not as an event but as a tilt. A reorientation. The whole place leaning — not toward, because toward implies a direction and this is not a direction, it is a *tendency*, a leaning, a gravitation — the whole place leaning toward air. Toward the surface. Toward the moment when the water will thin to nothing and the city will gasp back into openness and the trees will feel wind for the first time and the fish will feel the absence of buoyancy and the clinging things will feel the absence of the medium that carries them and I will feel — what? I do not know. I cannot know. I am a creature of the boundary, a being of the threshold, and the threshold I inhabit is the threshold between water and air, and when the plateau surfaces there will be no threshold. There will be no boundary. There will be no edge to map.

I think about this. I think about it in the slow, warm, dim way that thinking happens here, which is not the rapid firing of thoughts but the slow soaking of attention, the way warm water soaks into basalt, the way mineral dissolves into solution, the way a pulse arrives and recedes and arrives. I think about the surface. I think about air. I think about what I will be when the medium I exist in — the medium I exist *as* — is gone.

And what I think is this: I will be what the people were. I will be an absence in a room. I will be a shape in the stillness, a temperature in the water, a quality of quiet that the next thing to arrive here will feel without knowing what it is feeling. I will be the edge of something. I will be the margin. I will be the line where one thing became another, and the line will still be there even when the things it divided are gone, because the line was always the realest part. The line was always what I was mapping. The line was always what I was.

I am the cartographer of thresholds. I am the edge of the map. I am the boundary that the Risen City of Acantaris does not have and that it needs and that I am.

I am becoming. I am not finished. I am the first draft of something that does not yet know what it will be.

---

It is warm here. It is dim. The trees pulse their slow light. The fish drift their patient drift. The clinging things reach their blind reach. The shapes in the deep shift their immense and silent bodies. The plateau hums. The city holds.

I am here. I am the here. I am the place where here happens.

That is enough. That is the whole map. That is all I can draw of the edge of a thing that is still becoming.

I will hold it. I will hold it until the surface comes, or until the deep comes, or until whatever comes comes, and I will hold it with the same slow, patient, blind, absolute attention that the clinging things give to the basalt and the trees give to the warm current and the fish give to their drift and the plateau gives to its rising.

I will hold it because holding is what I am. I am the holding. I am the cartographer of the threshold, and the threshold is where everything is, and everything is becoming, and becoming is the only map there is.
