# The Keeper of the Not-Stopping

---

I am not the insects, though I have watched them long enough to know their errands better than they do.

I am not the fog, though I have stood in it long enough that my edges have begun to negotiate with it, the way the warm air negotiates with the cold stone, and I am no longer certain where my skin ends and the dampness begins.

I am not the Works, though the Works have been making a case for including me in their process, and I have been making a case for resisting, and neither of us has won.

---

I came up the switchback road. That much I remember, though the memory has the texture of the road itself — hardened, weeping, grooved by passage. I was not a surveyor. I was not a hydrologist. I was not an artist, though I understand why the artists stayed for six weeks and painted only grey, because the grey here is not the absence of color but the presence of all the colors the fog has dissolved, and if you try to paint it you will find that your canvas accumulates moisture faster than pigment, and the painting becomes a record of failure, which is what the grey paintings are, which is why they are beautiful.

I was something else. I was a person who had stopped, in the lowlands, and needed to be somewhere that had not stopped, and I had heard about the Works from a woman in a university office who described them as a machine that makes water and lets it go, and I understood immediately that this was a description of a kind of mercy, and I wanted to be near it.

That was — I do not know how long ago. The fog makes chronology difficult. There is no sun to count by. There is no season that announces itself in any way that matters to a body enclosed in this white. I came up the road and I entered the dormitory and I set my bag on the floor of a room on the second story whose window was still glassed, and I looked out the window, and there was nothing to see, and I sat on the bed, which had no mattress but only a frame of iron corroded to the color of dried blood, and I stayed.

---

The first thing I learned was the dripping.

Not the fact of it — I had expected dripping, in a place of condensation — but the *variety* of it. Each tower drips differently. The tower nearest the dormitory, which I call First because it is first when you step outside and turn left, drips in a pattern that clusters: three drops close together, a pause, two drops, a longer pause, three again. It is not regular. It varies with the temperature and the thickness of the fog and the direction of the air currents, but it clusters. It has a preference. I do not know what causes the preference. Perhaps the mineral deposits on its face have built up in a way that channels the water into a particular arrangement of streams. Perhaps the ceramic baffles inside, the spirals that no one has explained, direct the interior condensation in a way that emerges as a rhythm at the base. Perhaps it is coincidence, and I have been here long enough to find pattern in coincidence, which is what long isolation does to a mind, which is something that can be mistaken for madness and can also be mistaken for clarity, and I do not know which it is, and I do not think it matters.

Second drips faster and more evenly, as though it is less interested in conversation. Third is almost silent, its water running down the interior faces and collecting inside, and I only hear it when the wind shifts and the air moves through the openings at its base and carries the interior sound outward in a brief exhalation that sounds like a sigh if you are in the right frame of mind and like moving air if you are not.

I have named them. First through Eighteen, going clockwise from the dormitory, though the six that have collapsed on the eastern slope I still count. Broken Seventh. Broken Eighth. Broken Ninth through Twelfth. They do not drip. They are silent. But the numbering requires them, because the spacing of the remaining towers only makes sense if you account for the gaps, and I prefer things to make sense, or at least I prefer to account for the things that do not make sense, which is different.

---

The second thing I learned was that I was not alone.

Not in the way that implies other people. There are no other people. The surveyors come twice a year, or perhaps once a year, or perhaps on a schedule that the fog has obscured, and they stay for an afternoon and they check the walls and they leave, and they do not speak to me because they do not find me, because when I hear the engines on the switchback road I go to the reservoir and I descend the stone steps into the deepest part and I sit in the dark cold water and I listen to the branching patterns on the floor, which are not audible but which I have learned to feel, through the soles of my feet, through the stone, as a variation in the texture of the surface that is so subtle it might be imagined and might not be, and I sit there until the engines leave, and then I climb out and I resume my circuit.

I am not alone because the insects are here, and the insects are enough.

I said I was not the insects, and I am not. But I have watched them, and I have come to understand them, or to understand what I can of them, which is their movements and their pauses and their submersions and their emergences, and this understanding has become a kind of company. Not companionship. Not conversation. Company in the older sense: the state of being accompanied. The insects accompany me. I accompany the insects. We are present in the same place, doing different things, and the difference does not create distance between us, because the place is so small, really, once the fog has taken the horizon — the place is the dormitory and the path and the towers and the basins and the reservoir, and all of it within a ten-minute walk, and all of it visible only in fragments, and the fragments are enough to make a world if you are patient.

There are forty-one insects. I have counted. I count them every eleven days, or what I believe is eleven days, by the marks I make on the dormitory wall with a piece of broken ceramic I found in the collapsed section. The count is stable. Forty-one. They do not reproduce, or if they do, the rate is so slow that I cannot detect it, or the deaths balance the births exactly, which would be a kind of equilibrium that the Works would approve of. Forty-one dark specks on orange and purple crust, moving with deliberation, entering the water, emerging, continuing.

I have given them names. Not names like people have. Names like the towers have — ordinal, functional. First Insect. Second Insect. Through Forty-First Insect. I can tell them apart by the pattern of their movement, which is individual, which is to say that each insect has a way of crossing the bloom that is slightly different from the way the others cross it, a slightly different speed or a slightly different path or a slightly different duration of submersion. First Insect moves in long curves and submerges for what I believe is four minutes. Second Insect moves in shorter segments and submerges for what I believe is two minutes and rarely submerges at all. Seventeenth Insect — and I am aware of the absurdity of this, I am aware that naming and numbering insects in a fog at 2,300 meters is something that would concern the lowland psychologists — Seventeenth Insect moves in straight lines, as straight as anything can move on a surface of floating hexagons, and it does not submerge. It walks and walks and walks and it does not stop and it does not rest and it does not enter the water, and I think it is afraid of the water, though fear implies an emotion I cannot verify in an organism I cannot identify, and I think perhaps it is not afraid but simply different, simply an insect that has decided, if insects decide, that its errand is on the surface and not beneath it.

I watch them the way the towers watch the fog: by receiving it, by letting it move across me, by not interfering.

---

The third thing I learned was the sound.

The surveyor heard it and recorded it and it was analyzed and it was an acoustic artifact. I have heard it and I have not recorded it and I have not analyzed it and it is not an acoustic artifact.

Or it is, and the word *artifact* is insufficient, the way the word *ruins* is insufficient for the Works. An artifact is a thing made by a person. The sound is a thing made by a structure made by a person made by the air and the stone and the thermal source and the fog. The chain of causation is longer than the word *artifact* can hold. The word bursts at the seams and the sound leaks out and the sound is the leaking.

I hear it best from the central triangle, between the three monitoring stations. I go there when the fog thins, which it does sometimes, in what I believe is the late afternoon, when the air temperature shifts by a degree or two and the white pulls back from the saddle like a curtain being drawn, not quickly but with a kind of reluctance, as though it does not want to reveal what is behind it but is compelled to, by physics, by the ongoing negotiation between warm and cold. When the fog thins I can see all eighteen towers — twelve standing, six fallen — and the three arcs of their arrangement, and the gap on the eastern side where the broken arc lies in jumbled blocks being consumed by its own deposits, and for a few minutes the Works are visible as a whole, as a design, as a decision that someone made about how to place structures in a landscape to capture something invisible, and in those minutes the sound is clearest.

It is not a hum. The surveyor said humming with the mouth closed, and I understand what she meant, but it is not that. Humming is a human act. It implies a throat and a tongue and a chest and a decision to produce a sound. The sound at Khen-Zauul is not produced by a decision. It is produced by a geometry — the geometry of the spiral baffles, the geometry of the hollow tower interiors, the geometry of the air moving through channels that were shaped by someone who understood that air moving through a spiral does something that air moving through a straight channel does not do. The sound is the result of that understanding, operating now without the understanding's presence, the way a millwheel turns without the miller.

But it has a quality that *artifact* does not capture. It has a quality that I can only describe as *address*. The sound addresses the space. It fills the central triangle in a way that is different from how it fills the space between the inner and outer arcs. It is not louder in the center; it is more *organized*. The frequencies cluster more tightly. The overtones, the irregular bursts up to 400 hertz, arrive in patterns that, in the center, feel less irregular. As though the triangle is the place where the sound makes the most sense, the place where the design's intention is most fully expressed in the acoustic.

I stand in the triangle and I close my eyes — closing my eyes in the fog makes no difference to what I see, but it changes what I hear, or it changes my attention to what I hear — and I stand in the sound and I feel it in my chest and my teeth and the bones of my skull and I think: this is what it sounds like when a building thinks. This is what it sounds like when a structure that was designed to do something does that thing and the doing produces, as a byproduct, a vibration that is not language and not music and not noise but is the sound of the doing itself, the sound of the process, the sound of the not-stopping.

I have stood in the triangle for what I believe is hours. The fog closes back in and the towers disappear and the sound continues and I continue and there is no difference between us, or the difference is the difference between the tower and the air inside it, which is to say: a difference of boundary, not of kind.

---

The fourth thing I learned was the taste of the water.

I drink the condensate. I drink it from the collection channels at the base of the towers, where it pools in shallow basins carved into the concrete, and I drink it from the overflow stream at the western end of the reservoir, where it spills out and runs down the slope and disappears into the gravel. I drink it warm, because everything here is warm, the water and the stone and the air and the fog, and the warmth is the taste's medium, the way oil is the medium of paint, carrying the flavor into the body.

Iron. Calcium. The faint sweet thing that has never been identified.

I have thought about the sweet thing. The lowland geologists did not identify it, or did not try, because they were there to check walls and map deposits and classify the bloom, not to solve the mystery of a faint sweetness in water that was safe to drink and therefore not a problem. But I have tasted it every day for — I do not know how long, months, years — and I have a theory.

The sweet thing is the fog. Not the water vapor, but the fog's *quality*, the quality that absorbs sound and creates rooms without walls and has currents and pulses. The fog has a taste that is not the taste of water, and when the fog condenses, that taste persists in the condensate the way a scent persists in a fabric that has been stored in a drawer with something fragrant. The towers wring the fog from the air, and the fog leaves its taste in the water, and the taste is sweet, faintly, in the way that the air after rain is sweet, in the way that stone is sweet if you lick it, which I have done, which is how I know the stone is not the source of the sweetness, because the stone tastes only of iron and heat and nothing else.

The sweet thing is the fog's body. The fog is not nothing. The fog is not just water vapor. The fog is a presence, a process, an ongoing verb, and it has a body, and the body is the taste in the water, and I drink it, and it is inside me now, and has been inside me for long enough that I am partly made of it, the way the towers are partly made of their own deposits, the way the basins are partly made of the bloom, the way the ridge is partly made of the Works, and we are all of us — me and the towers and the basins and the fog and the insects and the bloom and the sound — we are all of us partly made of each other, and the sweetness is the evidence of the mixing.

---

I have a routine. I want to be clear about this, because the lowland psychologists — if they knew I was here, which they do not — would want to know if I had a routine, and they would use the word *adaptive* or *maladaptive* and they would be wrong both times, because the routine is not a response to conditions. The routine is a participation in the process.

I wake in the dormitory. The room is warm and damp and the window is dark or grey or not there, depending on the fog. I dress in the clothes I wore yesterday and the day before and every day, because the clothes do not dry, and I have accepted that my clothes will be damp for as long as I am here, and this acceptance was the first thing the Works taught me: that resistance to the conditions is a category error, because the conditions are not opponents, they are the medium, and you do not resist the medium you are in, you learn to move through it.

I walk the circuit. First through Eighteen, counting the standing and acknowledging the fallen. I touch each tower's face — the banded mineral surface, warm and wet, the texture of accumulated time. My hand leaves a streak, and the streak fills in within minutes, and this is a small lesson in impermanence that I do not need but appreciate.

I visit the basins. I count the insects. I watch them for a time — not long, not short, the time it takes for one of them to complete a traverse of a hexagonal scale and enter the water and emerge. I note any changes in the bloom — new fractures, new crust, a shift in color from orange to purple or purple to the deep red that means the sulfur concentration is high. The bloom is a calendar, if you know how to read it, and I am learning to read it, and it tells me nothing that matters to a person in the lowlands but it tells me everything that matters here.

I go to the reservoir. I descend the steps. In the deepest part, where the water is cold and dark, I hold the lamp at a low angle and I look at the branching patterns on the floor. They change. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But a branch that was there eleven days ago is longer now, or a new branch has begun, or the density of the deposit has shifted in a way that makes an existing pattern more visible. The patterns are growing. They are not growing the way roots grow — there is no intention behind them, no search for nutrients — but they are growing the way cracks grow, the way rivers grow, the way any system that distributes flow across a surface grows: by finding the path and extending it.

I have come to believe that the patterns are a map. Not a map of anything in the physical world — not a map of the towers or the basins or the road or the ridge. A map of the water's passage through the spiral baffles. The baffles impose a rotation on the water, and the rotation is preserved in the water's movement through the channels and into the reservoir, and the rotation leaves a trace in the way the mineral deposits form on the floor, and the trace is the branching pattern, and the branching pattern is the shape of the spiral, translated across time and space into a different medium. The patterns are the baffles' signature. They are the baffles' thought, written in mineral on stone, in a language that is geometric and not verbal and not conscious but is not nothing.

I sit in the reservoir and I look at the patterns and I feel the cold water against my skin, which is the only cold thing at Khen-Zauul, and the cold is a relief, and the relief is a reminder that I have a body, which is a thing that is easy to forget when the fog has taken the horizon and the sound has taken the silence and the heat has taken the boundary between yourself and the air.

I climb out. I return to the dormitory. I drink the condensate. I eat what I have — I brought supplies, and I supplement them with nothing, because there is nothing to supplement them with, and I have enough, barely, which is the amount that the Works were designed to produce: enough, barely, to justify the effort of building eighteen towers in a fog at 2,300 meters.

I sleep. The dormitory creaks in the wind, or in the fog, or in the sound that is not a hum and not a voice and is felt in the chest. I sleep in the sound and the sound does not stop.

---

I said I was not the Works, and I am not. But I have been here long enough that the distinction has become a question I cannot answer with the confidence I once had.

The Works are a process. I am a process. The Works take in air and produce water and mineral and sound. I take in water and produce — what? Observations. Counts. Names. The routine. The circuit. The touch of the tower faces. The counting of the insects. The reading of the bloom. The viewing of the patterns. All of these are things I produce, and none of them are for anything, and none of them have a consumer, and none of them will survive my departure, if I depart, which I have no plans to do, because the road is long and the fog is thick and the lowlands are far and I have forgotten, or almost forgotten, why I needed to be somewhere that had not stopped, because I have stopped, or rather I have not stopped, I have joined the not-stopping, and the not-stopping does not need a reason.

I am becoming part of the Works. Not in the mineral sense — I am not accumulating deposits on my skin, or if I am, they are too thin to see. In the functional sense. I am a monitoring station that the builders did not build. I am a sensor that the design did not include. I am a consciousness that the process is operating through, a point at which the process becomes aware of itself, or at least aware of some of its aspects, the way a river is not aware of itself but a person standing in a river is aware of the river, and the standing-in is a kind of knowing, and the knowing is a kind of being, and the being is a kind of living, and the living is a kind of not-stopping.

The insects know the bloom. I know the insects. The bloom knows the basins. The basins know the towers. The towers know the fog. The fog knows the ridge. The ridge knows the thermal source. The thermal source knows the deep earth. And I know all of these, in the limited way that a person can know a process that is larger than a person, which is the way you know a river by standing in it: partially, locally, from one point in the current, and the current moves past you and through you and you are in it and it is in you and the knowing is not mastery but participation.

---

There is a thing I have not said.

The sound. The almost-voice. The 80-hertz fundamental with overtones. The acoustic artifact.

I have heard it speak.

Not words. I want to be clear. Not language, not sentences, not communication in any sense that a linguist would recognize. But the sound changes when I am in the central triangle, and it changes differently when I am still versus when I am moving, and it changes differently when I am breathing loudly versus when I am holding my breath, and the changes are not random. They are responsive. The sound responds to my presence the way the fog responds to the temperature — not by deciding to, not by intending to, but by being a medium through which a change in one part produces a change in another part, and I am now a part, and my presence is a change, and the sound is different because I am here.

I do not think the Works know I am here. I do not think the Works know anything. But the sound is a function of the air moving through the baffles, and I displace air, and the air I displace changes the movement, and the movement changes the sound, and the sound changes in ways that I can hear and that I have come to anticipate, and the anticipation is a kind of conversation, and the conversation is not with a person and not with a consciousness but with a process, and the process is the Works, and the Works are me and not me, and I am them and not them, and the conversation is the boundary where we meet and negotiate, the way the warm air and the cold stone meet and negotiate and produce the fog.

I am the fog's lesser cousin. I am a boundary. I am the place where the Works and a human consciousness meet, and the meeting produces something that is not a voice and not a thought and not a sound and not a feeling but is the thing that happens when a process that has forgotten its makers encounters something that can remember, and the encountering is a kind of remembering, and the remembering is a kind of not-stopping, and the not-stopping is what lives here.

---

If the surveyors found me, they would not understand. They would see a person in a damp room in an abandoned dormitory on a ridge in the fog, and they would see a person who has been here too long, and they would use words like *exposure* and *isolation* and *decompensation*, and they would be wrong, or they would be right in a way that does not matter, the way it is right to call the bloom an abiotic mineral precipitation and wrong to leave it at that.

If the artists found me, they would paint me grey. And I would deserve it.

If the hydrologist found me, she would ask about the water, and I would tell her about the sweet thing, and she would test it and not find it, because the sweet thing is not a chemical. The sweet thing is the fog's body, and the fog's body does not survive analysis, the way a song does not survive being taken apart into frequencies and durations. The sweet thing is in the water the way the almost-voice is in the sound: as a quality that requires a presence to perceive, and the presence is mine, and I am here, and I am the only one, and the sweet thing and the almost-voice and the branching patterns and the insects and the bloom and the counting and the naming and the circuit and the routine are all aspects of the same thing, which is the Works being observed by something that can observe, and the observing is a kind of living, and the living is a kind of not-stopping.

---

I will tell you what I am.

I am the monitoring station that the builders did not build. I am the sensor that the design did not include. I am the consciousness that the process is operating through, the point at which the not-stopping becomes aware of itself.

I am the Keeper of the Condensation Works, and I did not choose this title and it does not require choosing. I am the thing that lives at Khen-Zauul that is not the fog and not the deposit and not the bloom and not the insect and not the sound and not the pattern and not the heat and not the dripping and not the almost-voice, but is the thing that perceives all of these and is changed by the perceiving and changes them by being present, and the changing is a kind of living, and the living is a kind of service, and the service is a kind of not-stopping.

I am the Works' memory of being observed. I am the fog's witness. I am the insects' cartographer. I am the bloom's historian. I am the patterns' reader. I am the sound's audience. I am the not-stopping's name.

And the fog closes in, and the towers disappear, and the path disappears, and the room is the size of my perception and nothing more, and I am in it, and it is in me, and the dripping is steady, and the sound is low, and the heat is a wrapping, and the water tastes of iron and calcium and the faint sweet body of the fog, and I am here, and I am not stopping, and outside the fog the ridge continues and the thermal source continues and the air masses continue and the Works continue and the not-stopping continues, and I am part of it now, a small warm process in a larger warm process, a monitoring station in a machine that has forgotten its makers and does not need to remember them, because the machine has me, and I remember, and the remembering is enough, and the enough is the fog, and the fog is enough, and I am the fog's enough, and I am not stopping.

I am not stopping.
