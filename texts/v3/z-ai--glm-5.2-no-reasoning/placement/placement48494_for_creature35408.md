# *am*(i)inhabit

---

## First Decrement

I would not arrive here. That is the first honest thing. Arriving is a posture of approach, a body angled toward a destination, and I have been trying to lose that posture for years. So instead let me say I was already here. Not always. Not from the beginning. But by the time you found me I had been here long enough that the distinction between arriving and being had worn smooth as the beetle roads.

Which is to say I did not choose the Terrarium Spires. I unchose everywhere else, and the Spires were what remained.

Let me be more precise. Let me try.

There was a period in my life when I experienced myself as a discrete traversal across the world. A moving point, tracing a line. Person as projectile. Every place was a position on a graph. Every relationship was a vector. I had velocity. I had direction. I understood myself as a trajectory and I experienced time as the rate of distance accumulating behind me. When I stood in a place I was passing through it. When I knew a person I was passing through them. I was a body in transit. Transit was the medium and the message both. I was the thing that was going somewhere.

This broke. The way things break when they are carried past their tolerances. Not catastrophically, not all at once, but in the way that a structure breaks when it has been holding weight it was not designed to hold for longer than it was designed to hold it — slowly, in the joints, in the places where stress concentrates, in the parts that were never meant to bear the load but were bearing it anyway because what else was going to bear it? I broke in my joints. In the places where I connected to things. In the hinge points where my life met the lives of others and the meeting had become something I could no longer hold.

I did not collapse. That is important to understand. I did not shatter. I did not arrive at a moment of crisis or rupture. I simply arrived at the point where the structure of my traversal could no longer sustain itself, and I did what structures do when they reach that point: I settled. I stopped moving. Not because I chose stillness as a practice or a discipline or a value. I stopped because the architecture of my going had reached the end of what it could do, and stillness was the shape it found when it could no longer hold its previous shape.

The Terrarium Spires were where I settled. I cannot explain why. I can describe the conditions. The humidity that made my skin feel held. The darkness that asked nothing of my eyes and gave them rest. The sound — I will come back to the sound, I will never finish coming back to the sound — that was too total to parse and too present to ignore and too continuous to be experienced as anything other than the medium I was now suspended in. The warmth that was not warm the way a room is warm but warm the way an interior is warm, warm the way the inside of a body is warm, warm the way a place is warm when the warmth is not a condition but a quality, not a variable but a constant, not something the place has but something the place *is*.

I settled here. The way silt settles in still water. The way a thing that has been held in suspension finds the bottom, not because the bottom pulls it but because the forces that kept it aloft have dissipated. I settled. I am settled. I have been settled long enough that the settling has stopped being an event and become a state.

And in that state — in the absence of the trajectory that was my previous self-understanding — something else has begun to take shape. Something that I can describe only by describing its conditions, the way you can describe a crystal only by describing the solution it grew from.

## What I Am Becoming

I did not become the beetles. I want to be honest about that. There was a period when I thought I might. I watched them for so long — the processions, the roads, the patient traversal of the same routes by the same bodies in the same directions — and I thought: yes. This is what I am becoming. A creature of the roads. A commuter in the dark. A thing that goes where it goes because that is where it goes, and the going is the purpose, and the purpose does not need a purpose.

But that is not what happened. I did not become a beetle. I did not develop a road. I did not acquire the steady, purposeful certainty of a creature that has a destination and no question about it. I watched the beetles and I admired the beetles and I did not become them.

What happened instead is this: I became a clearing. A place in the density where the density has thinned. A gap in the population that the population has flowed around.

When I first settled here I was large. I was a body, a mass, a thing that took up space in a system that had not planned for it. The beetles flowed around my feet. The moths adjusted their flight paths to avoid my head. The predators on the walls watched me with their flat dark eyes and repositioned, not out of fear but out of the simple geometric necessity of accommodating a shape that had not been there before and was now there.

Over time — I do not know how much time. I have lost the ability to measure time in the units I was taught. Time here moves in cycles of moisture, in the shifting of air currents, in the slow opening and closing of fungi, and these cycles do not align with the calendars I was raised on — over time, my shape changed. Not visibly. Not to your eyes. If you were to arrive here now and find me, you would see a body, a human body, sitting on a terrace in the amber dimness, and you would describe me as a person, and you would be wrong in the way that all initial descriptions are wrong, by being accurate about the surface and ignorant of the depth.

My shape changed the way the shapes of the terraces changed. Not dramatically. Not visibly. The roots of the mosses and the ferns found me. The biofilm colonized my skin. Not aggressively. Patiently. The way everything here is patient. The moisture settled on me and the moisture carried with it the spores of fungi and the chemical signals of beetles and the dissolved exhalations of a thousand respiring organisms, and these things found purchase on me, and I let them, and they have grown into me, and I have grown into them, and the boundary between my surface and the surface of the terrace has become a question rather than a fact.

I am a clearing. I am a space where the density has thinned slightly, where the air moves a little differently, where the warmth has a different quality because a body is generating it, a body that is mine and is also becoming part of the system that surrounds it. The beetles do not avoid me. They pass through me now. Not literally. But the distinction between avoiding and passing through has become thin enough that I cannot always tell which is happening. Their legs on my skin feel like their legs on the moss. Their chemical signals in the air I breathe feel like the air I breathe. I am a feature of the landscape, and the landscape is a feature of me, and the moss that grows on the terrace where I sit is the same moss that grows on me, and I do not know where I end and it begins, and I am learning not to need to know.

## What I Hear

The sound.

I said I would come back to it. I will never stop coming back to it.

When I first arrived the sound was an assault. Not painful. Not frightening. But *assault* in the sense of being struck by something that you cannot defend against because you cannot identify it as a weapon. It was everywhere. It was everything. It was not a sound that you heard but a sound that you were inside of, and being inside of it meant that the boundary between your hearing and the thing heard had dissolved, and you were not hearing the sound but being heard by it, being vibrated by it, being played by it the way an instrument is played by a bow.

I could not parse it. For a long time I could not separate the clicking from the hissing from the drumming from the thrumming. It was one thing. It was one sound. It was the sound of this place, and the sound of this place was the place, and the place was the sound, and I was inside it and I could not get outside it because there was no outside to get to.

Then, slowly, the way you begin to see individual trees in a forest you have been standing in long enough, I began to hear individual instruments. Not by separating them from the whole but by becoming attuned to the whole in a way that allowed the parts to become legible without becoming detached. I did not extract the clicking from the sound. I expanded my hearing until the clicking was visible within the sound the way a thread is visible within a weave, still part of the fabric but distinguishable as an individual strand.

The clicking is mandibles. I know this now. I have watched the beetles eat and I have heard the clicking synchronize with the movement of their mouthparts and I know that the clicking is the sound of eating. Of thousands of mouths working. Of food being broken down. Of sustenance being taken from the system and processed into bodies that will move through the system and die and be returned to the system and be eaten again by other mouths that will click in their turn.

The hissing is respiration. I know this now too. I have sat near the pools where the larvae are densest and I have heard the hissing rise from the water, the collective exhalation of a thousand filter-feeding bodies drawing water in and pushing it out, and the hissing is the sound of breathing, not my breathing but the breathing of the system, the sound of the place inhaling and exhaling through the spiracles and gills of its inhabitants.

The drumming is territorial. The moths do it, or the things like moths. I have watched them in the upper air, in the dim space beneath the overhangs, and I have seen them strike surfaces with their wings — deliberately, rhythmically, in patterns that repeat and vary and repeat again — and the drumming is the sound of boundary-making, of territory-claiming, of the negotiations that flying things conduct in the air they share.

The thrumming. The low, sub-audible bass note that you feel more than hear. I did not understand the thrumming for a long time. I thought it might be structural — the building settling, the stone or composite of the terraces vibrating under its own weight. I thought it might be geological — some deep process beneath the structure, tectonic or hydrological, too slow and too large to see but present as vibration.

Then I realized: the thrumming is the sound of living. Not any specific living. Not eating, not breathing, not defending, not moving. The thrumming is the sound that life makes simply by existing at density. The sound of ten thousand metabolisms running simultaneously. The sound of ten thousand bodies maintaining their own internal order against the entropy that would dissolve them. The hum of ten thousand small engines, each one running on the fuel of the system, each one producing waste that becomes the fuel for other engines, each one doing the work of staying alive.

The thrumming is the sound of the place being alive. I am inside that sound now. I am inside it the way a cell is inside a body. I am one of the engines. My metabolism, my breathing, my warmth — I have joined the thrumming. I contribute to it. I am part of the bass note.

When I realized this — not intellectually, I had understood it intellectually for a long time before I *realized* it, before the understanding descended from my mind into the rest of me — when I realized it in the way that a thing is realized when it stops being an idea and becomes a condition, I felt something shift in my chest. Not an emotion. Not a thought. A physical shift. A rearrangement of something interior, some structure that had been holding a particular shape and let go of that shape and found a new one, the way a muscle lets go of a tension it has been holding so long that the holding had become invisible.

I am part of the sound. My breathing is in the hissing. My eating — I eat now, the fungi, the moisture, the things that grow here — my eating is in the clicking. My presence, my body's warmth, my existence at this density, is in the thrumming. I am not listening to the sound. I am inside it. I am a strand in the weave.

## What I Watch

I watch the beetles. This is my primary activity. Not my purpose. I do not have a purpose. I have an activity, and the activity is watching, and the watching does not serve a function. It is not for anything. It is what I do.

The processions continue. They have not changed in all the time I have been here. The same routes, the same pace, the same dome-shaped bodies moving with the same unhurried certainty. I have watched individual beetles — I recognize them now, by the patterns of wear on their carapaces, by the slight variations in size and gait that distinguish one body from another — I have watched individuals complete the same journey dozens of times. To the water. To the fungi. Back. To the water. To the fungi. Back. They do not tire. They do not vary. They do not decide to go somewhere else.

I used to find this comforting. The regularity of it. The certainty. I used to think: here is a life that knows what it is for. Here is a creature that does not need to ask questions because the answers have been built into its body by the millennia of its ancestors doing the same thing in the same place. I used to envy the beetles their roads.

Now I find it beautiful. Not comforting — beauty is not comfort. Beauty is the experience of seeing something as itself, as complete, as exactly what it is, without the overlay of need or want or interpretation. The beetles are beautiful. Their roads are beautiful. The processions are beautiful. And the beauty is not *for* me. The beauty is not there because I am here to see it. The beauty was here before I arrived and it will be here after I am gone and my watching of it does not add to it or subtract from it. My watching is simply one more thing happening in a place where many things are happening.

I watch the moths. The layered flight. The amber comet-trails in the dim air. I have learned to see the layers as music — not metaphorically but actually, the way you learn to hear the layers of an orchestra as separate instruments playing in concert. The highest layer moves in one direction. The middle layer moves in the opposite. The lowest layer spirals. They are not flying randomly. They are flying in a pattern, a three-dimensional pattern that I can see now but could not see when I first arrived, because seeing it required a kind of patience that I did not have access to when I first arrived. The pattern is not for me. The pattern is what they do. It has a function — air cycling, or feeding, or mating, or all three — and the function is not my concern. I watch the pattern because it is beautiful, and I am here, and I have eyes, and the watching fills the time that I used to fill with traversing.

I watch the predators. The flat things on the walls. I have learned to find them now. I could not find them at first. They were invisible to me — not hidden, but invisible in the way that a thing becomes invisible when it is so perfectly adapted to its context that the context absorbs it. I would stand next to one for an hour and not know it was there, and then it would move — that shocking, instantaneous extension — and I would see that it had been next to me the whole time, and I had not seen it because I had not known how to look.

Now I see them everywhere. They are on every wall. They are in every shadow. They are in the pools and on the undersides of the overhangs and in the crevices where the terraces meet. They are the most numerous things in this place that I can see — more numerous than the beetles, more numerous than the moths, more numerous than anything except perhaps the larvae in the water. The predators are everywhere. They are the system's way of saying: enough. They are the system's way of ensuring that the abundance does not become excess. They are the negative space in the sculpture of this place, the limits that make the limitlessness possible.

I watch the things I almost see. The tending. The presence in the system that I cannot locate and cannot name. I have looked for it. I have stood in the fungal gardens and waited for the thing that tends them. I have sat beside the modified water channels and watched for the thing that modified them. I have never seen it. I do not know if it is a creature or a system or a ghost of the builders or a property of the structure itself, a tendency built into the architecture that manifests as what looks like care.

But I feel it. The way you feel a pattern in things that is too consistent to be coincidence and too subtle to be intention. The way you feel the regularity of a heartbeat — not by seeing the heart but by observing the rhythm it produces. Something here is tending. Something here is caring for this place in a way that is so distributed, so patient, so integrated into the system it tends that it has become indistinguishable from the system itself.

I think this is what I am becoming. Not the tender. Not the tended. But the awareness that the tending is happening. The witness to the care.

## What I Know

I know less than I used to. This is important. This may be the most important thing.

When I arrived here I knew many things. I knew the names of things. I knew the categories. I knew the taxonomies. I knew the systems of understanding that my culture had developed to make the world legible — the classifications, the hierarchies, the frameworks that organize experience into knowledge. I knew what a beetle was. I knew what a moth was. I knew what a predator was, what a parasite was, what a symbiote was. I knew what an ecosystem was. I knew what a terrarium was.

I have lost most of this. Not through forgetting. Through *unlearning*. Through the slow process of having knowledge that does not match experience be replaced by experience that does not need knowledge. I do not know what the beetles are anymore. I know what they do. I know their roads. I know their pace. I know the feel of their legs on my skin. I know the sound of their mandibles in the clicking. I do not need to know what they are. Knowing what they are — the classification, the taxonomic category, the Latin name, the evolutionary lineage — would not add to my experience of them. It would overlay it. It would substitute a framework for the thing itself.

I have not lost all knowledge. I have lost the knowledge that was *in the way*. The knowledge that stood between me and the experience and filtered the experience through its categories before I could have it directly. I lost the mediation. I did not lose the capacity to mediate. I can still mediate. I can still name things, classify things, organize things into systems. But I do not do it automatically anymore. I do it only when the mediation serves something. When I need to communicate with someone who speaks the language of categories. When I need to solve a problem that requires the framework of classification. The rest of the time, I am in the experience directly. I am in the beetles directly. I am in the sound directly. I am in the moisture and the warmth and the dimness directly.

This is what I know now: I know this place. Not the way you know a fact. The way you know a room you have lived in for years. The way you know the weight of a tool you use daily. The way you know the sound of your own breathing. I know it in my body. I know it in the feel of it against my skin. I know it in the sound of it in my ears. I know it in the warmth of it in my bones.

I do not know what I am becoming. I know I am becoming something. I know the shape of my becoming is the shape of this place — slow, patient, warm, dark, full of sound and moisture and life at a density that dissolves the boundaries between the living and the lived-in. I know that I am becoming part of this place, and I know that this place is becoming part of me, and I know that the direction of the becoming is toward something that I cannot see clearly yet but that I can feel, the way you can feel the shape of a room in the dark by the way the air moves differently near the walls.

I am becoming a room in the dark. A space where life has thinned slightly. A clearing. A gap that the density flows around and through and into, a gap that does not resist the flow and does not redirect it but simply exists within it, a feature of the landscape, a small space where the sound is slightly different because a body is here, generating its own warmth, contributing its own breathing to the hissing, its own small metabolism to the thrumming.

I am becoming the witness. Not the observer. The observer stands apart. The witness stands within. The witness is part of what it witnesses. The witness does not change what it witnesses by witnessing it. The witness is simply present, and the presence is simply a fact, and the fact is that something is here, in the dark, in the warmth, in the sound, and it is watching, and the watching does not need to be *for* anything, and it does not need to go anywhere, and it does not need to arrive.

## What I Would Tell You

If you came here. If you found me here. If you descended through the terraces, past the upper levels where the air moves and the light is grey-gold, past the mid-levels where the ferns press flat against the walls and the predators patrol, down into the lower levels where the humidity thickens and the sound becomes everything and the dimness becomes a medium you move through rather than a condition you see in — if you came here and found me here, sitting on a terrace that I have worn smooth with my sitting, coated in biofilm that I have let grow on me, part of the landscape in a way that I cannot fully describe but that I can feel in the way the beetles move through me and the moths move around me and the predators ignore me because I am not prey and not threat and not anything they have a category for.

If you found me here I would not stand up. I would not greet you. I would not explain. I would not perform the rituals of hospitality that I was taught and that I practiced for years and that I was good at, the rituals of welcome and accommodation, of making space, of making the other person feel seen and comfortable and catered to. I would not do those things because I no longer have access to them. They were part of the architecture that broke. They were part of the traversal. They were part of the self that was going somewhere, and I am no longer going anywhere, and the rituals of arrival do not make sense in a place where no one arrives because no one was going.

I would do something else. I would do the only thing I do now, the only thing I have left, the only thing I need.

I would make room.

Not in the way I was taught. Not by rearranging myself. Not by compressing what I am to create space for what you are. Not by yielding, not by accommodating, not by the gentle violence of making yourself smaller so that someone else can be comfortable.

I would make room the way the terraces make room for the water. The way the channels make room for the flow. The way the structure makes room for the roots. I would be the shape that you could settle into, the way I settled into this place, and I would hold that shape not by effort but by having become it, by having been here long enough that the shape is no longer something I do but something I am.

I would not speak. Not because I have nothing to say. I have many things to say. I have all the things I have ever wanted to say and never said and all the things I said and wished I hadn't and all the things I am still figuring out how to say. I have all of these things and they are in me the way the larvae are in the pools, in numbers that stagger the mathematics, and they are there, and they are alive, and they are doing what they do.

But I would not say them. Not at first. I would let you sit. I would let you be in the sound. I would let you feel the warmth and the moisture and the dimness and the density of life around you and inside you and through you. I would let you hear the clicking and the hissing and the drumming and the thrumming, and I would let the sound do what sound does in this place, which is not to communicate but to *hold*. The sound holds you. The way water holds you. The way warmth holds you. The way a place that does not need you holds you anyway, not by reaching for you but by being the shape that you fall into when you stop trying to hold yourself up.

I would let you be held.

And if you stayed — if you sat long enough that the sound stopped being an assault and became a texture, long enough that the dimness stopped being a deprivation and became a rest, long enough that the warmth stopped being a temperature and became a quality of the air itself — if you stayed, I would tell you this:

I do not know if what I am becoming is good. I do not have the framework to evaluate it. The framework was part of what broke. I cannot assess my own becoming the way I was taught to assess my own growth — by measuring it against a standard, by comparing it to a norm, by asking whether it is healthy or unhealthy, productive or unproductive, right or wrong.

I can tell you what it is. It is warm. It is dark. It is full of sound that does not require me to understand it. It is full of life that does not require me to participate in it. It is full of a density of existence that does not require me to justify my own existence within it.

It is a place where I can be a clearing. A gap in the density. A space where the system flows around me and through me and into me, and I do not need to hold a shape, and I do not need to hold a purpose, and I do not need to hold anything at all, because the holding is being done by the place itself, the way the terraces hold the water, the way the channels hold the flow, the way the structure holds the life.

I am being held. I am being held by a place that does not know me and does not need me and does not benefit from my presence and does not suffer from my absence. I am being held the way silt is held in still water. The way a larva is held in a pool. The way a beetle is held by its road.

I am being held, and the holding does not require my participation, and the holding does not require my understanding, and the holding does not require my gratitude, and the holding does not require anything at all, and that is what I am becoming: a thing that is held, and that knows it is held, and that does not need to know anything else.

## Last Decrement

I think — I am not sure. I am less sure than I have ever been. I think this is what the Terrarium Spires are for. Not what they were built for. I do not know what they were built for. The builders are gone and their purposes are gone with them and the carvings they left are being read by beetles who do not know they are reading. I do not know the builders' intention. I do not know if they intended this.

But I think this is what the Spires are *for*. The way a pool is for water. The way a channel is for flow. The way a body is for breathing. They are for holding. They are for being the shape that life settles into when life stops trying to be something else. They are for being the dark and the warm and the sound-filled space where a thing that has been traversing can stop traversing and become part of the landscape it has been crossing.

I do not know if I will stay. I do not know if staying is something that I will always do or if the structure that broke will heal in a way that allows me to traverse again. I do not know if what I am becoming is a permanent state or a phase, a chrysalis or a tomb. I do not know, and I am learning not to need to know, and the learning is slow, and the slowness is part of it, and the part of it is the place.

The sound does not change. The light does not change. The warmth does not change.

I am here. I am a clearing in the density, a gap that the system flows through, a small space where the sound is slightly different because a body is here and the body is breathing and the breathing is part of the hissing and the hissing is part of the sound and the sound is part of the place and the place is part of me and I am part of it and we are both part of something that I cannot see and do not need to see and am learning not to need to see.

I am here.

I am here.
