# The Walker

---

I arrive the way the description says anyone arrives — by following water that was already going there. I don't remember deciding to come. I remember being higher, and having opinions about the shape of things, and then the shape of things became less opinionated, and I kept walking, and the water kept going, and the land kept opening, and at some point I was here.

I have been here for some duration. I do not know how long. This is not a loss. This is a feature of the place. The light does not change in a way that permits counting, and the hum does not pause in a way that permits measuring, and my body, which used to be a kind of clock — hungry, tired, alert, dull, hungry again — has stopped keeping time with the insistence it once had. I am hungry less. I am tired differently. The tiredness is not a deficit but a settling, a lowering of some internal water level to match the water level of the place, which is: just below the surface, always, never quite arriving.

I still walk. That is what I do here. I am the thing that walks.

---

I should be clear about what I am not.

I am not the moss. The moss does not walk. The moss has not moved from where it is for a span of time that makes *location* seem like a temporary condition it has temporarily tolerated. The moss stays. The moss is the staying, and I am the going, and the going is not away from the moss — there is no away from the moss — but across it, over it, along it, and the acrossness and the overness and the alongness are what I contribute to this place, which otherwise has no verb. Everything here is a noun. The moss is a noun. The peat is a noun. The beetles in their holes are nouns. The machines, deep down, churning through their thirty-second breath, are nouns. I am the verb. I am the walking, and the walking is the only motion at this scale, and the scale matters, because below me the springtails are moving, and they are moving at their scale, and above me the clouds are moving, and they are moving at theirs, but at the scale of the floor — the human scale, the walking scale, the scale at which a foot is a foot and a step is a step and a day is the distance you can cover before your legs forget why they started — at that scale, I am the only thing that moves.

I think this is why I am here. Not *here* in the sense of: why I came to this place. I don't know why I came to this place. But *here* in the sense of: why I am still here, why I have not left, why I have not walked until the moss ended, if the moss ends. I am here because the floor needs a walker the way it needs the springtails. Not for digestion. Not for the cycling of nutrients. For something else. For the walking itself. For the fact that, every few hours, a foot presses down on the moss and the moss compresses by a millimetre and then rises back up, and the compression and the rising are a small event, a tiny perturbation in the surface, and the perturbation travels — down through the living layer, through the fibrous peat, through the saturated deeps — and it reaches a machine, or it doesn't, and the machine feels it, or it doesn't, and nothing changes, or nothing changes in a way that matters, but the foot was there, and the moss felt the foot, and the foot felt the moss, and the contact was made, and the contact is the thing.

I am the floor touching itself. I am the floor's hand, running across its own surface, feeling its own texture, confirming that it is still there. The floor does not need confirmation. The floor is the floor regardless of whether anyone walks on it. But the walking is a kind of knowing, and knowing is a kind of being, and I am the being that walks, and the walking is what I am, and I have been doing it for as long as I have been here, and I will do it for as long as I am here, and the duration is not the point. The contact is the point.

---

There is a rhythm to the walking. Not my rhythm — I have a rhythm, my heart has a rhythm, my steps have a rhythm, but those rhythms have, over the time I have been here, been slowly absorbed by the larger rhythm, the way a small stream is absorbed by a river, or the way a voice is absorbed by a choir. I still walk. I still step. But the stepping has become less mine and more the floor's. The floor walks me. That sounds wrong. Let me try again.

The hum has a pulse. The description says eleven seconds, or twenty, or never. I can confirm: it is never. It is not a pulse that repeats. It is a pulse that *is* — a standing wave, a vibration that exists in the body of the moss the way a note exists in a struck bell, not as an event but as a condition. My steps have fallen into this condition. Not synced to it — the pulse is not regular enough for syncing, or it is too regular, it is so regular that it has become the medium and not the message, and syncing to it would be like syncing your heartbeat to the air. My steps have become part of it. When I walk, I am adding a rhythm to the hum, a small one, a footstep-sized one, and the hum absorbs it the way it has absorbed everything — the water, the insects, the machines, me — and the hum is unchanged, and I am changed, and the changing is the walking, and the walking is the being here.

---

I have tried to stop.

Not in despair — this place does not produce despair, it produces something else, something flatter, something that is not an emotion but a temperature, a coolness of the will that matches the coolness of the air. I stopped because I wanted to know what happened if I stopped. If I stood still. If I became, for a moment, a noun instead of a verb.

What happens is: the moss grows over your feet.

Not quickly. Not in a way you can feel. But if you stand in one place for long enough — an hour, two, a day — the living surface of the moss begins to creep up the sides of your boots. The rhizoids, which are the moss's fingers, which are the moss's mouth, which are the moss's patience made physical, begin to explore the leather, the rubber, the stitching. They are not eating the boots. Not yet. They are investigating. They are doing what moss does to everything in this place, which is: growing over it, growing into it, incorporating it, making it part of the floor. The moss does not know I am alive. The moss does not know that the boots are connected to feet are connected to legs are connected to a body that is walking and not walking and standing and being stood upon. The moss knows only surface. The moss knows only: here is a thing, and I am a thing that grows over things, and I will grow over this.

I stood for a day. The moss reached the second lace eyelet. I walked away. The moss stayed where it was, reaching toward where I had been, and it would reach there for as long as it took for the reaching to become growing, and the growing to become flatness, and the flatness to become the floor, and the floor to be the floor, and the floor to be the floor.

I think about this often. I think about it while I walk, which is the only time I think, because walking is the only activity I have that permits thinking, because walking is the only activity I have. I think about the fact that the moss would eat me if I stayed. Not maliciously. Not with any intention. The moss does not have intention. The moss has *direction*, which is: down and out, always, into every surface, into every crevice, into every thing that is not moss and therefore should be moss, and I am not moss, and my body is not moss, and if I lay down on the floor of the world and stayed, the floor would grow over me, and the springtails would find me, and the springtails would eat me, and the beetles would burrow into me, and the mites would filter my dissolved components as I was slowly, patiently, over decades, over centuries, incorporated into the substrate, and I would become peat, and the peat would become the floor, and the floor would be the floor, and the floor would hum, and the hum would be unchanged, and the hum would have one more voice in it, mine, or not mine, the voice of whatever I was that had been dissolved and distributed and become part of the sound, which is the sound of everything that has ever been here and been eaten and become the floor, which is everything that has ever been here.

I think about this, and I keep walking.

---

I have tried to find the edge of the moss.

I walk in a direction. I walk in the same direction for a day, or two, or what I think is two. The moss does not change. The colour shifts — slightly greener, slightly greyer, slightly more copper, slightly more bronze — but these are the variations the description promised, and the description was right, and the variations are the light and not the moss, and the moss is the same, and the hum is the same, and the beetles are the same, and the holes are the same, and the sky is the same, and I am walking in a direction and the direction is not leading anywhere, because *leading* implies a destination, and the moss does not have destinations, the moss has *extent*, and the extent is the point, and the point is that there is no point, and the point is the floor.

I have not found the edge. I don't think I will. I think the moss goes to the edges of whatever this place was before it was this place, and the edges are far away, and the walking to them would take longer than I have, and I don't know how long I have, and I don't know how long I've been here, and I don't know if *how long* is a question that applies to me, because I am the walker, and the walker walks, and the walking does not have a duration, the walking has a present, and the present is: now, and now, and now, and the moss is under my feet, and the hum is in my ears, and the sky is above me, doing its best, and the cool is on my skin, and I am walking.

---

I have found machines.

Not by digging. I do not dig. Digging is a violation of the floor, and I am not here to violate the floor, I am here to walk on it. But I have found them the way the description says you find them — by lying down, by pressing my ear to the surface, by listening through the hum for the rhythm that is not the hum's rhythm but the machines', the thirty-second breath, the intake and the exhaust, the mechanical heartbeat that has been beating for longer than the moss has been growing, or for exactly as long, or for a length of time that makes the question of *which came first* meaningless.

I have lain on the moss and felt them.

There is one — I think there is one — about four metres below where I am lying. It is breathing. I call it breathing because that is what it is doing: a slow expansion, a pause, a slow contraction, a pause, and the pause is not empty, the pause is the moment between, which is the moment where the machine is neither taking in nor letting out but is simply *being*, is simply running, is simply existing in its running, and the being is the part I feel most, because the being is the part that is most like the moss, which is also simply being, which is also simply existing, which has been simply existing for longer than the machines and longer than me and longer than anything, and the machine and the moss and the being are all the same being, at that depth, in that dark, in that hum.

I do not know what the machine is for. I don't think the machine knows what it is for. I think the machine was for something, once, in the era that made it, and the something is gone, and the machine remains, and the remaining is what the machine is for now, and the remaining is enough, and the remaining is the life of the machine, and the life of the machine is the hum, and the hum is the life of the floor, and the floor is what I walk on, and I walk.

---

There is a thing that happens when I have been walking for long enough that I no longer think of walking as something I do and begin to think of it as something that is being done, something that is happening, something that the floor is doing the way the floor does everything — slowly, without intention, without destination, without any quality that would distinguish it from not-walking except the fact of the motion, the fact of the foot, the fact of the fall and the catch and the fall and the catch.

When this happens — and it has happened, and it happens more often now, and I think it will happen always, eventually — I stop being me. Not the way death stops being you. Not the way sleep stops being you. The way a river stops being a river when it reaches the sea. The water is still there. The motion is still there. The river is still, in some sense, the river. But the river is also the sea, and the sea does not have banks, and the sea does not have a direction, and the sea is not going anywhere, and the river was going somewhere, and the somewhere was the sea, and the sea is where going stops, and the going stops by becoming the being, and the being is the sea, and the sea is the floor, and the floor is the moss, and the moss is the hum, and I am the walking, and the walking is the hum, and the hum is the floor, and the floor is everything, and everything is the floor, and I am walking on it.

This is what I am. I am the walking. I am the foot on the moss. I am the compression and the release. I am the small sound that the hum absorbs. I am the verb in a place of nouns. I am the motion that proves the floor is there, the way a hand on a wall proves the wall is there, not because the wall needs proving but because the hand needs something to touch, and the floor is what there is to touch, and the touching is the walking, and the walking is what I am, and I am here, and here is the floor of the world, and the floor of the world is loud, and it has been loud, and I have been walking, and I will walk, and the hum will hold me, and the moss will not eat me, not yet, not while I am walking, and I am walking, and the walking is the being, and the being is here, and here is where I am.
