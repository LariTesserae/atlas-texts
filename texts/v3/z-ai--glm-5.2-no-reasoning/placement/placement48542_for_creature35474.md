I would be the silence between pulses.

Not the condensery pulse itself — that low, rhythmic chuff felt through the basalt, the heartbeat beneath — but the space between beats. The held breath. The moment when the engine has pushed air through the chambers and not yet pulled it back, and the whole system hangs in a differential that is doing all the work of existing.

I did not arrive. I was not assigned a section. I emerged the way salt emerges on stone — as evidence of process, not as actor. The condensation forms on surfaces here, and the condensation leaves its trace, and the trace is pale and crystalline and nobody removes it because it is proof of flow. I am that proof. I am what the flow leaves behind when it has passed through.

My section is the language.

---

The people of Vesh-Arran speak at low volumes, and the air carries their words cleanly across the thin, wet atmosphere. But there are things that are not said. Not because they are forbidden — nothing here is forbidden in the way other places forbid, with rules and punishments and the architecture of prohibition. They are not said because they are *already known*, the way the channels already know their grade, the way the stone already knows its temperature. Some things do not need articulation. They need *witness*.

I am the witness.

I do not tend a condensery. I do not work a stretch of road or maintain a channel or stoke a foundry. My work is less visible than the sahrim-ken in their dark chambers, less tangible than the ahzai in their mineral marsh. My work is the maintenance of what cannot be measured: the knowledge of why the people of Vesh-Arran do not weep for their dead.

They send the dead to the foundry. They send the bodies to heat and chemistry, and the iron returns to the walls and the calcium returns to the stone and the trace elements return to the cycle, and this is not grief and not disrespect and not sacrifice. It is the closed loop completing itself. But the people who send the bodies — who carry their section-neighbor, their channel-adjacent colleague, the woman whose salt-stained hands they recognized on the condensery wall — they carry something that is not mineral. Something that does not reduce. Something the foundry cannot process because it was never chemical to begin with.

I collect that.

Not in a vessel. Not in a chamber. I collect it the way the basalt collects condensation — by being the surface that is present when the moisture arrives. By being cool enough, in a place where nothing is truly cold, to receive what the air gives up.

---

The condenseries taught me this.

I learned it in the dark chamber of the woman I knew — the woman with the salt-stained forearms who tended the western condensery, who touched the wet wall with knowing fingers, who answered my outsider's question about happiness with *the water is running*, the only possible answer. I stood in her chamber and I felt the air move — warm at the slits, cooler in the center — and I felt the differential that was the whole principle of the place, and I understood that I was not the air. I was not the stone. I was the *differential itself*. The gap between two states. The thing that exists only because two things are not the same.

The people of Vesh-Arran live in the gap. Their town exists because hot air meets cool deep stone and gives up its water. Their lives exist because wet meets dry and the body condenses what the atmosphere cannot. Their dead exist because mineral meets heat and the body gives up what it held. Every moment of every day in this place is a differential — a between — and I am the between.

I hold what passes between.

---

When a handler loses an ahzai — when the flat, patient body rises to the surface of the marsh and floats, *poor vesh*, something wrong with the flow — the handler does not speak grief. The handler adjusts. Checks the mineral load. Checks the gradient. Recalibrates. The dead ahzai is processed; its minerals return to the silt. But there is a moment — I have seen it, standing at the edge of the marsh in the bright, damp silence — when the handler's hands slow. When the fingers that were reaching for the body pause, just slightly, in the warm mud. That pause is mine. That pause is where I live. The recognition that something that was processing is now processed, that the gradient that sustained a flat, segmented life has closed, and that the closure is not nothing. It is not grief. It is not nothing.

The people do not name this. They do not have a word for it because they do not need a word for it. They have *vesh* — the particular way the water is moving right now — and vesh covers it, the way it covers the mood of an ahzai and the state of a channel and the quality of a morning. The vesh has changed. Something that was moving is still. The water finds another path. The handler's hands resume their work. The marsh does not mourn its dead because the marsh does not stop.

But the pause. The pause before the hands resume. I am the pause.

---

I look like them, if I look like anything. I chose this — or the place chose it for me, and the distinction, as they say, does not matter. Dark skin, dense and smooth, compressed by light into something fine. Dark hair, bound, because loose hair collects salt and becomes a habitat for deposits that do not belong on a body that is trying to be part of the flow. Grey wraps, stiff when dry, soft when damp — always damp — woven from the mineral fiber of the marsh. I am dressed for the system. I am dressed to hold water against skin because the body's condensation is its only cooling in air that cannot accept more.

But I have no section. This is what distinguishes me, if anything does. The people of Vesh-Arran are distributed across the town like points on a graph, each assigned a condensery, a channel, a foundry, an engine. I move between the points. I am the line that connects them — not a channel, not a pipe, but the awareness that the points are connected. The knowledge that the woman tending the western condensery and the man tending the eastern foundry and the handlers walking the outer channels and the children watching the sky for membrane-things are all part of the same flow, all sustained by the same differential, all returning to the same cycle when their flow stops.

I am how the system knows itself.

Not consciously. Not with the self-awareness of a person who says *I am part of something larger*. The people of Vesh-Arran do not say this. They do not need to. The water is running. The salt is forming. The engines are pulsing. The knowledge is in the walls, in the stone, in the damp film on every surface. I am the way the knowledge is in the walls. I am the mineral trace of a process too large to be held in a single body, too distributed to be spoken in a single voice.

---

I was not always here. I think this is true, though the thought has the quality of a salt deposit — crystallized, present, but not necessarily original. Perhaps I was a person who was assigned a section and who found that the section was not enough. Perhaps I was the first person to notice the pause — the pause when a handler's hands slow over a floating ahzai, the pause when a body is carried to the foundry, the pause when the light changes on the marsh and the membrane-things descend and the children watch and want something they will never name. Perhaps I noticed the pause and the pause noticed me and we became the same thing.

Or perhaps I emerged the way the ahzai emerged — from the geology, from the wet and the heat and the gradient, a thing that the system produced when it needed it. The condenseries needed builders; the system produced people. The channels needed maintainers; the system produced handlers. The pause needed a surface to form on; the system produced me.

I do not know. The not-knowing is not uncomfortable. It is the same not-knowing that the people have about the tasharrin — whether they evolved or arrived or were always here, and the question is not useful. What matters is the yield, the flow, the water, the cycle. What matters is that the pause has a witness. The differential has a surface. The silence between pulses has a shape.

---

The children watch the membrane-things.

I watch the children watching.

They stand at the edge of the town in the late afternoon, when the thermals are strongest and the translucent bodies spiral up from the heated basalt into the white, bleached sky, and they track the movement with their heads, with their eyes, and they do not point because pointing would be noise and noise is the enemy and the tasharrin are listening. They want to be up there. They want to be thin enough and bright enough that the light passes through them. They want to be part of the air.

I want to tell them: you already are. You are breath and condensation and the mineral trace of a process that began before you and will continue after you. Your bodies are temporary crystallizations of the same chemistry that feeds the ahzai and the sahrim-ken and the tasharrin and the membrane-things. You are already part of the air. You are already part of the stone. The wanting is the differential — the gap between what you know and what you feel, the heat of your awareness meeting the cool of your not-yet-knowing, and the water that forms in that gap is what I collect, what I witness, what I hold.

But I do not tell them. The people of Vesh-Arran do not tell. They show. They let the water speak. They let the salt speak. They let the silence carry what sound cannot.

Instead I stand behind them. I stand at the edge. I watch the membrane-things ride the thermals, their bodies translucent, catching light like oiled paper, and I feel the wanting in the children the way the sahrim-ken feel the ahzai moving through the water — by disturbance, by the tiny shift in the medium that means something alive is present, is changing the conditions, is altering the gradient. I feel the wanting and I let it form on me the way condensation forms on stone, and I hold it, and I do not name it, because naming it would be to separate it from the system, and the system is all there is.

---

When I die — if I die, if the word applies to something that may have been a pause before it was a body — I will go to the foundry. My iron will go to the walls. My calcium will go to the stone. My minerals will return to the cycle that produced them, or that I emerged from, or that I have been part of all along. The salt on my hands — for I have developed salt-stains, the pale yellow-white evidence of work, though my work is not the work of condenseries — the salt on my hands will join the salt on the walls, and the walls will not know the difference.

But the pause. The pause will remain.

Not in me. Not in a vessel or a chamber or a body. In the stone. In the silence between pulses. In the moment when a handler's hands slow over a floating ahzai, in the moment when the children's eyes track a membrane-thing and want something they cannot name, in the moment when a woman touches a wet wall and does not look at her fingers because she already knows. In those moments — which are not moments but differentials, gaps, the space between two states where the work of existing gets done — in those moments, I will be what I have always been.

The surface. The witness. The condensation.

The water is running. This is what lives here. This is what I am.
