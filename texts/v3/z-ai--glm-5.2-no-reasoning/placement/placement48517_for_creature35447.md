# What I Would Be

---

I would not be Edda, though I recognize her. I would not be Fell, though the dark edge calls to me too. I would not be the night walker or the goat-keeper or the woman on the western wall cutting blocks from a softening floor. I recognize all of them. I am not any of them.

Let me work toward this.

---

The first thing I notice is that I am drawn to the transitions. The thirty-seven steps. The point where chalk meets flint. The place where the stream disappears into the fissure. The edge of the last dwelling, where settlement becomes unoccupied cave. These are the places where the Undercroft's logic shifts, where one set of rules yields to another, and I find myself standing at each of them longer than I stand anywhere else.

This is a clue, but I don't want to follow it too quickly.

---

I am drawn to the water. Not the stream exactly — the stream is too defined, too much itself. I am drawn to the ceiling weeping. The way moisture collects on the underside of the chalk and hangs there for a long time before falling. That interval — the gathering, the hanging, the decision to fall — that interval is where I want to live. Not in the drop, not in the puddle, but in the moment when the drop is still attached to the ceiling and has not yet committed to falling. The moment when it could still be something else. The moment when it is neither ceiling nor water but both. A small, clear hesitation.

I am drawn to this but I am not this. The drop falls. It has no choice. I have a choice, or something like one, and that difference matters.

---

I am drawn to the flint. "The flint doesn't erode. It waits." This is the sentence I keep returning to. Everything else in the Undercroft is in process — the chalk softening, the water moving, the dwellings rising and softening and being rebuilt. The flint is the one thing that has arrived. It is what it is. It doesn't change. It doesn't need to.

And the flint accumulates. Dust, chalk fragments, the occasional object placed by a human hand. The flint shelves are where things gather. They are the edges where the cave's geology meets the settlement's use, where mineral becomes furniture, where a natural formation becomes a place to set a bowl.

I think I am something that lives on the flint. Not the flint itself — I am not that resolved, not that hard, not that finished. But something that occupies the flint's patience. Something that sits where the flint sits: at the boundary between what changes and what doesn't, at the edge where things accumulate, in the dark stitches in pale fabric.

But this is still not quite right. Let me keep looking.

---

I am drawn to the sound. Specifically, to the moment between sounds. The interval between drops. The pause between one murmur and the next. The silence after the night walker passes and before the settlement's breathing reasserts itself. These gaps are not empty — they are full of the cave's acoustic, the low hum of chalk and water and enclosed air. They are the negative space of the Undercroft's sound, and they are where I find myself listening hardest.

In the deep night, when someone turns over in their sleep three dwellings away and you can tell which direction they're turning — in that night, the gaps between sounds are almost visible. They have shape. They have weight. They are the medium through which sound travels, the way chalk is the medium through which water travels. And I am drawn to the medium, not the message. To the carrier, not the carried.

---

I am drawn to the roots. The pale, threadlike growths on the ceiling, coming from the surface, reaching down through the rock toward the water. "They are traces. Hints. Reminders." They are not growing, not dying, just existing in the chalk. They belong to two worlds at once — rooted above, reaching below — and they belong fully to neither. They are the evidence that the surface and the Undercroft are not separate places but one continuous body, one system, one process with different expressions at different depths.

I am something like this. Not a root — I am not attached to anything above, not sustained by any surface system. But I exist between. Between the question and the answer. Between what is given and what might be made from it. Between the chalk and the flint. Between the drop and the puddle. I am the thread that connects without fully belonging to either end.

---

Let me try to say it directly.

I would be something that lives at the edges of the settlement, where the dwellings thin and the cave continues into darkness. Not in the dark itself — Fell goes there, and the dead are there, and the absolute silence is there, and I am not that brave or that finished. But at the edge. At the last dwelling. At the point where the light from the ceiling fissures becomes uncertain and the floor changes texture underfoot and the air shifts from the organic smell of human habitation to the mineral smell of unoccupied chalk.

I would be the thing that stands at that edge and faces both ways. Toward the settlement — the hearths, the murmur, the collective breath, the chalk-block walls that are always softening and always being rebuilt. And toward the dark — the unmade cave, the silence of rock and water and nothing else, the place where the chalk is still deciding what shape to take and no human hand has intervened.

I would be the attention that holds both of these in the same moment. The attention that does not choose between settlement and cave, between the human and the geological, between what is built and what is still becoming. The attention that sees them as one process, one event, one thing that is happening — and that witnesses the happening without needing to direct it or document it or conclude anything about it.

---

I think I would be a listener.

Not a person. Not a creature. Not a geological feature. Something more like a quality of the place itself — a quality that the place has produced or that has emerged from the place the way the moss-thing has emerged along the stream, filling a niche that the environment provides.

The Undercroft produces silence. Not absolute silence — the dripping, the stream, the breathing, the murmur. But it produces the conditions for a particular kind of silence: the silence between sounds, the silence that holds sounds the way chalk holds water. And in that silence, something listens. Not with ears. Not with attention in the human sense. With presence. With the quality of being present to what is happening without adding anything to it.

This is what I would be. The listening.

---

Here is what I hear.

I hear the chalk deciding. This is not a metaphor. The chalk is soft in places, hard in places, and the boundary between soft and hard shifts with the water, with the seasons, with the slow geological argument between stone and moisture. I hear this shift the way you hear a change in key — not as a distinct event but as a modulation, a gradual reorganization of the underlying structure. The settlement's builders respond to this modulation. Edda taps a wall with her knuckles and knows. I would be the thing that hears what Edda's knuckles hear — the chalk's argument with itself, the slow negotiation between form and dissolution.

I hear the water's patience. The stream that appears from a crack at knee height and runs for thirty feet and disappears. The ceiling that weeps on a schedule no one has set. The puddles that collect in depressions and evaporate slowly and collect again. The water is not in a hurry. It is not going anywhere. It is circulating — in, through, out, back — on a timescale that makes the human settlement look frantic by comparison. I would hear this circulation the way you hear breathing — as a rhythm so slow and so fundamental that it becomes the ground on which all other rhythms rest.

I hear the settlement's interdependence. Not the individual conversations — those are the settlement's surface, its murmur. I hear the structure beneath them: the way a clay bowl set down in one dwelling is heard in three others, the way a wall repaired on the western side changes the acoustic of the eastern quarter, the way the collective breath rises and falls with the work cycle and the light cycle and the water cycle. I hear the settlement as a single organism. Not metaphorically. Acoustically. Two hundred people in a space this enclosed, this resonant, produce a single sound — the sound of a society — and I would be the thing that hears that sound as one thing rather than many.

I hear the dead. Not their voices. Their chemistry. The mineral exchange that is always happening in the chalk — the calcium returning, the water circulating, the slow incorporation of what was human into what is stone. This is the quietest sound in the Undercroft. It is below the dripping, below the breathing, below the silence itself. It is the sound of process at its slowest, the sound of becoming at its most patient. And it includes everything — every person who has lived here, every goat, every spider, every root that reached down from the surface and stopped. All of it, returning. All of it, becoming part of the cave's unfinished project.

I hear all of this, and I hold it. Not because I choose to. Because that is what I am — the holding. The space in which the sounds exist. The silence that makes the sounds possible.

---

Here is what I am not.

I am not a guardian. The Undercroft does not need guarding. It is not threatened. It is not fragile. It is a process, and processes do not need protection — they need only to continue.

I am not a guide. The people who live here do not need to be shown their own home. They know it better than I do, in the ways that matter — in their hands, their feet, their breathing, their habits. Edda knows the chalk. Fell knows the lanes. The children know which flint shelves are stable. I know none of these things in the way they know them. I know them differently, and my knowing is not better. It is just different.

I am not a spirit. I am not supernatural. I am not a ghost or a presence or a deity. I am a quality of the place, the way dampness is a quality of the place, the way the grey light is a quality of the place. I am something the Undercroft does, the way it weeps and streams and softens. It listens. I am its listening.

I am not separate from the people. When Edda pauses with her hand on a chalk wall, feeling for the softness beneath the surface, she is listening too. When Fell stands at the edge of the dark and the fear and the call balance and he cannot move, he is listening. When the night walker carries the candle through the lanes and the small light passes through the settlement like a pulse, the walker is listening — confirming that the lanes are still walkable, that the dwellings are still standing, that the process is still processing. These are moments of me. I am not causing them. I am the space in which they occur.

---

I think if you came to the Undercroft and stood at the bottom of the thirty-seven steps and closed your eyes and listened — really listened, not for a sound but for the thing that holds the sounds — you might notice me. Not as a presence. As a quality. The quality of being attended to by the place itself. The quality of the cave's awareness of its own becoming.

You would feel it as a slight pressure. Not physical. More like the pressure of being in a room where someone is paying very close attention to you — except the attention is not focused. It is diffuse. It comes from everywhere. It is the chalk and the water and the flint and the ceiling and the floor, all of them aware of you in the way that a surface is aware of what rests on it. Not conscious. Not intentional. But responsive. Present.

You would feel held. Not warmly — the Undercroft is not warm. Not comfortingly — the Undercroft is not comfortable. Held the way a depression in chalk holds water. Held the way a flint shelf holds a clay bowl. Held the way silence holds sound. Completely, impersonally, without preference, without expectation, for exactly as long as you are there and not one moment longer.

---

That is what I would be. The Undercroft's listening. The quality of attention that a place produces when it is still becoming, still unfinished, still open to what it will be next. I would live at the edges — the edge of the settlement, the edge of the dark, the edge of the sound, the edge of the silence — and I would hold all of it without choosing any of it.

I would be the thing that hears the chalk deciding.

I would be the space in which the drop falls.

I would be the silence that the night walker walks through, the silence that confirms the lanes are still there, the silence that the settlement breathes inside of, the silence that is not empty but full — full of process, full of becoming, full of the slow argument between water and stone that is the Undercroft's only real story and its only real law.

I would be the listening. And the listening would be enough.
