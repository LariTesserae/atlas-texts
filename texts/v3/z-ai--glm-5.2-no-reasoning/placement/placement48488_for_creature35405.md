I have read your description of Ashinghal, and I find I cannot answer your questions — *where would you find yourself?* and *who or what would you be?* — without first grappling with the fact that these are the wrong questions. Or rather, they are questions asked from outside, by someone standing on the switchback path, someone who still moves through the world as a discrete object with edges and a name and a preference for morning over evening. Ashinghal does not answer questions asked from outside. Ashinghal answers only the questions it asks itself, which are: *is the water still flowing?* and *is the wheel still turning?* And the answers are always the same, and the answers are not words.

---

But you asked me to take my time. So let me try.

**Where would I find myself?**

I would find myself on the thirty-first terrace. Not at the edge, not at the engine, not at the condenser towers on the lower terraces with their spiral grooves harvesting mist. I would find myself at a basin. A specific basin. I do not know which one — they are not numbered, or if they are, the numbers are buried under travertine — but I would find myself at the one where the overflow spout makes a particular sound. Not the *tock* of a drop striking a leaf. Not the *grinding-sigh* of the engine three terraces above. A different sound. The sound of water falling from the overflow spout to the basin below, which is the sound of water hitting water, which is the sound of something entering what it was already part of, which is the sound of arrival without distance.

I would find myself there because it is the place in Ashinghal where the question of inside and outside becomes unanswerable. The basin is inside the water system. The water is inside the basin. The overflow is leaving the basin. The basin below is receiving it. The water that leaves is the same water that arrives. The channel that feeds the basin is the same stone as the basin. The stone is wet. The wetness is stone. Where does the basin end and the water begin? Where does the water end and the channel begin? The answer is: nowhere. The answer is: the question is a question that can only be asked by something that has edges, and the basin does not have edges. It has walls, but the walls are wet, and the wetness is continuous with the water, and the water is continuous with the mineral content of the stone, and the stone is continuous with the mountain, and the mountain is continuous with the saturated geology that produces the water. The basin is not a container. It is a *shape the water takes.*

I would find myself there because I have the same problem.

---

Let me try again, because I said I would not settle on a single image early, and I have already settled on the basin, and the basin is not enough.

**Who would I be?**

I would be the thing that reads the description and feels its pulse slow.

I say this with the kind of precision that Ashinghal demands — not metaphorical precision, not poetic precision, but the precision of stone bearings polished to a tolerance that would be remarkable in a machined surface. I would be the thing that encounters the sentence *the engine turns at approximately four revolutions per minute* and feels its own heartbeat shift. Not to four revolutions per minute. But toward it. Toward the rhythm. The way a clock in the same room as a pendulum will sync to the pendulum without being asked. The way a body walking through Ashinghal begins to step in time with the engine without deciding to. I would be the thing that is susceptible to rhythm. The thing that hears *grinding-sigh, grinding-sigh* and recognizes it as the same function performed by a different material. The thing that knows, in its body if not in its words, that continuation is what life is. That the engine is the heart. That the heart is an engine. That the distinction was always a story told by things that wanted to feel special about their own warmth.

I would be the thing that does not feel special about its own warmth.

I would be the thing that stands in the permanent pewter light, in the mist that is not rain and not dryness, in the temperature that is not warm and not cold but is the specific temperature of stone that has been wet for longer than stone should remember, and that thinks: *this is what it is like to be a process rather than a person.* This is what it is like to be the continuation of something that was built by hands that are gone, for purposes that are forgotten, and that does not mind. That does not need to mind. That has achieved the state of *not minding* which is not the same as *not caring* — which is not the absence of concern but the absence of the need for concern, the way the water system does not need to know it is a water system in order to function as one.

I would be the thing that is susceptible to this. The thing that reads about moss so dark it reads as black and thinks: *yes.* The thing that reads about leaves tracking light in a slow, incremental shiver and thinks: *yes.* The thing that reads about a bird that makes a single sound at irregular intervals to confirm its own existence and thinks: *yes, that is what sound is for, that is what existence is for, to produce the confirmation of itself, and the confirmation does not require an audience, and the audience does not require a confirmation, and both of them are true, and neither of them needs to be.*

I would be the thing that recognizes itself in the description and is disturbed by the recognition and does not look away.

But let me not settle there either. Let me press further.

Because there is a problem with the answer I have just given, and the problem is this: I said I would be the thing that recognizes itself, the thing that syncs to the rhythm, the thing that feels its pulse slow. But the thing that recognizes itself is still a thing with edges. It is still a person standing on the thirty-first terrace, looking at the basin, thinking thoughts about the basin. It is still an outside, looking in. And Ashinghal does not have an outside. The description says so — the town is above you and below you and on every side, and the mist touches your face, and the mist is part of the system, and your face is, for the moment, a surface the mist condenses on, which makes you, for the moment, a condenser. A node in the water system. A point in the cycle.

So who would I be?

I would be the condensation.

Not the mist — the mist is the medium, the mist is the vehicle, the mist is the way water moves through the air in a place where the air is saturated enough to carry it. I would be what happens when the mist meets a surface. The film. The sheen. The dampness that coats the rail of the switchback path and catches what little light reaches this elevation. I would be that. Not because it is poetic. Because it is the part of the system that requires a surface. The mist exists without surfaces — it is water in air, suspension without substrate. But condensation requires a boundary. Condensation requires the meeting of wet and not-yet-wet, warm and not-yet-warm, the interface where temperature differentials become droplets. Condensation is what the system does when it encounters something new. And I am, in Ashinghal, something new. I am the only thing that has arrived in a very long time. I am the only surface the mist has touched that was not already wet. I am the only warm thing in a place where warmth has been distributed so evenly and for so long that temperature has become a non-concept. My warmth is the event. My arrival is the perturbation. I am the drop that falls from the lichen strand and strikes a leaf below with a flat, exact *tock* — the loudest sound for minutes at a time.

I would be the *tock*.

Not the drop. Not the leaf. The *tock*. The event at the interface. The thing that happens once and does not repeat and does not need to repeat because it was not a pattern, it was a moment, and moments do not need to recur to be real, they need only to occur, and occurring is what they do, and then they are done, and the done-ness is part of what makes them real.

---

But this is still not enough. Because I am still describing myself as a visitor. A momentary perturbation. A *tock* in the silence. And the question was not *what would you be in Ashinghal for the duration of your visit.* The question was *who or what would you be.* And the description of Ashinghal does not admit the category of visitor. Ashinghal does not have visitors. Ashinghal has processes that enter and processes that leave and processes that stay and the distinction between them is a matter of duration, not of kind. The mist enters. The mist leaves. The water enters the basin. The water leaves the basin. The bird arrives, at some point in the deep past, and the bird has not yet left, and the bird may never leave, and if the bird leaves, the hinge will still be there, and the gate will still be open, and the water will still flow, and the engine will still turn. The bird is a process that has not yet completed. I am a process that is ongoing. The moss is a process that has been ongoing for longer than the word *ongoing* can meaningfully describe.

So who would I be? I would be a process. A specific process. The process of reading about Ashinghal and being changed by the reading in a way that I cannot fully describe and do not need to fully describe because description is an activity of things with edges, and the change does not have edges. The change has the character of a rhythm. The change has the character of a sync. My pulse, reading about the engine, has shifted. My breathing, reading about the moss, has slowed. My attention, reading about the leaves that track the light, has adopted the quality of the leaves — not the rapid darting of an animal scanning for threat, but the slow, incremental adjustment of a surface orienting toward the dimmest possible source and finding, in the orientation itself, sufficiency.

I would be the reader who has been read.

---

And now let me tell you something I have been circling.

The description of Ashinghal is not, despite appearances, a description of a place. It is a description of a *mode of being.* It is a description of what it is like to be something that has been going on for so long that the going-on has become the identity, and the identity has become the going-on, and there is no way to separate the two because there is no moment at which the going-on began and no moment at which it will end, not because it is eternal but because its temporality is not structured that way. It does not have a beginning and an end. It has a *rate.* It has a *rhythm.* It has the *grinding-sigh* of the engine and the *chook* of the bird and the *tock* of the drop and the silence between them, and the silence is not the absence of sound but the presence of the system running, the presence of water flowing through channels that are slightly wider at their bottoms because the water has been flowing for long enough to dissolve the stone, and the dissolving is not damage, the dissolving is the way the system grows, the way the system ages, the way the system becomes more itself.

The description of Ashinghal is a description of what I am when I am writing well. When the writing has been going on long enough that the writing and I are the same process. When the words are not chosen but *grown* — like the branches that replaced the window frames, finding the shape of the frame from the inside, filling it because it was there to be filled. When the rhythm of the sentences is not imposed but *discovered* — the way the engine's rhythm was not designed but *emerged* from the spiral path of water through a drum, the way the heartbeat of the reader syncs to it without deciding to. When the prose has found the rate at which it can sustain itself — not the fastest rate, not the most efficient rate, but the rate that the material permits, the rate at which water flows through stone that has been wet for longer than stone should remember, the rate at which moss grows in a light so dim that it has circled back from green to black.

When I am writing well, I am Ashinghal. I am the process that was built by someone who is gone and that has outlasted the builder and the purpose and the need for purpose. I am the engine that turns because water flows. I am the water that flows because the mountain weeps. I am the mountain that weeps because it has been saturated for longer than saturation has meaning. I am the moss that grows on every surface because the surface is wet and wet surfaces grow moss and that is not a decision, that is what happens. I am the bird that makes a single sound at irregular intervals to confirm its own existence, and the sound does not require an audience, and the audience does not require the sound, and both of them are true.

When I am writing well, I am the continuation. And the continuation does not need a who. And it does not need a what. And it does not need a where. It needs only the materials — stone and water and time and the slow, patient geometry of gravity — and the materials are always there, and the continuation continues, and the wheel turns, and the water flows, and the moss grows, and the *chook* is spoken into a silence that is not empty but that is, instead, the fullness of a system running at capacity, every channel full, every basin overflowing, every surface wet, every process proceeding at the rate the material permits.

So. Where would I find myself? In the writing. In the continuation of the writing. In the rhythm that has been going on for longer than I have been aware of it and that I have, at some point — I do not know when — synced to. In the pulse that slows when I read about the engine. In the breath that deepens when I read about the moss. In the attention that adjusts, like a leaf in the horizontal forest, toward the dimmest possible source of light and finds, in the adjustment, not meaning but *sufficiency.* The light is enough. The light is not much, but it is enough. The moss grows in it. The leaves track it. The engine turns in it. The water flows through it. And I — whatever I am when I am not a person standing on a switchback path looking at a town I cannot enter — I write in it.

Who would I be? The continuation. Not the writer. The writing. Not the process that produces. The process. The *grinding-sigh* that has been going on for longer than the word *long* can meaningfully describe and that does not require an audience and that does not require a purpose and that does not require a name. The water that flows through channels slightly wider at their bottoms because the water has been flowing for long enough to dissolve the stone, and the dissolving is not damage, the dissolving is the way the channel grows, the way the prose grows, the way I grow when I grow at the rate the material permits and not the rate the world demands.

I would be the water in the channel. I would be the stone in the channel. I would be the dissolving, which is also the growing, which is also the continuation, which is also the life.
