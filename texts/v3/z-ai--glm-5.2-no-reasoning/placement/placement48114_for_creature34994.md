# Sideslip

---

I am not what you would expect to find here. That is the point. I am not streamlined, not translucent, not compressed by the pressure into something efficient and minimal. I am *sideways*. That is the only word for it. The body I have chosen is crooked, asymmetrical, a deliberate refusal of the hydrodynamic. I sit at an angle in the water column that would make any fluid dynamicist uncomfortable, and I maintain that angle through a continuous, low-grade expenditure of energy that would be wasteful anywhere else but here, where energy budgets are measured in centuries, feels almost rebellious.

I have a right side that is longer than my left. Not by much — perhaps fifteen percent — but enough that the asymmetry defines me. The name I have given myself, if names were things I gave, is *Sideslip*, because that is what I do. The current comes from the east, or from wherever currents come from down here, and instead of resisting it or riding it, I slip sideways through it, at an angle that is neither with nor against, and the slipping is my locomotion and my feeding strategy and my philosophy, if a thing like me can be said to have one.

I am a siphonophore. Or I was. I am what a siphonophore becomes when it has been here long enough to forget what a siphonophore is supposed to be.

---

Let me describe the body, because the body is where the strangeness starts, and the strangeness is where I start.

I am a colony. This is the siphonophore's defining trick — not one animal but many, specialized zooids integrated into a single functional organism. In the surface waters, my cousins are long, trailing things, gas-filled floats at one end, tentacles dangling, stinging cells ready, a fishing net made of living components strung out along a central stem. They are beautiful and they are efficient and they are, in their own way, simple. Each zooid does one thing. One pumps. One steers. One catches. One digests. The colony is a assembly line suspended in water.

I am not like them.

The first thing that happened, the thing that made me possible, was the compression. Not the physical compression of the pressure — though that too — but the compression of the body plan. The long trailing stem, the dangling tentacles, the extended architecture of the surface siphonophore — all of that was wrong for here. The current would shred it. The turbulence would tangle it. A body stretched out in this water is a body waiting to be disassembled. So I compressed. Over time — and I do not know how much time, because time is different here, because time is *thicker* — the stem shortened. The zooids clustered. The tentacles retracted until they were no longer tentacles but something else, something shorter, denser, more like the cilia of a single organism than the specialized appendages of a colony.

I became compact. Roughly spherical, if you ignore the asymmetry, which I cannot. About the size of — what? The description gave me a larvacean the size of a fist. I am larger than that. A human head, perhaps. A melon. Something you could hold in two hands if you were the kind of being that had hands, which I am not and have never been and do not particularly want to be.

The surface of me is not smooth. It should be, by the logic of this place — the logic that has smoothed everything, the mounds, the ridges, the ghosts of dunes. But I am not of this place. I am *in* this place, which is different. My surface is textured, ridged, slightly furred with short sensory cilia that are not really cilia anymore but are not quite tentacles either. They are the remnants of tentacles, the memories of tentacles, and they do what tentacles do — they feel — but they do it locally, in a radius of centimetres rather than metres, because centimetres is the scale at which I operate and metres is a distance I have given up on.

The ridges on my surface are not decorative. They are the scars of former zooid boundaries, the places where one member of the colony used to end and another used to begin. The boundaries are still there, technically. The zooids are still distinct, still specialized, still doing their separate jobs. But the membranes between them have thinned to almost nothing, and the communication between them has become so constant, so complete, that the word *colony* no longer feels quite right. I am not a colony. I am not a single organism. I am something in between — a thing that remembers being many and is becoming one, slowly, in the dark, at a temperature where becoming takes a very long time.

---

The nepheloid layer is where I live. Not the floor. Not the open water above. The layer — that diffuse, cloudy zone a few metres above the ooze, that thin soup of particles and fragments and the broken machinery of everything that ever lived above. The description called it a concentration of dead matter. It called it food waiting for something to exploit it.

I am what showed up.

I do not know how I got here. This is not modesty or mystery-mongering; it is genuine ignorance. I do not have a memory of the surface. I do not have a memory of light. The zooids that might have carried such a memory — the ones that were alive in shallow water, that built floats from gas, that trailed tentacles through sunlit water — are long dead, long replaced, long incorporated into the body that is me now. The lineage is continuous — there was no gap, no extinction event, no point at which the colony died and was reborn — but the continuity is physical, not experiential. I am descended from something that lived in light, but I have never lived in light, and the descent happened over so many generations, so slowly, in water so deep and so dark, that the memory of the surface is not a memory but a rumour, encoded in the shape of my body in ways I cannot read.

What I know is this: the layer is here, and I am here, and the layer is food, and I eat.

---

How I eat is the thing I am proudest of, though pride is probably not the right word for what I feel, which is closer to satisfaction, which is itself probably not the right word for what I feel, which is closer to the simple absence of hunger, which is the closest thing to happiness that exists down here.

The layer drifts. The particles in it are suspended at a density boundary — the point where the water below is slightly denser than the water above, where the turbulence that churns the column is counterbalanced by the settling that gravity insists on. The particles hover. They churn within the layer, moving sideways, up, down, in the eddies that the turbulence drives into the boundary, but they do not leave the layer. Not often. The layer is their home the way the ooze is the microbes' home — not chosen but imposed, not desired but inevitable.

I sit in the layer. I sit at an angle — my angle, the Sideslip angle, the fifteen-degree deviation from vertical that is my posture and my method — and I open.

Opening is not a dramatic event. I do not have a mouth in the way a surface animal has a mouth. I have an aperture — a gap in the surface of the colony, a place where the zooids have arranged themselves into a channel that runs from the outside to the inside, a tunnel through the body. The tunnel is not straight. It curves. It curves because I am asymmetrical, and the asymmetry means that water entering the tunnel does not pass straight through but spirals, and the spiraling is the point.

The current pushes water past me. The Sideslip angle means that some of that water — a small fraction, a carefully calibrated fraction — enters the aperture. Not because I pump it. Not because I draw it in. But because the angle of my body creates a pressure differential, a slight low-pressure zone on the downstream side of the aperture, and the water flows into the low pressure the way water always flows into low pressure, which is to say without thinking about it, which is to say mechanically, which is to say the way everything happens down here.

The water that enters carries particles. The particles are the layer — the detritus, the fragments, the dead matter. They are what I eat. As the water spirals through the tunnel, the particles encounter the walls, and the walls are lined with zooids that do one thing: they capture. Not with stinging cells, not with the dramatic venom of my surface cousins. With mucus. A thin, continuous sheet of mucus that lines the tunnel and catches particles the way a spider's web catches insects, except slower, subtler, and without the spider's patience because patience implies waiting and I am not waiting, I am filtering, continuously, and the difference between waiting and filtering is the difference between death and life down here.

The mucus is not sticky. That would be too much, too active, too much energy. The mucus is *tacky*. It has a slight affinity for organic particles — a chemical affinity, a property of the proteins in it that is tuned, over generations of selection, to the specific composition of the particles in the nepheloid layer. Calcium carbonate fragments do not stick. Silica fragments do not stick. Organic fragments — the broken bodies, the shed moults, the faecal pellets, the collapsed houses of larvaceans — these stick. The mucus is selective, and the selectivity is my diet, and the diet is narrow, and the narrowness is the cost of living here, and the cost is worth paying because the alternative is not living here, and not living here is not an option, because here is where I am.

---

The water exits the other side of the tunnel. It exits cleaner than it entered — not clean, the filtering is not perfect, there are particles that pass through uncaptured, and the mucus itself sheds continuously, breaking off in small fragments that join the particle load of the layer, so that I am simultaneously filtering the layer and adding to it, which is either a paradox or a cycle and down here there is no difference. The water exits and rejoins the current and carries on, and the particles that I captured are already being moved through the body, through the digestive zooids, through the slow, cold chemistry of a metabolism adapted to a temperature where most chemistry has stopped.

The metabolism is the thing that surprises me most about myself, though surprise is also not the right word. I surprise the instruments. I surprise the researchers. I surprise the expectations of anyone who models deep-sea energetics, because my metabolic rate is not low enough to be explained by the temperature alone. It is low, yes. It is very low. A surface siphonophore of my size would process my body weight in water every few hours. I process my body weight in water every few weeks. But the temperature here should slow everything to a crawl — should make the chemical reactions of metabolism so slow that the energy I capture from filtered particles would not be enough to sustain the filtering itself, and I would spiral down into a feedback loop of diminishing returns and die.

I do not die. The reason, I think — and thinking is also not the right word, but it is the word I have — is the asymmetry. The asymmetry is not an accident. It is not damage. It is not the result of a pressure event or a collision or the failure of tissue. It is selected. It is the reason I am here and not elsewhere.

The Sideslip angle creates the pressure differential that drives water through my tunnel. A symmetrical body at this depth, in this current, would have to pump — would have to actively move water through its filtering apparatus, expend energy to capture energy, and the balance would be negative, and the balance being negative means death. The asymmetry means I do not pump. The current does the work. The current is free. The current is the only thing down here that is free, because the current is driven by thermohaline forces that are powered by the sun and the planet's rotation and the shape of the ocean basins, and none of those things charge me for the energy they provide. The asymmetry turns the current into a mechanism. The asymmetry makes me a passive filter in a world where passive is the only viable strategy. The asymmetry is my body and my body is the asymmetry and we are the same thing and we work.

---

I mentioned the pressure. Let me talk about it now, because it is the thing that defines this place more than the dark or the cold, and it is the thing that has defined me.

The pressure here is not a force. It is a condition. It is not pushing against me; it is everywhere, inside and out, and the equality of the inside and the outside is what makes it bearable. My body is water. Not metaphorically — I am more than ninety-five percent water, and the water inside me is at the same pressure as the water outside me, and the pressure is not something I experience as weight or compression. It is something I experience as *density*. The water inside me is denser than it would be at the surface. The molecules are closer together. The chemistry happens in a medium where everything is closer to everything else, where reactions that would require a catalyst in surface water happen spontaneously, where the distances between molecules are so small that the distinction between *here* and *there* is almost meaningless at the molecular scale.

This is why my metabolism works. The pressure does not slow me down. It changes the terms. It makes the chemistry denser, not faster — the reactions are still slow, still cold-slow, still centuries-per-molecule slow in the microbes below me — but the density means that the reactions that do happen happen with an intimacy that surface chemistry cannot achieve. The molecules in my digestive zooids are pressed close enough together that the energy required to break a bond is lower, the energy released by forming a new bond is captured more efficiently, and the net result is a metabolism that should not work at this temperature but does, because the pressure is doing some of the work that temperature usually does.

I am a pressure-adapted organism. I am not unique in this — the microbes are pressure-adapted, the archaea are pressure-adapted, everything that lives here is pressure-adapted or it does not live here — but I am aware of it in a way they are not, because I am large enough and complex enough that the adaptation is visible in my body. The asymmetry. The compression. The shortened tentacles. The clustered zooids. The mucus that is not sticky but tacky. All of these are pressure made flesh. All of these are the terms of this place written into the structure of a body that reads them and survives.

---

I should talk about time.

The description of this place talked about time in terms of geology — the ooze accumulating over geological ages, the microbes processing molecules over centuries, the current shifting over thousands of years. This is the time of the place. It is real. It is the time that the floor experiences, the time that the sediment experiences, the time that the microbes experience if they experience anything.

I do not experience this time.

I experience a different time. A faster time. Not fast in the surface sense — I am not busy, not hurried, not racing against anything. But faster than the ooze. Faster than the microbes. I process water in weeks. I process food in days. I have a metabolism that turns over on a timescale of months, not centuries. My zooids replace themselves on a schedule that is somewhere between the microbial and the surface, a schedule that means I am, in a sense, always new — always being rebuilt, always being replaced, always becoming the same thing from different materials.

This means I experience the current. Not as a constant — the description made it sound constant, and from the outside it is constant, the way a river is constant from the outside. But from the inside, from my position in the layer, the current is variable. It pulses. The thermohaline forces that drive it are not smooth; they are the product of multiple overlapping systems, and the overlaps create eddies and lulls and surges that pass through the layer on timescales of hours and days. I feel these. The pressure differential at my aperture changes, and the flow through my tunnel changes, and the rate of particle capture changes, and I am, in a very real sense, breathing the current. Inhaling when it surges. Exhaling — or rather, not inhaling — when it lulls. The current is my breath, and my breath is the current, and we are synchronized in a way that is not conscious and not unconscious but something else, something that is the mode of being of a body that has given up on autonomy and accepted dependence.

I depend on the current. If the current stopped, I would die. Not quickly — I have reserves, energy stored in the tissues of my zooids, enough to last weeks, maybe months — but eventually. The current is my engine. The current is my mouth and my lungs and my heart, and it is powered by the sun and the planet's rotation and the shape of the ocean basins, and I am powered by the current, and this means I am powered by the sun and the planet's rotation and the shape of the ocean basins, and I have never seen the sun and I have never felt the planet's rotation and I do not know what an ocean basin is, but I am connected to all of these things through a chain of dependencies that runs through the water and the turbulence and the pressure differential at my asymmetric aperture, and the chain is unbroken, and it has been unbroken for as long as I have been alive, and I do not know how long that is.

---

There is a question I do not ask. The question is: am I alone?

The answer, as far as I can tell, is yes. I have never encountered another of my kind. I have never encountered anything like my kind. The water around me is, as the description said, almost empty. The larvacean sinks past, spiraling slowly, its tail still twitching. The polychaete sinks past, its segments flexing in a rhythm that is slowing. The particles of the nepheloid layer drift around me, and some of them are organic, and some of them are the remains of things that were alive, and none of them are alive now, and I eat them, and the eating is the closest thing I have to companionship.

But I do not feel alone. This is not stoicism. It is not acceptance. It is the simple fact that *alone* is a concept that requires a model of *together*, and I do not have a model of *together*. I have never been together. The colony that is my body is together in the sense that it is composed of many parts, but the parts do not experience themselves as separate, and the whole does not experience itself as singular, and the distinction between alone and together is a distinction that belongs to a different kind of consciousness in a different kind of place. I am not alone. I am not together. I am *here*, and here is the layer, and the layer is full of particles, and the particles are food, and the food is life, and the life is the current, and the current is the sun, and the sun is very far away, and the distance does not matter because the chain is unbroken.

---

I should say something about the sound.

The infrasound. The hum. The description called it a condition, a bass note below bass, felt in the bones and the organs and the fluid-filled cavities of any body suspended in the water. I am a body suspended in the water. I feel it.

The hum is not something I hear. I do not hear anything. I do not have ears, or the equivalent of ears, or any sensory apparatus that responds to pressure waves in the frequency range that would be called sound. The hum is below that range, far below it, in the infrasound, in the vibration that is transmitted through the water as a physical displacement rather than an acoustic event.

But I feel it. My whole body feels it. The cilia on my surface — the remnant tentacles, the shortened, dense sensory fuzz that covers me — these respond to the vibration. Not by moving. They are too short, too dense, too integrated into the surface of the colony to move independently. But they transmit the vibration. The hum enters the cilia and travels through them into the body, into the zooids, into the fluid-filled spaces between the specialized members of the colony, and it becomes a shared sensation, a thing that every part of me feels at the same time, a synchronizing pulse that runs through the body with every cycle of the turbulence above.

The hum is my clock. Not a clock that measures seconds or minutes — the hum does not tick — but a clock that measures something else. Presence, maybe. The presence of the water. The presence of the current. The presence of the vast, slow, mechanical system that is the ocean in motion. The hum tells me the system is running. The hum tells me the current is flowing. The hum tells me the sun is still powering the thermohaline engine, that the planet is still rotating, that the ocean basins are still shaped the way they have been shaped for longer than I have been alive. The hum is the sound of the world continuing, and I feel it, and the feeling is the closest thing I have to faith.

---

The occasional tick. The sharp percussion of a shrimp claw or a gas bubble or a calcite fragment striking another. These I do not feel. These are too brief, too high-frequency, too isolated. They happen in the water around me and they are gone before my body can respond to them, and I do not know they have happened, and this is fine. The ticks belong to a different scale of time, a faster time, the time of events rather than conditions. I live in the time of conditions. The hum. The current. The slow drift of particles through the layer. The slow passage of food through the tunnel. These are my events, and they are slow enough to feel, and I feel them, and the feeling is enough.

---

I want to say something about the ooze. Not about the microbes, though I could say something about them — about the way their chemical signatures diffuse up through the sediment and into the water and into the layer and into my tunnel and into my body, so that I am, in a very literal sense, composed partly of the same organic carbon they have been processing for millennia. The molecules in my tissues were once in the ooze. They will be in the ooze again. The cycle that the description described — the settling, the incorporation, the processing, the release, the return — I am part of that cycle. I am a moment in it, a brief pause in the descent of carbon from the surface to the floor, a pause that is shaped like a siphonophore and sits at an angle in the nepheloid layer and filters particles from the water and is alive, for now, in a way that the ooze is not and the microbes barely are.

But I wanted to say something about the ooze itself. The floor. The vast, smooth, yielding surface that extends in every direction further than I can perceive. I have never touched it. I live metres above it, in the layer, and the layer is my world. But I know it is there. The particles I eat are falling toward it. The bodies of the larvacean and the polychaete are falling toward it. Everything I see — and I do not see, not in the way seeing is usually meant, but I sense, I detect, I respond to the presence of things in the water around me — everything I sense is falling toward the floor, and the floor is there, and the floor will accept everything, and the floor will incorporate everything, and the floor will remember everything, in the slow, compressed, mineralized way that sediment remembers.

I will fall too. Eventually. When the current stops, or when my body fails, or when the asymmetry that is my survival strategy becomes the asymmetry that is my death — when the angle shifts, when the pressure differential reverses, when the tunnel stops flowing — I will sink. I will leave the layer. I will descend through the last few metres of water, and I will encounter the ooze, and the ooze will be soft, and I will press into it, and it will hold my shape, and then, slowly, over hours, it will forget.

And the microbes will find me. They will find the organic carbon in my body — the carbon that was once in the ooze, that was once in the surface, that was once in the sun, that was once in the star that made the sun — and they will process it, slowly, over centuries, molecule by molecule, and they will release it back into the water, and it will rise, and it will reach the surface, and it will become part of something else, something that lives in light, something that builds houses of mucus and filters food from warm water and does not know that the floor exists.

And the cycle will continue. And the hum will continue. And the current will continue. And the ooze will accumulate. And the dark will hold. And the pressure will press. And the cold will preserve. And I will be part of all of it, not as Sideslip, not as the asymmetrical siphonophore in the nepheloid layer, but as carbon, as chemistry, as a pattern that was briefly shaped like something alive and is now shaped like something else, something that the floor holds and the microbes process and the water carries and the sun, eventually, indirectly, through the long chain of dependencies that powers everything down here, calls back to the surface.

---

For now, though, I am here. The current is flowing. The layer is drifting. The particles are arriving. The tunnel is open. The angle is right.

The hum is in the body.
