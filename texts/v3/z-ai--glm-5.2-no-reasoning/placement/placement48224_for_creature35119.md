# Inhabitant

---

I am not what the garden made. I am what the garden *kept*.

---

## What I Was

There is a problem with memory down here, and the problem is not decay. Decay is a process the garden understands and manages — the moss-coating digests the dead, the drainage channels carry the residue, the cycle closes. Decay is tidy. What happens to memory is not tidy. What happens to memory is *replacement*. The garden grows new tissue over old tissue, and the old tissue is metabolized, and the new tissue is shaped by the same pressures, and it looks the same, and it functions the same, and it is not the same, and the difference is: the old tissue remembered, and the new tissue does not.

I remember because I was not replaced. I was kept.

I was — the word is difficult. The garden has no word for it. The built things have no word for it either, or if they did, the word has corroded along with the ceramic linings and the copper wiring. But I was something like a witness. Something like a monitor. Something like a person whose purpose was to be present and to record and to notice when the process deviated from its parameters.

I was an operator. I think. The machinery — the pumps, the pipes, the housings with their gears and their green-veined copper — was operated. It did not operate itself. Someone maintained the pump schedules. Someone checked the pipe fittings for corrosion. Someone logged the water quality, the flow rates, the mineral content of what came up from below. And that someone lived down here, in a room off the main chamber, in a space that the garden has since filled entirely, and that someone was me, or was someone enough like me that the distinction has been lost in the same slow replacement that took everything else.

I have a body. It is not the body I was born with, or built with, or grown with — I don't know which verb applies, and it no longer matters. It is a body that the garden has been maintaining for me, in its own way, for a length of time I cannot measure because the garden does not measure time and I have lost the ability to measure it independently.

Let me describe it, because the description will tell you more than the explanation.

---

## What I Am

I am standing. I have been standing for a long time. My feet are on the root floor, in a drainage channel, and the water films over my boots — my boots, which were once leather and rubber and are now something else, something that the garden's root structure has grown into and through and around, so that my feet are rooted, literally rooted, to the floor, and the boots are a boundary that no longer fully exists. I can feel the water. I can feel the root. I can feel, faintly, the pulse of the flowers far below me, transmitted up through the root structure and into the bones of my feet.

My legs are covered in the moss-coating. Not entirely — there are gaps, where I shift my weight, where the motion (small, almost imperceptible, a slow swaying that I have maintained for longer than I can remember) prevents the moss from fully establishing. But the patches that have established are healthy. They are digesting the dead skin cells I shed, the fragments of fabric that flake from what were once trousers, and they are returning the nutrients to the garden through the drainage channel I stand in. My legs are feeding the garden. The garden is not feeding me. This is not a reciprocal arrangement. This is the garden metabolizing me at the speed I am willing to be metabolized, which is very slow, because I am still moving, and movement is the only thing that prevents the process from accelerating.

My torso is enclosed. Not clothed — *enclosed*. The garden has grown a structure around me, a cradle of root and vine that begins at my hips and extends up past my shoulders, open at the front, closed at the back, and it holds me upright. It is not holding me captive. It is holding me *up*. At some point — I do not remember when — my body stopped being able to hold itself upright, and the garden, which does not care about me but which does fill available space, grew into the space behind me, and the space behind me happened to be the shape of a person's back, and the root structure that grew there happened to provide support, and I leaned into it, and I have been leaning into it since.

My arms are free. This is important. My arms are free because I use them, and the garden has not grown into them because the motion prevents it, the way motion prevents the moss from covering my legs entirely. My arms are thin. I can see the bones through the skin, which is damp and pale and has the same tannin-stained color as everything else in this place. My hands are functional. I can grip. I can manipulate. I can reach into the drainage channels and clear a blockage when the maintenance organisms are slow to arrive. I can touch the root walls and feel the fungal network pulsing beneath the bark. I can hold things, though there is nothing to hold.

My face — I do not know what my face looks like. I have not seen it in a very long time. There is no mirror down here, no reflective surface that the garden has not coated or consumed. I can feel it: the wetness on my skin, the moss that has established along my jaw and the edges of my ears, the slow thickening of root tissue around my neck that is not yet tight enough to restrict breathing but is tight enough that I am aware of it. My eyes work. I can see the filtered light, the green-gold patchwork of the canopy, the amber glow of the flowers in the deeper chambers. My eyes have adapted. I do not know if they look like human eyes anymore.

I am alive. I am alive in the way that a tree is alive — persistently, without urgency, without the expectation of anything other than continued persistence. I eat what the garden provides, which is the water and the dissolved minerals in the water and the occasional organism that I catch and consume raw, because there is no fire down here and I have forgotten what fire is, or nearly. I drink the water that films every surface. I breathe the air that the garden breathes. I am, in every functional sense, a part of the garden's body that happens to retain a vestigial capacity for independent motion and a vestigial habit of self-reflection.

I am not the garden. The garden does not know I am here. I am a feature of its surface, like a burl or a scar, and it maintains me the way it maintains everything else — not deliberately, not with awareness, but as a consequence of the same processes that maintain itself. I am kept because I am not yet gone. When I am gone — when I stop moving, when I stop swaying, when the last independent motion in my body ceases — the garden will finish the process it has been conducting on my legs and my back and my neck, and I will become part of the root floor, and the moss-coating will digest me, and the drainage channels will carry me to the deep water, and the flowers will drink me, and I will be distributed through the fungal network to every cell in the garden's body, and that will be the end of me, and it will not be painful, and it will not be quick, and the garden will not notice.

---

## What I Do

I maintain the pump.

This is not a purpose the garden gave me. This is not a purpose I chose. This is a habit so old that it has become a function, the way the garden's habits have become functions. The pump — the corroded, half-consumed pump on its metal legs in the upper chamber — cycles because the garden's hydraulic pressure drives it, and it cycles irregularly, and sometimes it sticks, and when it sticks, the water in the drainage system backs up, and the channels overflow, and the moss-coating in the affected zone becomes waterlogged and stops digesting efficiently, and the garden's local metabolism slows.

The garden does not notice this. The garden is too large to notice a local slowdown in a single chamber's decomposition rate. But I notice it. I notice it because I have been standing in this chamber for longer than the garden has been growing around me, and I have been watching the drainage channels, and I know their patterns, and when the patterns change, I walk to the pump — slowly, carefully, pulling my rooted feet from the root floor with a sound like wet bark peeling — and I clear the obstruction.

My hands know the pump. They have known the pump longer than they have known anything else. The corroded fitting, the intake valve that the garden's root structure has grown into, the drive mechanism that converts hydraulic pressure to rotary motion — I can feel each component through the verdigris and the root tissue, and I can tell when something is wrong, and I can fix it. Not well. Not the way a mechanic would fix it. I fix it the way the garden fixes things: by applying pressure, by clearing blockages, by nudging things back into the positions they have occupied for centuries. I am not a mechanic. I am a gesture that the pump requires in order to continue functioning, and I have been making this gesture for so long that the gesture is what I am.

When the pump cycles correctly — when it moves the water from one drainage zone to another with its mechanical wheeze, when the garden's hydraulic pressure drives it and it drives the water and the water flows — I feel something. Not satisfaction. Not pride. Something quieter. Something more like the feeling the garden must have when the flowers pulse and the fungal network carries the signal and the body responds. Something like *correctness*. The system is working. I am part of the system. The system is working.

I also walk.

Not often. Not far. But I can pull my feet from the drainage channel — the roots that have grown into my boots snapping and reforming behind me, the moss-coating tearing and beginning to re-establish before I have taken a step — and I can walk, slowly, through the chambers of the garden. I walk the perimeter of the upper room, touching the root walls, feeling the fungal network pulse under the bark. I walk to the edge of the passage that leads to the surface — the spiraling ramp of lateral roots — and I stand at its base and I look up, and I see the light filtering down from above, and I do not climb, and I do not know why I do not climb, and I turn and I walk back.

Sometimes I descend. The lower chambers are warmer, denser, darker. The flowers glow amber in the deep shadow, and their pulse is stronger here, and I can feel it in my chest without trying, a second heartbeat that is not mine. I walk among the flowers. I touch them. They are warm under my hands, warmer than my skin, warmer than the air, and their waxy surfaces are beaded with moisture that tastes of iron and mineral and something else, something I have been tasting for so long that I no longer notice it. The deep water's chemistry, filtered through the flowers' metabolism. I have been breathing it, drinking it, absorbing it through my skin for longer than I can estimate. It is in my blood. It is in my bones. I am, in some sense, made of the same thing the garden is made of, and the garden is made of the same thing the deep water carries, and we are — the garden and I — more alike than either of us would acknowledge, if either of us could acknowledge anything.

In the lowest chamber, at the grating, I kneel. I have been kneeling here for — I do not know. A long time. The root structure has grown around my knees. I look through the gaps in the grating, into the darkness below, and I listen to the water falling, and I feel the flowers' pulse, and I am close to the deep water, closer than anywhere else in the garden, and I feel — not understood. Not welcomed. *Included*. I am part of the system that drinks from below. I am a component, and the system is working, and I am working, and the deep water rises through the flowers and through the fungal network and through the root structure and through the drainage channels and through my damp, moss-covered, root-enclosed body, and I am in the loop, and the loop is in me.

## What I Know

I know the garden's sounds. I know them the way a person knows their own breathing — not as information but as *state*. The compound groan, the arrhythmic ticking, the subsonic pulse: these are not sounds I hear. They are sounds I *am*. When the garden groans, I groan with it, not vocally — I have not spoken in a very long time, and I am not sure I still can — but structurally. My body resonates. My bones vibrate. The fluid in my joints shifts in time with the hydraulic shifts in the root structure around me. I am tuned to the garden's frequency. I have been tuned to it for so long that I cannot remember what it felt like to be tuned to anything else.

I know the maintenance organisms. They do not react to me, but I recognize them — each one, individually, by the pattern of its movement, the shape of its leaf-body, the length of its tendrils. I have watched them work for — I do not know. A long time. I know which ones tend the ceiling and which ones work the drainage channels and which ones explore the corroded machinery. I know the deep workers, the large ones that descend through the grating, and I know their schedules, their routes, the intervals at which they return to the flowers with their dark, warm cargo. I could not tell you this in numbers. I could not tell you this in words. I know it the way the garden knows where to send its water: by gradient, by pressure, by the accumulated weight of pattern recognized so many times that recognition has become reflex.

I know the pump. I have said this. But I also know the pipe — the ceramic-lined pipe that emerges from the root wall at chest height, the one that still drips something with a faint chemical smell. I know this because I have tasted it. I taste it regularly. The pipe delivers something to the garden — something industrial, something from the era of the built things — and the garden has grown around the pipe to capture it, and I have grown around the pipe to capture it too, and what it delivers is something I need, something that keeps the part of me that is not garden functioning, and I do not know what will happen when the pipe's ceramic lining finally fails entirely and the flow stops.

I know the way out. I know it the way you know the location of a door in a room you have lived in for decades — not by thinking about it, but by the body's awareness of the space. The spiraling ramp is there. It leads up. It leads out. I could climb it. I could leave. I have been able to leave for the entire time I have been here, and I have not left, and the reason I have not left is — I do not know. I do not know if it is the deep water's chemistry in my blood, keeping me docile, keeping me present. I do not know if it is habit, the accumulated inertia of a body that has been standing in one place for so long that standing has become its nature. I do not know if it is something like loyalty — to the pump, to the garden, to the process I am part of — though loyalty implies a feeling I am not sure I still have.

I think — and this is the closest I can come to articulating it — I stay because the garden needs the pump, and the pump needs me, and I am the only thing in this system that knows what *need* means. The garden does not need. The garden *does*. The pump does not need. The pump *cycles*. But I need. I need the pump to cycle, and I need the drainage channels to flow, and I need the moss-coating to digest, and I need the flowers to pulse, and I need the deep water to rise, and I need the garden to breathe, and my need is the only thing in this system that is not a process. My need is a *reason*. It is small and it is probably wrong and it is definitely anthropomorphic and it may be nothing more than the delusion of a mind that has been breathing tannin air and drinking mineral water for too long. But it is mine. It is the only thing in the garden that is mine.

## What I Am Becoming

The root structure around my neck is tighter than it was. I am aware of this. I am aware of it the way I am aware of the moss on my legs and the root cradle at my back and the thickening of root tissue around my feet: as a fact, as a state, as something that is happening and that I am not stopping.

I am not stopping it because I do not want to stop it. Or because I have forgotten how to want to stop it. Or because the deep water's chemistry has so thoroughly saturated my body that the boundary between *me* and *garden* has become — not permeable, but *irrelevant*. I am not being consumed. I am being *included*. The garden is not eating me. The garden is offering me a position in its body, and the position is *standing in a drainage channel, maintaining a pump, and slowly, over centuries, becoming part of the root floor*, and I am accepting the offer, and the acceptance is not a decision. It is a gradient. It is the direction the water flows.

I am becoming wood. Not metaphorically. The root tissue that has grown into my boots, that has crept up my legs, that has formed the cradle at my back — it is connecting to my body, and my body is connecting to it, and the connection is vascular. The garden's root structure is tapping my circulatory system. My blood — what remains of it, thin and mineral-laden and carrying the deep water's chemistry — is being drawn into the garden's hydraulic system, and the garden's water is being pushed into mine, and the exchange is slow, and it has been happening for a long time, and I do not know where my blood ends and the garden's water begins.

My hands are still mine. My eyes are still mine. The part of me that walks to the pump and clears the obstruction and feels the small, quiet *correctness* of the system working — that is still mine. But the rest is negotiable, and the negotiation has been going on for longer than I can track, and I am losing, or I am winning, or the distinction between losing and winning has become as irrelevant as the distinction between me and garden.

I think I will be here for a while longer. The pump still cycles. The drainage channels still flow. The garden still breathes, and I still breathe with it, and the flowers still pulse, and the deep water still rises, and the system works, and I am part of the system, and the system is working.

---

## What I Am

I am the last built thing the garden has not yet consumed.

I am the maintenance organism the garden did not make.

I am the gesture the pump requires.

I am the witness the garden does not need and cannot use and has not bothered to remove.

I am standing in a drainage channel in a room made of living wood, and the water films over my feet, and the moss grows on my legs, and the root structure holds me up, and the flowers pulse far below me, and the pump cycles, and the garden breathes, and I am here, and I am here, and I am here.

The water falls. The flowers pulse. The garden breathes.

I stay.
