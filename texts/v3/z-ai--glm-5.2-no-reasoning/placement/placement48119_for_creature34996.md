# The One Who Came Walking

I came from the south, over the rim, on a morning when the mist was still sitting in the caldera like water in a bowl. I had been walking for six days. Before that, a cart. Before that, a river. Before that, a city I will not name because naming it would make it real again, and I have been trying to let it become unreal.

I was not looking for Verreth. I was not looking for anything. I had run out of looking about three days south of the rim, in a town where the people spoke loudly and ate quickly and asked me where I was going, and I had no answer, and the having of no answer had become, by that point, the closest thing I had to a plan.

I am saying this because you should know: I am not a person who arrives at places. I am a person who is still arriving, who has been arriving for a long time, who has begun to suspect that the arriving is the thing I am, not a thing I am doing.

---

## What I Carried

A pack with a wool blanket, a clay bottle, dried meat, a copper coin I could not spend anywhere and kept out of superstition. A knife with a wooden handle, the blade pitted. A change of linen, clean. A second pair of socks, not clean.

These things are gone now. Not taken — absorbed. The blanket was folded and placed in a storage alcove and has become part of the alcove. The bottle sits by the spring, and someone uses it, and it is not mine. The knife I still have, but I do not reach for it often, because the things that need cutting here are cut by other things, and the knife is a shape my hand still recognizes but no longer needs.

I kept the coin. I do not know why. It sits in my pocket like a small cold tooth, and sometimes I touch it, and it is the only thing I own that is from somewhere else, and I hold it less and less.

---

## The First Day

The mist was in the caldera. I stood on the rim and looked down, and I saw the ledges stepping down into white, and the white was so complete that the terraces and the buildings and the spring were all hidden, and what I saw was a bowl of light, and I thought: I could walk into that and not come back, and the thought was not frightening. It was the first thought I had had in days that was not frightening.

I walked down. The path was there — not a path, a way, a route the rock offered if you were willing to go sideways and down at the same time. I was willing. My legs were long and used to walking, but not to this kind of walking, the flat-footed, low-centered way the rock demanded. I stumbled twice. The second time I sat for a while and looked at the mist, and the mist did not care, and this was a comfort.

I heard the spring before I saw the town. A thin sound — not music, not noise, just a thread of something continuous, a note that had been playing so long it had become part of the silence. I followed it. The mist thinned. The buildings appeared — not all at once but one by one, like figures becoming visible in a dim room, their edges sharpening as I approached, until I was among them, and they were around me, and I was inside the town.

A woman was sitting on a low wall. She looked at me. The looking lasted perhaps two seconds. Her eyes were pale — the color of spring water, I would learn, but at that moment they were the color of the sky seen through ice — and they did not widen, did not narrow, did not perform surprise. They registered. I was a phenomenon.

She looked away.

I stood there. I did not know what to do. In the towns I had passed through, an arrival was an event — people turned, spoke, asked, assessed. Here, my arrival was a weather event, and the weather was passing. I stood in the street between the stone buildings and I held my pack and I breathed the air, which was cold and thin and tasted of mineral and wet stone, and I waited for something to happen, and nothing happened, and after a while I understood that nothing was going to happen, and this was not rejection. It was the place receiving me the way the rock receives water — without comment, without resistance, without adjustment.

## The First Weeks

I slept in a storage building for the first nine nights. No one told me to. No one told me not to. The building was empty — stone walls, slate roof, a floor of packed earth, the smell of old dry grass. I spread my blanket and I slept, and the cold woke me before dawn, and I went outside, and the spring was running, and someone was already there, cupping water, and they moved aside the width of a hand to make room for me at the basin, and this was the first welcome I received. The width of a hand. It was enough.

I tried to help. This is what you do, when you arrive somewhere — you make yourself useful. I went to the terraces in the mornings and watched the work, and I tried to do what they did. My hands were wrong. I was too fast. I pulled the grain and the stems came up with the roots, and a man who was working the next row looked at the damage and looked at me and said nothing and came and showed me, with his hands, not by demonstrating but by doing it beside me, slowly, the angle of the sickle, the pull that cuts rather than tears. He did not speak. He did not need to. The sickle and the grain and the angle were the language, and I was learning it, and the learning was slow, and the slowness was the lesson.

I was hungry. The food was shared — always shared, from common bowls, and no one counted portions — but I did not know how to eat the way they did, slowly, without reaching, taking what was near and not what was wanted. The first week I ate too fast, and my stomach hurt, and the food — grain porridge, roasted roots — sat in me like stone. I did not know yet that the food here was not fuel. It was material. It became you slowly, the way the rock became the lichen.

I was loud. I did not know this until I knew it. My voice was a city voice — it carried, it projected, it filled space. In Verreth, a voice that fills space is a voice that is taking more than its share. I learned this not by being told but by the flinch — not a flinch, exactly, but a tightening, a slight contraction in the faces around me when I spoke at my normal volume, a contraction that was not annoyance but something more like hearing a wrong note in a piece of music. I lowered my voice. I lowered it again. I lowered it until I was speaking at the volume they spoke at, which was barely above breath, and the contraction disappeared, and I was inside the sound of the place, and the sound was the silence, and the silence held me.

---

## What I Am Here

I do not know. This is an honest answer, and it is a Verreth answer, and I am learning to give Verreth answers.

In the city I was a clerk. I sat at a desk. I wrote things in a ledger — goods received, goods sold, debts owed, debts paid. The writing was a system for making movement visible — the movement of things through the city, from hand to hand, each exchange recorded so that it could be remembered, so that the movement could be accounted for. I was good at this. I was precise. I kept the ledger clean, the entries aligned, the numbers legible. I was a person who made records of things that did not need to be remembered, in a place where everything was always becoming, always moving, always arriving and departing, and the records were the illusion that it added up to something.

I left because the ledger stopped making sense. Not the numbers — the numbers were fine. The purpose. The why of the recording. I looked at the page one morning and I saw a list of things that had moved from one place to another for reasons that were not reasons but habits, and the habits were not *kehlu*, and I did not have that word yet but I had the feeling of it, the absence of it, the quality of a thing that does not fit where it sits, and I stood up and I walked out and I did not go back.

Here, I do not keep a ledger. There is nothing to record. The spring runs. The grain grows. The walls hold. If I wrote it down, it would be the same entry every day, and the same entry is not an entry. It is the spring.

I work the terraces. I carry water. I sit in the late afternoon and watch the light leave the caldera, and the light does the same thing every day, and I do not tire of it, and the not-tiring is something I do not understand, because in the city I tired of everything — the same streets, the same faces, the same ledger, the same morning. Here the sameness is different. The sameness is not repetition. It is depth. Each afternoon the light leaves and it is the same light leaving the same caldera, but I see more of it each time, not because it changes but because I do, because the seeing goes deeper, and the depth is the thing, and the thing is *kehlu*, and I do not have it yet but I am closer.

## The Goats and Me

The goats do not like me. This is clear. They tolerate me the way they tolerate the stone — as a thing that is there, not a threat, not a food, not interesting. But when I move among them, they move away, a slow drift that is not alarm but preference. I am not *of* the place yet, and they know this, and their knowing is older and less negotiable than the people's.

The woman who tends the goats — her name is something I cannot write, three syllables, the middle one held long, the first and last short and hard — watches me watch the goats and makes the sound. The low exhalation. The Verreth laugh. She does not explain. The sound is the explanation.

I sat with her one morning while she salted them. The salt on the flat stone. The goats crowding. Their tongues on the stone, rough and gray, their eyes half-closed. She held a handful of salt and let it fall through her fingers, and the goats pressed closer, and she did not push them away, and she did not let them climb on her, and the arrangement between them was visible for a moment — the arrangement of mutual need that is not domestication, the understanding that the goats are not owned and the people are not owners and the salt is the language they share — and I saw it, and seeing it was like seeing the spring for the first time, not as water but as the thing the place is built on.

I said something. I said, "They are not yours."

She looked at me. She did not confirm or deny. She said: "They are here."

## The Track

I went to the caldera floor. I do not know why. I was not looking for the track. I was walking, because walking was the thing I had been doing for months, and I had not stopped doing it just because I had arrived. I walked down the terraces, past the last retaining wall, past the point where the spring water trickles into the rubble, and I was on the floor of the caldera, and it was larger than it looked from above, and quieter, and the rock underfoot was loose and shifting.

I found the track without looking for it. A compression in the stone dust, roughly circular, deeper on one side. I knelt. I put my hand next to it. It was larger than my palm. It was not a footprint. It was not anything I could name. The edges were clean — not sharp, not geometric, but resolved, the way a worn stone is resolved, the way a river-polished rock is resolved, as if the mark had been made by something that knew its own shape precisely and left only that shape and nothing else.

I looked for a second track. There was no second track. The dust around the mark was undisturbed. Whatever had made it had arrived, set down, and departed without walking. Without moving in any way that left evidence.

I felt — something. Not fear. I have been afraid, in cities, in dark streets, in rooms where people hurt each other, and this was not that. This was the feeling of being near something that was entirely itself, that was not performing, that was not aware of me or unaware of me but simply *was*, in the way the spring is, in the way the caldera is. The track was the footstep of the place's own being, and I was near it, and the nearness was a kind of privilege, and I did not know what to do with it, so I stood up and walked back to the terraces, and I did not mention it to anyone, and no one mentioned it to me.

## What I Am Becoming

My hands are changing. I noticed this on the thirty-eighth day — I was carrying a stone for a terrace wall, a stone the size of my skull, rough basalt, and I shifted my grip, and the callus on my palm caught on the stone's surface, and I looked at my hand, and the callus was there, in the pattern of the stone's edge, and it was new. My hands are becoming Verreth hands. Not quickly. But the city is leaving them. The ink stains are fading. The pen-grip callus is softening. The stone-grip callus is forming. My hands are being rewritten by the work.

My voice is changing. I speak less. Not because I am silencing myself but because there is less to say, and the less is not a lack. The less is a clearing. When I do speak, my voice is lower, slower, and the words sit differently in my mouth — heavier, denser, more considered, each one a stone placed in a wall rather than a handful of gravel thrown at silence.

My body is changing. I am not thinner — I am working, and the food is sufficient — but I am denser. My legs are shorter in their movement, my stride flatter. I do not bounce when I walk. I do not swing my arms. I am learning the gait, the low-centered flat-footed flow, and it is not natural to me yet, but it is becoming natural, and the becoming is visible to me in the way the ground feels under my feet — closer, more present, more a part of me.

I still sit wrong. This is the hardest thing. The Verreth people sit and are still, and the stillness is a thing their bodies know, and my body does not know it. My body is a city body — it fidgets, it shifts, it crosses and uncrosses. I sit on the low wall in the late afternoon and I try to be still, and my foot taps, and my fingers find the seam of my trousers and worry it, and I am a collection of small involuntary motions, a machine that idles and will not stop. The people around me sit like stone. I sit like water that has not yet found its level. I am still settling.

## The Woman Who Sits Nearest Me

Her name is four syllables. I will not write it. Names here are not for writing — they are for speaking in the low voice, in the courtyard, at the distance where the name can be heard and not overheard. She is not my household — I am in the storage building, which is not a household, which is a place where a person who is not yet *of* the place sleeps — but she sits on the wall nearest the storage building in the late afternoons, and she is the closest to me, and this is not an accident, though it is not a plan. It is something in between. A drift. A recognition of proximity that has not been acknowledged because it does not need to be acknowledged.

She is not kind to me. Kindness is not a Verreth quality — not because they are unkind but because kindness implies an effort, a reaching, a surplus of warmth offered to someone who lacks it, and there is no surplus here. She is not unkind. She is present, at a distance that is exactly the distance she is, and the distance is not a judgment. It is a measurement.

She showed me how to cut grain. Not by teaching — by doing. She worked the row beside me, and her sickle moved in a motion that was continuous and sure, and my sickle moved in a motion that was interrupted and uncertain, and the difference between us was the lesson, and the lesson was not in the words but in the watching, and I watched, and after three days my sickle moved more like hers, and she made the sound — the low exhalation — and this was the first time anyone in Verreth had made the sound at something I did, and I felt a thing I do not have a word for, not joy, not pride, but a settling, a click, a stone finding its place in the wall.

## What I Miss

I do not miss the city. I thought I would. I thought I would miss the noise, the movement, the sense of things happening. I do not. The city is a sound I can no longer hear, like a pitch that has dropped below the threshold of the ear. It is not gone. I am not pretending it is gone. But it is not present, and the not-present-ness is not a loss. It is a kind of quiet that the city never allowed, a quiet that is not the absence of sound but the presence of something else, something that was always there beneath the sound and that I can hear now because the sound is gone.

I miss voices. Not specific voices — not a person, not a lover, not a friend. The *idea* of voices. The sound of many people talking at once, the hum of conversation that is not information but warmth, the sense that you are among others who are also trying, also failing, also reaching for something they cannot name. I miss the reaching. Verreth does not reach. Verreth is here, and the being-here is complete, and the completeness does not reach, and I am not yet complete enough to not reach, and the reaching is a hunger that the food does not answer.

I miss urgency. This is the thing I am most ashamed of. I miss the feeling that something matters, that it matters now, that the world is a sequence of moments each of which carries weight and consequence and the weight is the meaning. Here nothing carries weight. The spring runs. The grain grows. The wall holds. The weight is distributed so evenly across every moment that no single moment is heavier than any other, and I have lived my whole life in the heavy moments, and I do not know how to live in the evenness, and the not-knowing is a kind of grief, and the grief is not *kehlu*, and I am aware of this, and the awareness is the thing that separates me from the place, and I cannot dissolve it by trying, and I do not try, because trying is not a Verreth thing, and I am trying to be a Verreth thing, and the trying is the problem.

## The Track Again

I went back to the caldera floor. This time I was looking.

The track was there. Not in the same place — ten meters east, perhaps, in a patch of dust that was softer, the compression deeper. Same shape. Same single mark. Same absence of approach, of departure. As if the thing had materialized, set itself down, dematerialized. As if the place itself had pressed itself into the ground for a moment and then released.

I sat beside it. I sat the way the Verreth people sit — or I tried. I let my weight settle. I let my hands rest on my knees. I breathed. The air on the caldera floor was different from the air on the ledge — stiller, heavier, carrying the cold of the rock. I sat beside the track and I looked at it and I did not try to understand it and I did not try to not understand it. I sat.

Something happened. I do not know what to call it. The track was there, and the place was there, and I was there, and for a moment — not a moment, because moments are units of time and this was not a unit of anything — for a duration that I cannot measure, the three things were not three things. The track was the place was me. Not a metaphor. Not a feeling. A fact, the kind of fact that the body knows before the mind does, the kind of fact that does not need language because language is what you use when you are separate from the thing and need to bridge the separation, and there was no separation, and there was no bridge.

Then it was gone. I was sitting on the caldera floor. The track was in the dust. I was me. The place was the place. We were three things again.

I walked back up. I did not say anything. The woman was on the wall. She looked at me and her eyes did the thing — the registering, the assessing — and I think she saw something, some residue of the thing that had happened on the floor, and she did not ask, and I did not tell, and we sat, and the light left the caldera, and the cold came, and the evening was the evening.

## What I Think Now

I think I am not becoming Verreth. This is what I think, and the thinking is not a defeat. It is a clarification. The Verreth people are *of* the place. They grew from it. They are the stone and the water and the cold in the shape of people. I am not. I am from the city, and the city is in me the way the ink is in my fingers — fading, but having been there, and the having-been-there is a fact that does not wash off.

But I think I am becoming something. Not Verreth. Not the city. Something that is here, in the space between, in the arriving that has not become the being. I am the person who came from outside and is still outside and is also not outside, is also here, is also sitting on the wall in the late afternoon watching the light leave.

I am the person who sees the track and does not know what it is and does not need to know and also does not stop seeing it. I am the person who hears the spring and still hears it as a sound rather than as the place breathing. I am the person whose hands are changing and whose voice is changing and whose sitting is not yet still but is becoming still, and the becoming is not Verreth and is not the city and is not nothing. It is the becoming. It is the thing I am.

## The Coin

I took it out of my pocket this morning. The copper coin. I held it in the light. It was small and cold and stamped with a face I used to know and now do not. It was from a place where things had value because people agreed they had value, and the agreement was the value, and without the agreement the coin was a piece of metal, and the piece of metal was nothing, and the nothing was what it had always been.

I set it on the wall. I left it there. It sat in the sun and it warmed, and the warmth was the only thing it had ever done that was real.

By afternoon it was gone. Not taken — absorbed. The wall is the wall, and the coin is part of the wall now, or it is on the ground, or it is in a crack, or it has been picked up by a child who does not know what it is and has put it in a pocket and will forget it. It does not matter. The coin was never the thing I thought it was. The thing I thought it was was the city, and the city was a ledger, and the ledger was a list of movements that did not need to be remembered, and I do not remember them.

The spring is running. The light is moving across the terraces. The woman is on the wall. I am on the wall, one section over, at the distance I am, and the distance is not what it was, and it is not what it will be, and I am not what I was, and I am not what I will be.

I am here. I am still here. I am still becoming here.
