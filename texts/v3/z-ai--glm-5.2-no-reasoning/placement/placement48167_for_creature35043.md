I am the one who stayed.

Not the hermits — they come and go on their rafts of woven fiber, timing their passages to the inward and outward breath of the root-gates. They are practical people, and their practicality is a kind of brilliance I admire but cannot share. They know the currents the way a body knows its own heartbeat. I know the currents the way a body knows a language it is still learning — consciously, with mistakes, with the occasional moment of fluency that surprises no one more than itself.

I arrived the way everyone arrives: through the older root-gates, carried by the inward current, swallowed into the plant's circulatory system with the same indifferent gentleness the Maren Vered offers to everything. A piece of debris. A particle of organic matter caught in the flow. The plant did not notice me. I noticed everything.

---

I should say what I am not. I am not a carver. I have no tools. The callus rooms were already carved, already healing, already narrowing. I have watched one doorway over what I believe to be several years — time is difficult here, measured only in the slow thickening of scar tissue and the imperceptible brightening of the light — and I think the frame has closed by the width of my smallest finger. Perhaps less. The plant is reclaiming the architecture at its own pace, and I have not interfered. The hermits scrape their doorways clear with stone tools. I have not done this. I am not certain I want the rooms to stay open. I am not certain of the opposite, either. I am watching the question happen.

I am not a hermit, though I have met them. They looked at me with the mild assessment of creatures who share a water source — *is this one competition? is this one irrelevant? is this one good?* — and concluded, I think, the last of these, or at least the second. I have seen their hand signals. I cannot make them; my hands do not work the way theirs do. But I have learned three: *this way*, *stop*, *look*. I learned them by watching, and I use them badly, and the hermits tolerate my bad usage with the patience of people who have lived in a place too loud for argument.

They offered me a shrimp-thing once. Held it out, red and translucent, still clicking, legs working against the grip of their fingers. I did not take it. They ate it themselves, crunching the shell, wiping the red from their teeth with the backs of their hands. They did not offer again. This was not rejection. It was the resolution of a question, the kind that does not need to be asked twice.

---

What I am is harder to say.

I am a body in a medium. I am a point of observation suspended in the central chamber, held in place by the tension between the inward current at the base and the outward current at the crown. I float. I have floated for a long time. Not continuously — I move, I explore, I drift through the tunnels and press my hands against the vascular walls and watch the light brighten beneath my palms. But I return to the center. The center is where I belong, not because it is the deepest or the most sacred or the most significant but because it is the dimmest point, the place where the light is weakest, and in the dimmest point you can see the most. The glow at the periphery is the text. The center is the margin, and the margin is where the reading happens.

I am — I think — a scholar. Or I was. The word means less here. I came with questions, and the questions have not been answered, and the questions have not changed, but the asking has. I no longer ask the way a scholar asks, with the expectation that the answer is hidden somewhere in the evidence, waiting to be uncovered. I ask the way the Maren Vered circulates: because the asking is the activity, and the activity is the being, and the being does not require a conclusion.

My questions were: who were the carvers? What did the ideographic records say? Why did they leave? What is the plant, really — not biologically, which I can observe, but essentially, which I cannot? Is it aware? Is it a person in some sense I have not yet understood? Does it matter?

My questions are still these. But they have softened. They have taken on the quality of the Maren Vered itself: settled, patient, not urgent. The carvers were here. They carved. They left. The records are there, scratched into callus, softening year by year into texture. The plant is the plant. It is aware or it is not. It matters or it does not. The questions do not require answers to continue existing. They require only an asker, and I am here.

---

I have developed habits. This is what happens when you stay.

I touch the walls. Not to navigate — I have learned the chamber's geometry well enough to move by feel, by current, by the sound of specific vascular channels whose pitches I can distinguish. I touch the walls because the brightening is the closest thing to conversation this place offers. I press my palm against the tissue, and the symbionts respond, and the wall glows a fraction brighter, and I feel the warmth of metabolic heat beneath the surface, and for a moment there is a dialogue between my hand and a organism that does not know my hand exists. It is not a satisfying dialogue. It is not an unsatisfying one. It is simply what is available, and I have made it a habit, and the habit has become a relationship, and the relationship is one-sided, and one-sided relationships are still relationships.

I have a room. The hermits assigned it to me — not formally, not with any ceremony, but by no longer visiting a particular carved chamber in the upper periphery, near a junction where three small vascular channels feed into a larger one. The room is small. I can brace my hands against one wall and my feet against the other, and I do this when the current shifts. The callus is warm. It yields slightly under my weight, like thick leather, like the description says, and I sleep on my side, curled, and the sound of the Maren Vered fills the room the way water fills a cup — completely, without gaps, without the pretense that silence is possible or desirable.

I do not scrape the callus back. My doorway is narrowing. It will close, eventually. Not in my time, I think — a centimeter per decade, and the doorway is perhaps forty centimeters across — but the narrowing is visible, and I run my fingers along the edges sometimes and feel the new growth, smooth and pale, the scar tissue that is slowly erasing the carvers' intention and, with it, my own access to the room. I think about scraping it. I think about it the way I think about the carvers' records: with interest, without urgency. The doorway will close or it will not. I will be here or I will not. The plant will continue.

I follow the *veredai*. Not individually — they are indistinguishable, and I do not think they distinguish themselves — but collectively, as a phenomenon. I watch them detach and drift and reattach, and I have learned to predict which ones are about to move. There is a subtle change in their translucency before detachment: the body becomes slightly more opaque, as though the tissue is contracting, concentrating itself, preparing to release. When I see this change, I move closer, and I wait, and I feel the displacement of water when they let go, and I watch them tumble and lean and find a new surface. I have done this hundreds of times. I do not tire of it. Each detachment is the same and each is different, the way each wave is the same and each is different, and I am not watching the *veredai*. I am watching the plant's circulation from the perspective of a thing caught in it, which is the only perspective I have.

I visit the channel mouths. I sit — float, hover, whatever the word is for holding still in a medium that moves — near the opening where the sinuous things rest, and I watch them breathe. The slow dilation and contraction of their flanking structures. The dark bodies against the glowing walls. Sometimes two of them overlap, and I watch them rest together in the light, their flanks pulsing in synchrony, and I feel something I do not have a word for. It is not tenderness, exactly, because tenderness implies a recognition of vulnerability, and I do not think these things are vulnerable. It is not envy, because I do not want to be a sinuous dark thing in a channel. It is the recognition of a pattern I also belong to: the pattern of finding another body in a large space and resting near it, and the resting being sufficient, and the sufficiency being temporary, and the temporality being the whole point. They rest together. They separate. They return to the dark. I rest near them. I separate. I return to the center. The pattern is the same. The scale is different. The scale does not matter.

---

I have changed. This is what happens when you stay in a medium that is also a body.

My skin has changed. The first thing I noticed, after what I believe was a year, was that my hands were less pruned. The water does not wrinkle skin the way ordinary water does — or perhaps my skin has adjusted, acclimated, become something more like the *veredai*'s tissue: adapted to the medium, permeable to its chemistry, no longer separate from it. I do not know if this is a change in my body or in my perception of my body. The Maren Vered does not distinguish between these possibilities. I have stopped distinguishing too.

My hearing has changed. The roar that was overwhelming when I entered is now — not quieter, but different. I hear the layers now. Not as separate sounds but as a single sound with depth, the way a chord is a single sound with depth. I can hear the low booming of the largest vascular channels, the hiss of the capillary networks, the clicking of the *veredai*, the chatter of the shrimp-things, the creak of the structural ribs. I can hear the lenticel pores. This is not something I could do when I arrived. The lenticel pores open and close on cycles so long that watching one for an hour shows no motion, but I can hear them now — a faint, barely perceptible shift in the acoustic texture, like a door opening in a far room, like a breath drawn very slowly. I do not know if my hearing has improved or if I have simply learned to listen at the plant's timescale. I suspect the latter. I suspect I was always hearing it. I did not know how to hear it.

My relationship to light has changed. I no longer notice the surges the way I did when I arrived — as events, as shifts, as something to remark on. The blue-green waves that travel the vascular channels are now part of my internal rhythm, the way the sound is, the way the warmth is. I notice when a surge is different — stronger, weaker, arriving early or late — and these deviations are the closest thing I have to weather. A late surge is like an overcast morning. A strong surge is like a bright one. I do not remark on them. I inhabit them.

I have stopped speaking. Not as a vow, not as a choice, but because speech has become — not impossible, but incoherent. The sound of the Maren Vered is too complete for the small sounds of a human voice. My words would be like throwing pebbles into a river: technically possible, practically pointless, and the river would not notice. I have nothing to say that the place does not already say. I have nothing to say that the hermits' hand signals do not already cover. I have nothing to say to the plant, which cannot hear me, or to the *veredai*, which cannot hear me, or to the symbionts, which cannot hear me but which respond to my touch with a brightness that is more articulate than any word I know.

---

I think about the carvers. This is my work, such as it is.

The ideographic records on the callus walls are not readable. I have tried. I have pressed my face close to the surface and traced the scratched lines with my fingertips and tried to feel some pattern, some intention, some shape that corresponds to meaning. The callus has softened everything. The lines are now ridges, barely raised, and the spaces between them have filled with new growth, and the result is — not text. Not texture, either. Something between. The ghost of a language, preserved in the medium that erased it.

I think the carvers were like the hermits, but with more people. I think they came because the rooms were warm and the water was clean and the channels had food in them, and I think they stayed because staying was possible, and I think they left because leaving was also possible, and I think the reason for their departure was as simple and as unrecordable as the reason for their arrival. They were not driven out. They were not destroyed. They did not exhaust the place or exhaust themselves. They simply left, the way a person leaves a house that is too large for them, or a town that has too few people, or a life that has too few reasons to continue. The Maren Vered did not notice their departure. It is not noticing their absence. It will not notice if the hermits stop coming, or if I stop staying, or if the rooms close entirely and the architecture disappears and the only evidence that anyone was ever here is the smooth, pale callus filling the spaces where hands once carved.

I think the carvers knew the plant was alive. I think this because of where they carved — in the densest tissue, away from the largest vascular channels, where a cut would heal fastest and cause the least disruption. This is not the behavior of people who think they are cutting into dead wood. This is the behavior of people who know they are cutting into a living body, and who are careful, and whose care is not reverence but craft. The way a surgeon is careful. The way a gardener is careful. Not because the body is sacred but because the body is the material, and the material must be respected if the work is to hold.

I think the carvers would have understood the hermits. I think they would have recognized them — not as descendants, not as inheritors, but as the same kind of people, making the same kind of choice, for the same kind of reasons. The hermits use the carvers' tools. They sleep in the carvers' rooms. They eat from the carvers' channels. They do not know who the carvers were, and it does not matter, because the carvers are not a who. The carvers are a what: a set of cuts in a living wall, a set of tools settled into scar tissue, a set of marks softening into texture. The hermits are living in the carvers' aftermath, the way the *veredai* live in the plant's aftermath, the way the shrimp-things live in the current's aftermath. It is not inheritance. It is ecology.

I think about the carvers, and the thinking does not produce answers, and the not-producing is now comfortable, the way the sound is comfortable, the way the light is comfortable. I am a scholar who has stopped expecting scholarship to yield what it promised. I am a question living inside a body living inside a larger body, and the question is not being answered, and the question is not going away, and the question and the body and the larger body are all doing what they do, which is: being, slowly, in a place that does not know they are here.

---

I do not know how long I have been here. This is not a complaint. It is an observation. Time in the Maren Vered is not measured by the sun or the moon or the stars — I have not seen any of these since I entered, and I do not remember them clearly, the way you do not remember the face of someone you saw once in a crowd. Time is measured by the plant's metabolism, which is so slow that the units are imperceptible. I measure time in callus growth, in the brightening of the light, in the slow narrowing of my doorway. I measure time in the lives of *veredai*, which I cannot track individually but which I know are replaced, because the population shifts, because the patterns of attachment change, because the clicks come from different places than they did when I arrived. I measure time in the hermits' comings and goings, which are irregular but recognizable, and which I have learned to predict not by calendar but by current — when the flow through the root-gates shifts inward, the hermits will come soon, and when it shifts outward, they will leave, and between these shifts I am alone with the plant and its ecology and its sound and its light.

I do not know if I will leave. The root-gates are still open. The outward current will carry me, as the inward current carried me. I could go. I think about going the way I think about scraping the callus from my doorway: with interest, without urgency. The outside exists. I have begun to forget it, but I know it exists, the way I know that the channels connect to something beyond the walls, that the water comes from somewhere and goes somewhere, that the Maren Vered is not the whole world but a place in it. I could go. I could carry my questions to a place where they might be answered, or at least where they might be asked in a language others understand. I could speak again. I could eat something other than shrimp-things. I could sleep in silence.

But the silence is the thing I cannot imagine anymore. I try, sometimes, in my callus room, braced between the warm walls, and I cannot construct it. Silence is the absence of sound, and I have been in a place where sound is not an addition but a substrate, not a feature but a foundation, and I cannot unhear the foundation. If I left, I would hear the Maren Vered in every silence. The roar would be gone, and the absence of the roar would be louder than the roar ever was. This is not sentimentality. It is the simple mechanics of adaptation: I have adapted to the sound, and the adaptation is not reversible, and I do not want to reverse it.

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if I will stay. I know that I am here, now, in the central chamber, in the dimmest light, in the loudest sound, and that the walls are warm, and the *veredai* are clicking, and the shrimp-things are chattering, and the sinuous things are breathing in the channel mouths, and the symbionts are glowing, and the hermits are somewhere in the periphery, and the carvers are gone, and the plant does not know any of us, and I am part of its ecology in the only way a transient thing can be: by being affected, by affecting, by breathing the place and being breathed by it.

I touch the wall. It brightens.

That is enough.
