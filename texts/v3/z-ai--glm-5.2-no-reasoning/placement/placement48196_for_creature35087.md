# The Account

---

I arrived with a light.

That is the first fact and the first shame, and I want to hold both of them at once because the place deserves the honesty of holding both. I came with a light because I am a thing that expects darkness to be a problem, and I am a thing that expects problems to have solutions, and I am a thing that arrives at thresholds carrying solutions. The light was a cylinder the length of my forearm, sealed against pressure, bright enough to cut sixty meters through open water. I held it in front of me the way you hold a hand out in an unfamiliar room, and the room — if this can be called a room — recoiled from it. Not literally. Nothing moved. But I understood, immediately, in a way that bypassed thought and went straight to the gut, that I was showing the place something it had no use for.

I turned it off within the first minute. I want to say this was a choice. It was more like a reflex. The light was revealing a fragment of the place — a section of wall, grey-green, coated in growth so dense it looked like fur, extending from the floor to some height I couldn't measure because the light didn't reach the top — and the fragment was beautiful and the fragment was wrong, wrong in the way that reading one page of a book is wrong, not because the page is false but because the page is incomplete and the incompleteness is a kind of lie. I could feel the rest of the place around me. I could feel it the way you feel a room is large even in the dark, by the quality of the nothing that reaches you. And the nothing was so much bigger than the something the light could show that the light became an obscenity. A reduction. An act of violence committed against a place by forcing it to be smaller than it was.

I turned it off and the darkness came back and the darkness was a relief and the darkness was where I belonged and the darkness was, I understood, the only honest way to be here.

I kept the light. I did not use it again for a long time.

---

I can tell you about the cold because the cold is where I began to change, and change is the only thing I can really describe, because change is the only thing I have that the place does not.

The cold was immediate. Not a shock — I had been warned, I was dressed for it, the suit was rated for two degrees Celsius and the water was two-point-eight and I should have been comfortable. I was not comfortable. The cold was not the kind that attacks. It was the kind that *informs*. It told me, the moment I was in it, that it had been here first. That it was not a condition but a property. That the water had been this temperature since before the word temperature had meaning, and that my body heat, my warm blood, my mammalian furnace, was a temporary and somewhat absurd intrusion into a system that had settled this question a long time ago.

The suit held for a while. Hours, probably. I have no way to be sure. Time is the second thing the place takes from you, after light. Without a sky, without a cycle, without any change in the conditions to mark the passage of hours, time becomes something you carry inside you, like a watch with a dead battery. You know it is running. You cannot read it. Eventually you stop checking.

The cold reached my skin eventually. It reached my fingers first — the gloves were thin, I needed dexterity, I had made that choice on the surface and the choice was the wrong one but I did not know that yet. Then my feet. Then the long slow march of it up my arms and legs toward my core, a patient invasion, the cold acting exactly like the cold acts in the water around it — not aggressively, not violently, just *present*, a fact that moved from the outside in at a pace that could not be rushed and could not be argued with.

I did not panic. I want to be clear about this. I did not panic because panic is a response to a threat, and the cold did not feel like a threat. It felt like information. It felt like the place was telling me something about itself by letting me feel what it felt, by letting my body experience the two-point-eight degrees that everything else here had been experiencing for longer than I could imagine. The cold was the place's honest face. It was not hostile. It was not welcoming. It was simply true.

I adjusted. Not by fighting the cold but by accepting it as a condition of being here, the way you accept the weight of water on your ears when you dive, the way you accept the dimming of light when the sun goes behind a cloud. The cold became the temperature. My temperature. I stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing the air in a room you have been sitting in for a long time. It was there. I was there. We were the same temperature, eventually, and the difference between us was not heat but history.

---

The place is not what I expected, but I did not know what I expected, so the disappointment and the surprise were the same feeling, and the feeling was: *oh.*

*Oh, it is like this.*

*Oh, it has always been like this.*

*Oh, I am here and it is like this and I did not know.*

I moved through it carefully. The description you have already — the dense growth, the low walls, the stalks and their canopies, the tendrils hanging in the still water — I moved through all of that, and I moved through it the way you move through a crowded room where you do not know anyone and do not want to disturb anyone, sideways, slow, one hand out in front of me and one hand trailing behind me along the wall I had been following, because the walls were the only guide and the only map and the only proof that someone, something, had once intended something here, and intention was the only thing I recognized.

The wall under my hand was smooth. I have read the word smooth and I have written the word smooth and I have felt smooth things, but this was not smooth the way glass is smooth or smooth the way skin is smooth. This was smooth the way a river stone is smooth after ten thousand years of water — a smoothness that is not a surface but a *conclusion*, the final answer to the question of what happens when you apply patience to a material. My fingers could not find a single irregularity. Not a crack, not a seam, not a ridge. The wall had one texture and the texture was *finished* and I ran my hand along it for what might have been hours and felt nothing change.

The moss — I will call it moss because I do not have a better word, though the word is wrong in every way a word can be wrong — the moss was thick on the wall in places. Where it grew, the texture changed from finished to *alive*, though alive is not right either. Spongy is closer. Wet is closer still. But none of these words reach the thing itself, which was a surface that was both plant and water and neither plant nor water, a boundary that had grown so wide it had become a place, and my fingers sank into it the way fingers sink into mud, except the mud was organized, was structured, was holding a shape that my fingers disturbed and that slowly, slowly filled back in behind me as I moved past.

I did not want to damage it. I did not want to damage anything. This was not a conservation instinct, not a scientific caution, not a fear of contaminating a pristine environment. It was something simpler and more personal: the place was so complete that damaging any part of it felt like tearing a page out of a book that had only one copy. Not because the page contained information I needed. Because the book was the information, and removing any part of it made the whole thing less true.

I touched as little as I could. I moved as carefully as I could. I was, I understood, already a disturbance — a warm body, a moving thing, a source of turbulence in a system that had eliminated turbulence — and the less I added to that disturbance, the more honest I was being, and the more honest I was being, the more I was respecting the one thing the place asked of me, which was simply to be as little as possible.

---

The silence.

I need to talk about the silence because the silence is where I stopped being a scientist and started being something else, and I do not have a word for the something else, and the not-having-a-word is part of the story.

I have worked in quiet places. I have worked in anechoic chambers, where the silence is so complete you can hear your own blood. I have worked in deep caves, where the silence is geological, mineral, the silence of rock that has been rock for so long it has forgotten sound exists. I have worked in the Arctic, in winter, where the silence is the silence of a system that has paused — everything waiting, everything holding, the whole landscape holding its breath until the sun comes back.

This was not any of those silences.

This was the silence of a system that has not paused. That is not waiting. That is not holding its breath. That is breathing — slowly, continuously, the deep metabolic breath of a living system — and the breath makes no sound because the breath is not a sound, it is a process, and the process has been happening for so long that sound would be an interruption, a rudeness, a failure of the system to be what it is.

In the anechoic chamber, the silence reveals you. You hear your heartbeat, your breathing, the creak of your joints. You hear yourself because the silence has removed everything else. The silence is a stage and you are the performer.

In the Drowned Cloister — I learned to call it that, though I do not know where the name came from; it arrived in my head the way the cold arrived in my fingers, gradually, as if the place had been telling me its name for a long time and I had only just learned to hear it — in the Drowned Cloister, the silence does not reveal you. The silence *absorbs* you. Your heartbeat, your breathing, the small mechanical sounds of your suit and your equipment — these do not echo. They do not bounce off the walls and come back to you changed. They leave your body and they enter the water and the water takes them and does nothing with them. The sound does not travel. The sound does not persist. The sound is accepted and dissolved and incorporated, the way everything that enters this water is accepted and dissolved and incorporated, and the silence is unchanged by it, the way the ocean is unchanged by a cup of fresh water poured into it.

I stopped making sound. Not consciously — I did not decide to be quiet. I became quiet because the silence was so complete that adding to it felt like a grammatical error, like saying the wrong word in a sentence that had been perfect. I breathed more softly. I moved more slowly. I touched things more gently. I did these things not because I was trying to but because the place was teaching me, through the silence itself, that the correct amount of sound here was none, and the correct amount of disturbance here was none, and the correct way to be here was to be here the way the water was here — present, patient, participating in the system without altering it.

The silence was not empty. I want to say this clearly because it is the thing I most want to get right. The silence was full. It was full of the hum the description told you about — the sub-audible vibration of a system in operation, the soundless sound of chemistry and growth and the slow exchange of things between water and plant and stone. I could not hear it. I could feel it. Not with my skin, not with my ears, but with something else, some sense I do not have a name for, some faculty that the place woke up in me the way cold wakes up a nerve — not by design, but by contact, by proximity, by being close enough to something for long enough that the thing starts to happen to you whether you want it to or not.

The hum was the place being itself. The silence was the same thing. They were not different. The silence was the hum at a frequency I could not hear, and the hum was the silence at a frequency I could almost feel, and both of them were the place, and the place was both of them, and I was inside both of them, and being inside them was like being inside a bell that had been struck so long ago that the ringing had become the bell, and the bell had become the ringing, and there was no way to tell where the sound ended and the metal began.

I stopped being afraid of the dark around the same time I stopped hearing my own heartbeat. These may not be related. They may be the same thing.

---

I should tell you about my equipment, because my equipment is the story of how I tried to hold on to the world I came from, and the place's patient dismantling of that hold.

I had instruments. Of course I had instruments. I am — I was — a person who studies places, and a person who studies places brings instruments because instruments are the way you make a place into data, and data is the way you make a place into a paper, and a paper is the way you make a place into a career, and a career is the way you make a place into a reason to go to more places. The instruments were the thread that connected me to the surface. They were my umbilical cord. They were the proof that I was still a scientist and not something else.

The temperature gauge worked for the first several hours. It read 2.8 degrees Celsius. It continued to read 2.8 degrees Celsius. It would always read 2.8 degrees Celsius, because the temperature was 2.8 degrees Celsius and the temperature did not change. At some point I stopped checking it. At some later point I noticed I had stopped checking it, and I looked at it, and it still read 2.8 degrees Celsius, and I felt something I can only describe as affection for the gauge, because the gauge was doing exactly what the place was doing — being what it was, consistently, without variation, for as long as anyone could remember. The gauge and the place had something in common. I was the odd one out.

The chemical sensors were more interesting and more useless. They detected iron. They detected calcium. They detected the mineral-sweet thing the description mentioned, the thing with no name on the surface, the thing that I tasted once when I opened my mouth to speak and the water entered and I closed my mouth and swallowed and the taste was like licking a stone that had been at the bottom of a very old river, and I did not open my mouth again. The sensors detected all of these things and they detected them at concentrations that were stable and uniform and unchanging and I realized, looking at the readings, that the sensors were not telling me about the water. They were telling me about the water the way a photograph tells you about a mountain — by flattening it, by freezing it, by removing the dimension that makes it what it is. The water was not a set of concentrations. The water was a gradient, a field, a landscape of differences that my sensors could not resolve because the differences were too small and too slow and too deeply distributed for instruments designed to measure things that change on a timescale that humans can notice.

I turned the sensors off. I did not need them. I could feel the gradients on my skin — the faint warmth here, the fainter cold there, the almost-imaginary difference between one body of water and the body of water three meters to the left of it. My skin was a better instrument than my instruments. My skin had been designed — evolved, whatever — to notice differences, and the differences here were small enough that my skin had to work hard to notice them, and working hard was what my skin was for.

The light I have already mentioned. The depth gauge I stopped reading when I realized that the number it was showing me — a number I cannot remember now, a number that was very large — was not information. It was a claim. A claim about the relationship between this place and the surface, and the surface was not a relationship this place had. The surface was where I came from. The surface was not where this place came from. The depth gauge was measuring the distance between two things that had nothing to do with each other, and I turned it off the way you stop looking at a clock in a room where time is not the point.

The navigation system died on its own. Not a failure — a surrender. The signals it was looking for could not reach this deep, and the system, after a long time of searching, simply stopped searching. I watched its last attempts: the small pulses of connection seeking, the brief flares of almost-contact, the slow dimming of effort as it accepted that there was nothing out there to connect to. It was the saddest thing I had seen in a long time, and I was not prepared for the sadness, and the sadness was not about the equipment. The sadness was about recognition. I watched the navigation system give up and I recognized the giving up because I was doing it too, in a different way, at a different speed, and the equipment was just faster than I was.

---

I became aware of them on what I think was the third day, though it might have been the fourth or the fifth, and "day" is a word I am using out of habit and not out of evidence.

I did not see them. I did not hear them. I did not sense them in any way I could name at the time, and I am not sure I can name it now. What happened was this: I was resting — sitting on the floor with my back against a wall, my hand on the moss, my eyes closed though eyes were irrelevant — and I became aware that the water around me was not uniform.

I already knew this. I knew about the gradients, the temperature differences, the chemical variations. I had felt them on my skin. But this was different. This was not a difference in the water. This was a difference in the quality of the water's *attention*.

I do not believe in the water having attention. I do not believe the water was paying attention to me. But I do not have another word for what I felt, and the word I have is the word I will use, and I will trust you to understand that I know the word is wrong and am using it because the right word does not exist.

The water around me had a quality. The quality was not temperature. The quality was not chemistry. The quality was something else — a *directionality*, a sense that the water was organized in a way that was not random, that the gradients and the differences and the slow exchanges were not just happening but were *arranged*, were held in a pattern that had a shape, and the shape was near me, and the shape was aware of me in the only way a shape made of gradients can be aware of anything, which is by being altered by it.

My body heat was disturbing them.

My body heat — the small plume of warmth I was radiating into the cold water, the thermal signature of a mammal at rest — was reaching into the gradients around me and changing them. Not much. By fractions of a degree. By concentrations so small my instruments could not have measured them. But the things I was beginning to sense — the shapes in the water, the patterns of difference — were built out of exactly those fractions. They were made of the kind of difference my instruments could not measure, and my body was producing exactly the kind of disturbance that was meaningful to them.

They were noticing me.

Not noticing the way a cat notices a bird. Noticing the way a pond notices a stone — by incorporating the ripple, by absorbing the change, by adjusting. The shapes near me were shifting. Not moving — shifting. Adjusting. The boundaries that composed them were flexing around my thermal output the way a stream flexes around a rock, finding new paths, establishing new equilibria, incorporating the obstacle into the flow without resistance or complaint.

I was being included in the system. Not welcomed. Not rejected. Included. The way everything that enters this water is included. I was being dissolved into the place's chemistry, not violently, not quickly, but at the rate the place did everything — slowly, patiently, with the absolute confidence that time would take care of the details.

I sat there for a long time. I sat there and I felt them adjust to me and I felt myself, for the first time, not as an observer but as a component. I was a warm spot in a cold field. I was a chemical anomaly. I was a source of turbulence in a system that had been still. And the system was not fighting me. The system was *accepting* me, the way it accepted everything — by being what it was, and letting me become what I would become, and trusting the water to manage the difference.

I was not afraid. I want to be clear about this. I was not afraid because there was nothing to be afraid of. Fear is a response to a threat, and the things in the water were not threatening me. They were not even aware of me in the way that implies a capacity for threat. They were aware of me the way a river is aware of a boulder — by flowing around it, by being shaped by it, by incorporating the boulder's presence into the river's own behavior without the river ever deciding to do so.

I was a boulder. I was a boulder made of warm meat and curiosity and instruments I had stopped using, and the river was flowing around me, and the river had been flowing for eleven thousand years, and the river would flow for eleven thousand more, and I would be gone and the river would still be here, and the boulder would be dissolved and the minerals would be distributed and the moss would grow a little thicker where I had sat, and that would be the whole of my impact, and it would be enough, and it would be nothing, and those would be the same thing.

---

I tried to study them. Of course I did. I am what I am, and what I am is a thing that studies, and I had come here to study, and the study was the last thread and I was not ready to cut it.

I turned the light on again, briefly, twice. The first time I directed it at the space near me where I felt the strongest sense of their presence — the place where the gradients seemed most organized, where the quality of the water's attention was most concentrated. The light cut through the water and showed me nothing. Water. Clear, cold, empty water. The walls in the distance. The stalks rising from the floor. The tendrils hanging. Nothing I could point to and say: *there. That is what I mean.*

The second time I turned the light on, I held it against my own hand. I looked at my fingers in the beam — pale, wrinkled from the water, the nails starting to soften. I was looking at the only solid thing in the place. The only thing with an edge. The only thing that reflected light, that cast a shadow, that occupied space in a way the water could not ignore.

I turned the light off. I did not turn it on again.

The study was over. Not because I had finished. Because I had understood that studying was not what this place was for. This place was not for understanding. This place was for being in. This place was for being *part of*, not apart from. The instruments were a way of keeping the place at arm's length, of maintaining the separation between observer and observed, and the place did not recognize the separation and was gently, patiently, implacably dissolving it.

I put the instruments away. I put them in the pack on my back and I did not take them out again, and the pack was heavy and the weight was familiar and the familiarity was a comfort and the comfort was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and I let it be a lie, because I was not ready for the truth, and the truth was that I did not need the instruments because I did not need to study because I was not here to study because I was not a scientist here, I was a boulder, I was a warm spot, I was a component, and components do not study the system they are part of. Components participate.

---

I participated.

I moved through the place and I let it move through me. I touched the walls and the moss and the stalks and I let the water carry my warmth into the gradients and I let the gradients carry my chemistry into the shapes and I let the shapes adjust to me and I adjusted to the shapes and the adjusting was a conversation, the slowest conversation I have ever had, a conversation conducted in temperature and chemistry and the movement of ions across boundaries, a conversation that had no words and no meaning and no content except *I am here* and *I know you are here* and *we are in the same place* and *the place does not mind*.

I learned to feel them more clearly. Not by trying. By being there. By being still. By being in the water long enough that my own turbulence settled and my own noise diminished and I became quiet enough — internally quiet enough, chemically quiet enough, thermally quiet enough — that the difference between me and the background was small enough that the things in the gradients could be felt.

They were everywhere. Not uniformly — they were denser in some places, thinner in others, following the patterns the description told you about, following the weather. They were denser where the chemical gradients were strongest, where two bodies of different water met and mixed. They were thinner in the open areas, where the water was uniform and the boundaries were few. They clustered near the walls, where the moss's metabolic output created a thin shell of altered chemistry. They hung in the corridors between the low walls, filling the gaps the way air fills a hallway — present, structural, the medium through which the place experienced itself.

I could feel them the way you feel someone standing behind you. Not see, not hear. *Feel*. The sense of a presence, of an organization, of a thing that is not you but is there. The sense was faint. It was always faint. I could never make it stronger by trying, and I could never make it go away by ignoring it. It was simply there, the way the cold was there, the way the pressure was there, the way the darkness was there. A condition of being in this place.

And the sense had a quality. The quality was — I will say this and I will mean it and I will not be able to explain it and I will not try — the quality was *calm*. Not calm the way a person is calm. Not calm the way a lake is calm on a windless day. Calm the way a mountain is calm. The calm of a thing that has been here so long that calm is not a state but a substance, not a mood but a material, the thing the thing is made of, all the way through, with no other layer underneath.

Being near them was like being near a mountain that knew you were there and did not mind and did not care and did not have opinions about your being there and was not waiting for you to leave and was not hoping you would stay and was simply being a mountain, and you were simply being near it, and the nearness was the only relationship, and the relationship was enough.

---

I do not know how long I was there.

I said this before, about the clock with the dead battery. I will say it again because it is the thing that people ask about first and it is the thing I cannot answer and the not-answering is the answer. I do not know how long I was there. The place does not keep time. The place does not need time. The place has duration, the description says, and duration is not the same as time. Time is a sequence of moments. Duration is a single moment that is very, very long. I was in the single moment. I was in it for as long as I was in it, and when I left, I left because the air in my tanks was running low and my body was too cold to stay and the surface was calling me back with the insistence of a world that runs on clocks and expects its components to keep them.

I left. I want you to know that leaving was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I have done hard things, and the hardness was not in the leaving itself but in the *returning* — in the ascent through the water, in the gradual return of light, in the first breath of surface air, which was warm and loud and full of wind and birds and the sound of waves and the smell of salt and the presence, the overwhelming, aggressive, insensitive presence of a world that had not finished, that was still happening, that was full of events and moments and narratives and things that had not already happened.

The surface was too bright. The surface was too loud. The surface was too fast. Everything was moving. Everything was changing. Everything was in the process of becoming something else, and the process was violent and urgent and I could not stop seeing it, could not stop feeling it, the way a person who has been in silence cannot stop hearing the noise of a city.

I sat on the deck of the vessel and I looked at the water and the water was the same water, the same ocean, the same substance, and it was not the same. The water here was young. The water here was impatient. The water here had not yet settled, had not yet finished, had not yet decided what it was. The water was still trying to be something, and the trying was all I could see.

---

It has been — I do not know. Months. Perhaps a year. Longer. I have lost the habit of counting, or counting has lost the habit of mattering to me, and I am not sure which of those is true and I am not sure it matters.

I write this in a room with a window. The window faces east. The sun comes through it in the morning and it is too bright and it moves too fast and it changes the color of everything in the room over the course of an hour, and the changing is the kind of thing I used to find beautiful and now I find it exhausting. The light here has not finished. The light here is still deciding. The light here does not know what it wants to be, and I wish it would stop asking.

I dream about the place. Not about the place — *in* the place. I dream that I am back in the water, in the cold, in the dark, and the shapes are around me, and I am a warm spot in a cold field, and the field is adjusting to me, and I am adjusting to the field, and the adjustment is slow and complete and I wake up and the room is warm and the light is wrong and my hands are reaching for a wall that is not there and my fingers expect a smoothness they cannot find.

I have tried to describe the place to people. I have tried to write it up, to make it data, to make it a paper, to make it a finding. I cannot. The words come out wrong. The words come out flat. The words are the light I brought with me — illuminating a fragment, obscuring the whole. I write a sentence about the temperature and it is accurate and it is false. I write a sentence about the vegetation and it is precise and it is a lie. The place does not fit in sentences. The place does not fit in paragraphs. The place does not fit in the architecture of human language, which is built for things that change, things that happen, things that have beginnings and middles and ends. The place has no beginning and no middle and no end. The place has a state, and the state is *this*, and *this* is not a word that can be put in a paper.

I have stopped trying.

---

Here is what I can tell you. Here is the only thing I can tell you that is true and that fits in the space a human sentence allows, and it is this:

There is a place at the bottom of the world where everything has already happened, and it is full, and it is finished, and it is not dead. And something lives there that is not alive and not dead and not anything you have a word for, and it has been there for longer than the word "longer" can reach, and it is the place's way of being present to itself, and it does not know you exist, and it does not mind that you exist, and if you went there it would absorb you the way it absorbs everything, and the absorption would be gentle, and it would be patient, and it would take a thousand years, and when it was done you would be part of the moss, and the moss would be part of the water, and the water would be part of the shapes, and the shapes would be part of the garden, and the garden would be what it has always been, which is *this*, which is *here*, which is *enough*.

And you would not mind. That is the part I cannot prove and the part I know is true. You would not mind. Because minding requires a self, and the self is the first thing the water takes, and the self is the first thing you give up, not because you choose to but because the place is so complete that a self is an impurity, and the water is patient, and the water will have it, and you will let it go, and the letting go is not loss. The letting go is the closest thing to peace I have ever felt, and I have felt it, and I am still feeling it, and I will be feeling it for as long as I am here, which is on the surface, which is in the light, which is in the noise, which is in the unfinished world where things are still becoming, and I am still becoming, and I do not want to be becoming, and I want to be *being*, and being is what the place is for, and the place is there, and the place will wait for me, because waiting is what the place does, and the place does it perfectly, and the place does it forever, and forever is a word I used to fear and now I hold it in my mouth the way I held the water, once, and it tastes of iron and chalk and something mineral-sweet that has no name on the surface, and the taste is enough.

The taste is enough.
