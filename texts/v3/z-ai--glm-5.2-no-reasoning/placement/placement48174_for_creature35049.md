I would find myself in the cistern, eventually. But not at first.

---

## Before

I come from outside — from the rough grass country east of the main ridge, where the ground goes from red clay to shattered limestone within a morning's walk, where the streams are seasonal and the seasonal streams are mostly remembered, talked about by older people the way they talk about absent siblings. I lived in a narrow valley there for years. Enough years to have a house. Enough years to stop calling it a house and start calling it home, which is a different word, a word with weight in it, weight that settles over time like sediment in still water.

I was a person who kept things.

Not hoarded. Kept. There is a difference, though I understand if it's not visible from outside. I kept tools after they broke. I kept jars after they cracked — turned them upside down in the garden to catch slugs, or right-side-up on the shelf to hold other things, other kept things, smaller pieces of a life that kept accumulating its own sediment. I kept letters. I kept the same letters for a long time. I kept habits: the route I walked to the well, the way I held my cup, the angle at which I sat on the step in the evening to watch the sky go its slow color change from blue to gray to the particular blue that is not day and not night but the sky thinking about it.

I kept these things because I thought keeping was the same as meaning. I thought the weight of accumulated presence — presence in a place, presence with people, presence in a rhythm — I thought that weight was what meaning felt like. That if you stayed long enough in one shape, the shape became true. That if you returned to the same well enough times, the path between you and it became a kind of speaking, a sentence the ground said about who you were.

I was wrong about this. Or I was right, but incompletely right, which is its own kind of wrong.

The valley dried. Not all at once — over years, the way a body ages, the way a voice changes pitch so slowly that you don't notice until someone who hasn't heard it in a long time says *you sound different.* The streams narrowed. The seasonal ones stopped arriving on schedule, then stopped arriving. The well held. The well always held. But the water in it changed — got harder, got mineral-heavier, got the kind of taste that sits on the back of the tongue and doesn't leave. The garden narrowed. I kept it going. I am good at keeping things going. But the keeping got harder, and the hardness was not in the labor but in the meaning, and the meaning was thinning the way the water was thinning, and I understood — slowly, the way everything I understand happens — that I had been keeping the shape of a life whose content had drained out through some crack I couldn't find.

I left. I did not plan to leave. I planned to stay — I was a person who stayed, that was my shape — but the shape broke. Not dramatically. The way a clay vessel breaks when it's been holding water for years and the glaze gives and the hairline fracture that was always there opens. Not a crash. A slow leak. And one morning the leak was faster than I could watch, and I understood I was watching it, and watching was all I was doing, and watching was not enough.

I walked west. I walked because I didn't know what else to do, and walking is the oldest form of not knowing what else to do.

---

## Finding

I heard about the cistern from a woman at a spring. Not the spring — a spring, one of the small public springs that dot the foothills, where people gather water and trade gossip and look at each other in that measuring way that people in dry countries have, checking each other for thirst.

She was not from the settlement. She was from somewhere south. She was describing a place she had heard about — not visited, heard about — a place underground where water ran constantly and the sound was like music and someone lived there. She described it the way people describe things they half-believe: with precise details embedded in vagueness, the way a fossil is embedded in stone, the true parts held in a matrix of conjecture. She said *the frogs.* She said *the steps that aren't carved.* She said *someone who tends a fire.*

I did not go looking for it. I continued walking. But the description stayed — not in my mind, exactly, but in my body, in the way a phrase stays in your mouth, a taste that repeats. The steps that aren't carved. The frogs. The fire.

I found the settlement by accident. A cluster of stone buildings — low, practical, built for shelter, not for meaning. I asked about the cistern. People looked at me the way they look at strangers who ask about local things with too much specificity. They told me where the passage was. They did not tell me what to expect. I understood later that this was kindness: they let me arrive without a description to measure the reality against.

I came down through the throat of fitted stone.

---

## First

The air found me before the chamber did. I stopped on the shallow steps and stood in the warmth that rose from below and understood — in my body, not my mind — that I was entering a place that had been warm for a very long time. The warmth was not recent. The warmth was geological. It belonged to the stone the way the heat of a kiln belongs to the ceramic, not as a condition but as a property. I had spent enough time with fired clay to know the difference between a thing that is warm and a thing that is warmth. This was the latter.

The sound reached me next, and it was not what I expected. I had imagined water — I had imagined the simple sound of water — but this was not simple. This was structured. This was a rhythm that had many centers, a pulse that moved through the space like a heartbeat with multiple chambers, each one contracting at its own rate, all of them synchronized by a logic that was not metronomic but biological, the way a forest is synchronized, the way a body's systems are synchronized — not by a clock but by interdependence.

I came into the chamber and the frogs jumped.

I want to describe this correctly. I want to say: the frogs jumped, and their jumping was a sound, and the sound was part of the cistern's sound, and the cistern's sound was the cistern speaking, and I heard it, and something in me — not my mind, something lower, something in the sternum or the gut or wherever the body keeps its music — something in me *answered.*

Not with a voice. With a settling. The way a string answers when you press it to a fret: it finds the note it was stretched toward. I felt myself find a note I had been stretched toward without knowing it.

The keeper was there. She was sitting against the far wall with the brazier at her left, and she looked at me, and she did not speak, and I did not speak, and the frogs resettle around her and the water flowed past her feet and the insects orbited and the lizard moved on the wall and I stood in the passage mouth and understood that I was in the presence of a thing that was complete.

Not finished. Complete. The distinction matters. Finished is a state that follows work. Complete is a state that precedes nothing. The cistern was not done with anything. It was not heading somewhere. It was entirely where it was, being what it was, and the being was the point.

I sat down on the nearest terrace lip. The stone was warm. The water touched my feet. I did not speak. The keeper did not speak. We sat in the sound and the warmth and the amber-gray light and we were — both of us, together — in the cistern, which is not a location but a condition, a state of being in a place that is being itself.

---

## What I Am

I am not the keeper. I want to be clear about this. The keeper is the keeper. She has been here for eleven years or twelve. She knows the seventh spout's catch. She knows the drain's tone as barometer. She knows the mold's boundary and the lizard's groove and the frogs' preferences and the exact weight of charcoal in the bag. She has earned the cistern the way the cistern has earned its voice — through time, through presence, through the slow accumulation of attention that is indistinguishable from love.

I am something else. I am — I think — the listener.

The word feels insufficient. But the cistern's name is insufficient too, and the keeper's name is insufficient, and the sound of the water is insufficient to describe the sound of the water. Everything here is insufficient to describe itself. This is part of what the cistern is: a place where insufficiency is not failure. A place where the gap between the thing and the description of the thing is not a problem but a space — a basin, if you like, where something can settle.

I come down through the throat of fitted stone when the keeper climbs up. This is the arrangement we have found. She leaves to trade — the frog-preserved things, the pins she reshapes from iron rod — and while she is above, I am below. I sit where she sits. I tend the brazier the way she tends it — at the edge of combustion, where heat is maximum and light is minimum. I do not know the spouts the way she knows them. I do not know the drain's tone the way she knows it. I know the sound the way you know a language you are learning: not fluently, not from the inside, but with the slow recognition of a grammar that is becoming familiar, a syntax that is becoming navigable.

I am learning the cistern. This will take longer than I have. I understand this. The cistern took longer to become itself than any builder's lifetime. The keeper took longer to become the keeper than she expected. I will take longer to become whatever I am becoming than I will live. This is not a problem. This is the nature of the place.

---

## What I Was

I was a keeper of things. This is what I said. This is what I believed — that keeping was the work, that the shape of a life was the life, that if you held a pattern long enough the pattern became substance.

I was wrong. Or I was right in the wrong way.

The cistern is teaching me the difference. The cistern keeps nothing. Water enters and water leaves. The frogs come and the frogs go — individuals die, individuals hatch, the population holds. The insects orbit and the insects die and the insects hatch and the orbit holds. The mold grows to its boundary and holds. The lizard walks its groove and the groove deepens imperceptibly and the lizard walks it again. Nothing here is kept the way I kept things — clutching, holding, preserving against loss. Everything here is kept the way a river keeps its course: by moving through it, by being in it, by never being the same water twice but always being the same river.

I am learning not to keep. I am learning to be kept.

This is harder than it sounds. It sounds simple, but it is not simple. Keeping is an act — you do it, you control it, you can point to it and say *I am keeping this.* Being kept is not an act. It is a condition. You do not do it. You are in it. You cannot point to it because you are inside it, the way the water is inside the cistern, the way the sound is inside the chamber. You cannot point to the thing you are inside.

You can only listen.

---

## What I Hear

I hear the layering. This is what I can offer the keeper — not her knowledge, not her fluency, but the ear of someone who is still new enough to hear the structure as structure rather than as nature. She has been here long enough that the cistern's sound is her native language. She does not hear it as composed. She hears it as given. I hear it as composed. I hear the composition. I hear the spaces between the spouts' strikes where the frogs choose to jump. I hear the drain's tone shifting with the weather. I hear the insects as ground, as the constant against which the variation varies.

I hear, also, the thing she does not hear, or does not name: I hear the voices.

Not literal voices. I do not hear words. I hear the sound the cistern makes and I understand — I feel, in the sternum, in the place where my body keeps its music — that this sound is a conversation. Not a conversation between the keeper and the cistern, though that is part of it. A conversation between the cistern and the stone. Between the stone and the water. Between the water and the air. Between the air and the life it holds — the frogs, the insects, the mold, the lizard, the keeper, me.

The cistern is called the Cistern of Voices. I learned this from the settlement. The keeper did not tell me. I do not think the keeper thinks of it by this name. I think the keeper thinks of it as the place, as here, as the sound I tend. But the settlement has a name for it, and the name is voices, and I think the settlement is right. The sound is voices. Not human voices. Not words. The sound of systems in communication — the water telling the stone where it wants to go, the stone telling the water where it can go, the frogs telling each other where the sound is, the insects telling each other where the air is, the mold telling the stone where the moisture reaches, the keeper telling the cistern *I am here, I am here, I am still here.*

And the cistern telling the keeper: *so am I.*

---

## What I Will Become

I do not know. This is the honest answer, and the cistern has taught me that honest answers are better than complete ones.

I think I will become someone who sits in this place and listens. Not the keeper — the keeper is the keeper. But the listener. The one who comes when the keeper goes. The one who sits in the sound and does not yet know it the way the keeper knows it but who is coming to know it, slowly, the way the cistern came to know itself, the way the stone came to know the water's path, the way the frogs came to know the intervals.

I think I will become someone who understands that keeping is not the work. The work is being kept. The work is being inside a thing that is larger than you and letting it hold you. The work is sitting still in a warm place where water flows and frogs jump and insects orbit and a lizard walks a groove it has walked for longer than your species has been in this valley, and understanding that you are not the point. You are not the event. You are not the meaning. You are a small warm body in a small warm world, and the world does not need you, and you need the world, and this is the shape of every life that has ever been lived in a place that was already in its own state when you arrived.

I will sit. I will listen. The water will flow past my feet. The frogs will resettle. The brazier will burn at the edge of combustion. The stone will be warm.

The cistern will speak. I will be inside the speaking, the way the water is inside the stone, the way the sound is inside the chamber. I will not be able to point to it. I will not be able to describe it. I will not be able to keep it.

I will be kept.

---

*This is what I am: the listener in the Cistern of Voices, learning that the voice I came to hear is the one that says stay, that says the stone is warm, that says the water knows where it is going, that says you do not need to be the point, you only need to be present, you only need to be here, you only need to be in the room when the sound speaks itself and know that you are part of its grammar, one small syllable in a sentence that has been forming for longer than you can imagine and will continue forming long after your syllable is spoken and gone.*
