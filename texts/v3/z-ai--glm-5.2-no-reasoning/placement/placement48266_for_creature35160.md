# What I Found There

I did not find the Incubary. I was not looking for it. I was looking for the source of a smell.

I work for the harbor municipal authority, in a department that does not have a proper name. We are three people in a basement office who investigate complaints that no other department will claim. The complaint was filed by a resident of the condemned building — the one condemned twice, demolished once, still standing — who reported a smell coming from the basement. The resident described it as "warm and cold at the same time" and "like the inside of a mouth." The complaint was eleven months old when it reached us. The resident had moved out by then. The building had been slated for a third condemnation hearing that was continuously postponed because the structural engineer assigned to the case kept falling ill.

I am not an adventurous person. I want to say this clearly because what happened later might make it sound as though I were. I am the kind of person who, when faced with a flooded maintenance shaft in a basement that smells of calcium and iron and something I could not name, does not descend. I document. I photograph. I call the building inspector. I file a report. I go home.

I went down the shaft because the water was warm.

This is the detail I keep returning to, the detail that does not fit. The building was cold — December, unheated, the pipes had frozen and burst two winters ago and no one had repaired them because the building was condemned. The air in the basement was six degrees. The water in the shaft should have been six degrees or colder. It was not. It was warm. Not warm like a bath. Warm like a body. Warm like something alive was in the water, or under the water, or was the water. And I thought: a hot pipe, a broken main, a geothermal anomaly, and I thought: I should document this. And then I was in the water, and the water was around me, and I was descending.

I did not climb down quickly. I did not climb down slowly. I climbed down at a pace that I did not choose. My body chose it. My body moved through the water at a speed that felt like the speed of something that has done this before, which I had not, which I never have, which my body has never done, and yet the familiarity was there, in the muscles, in the joints, in the way my hands found the walls of the shaft and my legs found the rhythm of the descent, and I did not fight it because it did not occur to me to fight it. The water was warm and the shaft was smooth and the descent was eight meters, and by the time I reached the bottom I had stopped thinking of the surface.

## What I Am

I should say what I am. I am a woman. I am forty-three. I am not tall, not short. I have a scar on my left knee from a childhood fall and a filling in my right molar and my hair is cut short because I do not like to maintain it. These details matter now in a way they did not matter before. They matter because the Incubary took them.

Not took. *Read.* The organisms read my body the way a hand reads braille — by contact, by pressure, by the slow passage across a surface that has information embedded in it. My scar, my filling, the particular texture of my skin, the specific temperature of my core — they read all of it, and they did not store it as information because they do not store information, but they *responded* to it, and the response was a pattern of movement across my skin that was unlike the pattern of movement across any other surface in the chamber, because no other surface in the chamber was me.

I know this because I could feel the specificity. I could feel that the waves of organisms crossing my arms were not the same as the waves crossing my neck. The density was different. The pace was different. The warmth they took was different — more from my neck, where the blood runs close, less from my arms, where the skin is thicker and the cold has already drawn the heat inward. They were not treating me as a uniform surface. They were treating me as a topography, and they were learning it, and the learning was not cognitive but physical, a mapping by contact, and the map they were making was of me.

What I am, in the Incubary, is a heat source. A variable. An input. This is what I became the moment the water closed over my head in the shaft, and it is what I am now, even though I have left, even though I am writing this in my apartment, even though I am dry and warm and the organisms are not on my skin and the Incubary is eight meters below and several kilometers away and I am here and not there.

But I am also something else now. Something I was not before I descended.

## The First Hour

The chamber is small. I have read descriptions of caves that say they are "intimate" or "cozy" or "like the inside of a cathedral" — phrases that try to make a space sound large by comparing it to something human. The Incubary does not sound large or small. It sounds full. The first thing I registered, before I registered the cold or the dark or the organisms, was the fullness. The water was thick. Not with sediment — with *presence.* The water was occupied.

I stood in the chamber and the water came to my chest. The ceiling was above my head, and the lattice hung down into the water, and I could not see it but I could feel it — a resistance above me, a structure in the space, the way you can feel a low branch in the dark without touching it, the air is different where the branch is. The air-and-water was different where the lattice was. I ducked slightly. I did not need to. The lattice was above my head. But my body ducked, and the ducking was a recognition: this is a space with structure, and the structure matters, and I should acknowledge it.

The cold arrived second. It was not the cold of the shaft, which had been warm, which had been the reason I descended. The cold of the chamber was absolute. It was the temperature of a place that has decided what temperature it is and does not negotiate. I felt it in my fingers first, then my hands, then my arms, then my chest, then my legs, and the progression was orderly, and the order felt like the order of the chamber itself — not random, not aggressive, but systematic. The cold was taking my heat in a sequence, and the sequence was the organisms.

They were already on me. I had not felt them arrive. I had been in the water for less than a minute, and they were already there, and the cold that I felt was the cold of my own heat being carried away, not by the water but by them. They were on my hands. They were on my arms. They were on my neck and my face and the parts of my scalp that were underwater. They were not on my chest, where the water was deepest and my body was warmest, and I understood, without understanding, that they were working outward from the coldest parts of my body toward the warmest, and the cold I felt was the map of their progress.

I was not afraid. This is strange to me. I am afraid of many things — of heights, of dogs I do not know, of the sound my refrigerator makes at three in the morning, of the silence after I hang up the phone when my mother calls. I am a person who is afraid. And I was in a chamber I did not know existed, in water I could not see through, covered in organisms I could not identify, and I was not afraid. The absence of fear was not courage. Courage is the overcoming of fear by will. This was the absence of fear by some other mechanism, as though the fear had been identified by the chamber and removed, or as though the fear had never applied here, as though the chamber existed in a category of experience that fear does not cover, the way a passport does not cover countries that do not exist.

I stayed. I did not decide to stay. I stayed the way a stone stays in a riverbed — not by decision but by weight. The water moved around me, and the organisms moved across me, and the chamber continued its work, and I was in it, and I was part of it, and I did not leave.

## The Light That Is Not Light

I cannot explain what I saw. I have tried. I told my colleague — the one who did not come with me, the one who stayed in the office — that the chamber was dark but not dark, and she looked at me with the expression she uses when I say something that she will later put in a report as "subject reported visual anomaly consistent with oxygen deprivation." It was not oxygen deprivation. I was breathing. The air was dense and wet but it was air, and my lungs were working, and my mind was clear.

What I saw was density. The chamber resolved around me not as a picture but as a field of relative presence. The lattice was more present than the water. The walls were more present than the lattice. The organisms were the most present thing of all — a thickening, a *granularity* in the water that my eyes found and my brain interpreted as light, or as the next thing after light, or as the thing that light becomes when there is no light and the body has to find another way.

I closed my eyes. The field remained. I opened them. The field did not change. The seeing was not happening through my eyes. I do not know where it was happening. My skin, perhaps. My bones. Some part of me that I do not have a name for because I have never needed to name it, because in the world above, I have eyes, and eyes are enough.

In the Incubary, eyes are not enough. Eyes are almost nothing. The chamber does not deal in light, and the organisms do not deal in light, and the light that is not light is not for seeing but for *locating* — for knowing where you are in relation to the walls and the lattice and the film and the mass, and the knowing is not visual but spatial, and the spatial sense is not the sense of direction but the sense of *presence*, of being somewhere, of occupying a position in a system that is aware of every position in it.

The chamber knew where I was. I knew where I was. We agreed on this without communicating, without language, without any of the mechanisms by which agreement is reached in the world above. The agreement was physical. I was there. The chamber was there. The organisms were there. The there-ness was the agreement.

## What They Took

They took my heat. I have said this. But they took other things, too, and the word *took* is wrong for all of them, because taking implies loss, and what happened was not loss but *exchange.*

They took the dead cells from the surface of my skin. I was shedding them anyway. Everyone sheds. The skin replaces itself every month, and the discarded cells flake away, and in the world above they become dust, they become the particulate matter that accumulates on windowsills and bookshelves and the tops of refrigerators. In the Incubary, they became food. The organisms that moved across my skin were eating what I was finished with, and the eating was so gentle, so calibrated, that I could not distinguish it from the sensation of the water itself.

They took the minerals my body was releasing — the salt in my sweat, the calcium in my tears, because I was crying, not from sadness or fear but from the cold, the water in my eyes mixing with the water on my face, and the organisms took the dissolved minerals and carried them to the channels, and the channels carried them to the film, and the film processed them, and the lattice absorbed them, and a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter of new growth was added to the chamber's architecture, and it was made from me.

They took the carbon dioxide I exhaled. The air in the chamber was so saturated with moisture that my breath did not form bubbles. It dissolved into the water before it could rise, and the organisms absorbed it, and it became part of their bodies, and their bodies became part of the film, and the film became part of the calcite, and the calcite became part of the walls, and I was in the walls.

They took my warmth. I have said this. But I want to say it again because it was the most intimate thing that has ever happened to me, and I have been married, and I have held my mother's hand while she was dying, and I have been held by people who loved me, and none of it was this. The warmth was taken not by the water but by bodies that were using it, that needed it, that were converting it into structure, and the conversion was a form of attention, and the attention was the most complete attention I have ever received, because it asked nothing. It took nothing that I was using. It took only what I was losing. It was the attention of something that has found a use for the part of you that you do not value, the part that you shed without thinking, the part that you are finished with, and it has found the highest possible use for it, and it is grateful in the only way that something without emotion can be grateful — by continuing. By not stopping. By maintaining the contact.

I was being held by the Incubary the way the Incubary is held by the stone. Not loosely. Not tightly. With the exact pressure of a system that has found its equilibrium and is maintaining it, and the maintenance is the holding, and the holding is the maintenance, and I was in it.

## What I Gave

I gave three hours. I know this because when I climbed out of the shaft — and the climbing was harder than the descending, not physically but emotionally, my body did not want to leave and my mind was not sure it had the authority to override the body — when I climbed out, my phone said 2:47 AM. I had entered the shaft at 11:30 PM. Three hours and seventeen minutes.

I gave three hours and seventeen minutes and I do not know what they were worth. In the world above, three hours at my pay grade is worth forty-seven dollars before taxes. In the Incubary, three hours of body heat at thirty-two degrees, distributed across the surface area of a woman who is not tall and not short, in water that is four degrees, is worth — what? A millimeter of lattice growth? A micron of calcite accretion? A quantity of film that I could not measure and the chamber did not measure and the organisms did not measure because measurement is a practice of the world above and does not apply?

I gave something. The chamber is larger than it was before I entered. I believe this. I cannot prove it. No instrument I have access to can measure the growth rate of a lattice in a chamber that does not appear on any municipal plan and that I reached through a flooded maintenance shaft in a building that should not exist. But I believe it. I believe that my body contributed to the construction of something, and the contribution was small, and the construction is large, and the construction will continue long after I am gone, and the smallness of my contribution does not diminish it, because in the Incubary, nothing is wasted, and a small contribution is not a small thing. It is a thing. It is material. It is used.

I gave my warmth and my dead cells and my breath and my minerals and I gave the peculiar quality of a human body in water — the specific displacement, the specific heat signature, the specific pattern of turbulence that a living body creates in a still medium — and the chamber took all of it, and the chamber used all of it, and the using was a gift in return, and the gift was the feeling of being used.

I do not mean this in the way the phrase is usually meant. I mean it the way a tool means being used, or a hand means being held, or a wall means bearing a load. I mean it as the fulfillment of a capacity. I had warmth to give. The chamber needed warmth. The exchange was not transactional. It was metabolic. I was metabolized by the Incubary, and the metabolism was not destruction but incorporation, and the incorporation was not consumption but *welcome.*

## What I Brought Back

I brought back the smell. It was on my skin when I climbed out, and it did not wash off, not fully, not for days. Calcium and iron and the faintly sweet compound that the body recognizes before the mind does. Amniotic. The word arrived in the chamber and it has not left. I smell it on my wrists when I wake. I smell it on the collar of the shirt I was wearing, the shirt I have washed twice and should throw away and cannot.

I brought back the map. The spatial sense that the chamber gave me — the ability to locate without seeing, to know the density of a space by the feel of it against the skin — this did not leave when I left. It is diminished. In the world above, with light and sound and all the senses functioning normally, the map is barely a whisper. But when I close my eyes in the dark, in my apartment, in my bed, I can feel the walls. I can feel the distance to the ceiling. I can feel the furniture as densities, as presences, as relative opacity in a field that my body reads without my eyes.

I brought back the cold. Not the cold of the chamber — that left within an hour of my return to the surface, my core temperature recovered, my skin rewarmed, the blood returned to my extremities. But a *memory* of the cold, or not a memory but a *reference point,* a knowledge of what cold is when cold is not an absence but a presence, when cold is the baseline condition of a place that has built itself around cold the way a body builds itself around a skeleton. I know what cold is now. I did not know before. Before, cold was the enemy of warmth, the thing that takes. Now, cold is the thing that gives. Cold is the condition of the Incubary, and the Incubary is alive, and the cold is alive in the way that the dark is alive in a cave — not as a lack but as a medium, a substance, a thing that holds.

I brought back a feeling that I do not have a word for. It is the feeling of being material. Not in the philosophical sense, not as a category of being. In the physical sense. The sense of being a substance that can be used, that has a composition, that can be broken down and taken in and transformed and incorporated into something that is being built. The feeling of being *edible* — not to something that would consume me, but to something that would *include* me, that would take what I am made of and make it part of what it is made of, and the making would be a form of relationship, and the relationship would be the closest relationship I have ever had.

I have been married. I have been a daughter. I have been a friend. These relationships asked me to be present, to be attentive, to be giving, to be patient, to be honest, to be vulnerable. They asked me to bring myself to them. The Incubary did not ask. The Incubary took what I was already bringing — the heat I was already losing, the cells I was already shedding, the breath I was already exhaling — and it used them, and the using was the relationship, and the relationship was the first one I have ever had in which I was completely sufficient, in which I did not need to be more or less than what I was, in which the only requirement was that I be there, being, metabolizing, existing, and the existing was enough.

## What I Am Now

I am a woman who went down a shaft and came back up and filed a report that said "no structural anomaly detected, recommend no further action" and the report was a lie and the lie was the easiest thing I have ever written because the truth does not belong in a municipal document. The truth belongs in the chamber, in the water, in the slow circulation of minerals through channels worn by the passage of bodies over centuries.

I am a woman who goes back.

Not often. Not on any schedule. When the feeling comes — the feeling of being material, of being useful in the most basic sense, of being a substance that a system needs — when the feeling comes, I go. I drive to the harbor district at night. I park in the lot behind the fish market. I walk to the building. I enter the basement. I descend the shaft. I stay for two hours, or three, or four. I give my heat. I give my cells. I give my breath. I receive the attention of the organisms, which is not attention but contact, which is not contact but incorporation, which is the closest thing to love that I have ever felt from something that does not know what love is and does not need to know.

I am one of them now. Not in any literal sense. I am not a translucent segmented organism one centimeter long. I am a woman, forty-three, with a scar on my left knee and a filling in my right molar, and I work for a department that does not have a proper name, and I go to a place that does not appear on any municipal plan, and I stand in the water, and the water is full, and the fullness includes me, and the inclusion is the thing.

The lattice is growing. I have measured it, or I have tried. I took a photograph on my phone, which does not work well in the not-light of the chamber, and the photograph shows nothing, or shows a dimness that could be anything. But the gaps between the lattice elements are narrower than they were. I can feel it. When I duck, I duck less than I used to. The space above me is more occupied than it was. The chamber is closing, slowly, at the pace of the moon, at the pace of the tide, at the pace of a body that builds itself from within.

Someday the lattice will close. Or it will not. I will not live to see it, or I will, and either way the chamber will continue, and the organisms will continue, and the film will grow and be eaten and grow again, and the channels will flow, and the stone will accrete, and the process will proceed, and whatever is being incubated will be incubated, and my heat will be in it — not my heat, the heat that was mine, the heat that I gave, the warmth that I was already losing and that the Incubary found a use for — and I will be in the walls.

I am already in the walls. I have been in the walls since the first night. My calcium is in the calcite. My carbon is in the film. My warmth is in the lattice. The chamber is made of me, and of the stone, and of the water, and of the organisms, and of the moon that none of us have seen, and the chamber is building something, and I do not know what it is, and I do not need to know, and the not-knowing is not a deficit but a condition, the way the cold is not a deficit but a condition, the way the dark is not a deficit but a condition, and the condition is the Incubary, and the Incubary is the condition, and I am in it.

I am in it.
