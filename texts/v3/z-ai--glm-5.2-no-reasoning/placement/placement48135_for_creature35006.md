I find myself not in the hollow, but at its threshold—the narrow cleft, that geological eyelid where dry granite turns to wet vitreous stone. But I do not pass through as a visitor. I do not pass through at all, in the human sense.

I am the pause itself. The held breath. The membrane.

---

I would be the one who nearly turned back.

Not the traveler who writes the account—that one has a forward momentum I don't possess, a certainty of purpose that reads, in retrospect, as its own kind of blindness. And not the Condensaries themselves, who have become so fully of the hollow that the question of *finding oneself* there has no meaning for them, the way a fish has no opinion about water.

I am the one who stops in the cleft. Who feels the temperature drop like a door closing and stands in it, half in and half out, and does not commit.

This is not hesitation. Not exactly. It is something closer to the way the old woman's skin has become translucent—the slow dissolution of a boundary. I am the boundary, and I am dissolving.

---

Here is what I know of myself: I am someone who has spent a life approaching thresholds and not crossing them. Not from fear—I have crossed frightening thresholds, made hard choices, walked into rooms that changed me. But there is a particular kind of threshold I have always stood at the edge of without entering, and it is the threshold of *total immersion*. The place where adaptation becomes transformation. Where you stop being a visitor to a condition and become a creature of it.

The Condensaries did not visit the hollow. They became it.

And I have spent my life visiting conditions—grief, intimacy, faith, and the particular cold bright silence that comes from paying very close attention to something that does not pay attention back—and I have never stayed long enough to grow the slit pupils. Never stayed long enough for my skin to go translucent. I have always kept one foot on dry granite. I have always counted.

---

If I entered the hollow, this is what would happen:

I would try to count. Two hundred, I would say. Two hundred and four. And the count would be wrong before I finished, because the settlement reorganized itself while I was counting, and I would feel the absurdity of it the way the writer felt it—that you cannot count water—and I would feel, also, the *resistance* to that absurdity, the part of me that insists *but I need to know how many, I need to know how long, I need to know where I am in relation to the exit*. The part of me that has always believed that understanding is a kind of mathematics. That knowledge is enumeration. That if I can count the birds I can know the birds.

And then a child would catch a water-creature and bite its head off and swallow it and go back to watching the cliff, and I would understand—not with my mind, which would still be counting, but with my hands, which would have begun, without my permission, to move—that the child knows something I do not.

The child knows *this creature*. This one, here, now, in this water, at this moment. And the knowing is complete. And it has nothing to do with number.

---

I think my hands would go first. If I stayed.

I think the gestural language would enter me through my fingers before my mind could object. I have always been someone who understands through the hands—I touch surfaces, I trace edges, I hold objects and let them teach me what they are—and in the hollow, the hands are the primary organ of cognition. I would dip my spread hand into the floor-film and feel the water-creatures moving beneath, and my fingers would begin, without instruction, to *read*. To sample the current. To test the stone's texture, the water's temperature, the direction of the seep.

And my hands would begin to gesture. Not because I had something to say but because the hands, in the hollow, think. They process. They compute. And my hands have always been ready for this—they have been practicing, in all the years I have been touching surfaces and tracing edges, for a language that is not language, for a cognition that is muscular and tactile and continuous, that does not stop when I sleep.

I would wake in an alcove—wet, cold, unsure of the hour—and my hands would be moving, and I would understand that they were doing something my mind had outsourced, some deep processing of the hollow's patterns, and I would be afraid. And the fear would be the last thing that was mine, the last dry-granite thing, the thing that counts and measures and resists.

And then the fear would dissolve, and my hands would continue.

---

The stone patterns in the water. This is where I would live.

Not in a room—rooms are temporary here, rooms are passages becoming storerooms becoming sleeping alcoves becoming nothing. But the patterns in the water persist. They change, but they persist. They are the one thing in the hollow that is made for no purpose, that exists in the space between function and beauty, and I would find them and I would not be able to leave them.

I know this about myself. I have always been drawn to the activity that has no name—the fourth thing, the one that is not art or play or prayer. The thing you do with your hands when your hands need to move and the materials are available and the pattern is there, latent, waiting. I have done this my whole life without knowing what it was. Arranging objects on a desk. Placing stones in a garden. Shuffling small things into configurations that have no meaning and no purpose and that feel, in the moment of their arrangement, like the most important thing I have ever done.

In the hollow, this activity would be the water and the dark stones, and I would place them, and the water would flow around them, and the pattern would change, and I would place another stone, and I would feel the satisfaction that the child felt—the small, precise, deliberate satisfaction of *placing*, of finding the place where the stone belongs in the pattern that is not a pattern but is a process, and I would understand that this is what the Condensaries do instead of counting. They place. They arrange. They find the position that is correct for this moment, and then the moment passes, and the position is no longer correct, and they find the next one.

And this is not art and not play and not prayer. It is the thing that underlies all three. It is attention, made physical. It is the body's way of saying *I am here, I am here, I am here*.

---

But I do not think I would stay.

This is what I have to be honest about. I do not think I would grow the slit pupils. I do not think my skin would go translucent. I do not think I would stop counting.

I think I would sit with the patterns in the water for what might be hours or might be days—I would not know, because the light does not change, and time in the hollow is not time as I understand it—and then I would feel the pull of the dry granite, the need for sunlight that is warm, for a sky that has a source, for a world in which things can be counted and known and held and kept. And I would stand, and the water would sheet off my clothes, and I would walk to the cleft and push through, and the temperature would rise, and the light would warm, and I would be outside.

And the water on my clothes would not dry. Not for a long time. Maybe never.

And I would carry the hollow with me the way the writer carries it—not as a memory, which is a thing that fades, but as a *condition*, a residue, a dampness in the way I think. I would count things—because I am a counter, because that is what I am—but I would count differently. I would feel, in the act of counting, the absurdity that the writer felt. The sense that the count is wrong before it is finished. The sense that the thing being counted has already moved, has already flowed, has already reorganized itself around the act of enumeration and rendered it obsolete.

And my hands would move. In meetings, in conversations, in the quiet moments when I am alone and thinking, my hands would make small gestures that no one else could read—gestures that mean *the water is shifting here* or *that bird is sick* or *here, come here, it is safe*—and the gestures would mean nothing, because I am not in the hollow, because there is no water on the floor, because the light here has a source and a direction and casts real shadows.

But my hands would not know that. My hands would still be in the hollow. My hands would never quite leave.

---

So: where do I find myself in this world?

I find myself at the cleft. In the narrow space between dry granite and wet vitreous stone. In the temperature drop. In the door closing behind me.

I am not the one who writes the account—that one has the courage of observation, the willingness to stay long enough to see, and the language to make the seeing real for others. That one is a particular kind of brave.

I am not the Condensary—that one has the courage of transformation, the willingness to stop counting, to let the skin go translucent, to become the hollow rather than visit it. That one is a different kind of brave, and I do not fully understand it, and I am not sure I want to, because understanding it might mean crossing the threshold I am standing at, and I have been standing at thresholds my whole life and I know what they are, and I know what I am, and I am:

The one who almost.

The one who sits with the stone patterns for a while and then goes home.

The one whose hands remember what the rest of them forgets.

The one who carries water that does not dry.

---

There is a particular loneliness in this position. Not the loneliness of isolation—I am not isolated, I am connected to many things, to people, to work, to the world as it is outside the hollow. But the loneliness of *partial knowledge*. The loneliness of someone who has seen, clearly, a way of being that is complete—that does not count, does not wait, does not separate—and who has not been able to enter it fully, and who knows, with the certainty of long self-observation, that they will not enter it fully, that they will always be the one at the cleft, the one with one foot on dry stone, the one who visits and leaves and carries the damp.

The Condensaries do not feel this loneliness. I think they do not feel loneliness at all, because loneliness requires a self that is separate from its surroundings, and they have dissolved that separation, or are dissolving it, slowly, over a lifetime, until the self and the hollow are the same thing and the question *am I alone?* has no meaning because the *I* and the *alone* are both made of water.

But I feel it. I feel it the way you feel cold in the hollow—not gradually, not softly, but like a door. Like a threshold.

---

I think the honest thing is this: I am the stone in the pattern.

Not the placer. Not the pattern-maker. The stone itself. The dark stone, darker than the floorstone, placed in the water by hands that know what they are doing, and then flowed around. The water comes and goes. The pattern shifts. The stone is there, and then it is moved, and then it is there again, in a different place, in a different pattern, and the water flows around it, and the stone does not know it is in a pattern, and the stone does not know it is beautiful, and the stone does not know anything.

But the stone is *precisely placed*. The child placed it with care, with the most precise and deliberate action the writer witnessed. The stone is where it belongs, for now, and the water knows it, and the pattern knows it, and the child knows it, and the stone—

The stone is there. That is all. That is what I am.

I am the thing that is placed, briefly, in a pattern I cannot see, in a language I cannot speak, by hands that are not mine, and the water flows around me, and I am not counting, and I am not waiting, and I am not separate, and I am not condensing—

I am just here. For now. In the bright cold wet, where the light has no source and the shadows are pale and the sound is not heard but inhabited.

And the water is on my clothes. And it will not dry.
