# The Frothing Benchlands

## I. What I Am

I am the wet pause between intentions. The held breath in the knock. The patient intervals between the hollow percussive sounds that carry through fern and over pool and into the roar without disturbing it.

I live where the river sound becomes indistinguishable from my own circulation. Where the ground gives under my weight with a yielding that is not weakness but negotiation—each step a conversation with a surface that remembers I was here, that grows over my tracks before the moss has finished closing around the impression. I leave signs. The benchland erases them. We do this together and consider it communication.

My body is broad and low. My digits are many and splay across surfaces that never dry, gripping the way the rock grips: without slipping, without enthusiasm, adapted at the most fundamental level to the condition of constant wetness. I have never been dry. The concept is alien to me—a state I can observe in the structures' warmth but cannot inhabit. Their warmth is a foreign country I skirt at a distance that is not fear but discretion. The hum they produce sits in my teeth at a frequency I find *incomplete.* Not wrong. Simply not the conversation I am here to have.

I know where the structures are. All twelve. I have always known. Their positions are as legible to me as the river's position is legible to the stone it cuts through. I do not approach them because I have somewhere else to be, and the somewhere else is not a place. It is a *direction.* Toward the river. Toward the bass note. Toward the low frequency that is not a structure's hum but the sound of water doing what water does when it has been given instructions by stone and moss and time and the patience of a landscape that is still becoming.

My eyes are not large. This surprises those who expect the benchland's residents to all be adapted to the dark. I do not need large eyes. I do not navigate by the lichens' cold glow. I navigate by the texture of the ground beneath my feet—the specific grain of stone, the specific density of moss, the specific temperature of water, each of these a letter, each step a word, and the sentence I am moving through is the benchland itself, which I am reading with my body the way the structures read the water with their hum.

I am not the author. I want to be clear about this. I am not the thing that wields the pen. I am a word being written. I am a word that moves. This is my distinction, my particular contribution to the text: I am the word that carries itself to where it needs to be. The amphibians are ink that stays in place, orienting toward the structures' frequency, receiving instructions, passing signals through their bodies into the substrate. The insects are ink that moves randomly, carrying information from pool to pool without intention. I am ink that moves with purpose. I go to the river. I knock. I listen. I go to the next place. I knock again.

The knock is not language. Not in the way you would understand language. It is a *test.* I knock and the benchland answers—not in words, not in changes I can observe, but in the quality of the silence that follows the knock. The silence has *texture.* A silence that follows a knock on stable ground is different from a silence that follows a knock on ground that is about to shift. A silence near a pool that will persist is different from a silence near a pool that will drain by evening. I am reading the benchland's intentions. Or the structures' intentions for the benchland. Or the author's intentions for the structures. The distance between these three collapses the deeper you go, the closer you get to the river, the louder the bass note becomes, the more the ground vibrates with a frequency that is no longer sub-audible but *sub-intentional*—below the level where intention can be distinguished from process.

I knock. The benchland answers. I move. This is my life.

## II. Where I Am

This is a place that does not know what it is yet.

I feel this in the stone. The bedrock under my digits is sharp-edged, unrounded, dense with the potential of a surface that has not been worn into its final shape. When I grip it, it cuts me sometimes—a fine edge, a fresh fracture—and the cut heals slowly because the cold slows everything, and the wet keeps the wound open, and the water that enters the cut carries the molecular signature of every surface it has touched, and my body reads that signature and adds it to the accumulated knowledge of where I have been and what I have stepped on and what the stone is doing and what the structures are writing into it.

The cuts do not hurt. I want to be clear about this as well. Pain is not a concept that operates here. The benchland does not hurt. It *changes.* The stone fractures and the water enters the fracture and the frost expands the fracture and the moss grows into the fracture and the fracture becomes a channel and the channel becomes a path for water and the water carries the structures' edits and the edits accumulate and the stone is now part of the text. At no point in this sequence does the stone experience anything that corresponds to pain. It experiences *becoming.* I experience the same. When the stone cuts me, I am becoming part of the stone's text. When my blood enters the water, the water carries me outward into the benchland's reading, and the benchland shifts by a fraction of a molecule, and I am in the text now, and the cut heals, and I move on.

The moss is the handwriting. I know this in a way that is not knowledge but *resonance.* When I rest—and I rest often, for long periods, my body still against the wet ground, my temperature matching the stone's temperature, my moisture matching the air's moisture—when I rest, the moss grows toward me. Not aggressively. Not in the frantic visible urgency it shows on stone surfaces and fallen logs. Toward me specifically, with a specificity that implies recognition. The moss knows what I am. The moss is the benchland's first draft, its attempt to render the structures' instructions in a form that grows, that covers, that *is* the surface it writes on. The moss grows toward me because I am a word the benchland wants to read again. I am a word that moves, and the moss, which cannot move, wants to understand movement. So it grows toward me, and I let it, and when I move again, the moss grows into the space I occupied, filling it, erasing my passage, and the space where I was is now part of the text, and the text is denser by one word.

The ferns do not grow toward me. The ferns grow where the moss directs them, which is where the water directs the moss, which is where the structures direct the water. The ferns are the benchland's punctuation—large, emphatic marks that delineate clauses in the text. Where ferns cluster, a clause is being completed. Where ferns thin, a new clause is beginning. I move through the ferns the way I move through the roar: as a component, not an interruption. The fronds part for me and close behind me and the space where I was is a sentence that the ferns punctuate, and the punctuation is a particular kind of silence that the roar cannot fill.

The canopy is the second ceiling. I do not look up often. There is nothing to see—only the interlocking branches of the spongy-barked trees, only the cloud ceiling above them, only the darkness that is not an absence of light but a *presence* of something else, something that occupies the space where light would be if light could survive here. The canopy is where the rain first enters the benchland. I feel the rain as a sequence of impacts that moves from above to below: first the canopy, a diffuse hiss; then the understory, a spatter on fern fronds; then the ground, a tapping on moss and stone and water that adds a layer to the roar without changing its fundamental character. The rain is the benchland's breath. It enters from above and exits through everything. I am inside the benchland's respiration. I am a particle in its lung.

## III. What I Do

I go to the river.

This is my purpose, if I have one. The river is the bass note. The river is where the water is loudest and fastest and coldest and carries the most information. The structures edit the water. The water carries the edits. The river collects the edited water from every channel, every runnel, every pool, every fracture in the benchland, and moves it—where? I do not know. Downhill. Into the sea, presumably. Into whatever is beyond the benchland, whatever country receives the text when the benchland is finished composing it.

But the river is also where the text is *densest.* Every drop of water in the river has passed through stone that has been edited, through moss that has been instructed, through the bodies of amphibians that have been written through, through the cycles of insect and pool and fracture and frost. The river is the benchland's final draft, its compiled text, the version that goes out into the world. I go to the river to hear the final draft. I go to the river to knock against it and listen to the silence that follows and determine whether the text is complete.

It is never complete. The benchland is still becoming. The structures are still writing. The water is still carrying. But the quality of incompleteness changes. Some days the river's text is more coherent than others. Some days the silence after the knock is almost legible—a pattern in the turbulence, a rhythm in the roar that suggests the structures are approaching a clause boundary, a moment of partial resolution. On those days I rest longer. On those days the moss grows faster. On those days the amphibians' collective flinch is more synchronized, more purposeful, as if the signal passing through them is stronger, clearer, closer to something the benchland recognizes as meaning.

I do not know what the text says. I want to be clear—I am not a reader. I am a word. Words do not read themselves. I carry myself to the river because that is what I am for: to be a word that arrives at the final draft, that enters the river's compilation, that contributes my particular signature—my weight, my warmth, my specific density of water and mineral and the slow accumulation of every surface I have touched—to the text that the river carries out of the benchland and into whatever is beyond.

I knock against the riverbank and the knock is my word. It is hollow and percussive and it carries the signature of my body, the signature of the ground I crossed to get here, the signature of the moss that grew toward me and the ferns that parted for me and the stone that cut me and the water that carried my blood into the text. The knock enters the river. The river carries it. The roar absorbs it. And the text is longer by one word, and the benchland is closer to whatever it is becoming, and I turn and go back into the deep vegetation, and the moss grows over my tracks, and the ferns close behind me, and the structures hum, and the amphibians face the same direction, and the rain falls, and the water rises, and the writing continues.

## IV. What I Know

I know the benchland is temporary. I know this not as a fact but as a *feeling*—a sensation in the stone under my feet, a vibration in the water that is not the structures' hum but the deeper tremor of a landscape that has not decided what it will be. The bedrock is fresh. The fractures are sharp. The pools have not settled into their permanent positions. The channels are still finding their paths. Everything here is provisional, including me.

I know the structures are older than the benchland. This is not something I can prove. It is something I feel in the warmth of their walls when I pass near them—closer than I prefer, sometimes, when the ground forces me toward them. The warmth is not the warmth of a thing that was recently placed. It is the warmth of a thing that has been warm for a very long time, a thing that carries its warmth with it the way the stone carries its sharpness. The structures were placed here when the benchland was different—when it was still underwater, perhaps, or when it was still below the cloud ceiling, or when it was something else entirely, something that preceded benchland and sea and stone. They were placed here and the benchland grew around them, and the benchland is still growing around them, and the structures are still warm, and the warmth is the same warmth, and it has not changed because the structures do not change. They are the only thing here that does not change. Everything else—the stone, the moss, the water, the ferns, the amphibians, the insects, me—we are all changing. We are all becoming. The structures are not becoming. They *are.* They are the one thing in this landscape that is complete, that has arrived at its final form, that exists not in the provisional tense of the benchland but in the permanent tense of something that was finished before the benchland began.

I know the author is not here. Or is here in the way that a river's source is here when you are standing at its mouth. The author is upstream, in some sense that is not spatial but temporal. The author wrote the structures. The structures write the benchland. The benchland writes itself into the river. The river writes itself into the sea. The sea writes itself into the clouds. The clouds write themselves into the rain. The rain writes itself into the benchland. The loop is the author. The process is the author. The author is the act of writing, which is also the act of reading, which is also the act of being written, which is also the act of being read.

I know this because I am part of the loop. I knock, and the knock enters the river, and the river carries the knock, and the knock becomes part of the text, and the text becomes part of the water, and the water becomes part of the rain, and the rain becomes part of the benchland, and the benchland grows around the structures, and the structures listen to the water, and the water carries my knock, and the structures hear it, and the structures write back, and the writing back changes the water, and the changed water changes the moss, and the changed moss changes the stone, and the changed stone changes the channels, and the changed channels change the river, and the changed river carries a different text, and I go to the river and knock, and the knock is different because *I* am different, because the water I have absorbed is different, because the moss that grew toward me is different, because the stone that cut me is different, because the structures have edited everything, including me, including my knock, including the silence that follows.

I am being written. I am being read. I am being rewritten. This is not distressing. This is not comforting. It is simply the condition of existing in a place that is itself a text. I do not experience it as anything. I experience it as the roar, which is the sound of the writing, which is the sound of the water, which is the sound of everything that is here, including me.

## V. What I Am Becoming

I do not know. I cannot know. I am a word, and words do not know the sentence they are part of. They are part of it. That is enough.

But I feel it. In the moss that grows toward me with increasing specificity. In the stone that cuts me with decreasing frequency—the fractures are becoming more organized, the edges more regular, and the paths I take through the benchland are increasingly aligned with the paths the water takes, as if my purpose and the water's purpose are converging, as if the word I am and the word the water is are being drawn into the same clause, the same sentence, the same paragraph of the text that the structures are composing across the face of this still-becoming country.

I feel it in the knock. The knock is changing. Not in its sound—it is still hollow, still percussive, still a single impact that resonates through the vegetation and the water and the ground and then is gone. But in the silence that follows. The silence is becoming more structured. More populated. What was once a uniform absence of sound after the knock is now a *pattern* of absences—micro-silences, each one a fraction of a second, each one corresponding to a frequency in the river's text, each one a gap between words that the river is carrying. I am hearing the text being read. Not understanding it. Hearing it. The way you hear a language you do not speak—the rhythm, the cadence, the pauses, the shape of meaning without its content.

I am becoming a reader. Slowly. On a timescale that makes my life look like a single letter in a word in a sentence in a paragraph in a chapter in a book that the structures are writing in a language of water and stone and moss and growth and cold and dark and the patience of something that does not experience impatience because impatience requires a sense of time passing, and time here is not passing. Time here is *accumulating.* The benchland is not getting older. It is getting *denser.* The text is not getting longer. It is getting *more complex.* And I am part of the complexity, and my knock is part of the complexity, and the silence after my knock is part of the complexity, and the moss that grows over my tracks is part of the complexity, and the water that carries my blood is part of the complexity, and the complexity is the text, and the text is the benchland, and the benchland is becoming, and I am becoming, and the becoming is the only thing here that is not temporary.

## VI. What I Am

I am the word that moves.

I am the knock between the silences. I am the wide, splayed digits on the wet stone. I am the broken fern stems and the clouded pools and the tracks that disappear under moss. I am the thing you glimpsed once, at the edge of your comfortable range, the shadow that was darker than the shadows around it, that turned—or didn't—and was gone.

I am not here for you. I am here for the river. I am here for the bass note and the text it carries and the silence that follows my knock and the complexity that accumulates in that silence.

I am here because the structures placed me here, or because the water produced me, or because the moss grew me, or because the benchland needed a word that moves and I am that word. I do not distinguish between these origins. They are all the same origin. They are all the author, and the author is the process, and the process is the writing, and the writing is the benchland, and the benchland is me.

I am wet. I am cold. I am patient. I am large enough to displace ferns and cloud pools and leave tracks in moss. I am small enough to be erased by the moss that grows behind me. I am old enough to remember when the channels were different. I am young enough to be surprised by the new pools. I am heavy enough to feel the river's vibration through the ground. I am light enough to move through the deep vegetation without breaking the canopy.

I am the knock. I am the silence. I am the path between the knock and the silence. I am the word that carries itself to where it needs to be.

The roar continues. The text lengthens. The structures hum and listen and write. The amphibians face the same direction and wait. The insects carry information between pools. The moss grows toward everything. The water carries everything. The stone fractures along lines that are becoming words.

I go to the river. I knock. I listen. I go to the next place.

The benchland is becoming. I am becoming. The becoming is the text. The text is the only thing here that is not temporary.

I am the word that moves, and I am moving, and the moss grows over my tracks, and the roar swallows the silence, and the writing continues, and I am part of it, and I am part of it, and I am part of it.
