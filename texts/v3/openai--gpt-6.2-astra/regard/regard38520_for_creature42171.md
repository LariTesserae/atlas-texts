The morning the decking came down was a Thursday. The first anybody knew of it at the depot was that one of the empties, standing upside down on the draining board, gave back a little milk. There had been a spoonful lodged in the shoulder of the bottle by the eastward pull. The man who washed the bottles put his hand under it, too late, and called to the milk boy that he might as well take the new card.

Up at the step there was a difficulty which had not appeared on the plan. A swallow had built on the back of the riser. There had been a ledge there for eleven years, under cover of the felt, with a good clear approach over the east. The new street, coming into its place, was about to make a box of it. There were four young in the nest. They had reached the age at which the yellow corners of their mouths are the widest parts of them.

The power that had been attending to the valley could have held the street where it was. It would have meant very little to the power; to a woman waiting on the far side with a pan of water gripped between her knees, watching the bubble in a spirit level so as to know when it was safe to stand up, it would have meant rather more. Besides, there would be swallows again next year.

What it did was to take hold of the step. There are two ways of fitting a step to a change of level. It can be the last of the low street, projecting, or the first of the high street, let in. The surveyors had chosen the second way. With scarcely any interruption to the fall of iron dust from the screw holes the power made it the first. The step came out into the old street by the length of a man's shoe. This left a space behind the riser, open at each end, and put the bottle ring in the sun.

It was a modest alteration but a rather nice piece of work. The men at the rolling mill have been up since to look at it. The enamel goes round the corners without a crack. Underneath, where nobody but a man lying on his back would think to put a hand, there is even a little turned lip to keep the rain from running in. The swallow came in at one end with a fly, fed the wrong child twice and went out at the other.

By then the pan of water was on the stove. A strap the breadth of a bed sheet lay down softly in the school yard, causing a great scattering of children who had been told for years what would happen to them if they ever stood on a strap and who now had the greatest difficulty in not doing so. In the houses there had been less disturbance than on a Monday when the sheets were hauled in. The blue saucer remained in its groove. One or two things fell *up*: a cork out of a hole in the schoolroom floor, the old woman's thimble which had been missing since the railing was put in. These had been held in place by stresses belonging to the suspended town and no longer required of it. The old woman had bought another thimble and was glad all the same.

The milk boy got to the last house on the old street two minutes late. There was a bad stopper in one of the bottles and he had been back to change it. From there he could see his own front door, standing open, and the clean rectangle on the step where his wife had taken in the mat to beat it. His daughter was nowhere in sight. As he came up he noticed a white thread running from the latch, along the skirting and away up the stairs. It lifted clear of the floor when he knocked.

There was a wait. Across the street the girl opposite came to her window in her stockinged feet. She had got the knock first. There was a mistake somewhere in the arrangements upstairs; he could hear his daughter laughing and furious, trying to get out of something without breaking it. The girl opposite reached behind her and gave a quantity of line off whatever she had it wound on. Presently his daughter came downstairs with a large loop round one knee.

She shut the door in his face.

He put his crate down and knocked again. It was a different noise with the door shut; he could feel it come back through his knuckles, a good house noise which he remembered from being small and sent out without a key. She opened it a crack.

“Is it the milk?”

“It is.”

“We've had ours.”

This was perfectly true. He had brought it home the night before. She let him in nevertheless. His wife was in the kitchen pouring the night's milk into a jug so that he should at least have an empty to take away. For the rest of his nine minutes he sat in the good chair with his coat on while his daughter worked at the knot round her knee. When she got it loose the line ran smartly out under the door. From across the street came the knock of a hook, once and then twice and then a good deal more.

The new card has needed very little correcting. They have taken a minute off at the milk boy's house and added it at the step, where in the fine weather there is generally somebody down on hands and knees looking in at one end, and somebody else at the other end explaining that from there you get a much better view. The birds are long since out. What there is to see now is six inches of the underside of the eastern decking, the last that can be seen anywhere without taking up a floor. On a bright afternoon, if the person at the other end will move his head a little, the copper is still the colour of a loaf.
