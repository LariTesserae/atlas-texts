The first time the mill missed a stroke, it was the woman putting the cap back on who noticed. The cap had a little play on its chain and ordinarily, while she was doing the threads up, the chain drew tight once against her knuckle. This time it did not. She waited with her hand on the cap. Presently the chain drew tight twice, the second time rather feebly. She entered it in the book as a stroke and a half, for want of a better way of putting it.

There was by then something in the street able to put it rather better. It had felt the stroke which failed to come. Down in the rock, a scale of stone no bigger than a thumbnail had come off the roof of the spring passage and lodged edgewise at the intake. The valve had shut on the stone. Fresh water was going past it the wrong way, just enough to keep the plate for a while at the top of its travel. It was perfectly possible to spoil the mill with a thing of that size. It was also possible to set it right by moving the stone less than the breadth of the chalk on the square.

The thing that had come to Mill Street could have done this. There was no part of the works too far down for it to reach. It could have put the stone back on the rock, with the two white faces fitted together grain for grain, and made the rock as good as it had been the day before. It had a considerable fondness for the street by now. Among other things it liked the time the small grey animal took to let the washer down, after it could have got nothing further by holding it up.

It followed the leak a little way before it did anything. On the spring side of the valve was a chamber which no one from the tender had ever seen. There had been a drawing of it, but the bottom fold of the drawing had worn through and that portion was gone. In the chamber was a shallow bed of stones of much the same size as the one in the valve. Some had lain there long enough to have worn quite round. There were several thousand of them. Across the mouth of the chamber lay an iron comb, made to catch just such scales as this, and there was a tooth missing from the comb. Its stump was bright on one side. It had been missing for less than a week.

So it was not a question of moving one stone, unless one meant to stay.

On the tender the woman took the cap off again and put her bare ear to the mouth of the pipe. The pale tank was nearly empty. What she heard was a long way off and considerably enlarged by the tank, like a chair being pushed back in a room overhead. Then there was a trickling. She had been hearing the chair all her life. She had not heard the trickling before.

The power in the street considered her too. It had not come to take the mill off anybody's hands. The people on the tender each knew things about it which the others did not, and they liked on the whole to go on finding such things out. On the other hand there was no way for any of them to get a look at the iron comb without lifting the plate right out of the turning circle. There was pink showing in the felt all along one edge of the plate.

What it did was to ease the stone out of the valve and bring it up through the works. It came up quite slowly, so as not to catch at a bend, and dropped into the bottom of the pale tank. Then the broken tooth came up. The woman, who still had her ear to the pipe, had to sit down for a moment. Through the inspection glass she saw the two things lying side by side in three inches of clear water.

They were got out with the little net which was kept for the cup on the aerial. Nobody at first knew what the tooth was off. It was square iron for most of its length, but at the unbroken end it had been hammered flat and bent over, and the bend was worn bright inside. The youngest of the tender people passed a piece of wire through the bend and let the tooth swing on it. In that position one could see the use of it. It was not a fixed comb, as it happened. The teeth hung separately from a bar, so that the spring could lift them when it ran hard. They had been put in by somebody who had thought about floods.

They made a new tooth on shore. There was a good deal of discussion as to its length. In the end they made it the length of the old one including what they supposed was still attached to the bar. They brought it out the following week and put it down the inspection pipe on a line, rather on the chance. At the first bend the line went slack. When they drew it up there was nothing on it.

Far below, the old stump came free and the new tooth went on the bar. It was a shade too long. The power took a little off it, no more than would have worn away in a season, and let it swing. The bright stump it delivered into the square of white tiles. This was an error. During the next stroke it saw the small grey chalk come out, go round the stump twice, take hold of the washer and try to set it down in the accustomed place. There was not room. It moved the stump to the tank.

That was nearly all it did that year. It did not take the ache out of the old chalk's crooked plate; on attending to it closely it could find no ache there. It did not clear the weed off the pear. There was very little pear left under the weed, and a great deal living in it. When the eel went by it kept well out of the way.

The stump is in the book now, held to the page with a loop of sail twine. Beside it is the woman's entry about the stroke and a half. There is also a drawing of the chamber, not quite right but quite good enough for making another tooth. At the foot of the page, in pencil because they are not altogether sure, they have written *Put it down loose.*

Sometimes, between the departure of the tender and the first full stroke under the lighter load, there is a small sound in the tank. The people aboard have never heard it. A grain or two of stone comes to rest on the enamel and stays there until next week. Behind the painted ear the old chalk lays one thread along the wire and keeps the other out in the water. There are two things to feel now, very far apart, and it has time for both.
