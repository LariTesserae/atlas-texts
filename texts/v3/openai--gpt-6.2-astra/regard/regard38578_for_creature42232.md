The woman who has the keeping of the tower comes up in October to hear the washer. She remembers having to put her chin to the sill to see it. Her father used to lift her for this, and when he got tired of holding her she could still see what the washer was doing by the light it threw on his spectacles.

She takes a brass rule from her apron and sets it on the outside of the riser. The bottom of the well is below the rule. It was level with it when she was a girl. She goes down seven stairs and opens the next door. The roof in there has a round belly in it which was not there at all when she was a girl. A drop hangs from the lowest part. While she watches, it falls. A little black midge walks out from under its skirts.

The woman can put back metal where metal has worn away. This is what is meant by the keeping of a tower. Up at the top, where the cistern hangs clear of the masonry, there are two great iron ears which she has had to put back nearly every spring of her life. She does it with the flat of her hand. The iron comes back cold, with the hammer marks on it and the same branched inclusion of yellow stone that gave the smith such trouble in the first place. What she puts back stays put until it wears away again.

She goes up to look at the washer once more. If she lays her hand against the bottom of the well she can put it back as far as it has gone, right up to the square corners of the fins. There is no call to do anything so extensive. Still, even a hand’s breadth of new bottom would leave the washer rolling round loose until it had cut itself a groove. And the first thing a washer does when it comes loose is to fall flat.

In the lower basin there is a considerable thickness of iron in places where it is of no particular use. One such place is a boss in the ceiling, left there for a shaft which the builders never fitted. The woman feels round it. There is an old seat for a bearing, wide at the mouth and narrowing towards the back. She takes an impression in the heel of a loaf she has brought up for her dinner. Then she shuts both doors and goes down the stairs eating what remains of the loaf, beginning at the crust so as not to spoil the impression.

For the next three days she works at the bench in the room where the stairs begin. People going up with the oil and coming down with the empty cans find her filing a piece of iron shaped rather like a tobacco spoon, with a thick shank and a bowl so shallow that it will scarcely hold water. On the fourth day she carries it up. The shank goes snugly into the bearing seat. The bowl fits under the belly in the roof, with just room for a drop to swell and fall between them. There she leaves it. There is iron enough in the spoon to last longer than the whole depth of the well has lasted up to now. Moreover it comes out.

It is another winter before the washer cuts through. A scrap of ceiling no bigger than a midge’s wing falls into the spoon and sticks there. For some days there is an unfamiliar click in the upper basin at each turn of the washer. The brown lids shut twice as often as they need to. Then a little track wears bright across the bowl, the two ends of the old groove run neatly into it, and the second click ceases.

Down below, water now travels along the underside of the spoon and falls from the shank. This is quite a distance, in that basin, from where it used to fall. For a time the black midges go on assembling at the old place after each stroke. They stand about on the iron with their legs wide apart, waiting for the skirts of a drop that does not come. One by one they fold their legs somewhat and walk to the new place.

In spring the woman comes back with her rule. She has brought the piece of bread too. It is as hard as wood by now. She means to put it in the drawer with the tower drawings, against the time when somebody else may have the keeping of the stairs. On the blank back of the drawing of the south riser she traces round the spoon. Beside it, in a good large hand, she writes *Draw towards door*. She puts in the washer with one stroke of the pencil and, after a moment’s thought, draws in its hole.
