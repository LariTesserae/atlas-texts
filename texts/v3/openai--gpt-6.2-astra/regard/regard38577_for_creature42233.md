The power came while the thread was still coming in. It had been caught in the moss at its other end. Each pull straightened a little more of it, until the last turn came out from between two leaves and the shrimp went over backwards with the whole thing in its feet. It lay a moment that way. Then it got the thread under the washer. There was nothing for the power to do about the thread.

The silence lasted eleven days. Upstairs they had the end cover off the pump and a canvas tent over the pieces. For the first two days the elbow stayed cold. On the third day there was no longer a wet way down the foot. The moss kept giving water out at the bottom almost as before, though if one had pressed a finger into a cushion it would not have filled up the hollow behind the finger. By the fourth afternoon there was a bright line of iron along the waste lip. Water could still reach it, but none went over.

The power had heard the pump both ways, through the rim and through the feet of the grey animal. They were not at all the same. It would not have stopped the pump on account of what a man heard with his ear against the iron. Neither did it start it now. There was a man sitting in the open casing with his lunch on one knee.

At the back of the condenser house was a stone sill, under a louvre through which the weather came a little way into the building. On a wet day the sill took up water almost to the colour of slate. It held a surprising quantity. The bed of mortar under it went through the wall and down the side of the box, and between the bed and the grout of the fern tile there lacked about the thickness of a sheet of paper. This the power made good. The place where it did so would have come away as grit on the end of a wet match.

Towards evening the bare tile under the fern darkened in the shape of a pear. At the small end of the pear a bead stood out, grew heavy and ran down. A second followed the wet track of the first without having to grow so large. After that it was difficult to say whether anything was coming in there or not. By morning there was no brightness on the lip of the waste.

Nothing in the bowl of the P had to come out of it. The shrimp did come out, towards the end of the afternoon, and spent the better part of an hour on a bolt head which had only a skin of water over its top. Going down the other side it met a much smaller shrimp coming up. There was room for both, if one went a little sideways. The larger one went sideways.

When the cover went back on the pump, the water in the box had fallen short of the old brown mark by less than the height of a printed letter. The first stroke shook a quantity of dust out of the canvas tent. Down below, the teeth came back on the white tiles, beginning at the bolted foot and running the length of the wall. By noon the elbow had sweated through its fresh coat of grey paint. The dark pear under the fern paled at the edges and presently there was bare tile there again.

The power left the grain where it was. In wet weather, with the pump running, the mortar and the grout were wet alike and very little passed between them. In dry weather the old sill could be a fortnight giving up what it had. There was nothing to see on the drain drawing. Even if the grain had been put in, to scale, a line drawn with the very sharpest pencil would have covered it many times over.

On the first afternoon of ordinary running a man knelt at the glass to see if the new joint leaked. He stayed there some time after he had seen that it did not. What held him was a short brown stroke going over a ridge of lime. Just at the ridge it divided into three strokes. Two went down on the far side and were lost at once. The third stayed on top, and presently he made out, a little in advance of it, two fine feelers trying the water on either side. He shifted his hand to give them better light.
