One morning a shell went into a crack point first. The wind had been taking it along in little turns, each turn about as long as the last, so that the flies inside had done no more than shift their feet. At the crack it made half a turn more. The rim came up against one wall and the high part of the back against the other. There was still a good deal of country below it. A grain of grit dislodged by the shell went down tapping for quite a while.

Of the six flies in it, five got out. The sixth had begun to unfold a hind leg just as the shell tipped. A joint of the leg was caught between the horn and the stone. From above there was nothing to see of this animal but its two forefeet, with which it took hold of the rim and pulled at fairly long intervals. On each pull a little more red could be seen, and then rather less than before.

The power that had come to the plateau attended to the fly. It could attend to one thing without the others going dim. It knew where the mother hunter was lying with her foreleg out of place, and how far the damp had come along a bed that had shown no pink in living memory. It knew the weight of the stone on either side of the red joint. This was not much. It was chiefly the fly's own pulling that kept the joint fast.

When the forefeet next let go, the power held the shell still. Without the very slight settling of the horn after each effort there was room for the fly to fold the leg the way it had been folded in the night. For a time it did nothing of the sort. It washed its face. Its forelegs went up together and came down separately, drawing the fine hairs on either side of the mouth to a point. Then the hind leg bent and came free. The fly walked out backwards onto the stone. The shell settled with a sound too small for the fly to feel through its feet, though it was standing quite near.

It was a male fly. There was a comb on the insides of its hind legs with which, after rain, it would dress the two small sails of its wings. For the present it used the leg to walk on. It went down over the lip where there were shellbacks feeding, stepped from a gray back to a clear one, and stood drinking at the edge of a drop. With its head down it looked much longer than it had in the shell.

The power had no occasion to keep it in sight. Far below, the bird had put its bill into its breast feathers and was working out a shaft that had broken close to the skin. There was a considerable itch there. For all the wonderful reach of the neck, the bend required was a difficult one, and the bird had risen on the toes of one foot to make it. The shaft came away. The bird held it crosswise for a moment, as though surprised at how little of a feather had taken up so much of the morning, and let it go. There had been no need to do anything for the bird.

By noon the crack above was in full light. The empty shell was visible some way down, a white crescent with a shadow as fine as a pen stroke along its lower edge. In the evening water began to come out there. It filled the shell before it went any farther. The first shellback to arrive put its foot over the rim, found no bottom at the usual depth, and drew back. It tried again a little to one side. Presently it was hanging quite comfortably above the water, passing the soft end of its foot to and fro along the inner wall. A second animal came up behind it. There was room.
