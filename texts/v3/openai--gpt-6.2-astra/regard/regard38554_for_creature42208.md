On one part of the plateau the seam ran straight out to the lip of an opening. It could be picked up again on the other side, a yellow mark in the white face, a little lower down. There had been a slip there. The two ends of a flute could also be made out, though it would have taken half a day on foot, going round by the head of the opening, to put a hand in one and then in the other.

A family had come up along this seam over a great many seasons. There were the usual knots of trees behind it. The oldest knot had been taken into the grove and could hardly be distinguished from the trees on either side, except in the morning when its leaves turned a little earlier than the rest. The youngest was six trees, still low enough for a travelling animal to catch its loose skin on the upper twigs. The family lived between these and the edge. There was room for one more removal, perhaps two.

Here the attention that had been given to the whole bright surface of the continent came to rest for a while. What it was like to give such attention is difficult to say. The nearest thing in the experience of the little animals was the way an old one, busy with a splinter in one of its feet, could feel a young one beginning to slip from the far end of its back, and put an arm there without leaving off what it was doing. Only in this case there was no far end.

It would have been a small matter to close the opening. But there were things living on the shelves. Some depended on the few minutes of direct light they got when the sun stood along the opening in summer. Down there a change in the shape of the sky no wider than a twig held at arm’s length might make the difference between a summer and no summer at all.

For a long time nothing was done.

In their last room the animals had taken to pushing the empty husks into a disused passage. There was already a good arm’s length of them, packed close. One night an old animal went in after a husk it had dropped and came back with salt on its plate, not the fine dust of the surface but large, square-ended grains. There was a cross seam there. It ran back at a very slight angle to the first, inland, under a lid of salt about as thick as a leaf. Above ground it did not show at all.

The power loosened one grain.

It fell into the passage that night, long after the animal had gone out. There was nothing to hear it. In the morning a second grain, which had rested against the first, came away under the touch of an apricot hair. A little thread of white light lay across the husks. By evening the light was pink. One of the young went up to it and spent some time putting an arm through. It was able to get hold of something on the other side, which proved to be the tip of its own arm coming through another hole a little farther along.

The place was quite unsuitable for a door. It took the old animals nine nights to make a door of it. For the first three they carried the square grains out through the old opening, one at a time, although by the second night there was a hole large enough to put a grain through directly. They had their ways of doing things. Nor did they all take to the new seam. Two started back towards the six trees and made a room among the roots, where, contrary to what might have been expected, they did very well. The others followed the cross seam inland. Their first room there had an awkward step across the middle. For several generations the young of that family learned to go up a step backwards before they learned to go down it forwards.

The grove has turned a corner. From the opposite lip it is possible to see the whole turn, dark trees at the elbow, a thinning line of pink going away over the rise. Someone looking for the work of the power would be likely to point to that. In fact the turn belongs to the animals. Of the work itself there is very little to see. The grain lay for a time in the old passage. An arm swept it up with some others and it was carried outside. It is out on the white ground somewhere, one face still sharp, the others taking the weather.
