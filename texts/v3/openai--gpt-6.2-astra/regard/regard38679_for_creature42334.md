It was the feet that first attracted the attention of the power. It had been about to warm them. It noticed, just in time, that at the other end of the pipe a flier which had got quite comfortably settled was at some pains to put its feet out too.

The power stayed to see what else it might be wrong about.

For a considerable time it did nothing in the court. It went a little way into the wood, where a human being could not go, and followed one of the streams of ivory animals. At a fork they did not, as appeared to be the case from listening at a wall, divide into two streams. Two streams met there. The animals came out of the damp wood carrying fibre and went back into it empty, passing one another so closely that a loaded one could put its load on an empty one and turn round. A good many did. There were animals that had probably never been more than a foot from the fork, though the stuff they carried went the length of the court.

What became of the fibre interested the power. Some went to the undersides of the sills. Some went to close up a bored hole from which a pipe had been withdrawn eighty years before. A great deal went down. Following it, the power came to water, and under the water to another root, white, with none of the handsome figuring of the pavement. It had grown against a brick arch. All along the arch, in the dark, were the impressions of the brickmaker's thumbs.

Here there was a difficulty. The root was putting out a tip into a space between two bricks. On the other side of the bricks was a tank, in the bottom of which lay six inches of heavy oil. There was no particular hurry. It would take the tip a year to get through the mortar and rather longer to reach the oil. The difficulty was that nobody in the town had the least reason to look there.

The power considered turning the tip. This would have been quite a small piece of work. But there were other tips, and some of the oil had already got into the mortar. There was a plug in the floor of the tank. To draw it would let the oil out into running water which went, in the course of half a mile, under the foundations of a laundry and out into a ditch full of cress. The power left the plug alone.

In the weighbridge office there was a drawer that had not been opened since the works were taken out of the dial. The key had been lost; indeed the lock had been lost, though this was not apparent from the front. The drawer stuck on account of a drawing pin wedged under the left runner. The power pulled the pin out.

Nothing happened for eleven days. Then the girl who had made the observation about the white foot came into the office in search of a pencil. She pulled the drawer by its two handles, as she had done fruitlessly on several previous occasions, and sat down rather hard on the floor with it in her lap.

There were three pencils in it. There was also a plan of the yard on blue linen, and a small brass stencil for putting the word *TARE* on things. By the time Nell had been fetched, the word had been put on the back of the girl's hand and on two of the more expensive copies of the printed account.

The tank was quite plainly marked on the plan. What made Nell look at it twice was not the tank but the flight of steps beside it. According to the plan there were nine steps down from the office to a little landing, and then a turn and seven more. Nell had worked in and out of that office for twenty-six years. She would have said there wasn't room for a broom cupboard behind it. She took the girl round the back to prove this to her and found, under a sheet of corrugated iron on which they were standing, the top step.

After that there was remarkably little for the power to do. Nell had a length of clean glass tube lowered into the tank and brought it up with a finger over the end. The bottom six inches were black. A man came out from the laundry to see what the tank was like, since the laundry had two very like it. He knew a firm that would take the oil away; they would charge for the cartage but pay for the oil. There was some correspondence as to who, exactly, was entitled to sell it. In the end the bill and the payment were made out to the weighbridge, which had never before had any money of its own. The balance bought a handrail for the steps.

When the tank was clean, Nell went down into it. There was a little wet star in the mortar at the bottom. She held the lamp for the girl to see. In the middle of the star, hardly bigger than the end of the pencil she had gone looking for, was a point of white wood, with two ivory animals working at it. The girl lay down at once. Nell, who had brought a hammer and a bag of quick-setting cement, sat on the bag to keep it off the damp.

After a while she took them both upstairs. There seemed no great necessity for stopping the hole.

The tank is not shown to visitors. It is a steep climb back and the air at the bottom makes the lamp smoke. But in the office the plan has been hung under glass at a height from which it can be taken down, and beside it is a book in which anybody may put anything about the town that they know to be the case. The first entry concerns an animal with a white foot. Underneath, in Nell's upright figures, are the dimensions of the tank. Somebody has drawn the flier's feet in the wedding photograph rather well in the margin.

The power comes back occasionally. On one such visit it finds Nell on the bottom step with her boots off. The white tip has divided. One part is travelling up the side of the tank and the other has made a little shelf just clear of the floor, on which she has set her lamp. There is a sound down here so far below the note in the houses that at first she took it for the blood in her ears. She can feel it best through the soles of her feet.

The power sits down a step above her. It has not heard this either.
