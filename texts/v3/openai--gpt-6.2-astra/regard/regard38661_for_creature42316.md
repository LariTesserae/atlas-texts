What took notice of the bend could have brought the rain that morning. It knew where the rain was. Out beyond the mouth of the river, flying fish were already going through patches of fresh water on the skin of the sea. In a few days the wind would turn. There was no advantage in turning it now. The cub that had caught the cane leaf had at least another week in which it could put a foot wrong at the little pool and get no wetter than the shoulder.

For much of the morning the power did nothing. It was good to see the mother go into the hollow smelling of fish and come out smelling of both cubs. It was good, too, when she was asleep, to see a water snake go the whole length of her back within a hand’s breadth of it, on the other side of a fallen stem, neither animal having the least occasion to know about the other.

What the power did was far upriver, at the pump.

There was a board there with the levels of the river marked on it. An older board, which had been on the same post, lay face down under the boards of the pump house floor. The man who ran the pump had never seen it. He had been given a number below which he was to stop pumping, and for the last three nights, though there was still a good deal of work to do in the nursery beds, he had shut the engine off when the water reached the mark. By morning the level always came up a little. He understood this to mean that he was taking no more than the river could spare.

The post had sunk. It had gone down gradually, with the weight of the little landing on it, until the heads of two bolts that had once stood clear of the highest water had mussel shells on them. The numbers on the board were still perfectly legible. It is difficult to see anything wrong with a number which one can read from a boat in the dark.

The power might have lifted the post. Instead it let the man lose a pencil. He was kneeling to put a little oil into the engine when the pencil slipped out of his shirt and went down between the floorboards. It was a good soft pencil, of a sort he could not get locally; he fetched a bar and lifted a board. In feeling about below it his fingers came on the old gauge. There was writing on the back, in the hand of the woman who had taught him to run the pump. It gave the height of the lowest mark in relation to a notch cut in the stone doorsill. The sill was part of an outcrop. It had not moved since there had been a river there.

He spent a hot and rather vexing afternoon with a length of clear hose half full of water. At one end he needed somebody to hold the hose still. By the time the nursery workers went home, three people besides himself had seen what had happened to the post. They set the board right. There was water in the nursery tank for the young trees; the older ones went four days without. He found his pencil, too, though not until he had put the tool box away and was sweeping up.

Nothing much showed in the bend. The gravel under it went on giving up water very slowly. At the pool where the cat had fished there was a bead at the tip of a root, a space of time without a bead, then another bead. A prawn stood on the clay below it with its long feelers bent back by the fall of a drop. In the late afternoon the cubs came out behind their mother. One went down on its belly where the lip was too round to get a purchase. The other saw this happen and went down on its belly too.

The power had no wish to make a habit of losing things for people. It liked the notch in the stone, and the fact that the woman, going away from a place where she had worked for thirty years, had thought to write on a piece of wood that anyone else would have thrown in the stove. It left the bend while there was still light on the water. The last thing it saw there was the mother on her side in the warm leaves, one cub busy at a teat and the other washing, with great application, a forefoot that belonged to its brother.
