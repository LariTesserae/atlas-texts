There is something out beyond the salt which has been attending to all this. It has attended before, though not from so close. Ordinarily what it sees of the town is the change from green to green as the nap lies down under the flood and stands up afterwards. It has taken both colors for the plant. It is interested to find that some of the dark green, just now, is the shirt on a small boy's back.

It could come nearer still. There would be a difficulty about weight. Already the clerk in the freight court has got up from his fruit and is standing with both hands on the table patch. He feels something which it is hard to describe as a load, since there seems to be no street on which it will come down rather than another. Out by the frogs the carters have one last lift to make. It would be awkward if all the ribs were to go soft now and come up somewhere else.

The power puts its weight a long way off, on a floor of bare stone under the departed sea. The clerk feels the table go slack under his hands. He takes one hand away and finds with the other the quite familiar weight of the old woman's bedroom. He gives a short pull on the roller. The room goes up the last six inches so easily that one of the daughters, who has braced herself for something considerable, sits down in her mother's lap.

There is no particular reason to do anything about the town. It is a good size; the frogs are audible; the boy has got his thumbnail in where it ought to go. As for the plant, the power has a notion it would like to give the sea back to it. It could be done by evening. There is still a slight inclination in the plain, impossible to see but apparent if you lay a great deal of water on it. The water would come feeling along the old bed, blue at first and then the color of the soil, and by the time the lamps were lit there would be surf at the foot of Dye Street.

Before doing anything of the kind the power attends a little more closely to the plant.

At ebb the pink skin under the puddles contracts. Much farther down, a considerable thickness of flesh contracts with it. There is a pull on the white hairs in the stone. In fact the whole plain as far as the salt gives a little tug twice a day on what is left of the mountain. The power, which knows something about mountains, had supposed this to be the weight of the absent water, remembered. But there is a little variation in the tug. This evening it comes in two parts, with a wait between. Down in the dark the sewing woman's husband has put his shoulder to a fold. The fold lifts; the fish get past him; and only when his foot is clear does the plant finish drawing in.

It is rather a long way from there to the salt for news of a man's foot to go. The power waits for another tide. By then it has given up the idea about the sea.

What it does, in the end, concerns the stone by the bathhouse. There is a split in the shelf, a very old one, running in from the plain. It has come within the breadth of a blade of grass of the upper face. Some noon quite soon a child landing hard on the shelf will bring away a piece about the size of a pillow, and the white hairs in the grain, which are wonderfully good at holding water, will not be much good at holding that. The power could close the split. Instead it takes advantage of a moment when the bathhouse steps are empty to put a little of its weight on the loose piece.

There is a crack and a flop. The bathhouse keeper comes to the door in time to see a flat stone settle on the velvet. She stands looking at it with her hands under her apron. Then she tries it with a bare foot. It lies quite steady, with a shallow hollow on top and a low edge towards the sun. By supper somebody has put a baby in the hollow. The mother sits on the broken shelf to feed it, which she finds a more convenient height than formerly. There is still a good slide at the other end.

At the foot of Dye Street the boy has the little grit house off the spoon. The underside, which he could not have seen however long he waited on the step, is quite smooth. He puts it against his lip to feel how smooth, and gets sand in his mouth from the other side. He keeps it for three days in one of the little shallow pockets and then loses it getting out of his shirt. For a long while it lies smooth side up among the white things at the base of the nap.

The power goes back beyond the salt. It can tell the boy's shirt from the plant now, even at that distance. On washing days it sometimes watches the wrong house for hours.
