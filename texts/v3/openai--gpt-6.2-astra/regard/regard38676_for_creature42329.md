At the lower end of the pipe, after the water has done its work, it goes out through a stone trough into the river. There is a footboard across the trough. Linnet's sister crosses it twice a day on her way to and from the house where she sorts the pears. She is in the habit of stopping on the board and laying a hand on the rail. It has a shake in it from the wheels, very perceptible through the heel of the hand. She likes to think that some of this is due to water she drank as a girl. She is probably right, though not to anything like the extent she supposes.

The power that had taken an interest in Linnet could feel both ends of the pipe at once. Indeed it took some pains to distinguish them. Things joined by water seemed to it, on first acquaintance, much more nearly in the same place than they do to people who have to buy a ticket. It had seen Linnet read the slip that came in a box of pears, turn it over for the rest of the news, and get up and go about her work on finding the back blank. It had seen her sister, at the other end, lick a pencil which had no business to be in her mouth after the places she had kept it, and sit a long time over that slip before writing, *Are there any of the white ones still?*

It could have let a voice through. The difficulty was deciding when. There are things one says quite freely in a kitchen which one would not choose to say down a telephone, even to a sister. And there was a further difficulty about the boy, on whose account each woman had left several things unsaid for fear of troubling the other. On the whole the power thought it would do better to provide something in the nature of a bell.

The first that Linnet knew of it was at the scullery tap. She had run the can half full for the moss and was waiting for a bit of leaf to come round the inside within reach of her finger. Three little blows came on the pipe. The water, which ordinarily fell in a clear twist, parted into beads for the length of a finger and came together again. After an interval the thing was repeated. Linnet knew that knock. It was the one the two girls had used on the underside of the table to signify that something had better not be said in front of their mother.

She put her mouth rather foolishly to the tap.

‘Grena?’

Nothing happened. She took the handle of the can and gave three taps on the bend of the pipe. Somewhere a long way off her sister let go of an iron rail as if it had grown hot.

The next evening Grena took a spoon with her to work. It was the boy who worked out, in the end, what they could do. There was no use calling words into the pipes. You could shout yourself purple at the scullery end and all that came out below was a noise like a chair being moved in a room overhead. But a blow travelled very well, provided it was struck on a part without paint. The boy brought home the card the gangs used for signalling in fog when there was a break in the channel. It had several expressions of no immediate interest to either woman, including *Send up another man with boots*, but the letters were all there along the bottom.

Their first conversation occupied the better part of two evenings and consisted of the word *pears*, followed by a doubtful letter which might have been an *e* struck twice, and then by *pears* again. Grena was sitting on the footboard in the dusk with her shoes dangling above several hundred tons an hour of used water, laughing so much that the wheelman came out to see what she had lost.

They got quicker. They never got very quick. It was a considerable undertaking to mention a person with a long name, and a piece of news with three such people in it generally went by post. For all that, the slips in the pear boxes began to be written on both sides. It was easier to get on with a letter once one had made a start on it with a spoon.

The power attended for a time to see that the arrangement suited. It had been prepared to do considerably more. The boy's chest, for instance, had occupied its attention; but what his chest chiefly wanted just then was a shorter day in the wet, and this was managed between the sisters without any alteration in the constitution of the boy. They got him taken on for the last two hours of each shift in the shed, painting the numbers a fresh white without filling up the hollows round the figures. He had a good hand for it. He also had the advantage, not shared by everybody in the shed, of knowing a six from a nine by feel.

One evening, coming in late from the laundry, Linnet caught the scullery pipe giving a little jump in its collar. She set down her pole and went to answer it. Grena had nothing in particular to report. She was on the board; it had been warm down there; a fish was standing in the rush of water from the trough, keeping in exactly the same place by means of a prodigious amount of swimming. It took a long time to get this across. Linnet stood with her hip against the dresser, spelling it out, while beyond the window a fullness went out under the moss and another came in, and one of them must have given way as usual. By the time she looked they were both across.

She tapped back that she was there. That was all. Below, her sister put the spoon in her pocket and stayed to watch the fish a little longer. The power, which could have looked through the moss as easily as through the clear water at the foot of the trough, went down to look at the fish too.
