The power that took an interest in the high country first made itself felt there as a difficulty with the wind. On a perfectly still evening a tuft of dun hair on the doorpost blew into the enclosure and blew out again. The rail stretched her neck at it. She had been at some pains to pull that hair through from the other side.

The power had come close in order to listen. There was a sound inside one of the eggs. It was a scraping sound, with a rather long rest after each scrape, and the hen answered it with a note hardly loud enough to be heard beyond her breast. Presently a second egg began. The power withdrew a little way, until the hair hung quiet. It could hear quite well from there.

By morning there were four young rails. They were black, with a sprinkling of silver wires about the head, and had feet of the full size folded up under them. The first to discover what its feet were for walked over the other three and fell off the shelf. It made the descent with its legs working busily and continued across the floor without any noticeable change of gait. The others came down when their mother called. For a while the whole household occupied a patch of wet leaf scarcely larger than the shelf it had left. There was a good crop of wood-shrimps there. A very young rail, given a shrimp, has to run off with it, although there is nobody in pursuit; by the time it has found a sufficiently private place to eat, the shrimp has usually got well down among the roots again.

It was some days before there was anything for the power to do. It spent them partly with the rails and partly at the margin of the country, where the little warm stream went over. The snails there were putting down their eggs. Each snail had found a pocket on the underside of the turf and was busy cementing small clear globes to it. The water, in passing over the lip, drew a little air after it under the bank, so that the eggs, though kept continually damp, were never quite drowned. From below, against the light, the bank had a narrow border of pearls. There was no position from which a person of the ordinary shape could have seen them with any comfort. This was one of the advantages the power enjoyed.

One afternoon it heard the hen calling in an unusual way. She had taken the young along the shelf that grew out into the marsh. Where it crossed a piece of open water, one of them had gone down through a split in the wood. The split widened below into an old hollow, with a little water in the bottom. It was quite a good place for a chick to be, except for the matter of getting out. There were shrimps in it. The chick had eaten two and was trying to climb up the streak of daylight by which they had been visible.

The hen could see it. She put a foot in and drew it out, and for some time the chick continued to jump at the place where the purple toes had been. Then it stood still. The water reached to the loose black fluff of its belly, and presently the fluff began to take up water.

Just by the split a strip of bark had lifted from the shelf. Its lower end was still attached. The power put the least pressure on the other end, rather less than the weight of a rail, and bent it down into the hollow. For a moment nothing happened. Seen from below, the bark looked very much like the rest of the ceiling. Then a shrimp on its underside moved one of its long feelers. The chick reached for it, got a foot on the bark, and went scrambling up into the light with the shrimp held crosswise in its bill. Its mother struck it a light blow on the head and took the shrimp. She divided it between two of the others, who had already been fed.

When the power took its weight off, the bark came up again, but not quite to its former position. A green fibre at the attached end had given a little. There was now a shallow cup in the top of the shelf, which filled at the next shower. For the rest of that summer it was much resorted to by a small yellow fly. The rails went elsewhere. They did not acquire a habit of looking for assistance at a difficulty, nor did the power acquire a particular opinion of the depth at which water ought to stand under a rail.

It came back in the autumn. The homegrown cloud was thinner then, and had a faint smell of ripe seed. At the ring the shelf was empty, the old nest full of round leaves which had fallen silver side up. Out on a dun cow's back stood five brown rails, all of nearly the same size. One had found a tender place at the base of the cow's tail where a considerable quantity of hair could be had for very little pulling. The cow put up with this for a time. Then she gave her skin a shake which travelled the length of her back, lifting each bird in turn an inch into the air. Four came down facing as before. The fifth came down the other way round and discovered a fly.

The power could have told which was the bird it had helped. It was more interested to see how the cow did that with her skin. It stayed until she did it again.
