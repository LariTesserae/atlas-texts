What drew the power's attention was the sound of a rail knocking three times. This was not a signal, though for a while the chicks treated it as one, running first up the gully and then down. It was a double knock coming from the bank and a single knock coming back from across the clearing. The far bank had moved in the night. It now presented a pretty smooth face to the sound, where the day before there had been a skirt of fig roots.

The roots were still there. You had to look under the bank to see them. They hung all in a bunch, with the end of a heron's nest sticking out among them like the handle of a brush. The fig, a good big one with a fork low enough for a rail to walk into without jumping, was standing as straight as ever. Only the ground it stood on had begun to turn over.

The power put a hand under it. A shrimp as clear as glass was tumbling out of a nest cup, turning over and over in the thread of water that had been its home. The water ran down the length of a finger. At the first joint it stood in a pool quite sufficient for the shrimp, which settled at once and began to comb its legs.

It would have been an easy thing to put the bank back. The power could feel, through the breadth of its hand, the dozen or so sound sticks on which the weight would come to rest. It could also feel what lay up against the ends of the sticks. One ended in a rotten fork full of small white beetles; another rested against the roof of a room with a rail sitting in it. The rail had its head turned sideways and was listening to a noise under its breast. From time to time it got up, turned one of the eggs a little way and sat down again. There was grit in the creases of its eyelids.

For the rest of that day the power held the bank where it was. Rain came. The shrimp went off the knuckle backward in a rush of brown water and fetched up in a cup with three others. At evening the herons came in so thickly that for a time the hand felt nothing but the little separate shocks of their feet. After dark a piece of eggshell rolled out of the room. It was white on the inside and so thin that a fly settling on the rim rocked it.

There were five chicks. The last came out well after the other four, in a great state of disorder about the head, with a bit of shell stuck over one eye. Its parent removed this and ate it. By the following evening they were all out of the bank and picking at things on the gully floor. A chick that has spent its whole life so far in a hole will go into another hole at the least alarm. The power waited till they had gone a good way up the gully.

Then, over the better part of a night, it let its hand down. The fig went with it, slowly enough that the gecko on its trunk kept its head uppermost simply by walking round the trunk as it turned. There was a place lower down where the crown of the fig caught in the living branches of two trees. The power tried taking a little weight off its hand and then a little more. The trees bent. Presently the hand came away quite easily, with a few sticks and a damp feather adhering to the palm. Up above there was an opening broad enough to admit the first real fall of sunshine that part of the valley had had in seventy years.

It was not a particularly good place for rails anymore. They had to go some distance round the head of the gully to get from the soft banks to water. But the fall had laid open a quantity of old wood, and for six mornings running you could have seen two adult birds there with five small ones, all taking woodlice out of the same split stick. Far down in the gap the fig put out little rosy points along what had been the underside of a branch.

The power stayed until the first of these had opened. It had never seen a fig leaf open from below. In the light coming through it the veins were dark, and there was the shadow of a very small foot. The foot lifted, and another took its place a little farther on.
