The first thing the power did was to go up to the street end of the vehicle ramp. It had seen the wagtail come down that way, and had a notion of providing a little more cover at the top. There was a hawthorn there already, growing out through the railings, with a very serviceable hole in the middle of it. The wagtail went through the hole without folding its wings. The power went down again.

It took a particular interest in the fourth chick. This was not, as the people at the eastern station supposed, the small one. The small one had been out of its egg for several hours when the fourth chick was still engaged in getting the lid off. The fourth was a capable bird, a little inclined to go on feeding after the others had gone. When a sheet of water came down towards it, it ran ahead of the edge, picking things up; when it wanted to get out it ran to one side. There was a nice distinction between the speed at which it did these two things. The power, which could have held the water back with no more trouble than it cost the bird to lift a foot, had the pleasure of seeing it done the other way.

In the eastern pump house there was a pencil mark on the window frame. If the fitter put the side of her thumb against it, the end of her thumb just covered the brass bolt. It saved a good deal of hunting with the glasses. The mark was on the frame of the upper sash, however, and on warm days somebody opened the window. Several entries in the book to the effect that the family had moved west were due to this circumstance. The power did nothing about the mark either. On quite a number of subjects its opinion agreed with that of the woman who watered the geraniums, which was that a person could look.

There was one thing it altered.

The hollow ridge has an inspection stair every half mile. The stairs go down inside it, with a landing at the bottom and a little glass window through which a person on the landing can see what is happening in the return main. Behind the third stair a length of the old tram rail had been left lying against the wall. At the top end the rail came within an inch of the underside of the loose cover. It was this cover, lifted by the fans and falling back on its rim, that made the leisurely banging sound. Nobody had been able to find it. Stand on it and the noise was somewhere else; get off it, and by the time it banged again you had tried two or three others.

The station people rather disliked the noise. The power did not mind it, but had noticed that an inch or so of movement at the bottom end of the rail would bring the top end under the cover. It waited until the fitter was on the stair for her Thursday reading. As she turned to come up she caught her overall pocket on the rail. This had happened a number of times. On this occasion the power held the stitching of the pocket, which was in rather poor condition. The rail swung round, fell against the back of the stair with a clap, and the fitter said something which was not audible on the flats. After a while it was apparent that neither was the cover.

She went up with a piece of chalk and put a cross on it. On the following shift they packed the rim. It made surprisingly little difference to the general volume of sound. There was still no possibility of shouting to a person on the ridge. What was gone was the business of waiting for it to happen again.

That evening the woman at the eastern window heard a small clear piping. She had the lower sash up and was leaning out to knock the soil off a geranium saucer. There was one note, a space, and then three close together. Out on the flats the hen had settled down with her back to the window. Three chicks were pushing in under her and a fourth was coming along the joint, stopping at intervals to pipe. The woman stayed where she was with the saucer in her hand. She had seen them gather in like this a dozen times and had supposed it to be an entirely silent proceeding.

There was a column for remarks, but it didn't seem quite a remark. She washed the saucer and put it back.

As for the power, it went on attending the place. There need be no misunderstanding on this account. When the river came up the basin would have to take it, and there was no undertaking that a particular bolt, or the warm seam, or even the soil in the hawthorn roots would be where they had been when the water went down. For the present there were four young birds well able to use their legs, with the quills of their first wing feathers beginning to show when they stretched. There was also, at the third inspection stair, a fitter who could once more carry a folding rule in her pocket without its coming out at the bottom. The power had seen to the last two stitches while it was there.
