The power went out the way it had come in, on its knees. It had not needed to come in that way, but in doing so it had got the advice about the wide bit, which it would have been sorry to miss.

There were three stones on the beach that might have been the one. It tried the biggest first. From the seaward side, however low one got, it looked like a bigger stone with a piece off it. The next was a very good loaf indeed. There was even a split along the top where the crust had drawn apart. The power spent some time on this one before it occurred to it that the man had last seen the beach from low down, and that what he called a big stone need not come much past one's shin.

The third was nearly buried. On the landward side there was a hollow scoured out of the shingle; on the seaward side the little stones came well up its flank. The power lay down. A sand hopper sprang from under its cheek. There was the hen. A rusty streak in the limestone, which from above looked no more like a feather than a crack in a plate, made the folded edge of a wing. The neck went down out of sight.

It could have taken away a handful of shingle and uncovered the beak. It nearly did. But the man had asked what it looked like now.

When it got its head under the slab again, the man said, “Have you left something?”

“No. She's still there. You can't see her head.”

“Ah,” he said. “Drinking deep.”

It told him about the rusty mark. He had not seen that. It told him about the other loaf too, and he laughed at the time it had spent trying to get a hen out of that one. He moved along the shelf.

After a while the power asked him if he would like to go out and see. It could manage the weight of him, it said, and there were ways of keeping the light out of his eyes.

“I should like to have a look,” he said. Then, after considering it a little, “What would you take?”

The power put a hand under his elbow. There was an elbow in its hand, warm and rather slippery. When it lifted, very gently, the man did not rise; instead, just beyond him, the earthenware bowl tipped back on its base. The pebble that had chocked it rolled down the step. They heard it go into the water.

“Yes,” he said. “That's the difficulty I thought there might be.”

The power set the elbow down. It found the pebble by the feel of it among several others at the bottom of the basin. One side had a little flat on it. The bowl sat nicely against it.

“There's another thing we might try,” it said.

Next morning it came back with two bits of looking glass and a length of old drainpipe with a bend at either end. It had spent part of the night on a kitchen floor, sighting along the pipe at the leg of a chair. What had taken longest was getting the leg the right way up. It felt that the man had quite enough to explain to himself on a beach without having to account for stones hanging from the sky.

They laid the pipe between two bosses, with the far end under the slab. From the shelf one could see, in a dim oblong no bigger than the missing piece of the bowl, several yards of shingle. The man put his face to it. For a good while he said nothing. Then he put up a finger and felt the edge of the glass.

“I thought that one was here,” he said.

The power went outside to turn the far end. It had meant to show him the hen, but he called to it to stop almost at once. A few feet from the mouth of the hollow a child's wet sandal lay on its side. While they watched, a hand came down, took the strap between two fingers and carried it off.

What the man wanted to know was how the hand had known where to come. The power went to look, and found a child sitting on the slab itself, with one bare foot and one shod one. She had only had to lean over.

This was considerably more interesting to him than the hen. He had heard people on top of the slab often enough. It had not occurred to him that they sat there with their legs dangling in front of his door. The child, for her part, was interested in the pipe. She came in to see what could be seen through it and was disappointed to find that the sandal was gone.

“But that's because you've got it on,” he told her, delighted.

They left the pipe there that day. Toward evening, when the power came back, the man asked it to take the thing out from under the slab. The trouble was that where it lay it cut a notch out of the square of light on the water. He had not expected to mind this, but he did. They found a place for it on the dry stone above the chalk marks. He could fetch it down when he wanted it.

“And if you're going past that bird again,” he said, “see whether her head's come up.”

The power did go past, not every day, but often enough to see the shingle bank and fall away and bank again. It got into the habit of looking before it went in. Sometimes there was news of the hen; sometimes it had only a pocket apple to show him, and a knife to cut it with. He liked a slice very thin, so that when he held it toward the door the pips showed dark in their beds. He always gave it back to be eaten.

Once, after rough weather, the beak came clear. There was a little white barnacle right at the tip of it, fast shut though the tide was out. The power hurried over the stones with this news. At the slot it had to stop and wait. Somebody was coming out backwards, taking great care not to knock his head. Inside, a child's voice was saying, with the impatience of one who has already answered the question twice, “Right on the end. As if she had a grain of rice.”

The power sat down on the slab. It had its legs over the edge. After a little while the bent end of the pipe came out between its heels. It moved one foot aside to give them a better view.
