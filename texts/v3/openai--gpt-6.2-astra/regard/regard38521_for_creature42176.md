The power that had taken an interest in the mill quarter had seen the boy lying with his cheek on the ticking at the foot of the bunk. It had seen Enna, too, just inside the lighted part of the kitchen, trying to undo the knot at the neck of her workbag. There had been nine minutes, by the clock in the pay office, during which it might have brought her to the door. A little cool air on the back of her neck would probably have done it.

It had let the nine minutes go. Now the boy was at the table, pulling the knot out for her with his teeth, and had told her what he had waited there to see. She put a finger into the place on his head where the hair stood up. It was quite warm underneath.

On the following Thursday a length of the creek bank slipped into the water. It was at the upper end of the landing, where the carts turned. Nothing went in with it except a good iron ring and a bush on which the office clerk used to hang his coat when he had business on the scales. The bush floated for a day or two with the sleeves of last year’s leaves dipped in the water. There was scarcely room now for a cart to turn. A man came down from the upper mill with a roll of drawings, and spent the morning taking levels. He proposed to bring the carts round the back of the pay office. There would be room enough if they took out the herb bed and put the last few feet of the drain in a pipe. He meant to give Enna a good level step while he was about it.

She heard him telling the clerk this through the wall. She was turning a collar. There is a point in turning a collar when the whole shirt has to come through a surprisingly small hole. By the time she had it right side out the man and the clerk were halfway up the lane.

The power could have put the bank back. It could have put the ring back with the old bright place uppermost where the rope had worked on it. But the water ran hard there on an ebb, and the ground under the turning place had been going for years. The boy was by no means the only child who went down there out of hours.

What it did was to give Enna back for a little while the voice she had had at the dry end. She had quite forgotten about that voice. It had been necessary sometimes to make the man at the far end of a moving stack understand that he must keep still.

“Here,” she called.

Both men stopped. A woman two doors up took her hands out of a tub.

Enna was a little abashed. She had not wanted particularly to keep the step. On bad mornings it took her some time to get down it, sideways, with an empty pail, and rather longer to get up it with a full one. What she wanted to know was how people were to get to her back door when there were carts going by it.

The man came back and looked. On his drawing there was no way through between the office and the bed. The two lines touched. While he was standing there the cutter girl came through the gap, turning one shoulder as she passed the projecting office sill. She went in and sat down in the low chair. A minute later the boy came through without turning anything.

They did in the end take out most of the mint. They set the new step farther back, in what had been the kitchen, and hung the door to open the other way. The carts went by outside a low brick wall. Between the wall and the door there was a footway wide enough for the cutter girl to come through carrying her father’s clean shirts without brushing one against the brick. It was her suggestion to put a seat on top of the wall at the end that got the evening sun. For a time everybody sat there except Enna, who found the wood too full of splinters. Presently it wore smooth.

Nobody found any frogs under the old stone when they lifted it. The boy was inclined to count this as a point in his favor until Enna asked whether anybody had found any in the drain. Some weeks later, in the middle of a piece of rather fine sewing, she heard the comb noise again. It seemed to come from the cart side of the wall. She let the stitch go crooked listening for a second one.

By then the power had ceased to attend to the place in any very particular way. It did not arrange for the boy to go to sea, or to stay ashore. One evening, having seen the bargeman take him aboard, Enna put her work down and went out. From the new seat she could see the round window quite well. She sat for a while with her hands spread in the last of the warmth on the wood. She had no idea whether he had his head at that end or not.
