The power first took notice of the mouse when it was lying on the dock. It could feel the weight of it perfectly well. It could feel, separately, the little hard places along the chin where the jaw rested on the wood. The mouse was comfortable. After a while it drew one hind foot up against its belly, for the same reason a person turns a pillow, and stayed another quarter of an hour.

There was a great deal the power could have done about the heat. It went down some way into the mountain to look. The long warm season of the shelves was paid for down there; so was the hatching of the gray crickets before the finches had young. Even the woman, who had uncommonly good reason to find fault with the tiles, put her stiff hand flat on them when she came to work, and could close it afterwards round the pole. The power came up again without having cooled anything.

Toward the end of that summer the woman went down on the cable for six days to have a tooth seen to. A man came up in her place. He knew the work, having done it at the lower end for years, and he brought his own pole, which had a leather loop on it. On the second day he noticed that the footboard gave a little when he took the weight of a full yoke. He marked the board with chalk. In the morning, before the first cans came, he fetched a crowbar and a square of sound wood from the shed.

There were four young under the board. They had got as far as having the parting in the fur, but not as far as going anywhere without their mother. The mother had gone out by the knot hole and was at the back of the shed, engaged in getting the last of a cricket off one of her whiskers.

The man put the thin end of the bar under the chalk mark. The power, which had no objection at all to his having a sound place to put his foot, let him get the board up. The leaf skins came up with it, held to the underside by some old deposit of axle grease. The young lay quite bare on the crosspiece. From above, in that light, they looked like the four black ends of nails driven home and painted over.

Here the power did a small thing. It drew the end of one of the man's bootlaces across the back of his hand.

He thought it was a spider. He took his hand away rather quickly and then bent close, being a man who liked to know where a spider had gone. By the time he had made out the four noses his eyes were near enough for the breath from his nostrils to stir the fur.

He sat on the dock with the board tipped up against his knee. Presently the mother came to the knot hole, which was practically in the air now, on account of the angle at which he held the board. She looked through it from the wrong side. It took her some time to arrive at her family by a way she knew. The man had a good view of the business with the whisker. It happened twice with the same young one.

By then there were empty cans hanging at the lip and somebody below was ringing. He laid the old board back, chalk side down, and worked that morning with one foot rather farther to the left than he liked.

In the afternoon he fitted the new board. He cut a shallow channel across its underside, clear of the place that took his weight, and bored a hole at the shed end where rain could not readily get in. It was a fine piece of work. No mouse ever used it. The mother had taken the four of them under the sill of the oil cupboard and seemed disposed to stay there. In later years the channel was of considerable service to a kind of pale beetle.

When the woman came back, the man showed her the chalk mark and the old wood, which was soft enough in the middle to take the print of a thumb. He told her about the mice. He did not mention the spider, having found none.

The power stayed on the mountain. There were whole afternoons when the closest attention it could give a thing disclosed no occasion to lay a hand on it. These were especially good afternoons in the gullies, when one could follow a spoonful of water a long way by going along very slowly beside it.

At the station the young began coming out singly. One of them found a crossing to the dock by way of the leather loop, which the man had left behind on a nail. For a time it went round by the loop even when it started on the dock itself. The woman, sitting there at the close of work with her jaw at last quite easy, could feel it coming the long way over the boards. She kept her place until it had settled.
