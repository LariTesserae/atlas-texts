What first engaged the attention of the power was the half pear on the paving. It was accustomed to finding things set out for it on high places. For a moment it took the pear for one of these. Then Ada whisked a fly off it with a blue slip, and presently the girl came up and ate it, including the skin.

The power stayed to hear what it was like under the orchard.

There was a pigeon down there with one pink foot and one black one. There was a whole other wire under the pear wire, which nobody had mentioned, and on it hung a brush and a little pot of grease. The girl had been able to see into a room where a man was having his hair cut. This last surprised Ada. She had worked above that room for forty years under the impression that it was a cupboard.

“Did he see you?”

“The barber did. He turned the chair a bit so the other one could see.”

In October a letter came up from Ada’s sister, written for once on proper paper. She had been asked a question by the small boy who delivered for the shop. He wanted to know what the cloud looked like on top. She had told him it was very like a field under snow, only that here and there a piece would get up and walk away from the rest. But was this right? She had been trying all week to see it again and could see perfectly well the box they had come up in, even to a bent nail which used to catch in the hem of her coat, and nothing whatever of the cloud.

Ada sat over the letter for some time. It had never occurred to her that her sister could lose the view. She went out to have a look at it for her. There was no cloud that day. There were miles of russet country with the sun glancing off water in the wheel marks of a lane.

The power could have cleared the air between the sisters’ houses so thoroughly that one might have stood at her window and seen the other take off her apron. It considered doing this. But Ada’s sister had seen Ada take off her apron, and what she wanted to see was a cloud.

On the following Thursday, when the shop below was shut for its half day, a bank of ordinary grey weather came up the valley. The power did a small piece of work on it. Where it passed the shop door it let the bottom of the cloud hang down rather farther than usual. Then it gave the loose end the twist that a laundress gives a sheet before she takes the two corners together. For rather less than a minute, in a space about the size of a hearthrug, the upper side was out.

Ada’s sister was on the step knocking the dust out of the green baize that lined the pear shelf. She stopped with the baize over her arm. There was the long easy gleam on the cloud, which you never saw from below; there were the blue hollows in it, deep enough, to a child who had been looking into a box for most of the journey, to contain ships and houses. A little crest tore loose and went travelling by itself. She put out a hand and it came apart on the dark green cloth in a quantity of very fine beads. She remembered now the wet sleeves of her father’s coat, and how forbearing he had been about the two dry places the children had made on them with their fingers.

By the time the boy came round the corner it was a common fog and the steps were slippery. She took him inside and got out the sugar basin, which was blue glass, and a handful of flour. She found she could give a rather good account of it now.

On the next blue slip to go up she wrote, *No need to look. I was right about the walking.* After a moment she added, *Do you remember how wet Father got?*

The power did not wait about for thanks. It had got some damp into a neighbouring shop where a man was trying to set a very small pane of glass in putty, and it spent the remainder of the afternoon giving him the good drying weather he had been led to expect.
