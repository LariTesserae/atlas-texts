The power was there one evening when the first moth came to the fork and could not get through. A sheep had put its foot on the turn of the root. Up on the turf there was a neat divided print, already filling in as the grass lifted; underneath, a tongue of slate had gone between the two arms of the fork and stayed there. The moth put a forefoot on the edge of it. It felt along as far as it could reach and then, walking backwards, came down the root into the dark.

The power could have lifted the slate. It waited to see what the moth would do. There was a good hour of flying weather left; the heads of the thrift scarcely stirred. Twice more the moth went up to the fork. On the third journey down it met another coming up. For a little they stood with their feelers going over one another in the draught. Then the second moth stepped onto the back of the first and from there onto the slate.

There was a way out at the very tip of the tongue. It took a moth some time to go round it, since the feet on one side had to let go before those on the other had found anything to hold. The second moth managed it. The first went up again and managed it too. Presently there were little stirrings of yellow all along the grass.

One moth, with a nick in the edge of a wing, lost its footing at the turn. It fell without opening its wings, struck the lowest step and went over. Down on the chips it righted itself. It walked to the wall and began the long business of coming up again.

For this one the power put out a hand. It did not pick the moth up. It laid a finger from the chips to the foot of the step, where there was an undercut the moth would otherwise have had to go a long way round. The moth walked alongside the finger for a little, as if it were another face of rock. Then it got onto the knuckle. The power felt six points of pressure, so slight that to feel them separately was something even a power had to attend to. At the step the moth got off. By the time it reached the root there was still light enough outside to show the nick in its wing against the sky.

In the night a shower came over from the sea. The earth in the sheep's print softened and the tongue of slate slipped a little farther down. This left the old way clear, with a bit of roof over it which had not been there before. In the morning the wren got one moth at the mouth and missed another. It took the one it got inland, over two walls and into a bank, where the power, being also there, felt the small hard knock of its bill against the gape of a young bird in the dark.

Nothing was put up to mark the place. The person who had left the pebble had once come in out of just such a shower. There had been a cord then, wet and rather stiff, and in setting down the weight the person had been careful not to crush a moth. The power had felt that too: the weight taken back into the fingers just as it was about to leave them, the small adjustment, hardly the breadth of a thumb, and then the stone set down.
