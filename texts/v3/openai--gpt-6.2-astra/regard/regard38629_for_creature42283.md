The first time the woman asked for a hand, it was with the white chair. One of the back legs had gone through the crust of lime that forms beside the kitchen door. She could have got it out by pulling, but there was a fish in the hollow the leg had made, and every time she tipped the chair to get the spoon in, the sides of the hollow came a little closer together.

“Here,” she said, to nobody in particular, “hold that a minute.”

The chair stayed tipped. She got the fish out. Then she got down on her knees, for she had seen what looked like a bit of black sewing thread go under the crust. There were five more fish in there, in a space about as big as the bowl of her spoon. By the time she stood up she had quite forgotten that there was anything unusual about the chair. She took hold of it and felt the weight come back into her hands.

“Oh,” she said. “Thank you.”

Whatever had hold of the chair had been in the valley some time. It had felt the pull of the water and gone down through the rock to where the stones hung. It could have taken one between finger and thumb. There were small blind things living on the undersides of the stones, with their fine white legs all streaming upwards. It had come away without touching them.

After the business with the chair the woman would occasionally speak to it. She had lived alone long enough to be quite easy about talking aloud, but there was a difference between saying that it looked like a three-day rain and asking somebody else what they thought. To the latter she got no answer. It took her a while to work out that it had no more idea than she did how long a rain would last. There were things it could do that she could not, and apparently this worked the other way round too.

One morning when she was doing the long gutter she stopped at the place where it crossed the old river bed. There was a step up there that had been getting higher for several years. On her last trip she had sat down on it and swung her legs round, which was quite a good way of getting up except for the business of getting to her feet afterwards. Today she had brought a hammer and a little cold chisel with the idea of cutting a nick for her toe. She laid the chisel against the face of the step. A flake of limestone as long as her forearm came away, though she had not yet struck it.

Under the flake, quite undamaged, was a mouse’s room. It had a domed ceiling and a floor of pale horsehair and in the middle of the floor a single cyan-blue feather. Where a mouse in that valley had got such a thing the woman could not imagine. She fitted the flake back. Something held it while she rubbed a thumbful of wet grit along the join.

“I suppose,” she said, sitting down on the ledge, “you could just lift me.”

She felt a little pressure under her, as of a hand slipped under a broody hen.

“Not yet. I’m thinking.”

The pressure went away. Across the river bed the mare with the white lower lip was watching her. She had seen the bent wire sticking out of the woman’s pocket. Presently she came over to see what the delay was, taking the step at a slant where a rib of harder rock ran up the face of it. The woman tried going up that way. It was easy enough if she put a hand out, and there was the mare to put it on.

By late summer they had got into a habit, the woman and the power that had held the chair. It went the gutter walks with her. She did not need lifting very often. What she liked, especially on the way home, was to be able to stop and look at something without having to consider how much of the morning it would cost her to get down to it and back up again. She could lie full length on the pavement now, with her eye to one of the little green doors. When she was ready she held out her bent wire, and up she came, hand over hand, as though the other end were hooked over a branch. She had begun to carry a piece of wood to slip through the bend, to make a better grip. It was getting quite polished.

Near the end of the apricots they went down to the tree. There had been a fall of fruit in the night. The large tortoise had a smear of pulp clear up over one eye. The little one was nowhere to be seen. The woman filled her pockets and sat with her legs hanging over the basin and presently heard the thin knock, not under the tree this time but almost directly beneath her. She put her hand out for a hold and leaned a very long way over.

There was the little tortoise in a fold of the rock. It had grown; there was a pale line round each of the plates on its back, like the new cane in a mended basket. In front of it lay an apricot stone. It picked the stone up, stretched its neck and let go. Before the stone had quite settled, a little dust jumped from a hollow a foot or so away.

The woman drew herself up slowly. She took a clean stone out of her pocket and dropped it on the sill. The sound was full and hard. After a while, from below, came the thin answer. Then from the hollow in the rock came a third sound, so faint she felt it through the seat of her dress rather than heard it.

She lay down again. There was nothing in the hollow but dust. As she watched, a little cup formed in it, the shape an apricot stone would make if someone had picked it up and dropped it from no great height.

For some reason it had never occurred to her that the thing might like to do anything in the valley on its own account. She had imagined that when she put her wire away in the kitchen drawer it went off somewhere until she needed it again.

She emptied her pocket. Most of the stones went back into it, but she left a particularly nice one on the lip of the hollow, pink at the tip and polished all over by the big tortoise’s tongue. Then, as the sun was getting low, she took the slant way up out of the river bed and went home.

From the east wall of the rain house that evening she could see the horses leaving the trough and the dust going up along the sills. She had her feet in the wash and was easing a loose strand of wicker back into the rim of the chair with her thumbnail. At that distance of course she could hear nothing. But in the last light the little pink stone was visible, going up and down.
