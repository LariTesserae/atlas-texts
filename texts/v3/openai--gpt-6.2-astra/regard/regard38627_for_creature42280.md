On top of the leaf there is another bird.

This is an effect of the water. A thin skin of it lies on the leaf, held in at the edges by hairs, and the bank of cloud is rather brighter than the sky. The two little patches of fluff above the eyes show particularly well. The young bird puts its bill to one of them. The other bird opens from the bill outwards in a set of white rings, and there is a taste of fern.

It is through the attention of this bird that the shelf first comes to the notice of the power. There are other ways of finding a mountain. This particular way gives, to begin with, a very small mountain indeed: a wet brown root across the sky, a green piece of sky with a bird in it, and an immense difficulty about the feet. By the time the power has made out which way up things are, the bird has let go. It does quite well on its own wings. It goes up past the sedge altogether and lands with a thump among the alder leaves, where for some time nobody can see it but a moth.

The power stays. The expenditure on the shelf interests it less than one might suppose. It has seen a sun spend the better part of an afternoon putting a red shine on the closed eyelid of a fox. It is the double knock that holds its attention. Heard from outside it is very regular. Heard from where the animal with the webbed feet sits licking its toe, there is another knock, much softer, which does not come at a regular interval at all.

Across the bath a small thing is jumping against the enamelled side. It has fallen in. There is a margin to the bath which turns inwards to prevent water being lost at the wheel's entry; the creature gets as high as the underside of this margin and knocks its head. It might be a young one of the animal on the rim. Wet, these things are extraordinarily narrow. There is no telling its size until it spreads its feet to jump, when it seems to come all to pieces in the water and gather itself together again a little farther along.

The power could empty the bath. It could give the creature, for the instant necessary, a foot that would take hold of enamel. What it does is to put a roughness no longer than a barley awn on the underside of the rim. The next jump misses it. So does the next. It is a surprisingly unpleasant business, waiting through these jumps, when one has the means of stopping and starting mountains. The power moves the roughness a little to the left.

There. A nose comes over, then the two hands, which are quite different from the feet of the animal that was drinking. They have thumbs. The creature lies on its front on the warm rim for a while. From its flank there projects a single long transparent wing, stuck fast with water. Presently this comes unstuck and divides into two wings, and what goes away down the hall goes by air.

The roughness is still there. The power has an inclination to run something of the kind round the inside of every bath. Before doing so it attends to this one for a time. A fly lands on the awn. Something very fine and grey that has been lying along the rim puts out a loop, and the fly is gone. By the third lighting of the lamps the grey thing has moved its whole length over to the awn. There are the remains of several wings in the loop. At the fourth lighting a swimmer comes up from deep in the bath, takes hold, and pulls itself out, grey thing and all. It eats the grey thing sitting up, holding one end in either hand like a person measuring thread.

The power leaves the rough place there. It does not run one round every bath.

Outside, the old mouse is sitting down in the midst of his crossing. His lifted hind foot is perfectly clean. There is a smell from the second pocket which he very much wishes to arrive at, but just now a considerable part of what it is to be this mouse consists in the sunless warmth of a piece of slate against his seat. It would be easy to miss that. The power, which has been considering the foot, discovers the slate. It is warm because a bearing runs immediately under it, though if one were to lift it one would find only stone. In time the mouse gets up and makes the rest of the crossing. There is a dead beetle in the grass, quite a good one except for the head.

For a long while there is nothing else on the shelf that the power sees its way to doing. It comes back, all the same. It comes in hard weather, when the moths come down early and cling so thickly under the fronds that the fronds have brown furred edges. It comes on a very still night when the shrew with the nick in its ear, now in possession of an extensive run of wet stones high above the shelf, comes down the seam, smells once at the mouth of its old hole, and passes on. Once it comes expressly for the young grey bird and cannot find it anywhere. There are five or six birds by then that have lost the pale fluff and acquired the trick with the feet. It could follow back the whole history of each, but that is not quite the same thing as recognizing a bird.

Instead it goes below the precipice, to the place where there is nowhere to stand. The cloud has lifted a little. Seen from here the five pockets make five dark bulges in the belly of the rock. Nothing of the prodigious lighted distance under them shows through. A drop shines at the end of a root in the open air; there is the thinnest of threads from the root to the drop, which turns out to be a spider descending backwards with its wet catch. Then the call sounds up above. Out into all that empty air come two small grey birds, their feet tucked tidily away. One turns at once. The other goes rather farther down than is necessary, banks in the light off the cloud and comes up under a red fern stalk. Both feet open. The stalk begins its long slow bow.

The power knows this one.
