The first thing the power felt was the knock of a young pear against an old one. It had been listening to the mountain from a long way off. From there the note was very nearly pure. One had to come all the way in, and be for a while no bigger than the bag of jelly, to feel the two knocks where from outside there seemed to be only one. The old animal had a worn place on its side. The young one fitted there.

By then the power could feel a movement on the downhill side which did not belong to the grinders. A flake had come off the granite under the blue lip. It was no bigger than a scale off a fish, but it had come to rest on edge. Water was stealing past it. On the air side of the lip there was already a wet place the size of a thumbprint. The cold was not sufficient to freeze it. By morning it would be a runnel, and after that the weight of water in the pocket would do the rest. There was an old gray stripe down the face of the ridge where this had happened before.

It would have been an easy thing to put the flake back. There were three of the animals on hairs in the space it had left; they had gone flat at the first pull of water and were holding on. Besides, the loss of the flake had done the granite no harm. It was the ice that needed mending, and only on the outside, and only for the night.

The power laid the cold of a little distance up the ridge against the wet thumbprint. There was a faint tick. The trickle went on for a while under a shell so clear that a grain of red meal could be seen traveling down it, stopping, traveling a little farther. The grain stopped for good. Before the patch of sun came round again there was a sound blue bead at the foot of the lip. Behind it the scale lay over in the slack water of its own accord. The three little animals stood up.

There was no change in the note. It rather pleased the power that there was no change. A sole was coming down not far from the pear with the worn side, and for a while there was a temptation to put a little water under its descending edge. But the sole too was occupied with living. One corner touched first; the rest of it settled a little crosswise to the way it had hung. The old pear went under. The young one was just clear. For the better part of a day it went on presenting its tough cheek to the still edge of the sole.

The power stayed. It had nowhere it was obliged to be instead. It felt the old pear cease, and felt, through another breadth of flesh, the first substantial meal the sole had had in a summer. These were quite distinct things even where the two animals lay one on the other. Toward evening a working socket a little way off sent a knock through the young pear. After several such knocks the bag of jelly let go on one side and hitched itself round.

In another year the bead was an arm's length out of place, carried downhill in the ice, with its red grain still fixed in the middle. Where it had been, the lip now rested squarely on a different shelf of granite. High up in the quartz a crack had widened enough to take a pear. It was a poor place for a grinder until a little before noon, when the warmish water began to come through. Then for an hour or two a white stone turned there. If one listened from the opposite mountain one could not hear the least difference. The power liked to come in close for it.
