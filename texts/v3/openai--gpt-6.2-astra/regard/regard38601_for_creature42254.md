There was a place on land where the upper river could be heard. It was in the bottom of a disused sheep wash, a long stone trough set into the hillside. Most of the water that came down the hill went on over the lower lip of the trough. A little went away through a crack in the bottom. If one put an ear to the stone when the trough was nearly empty, one could hear that little water falling. How far it fell before it struck anything was not generally known.

The power that had taken notice of the valley followed the fresh water uphill. At a fork it followed both branches. One came from a wet patch no bigger than a bedspread, where rushes grew and a snipe had made three holes close together with its bill. One came from the trough. There were other threads too fine to follow singly, but the power had time.

On the bank above the sheep wash lay six lengths of black pipe. A woman came up to fit them together. The spring a field below had failed that summer, and there were cattle standing about its iron basin, licking the damp round the plug. She meant to take the water from the bottom of the old trough, where it ran even in a dry week. She had brought a little sack of cement for stopping the crack.

The power could have broken the pipe. There were flat stones on the bank quite heavy enough to do it. The woman would have had to walk down for another length and the cattle would have stood another morning at the basin.

Instead, while she was kneeling to see how much cement she would need, it let her put her hand into the water at the other end.

She felt the stone over the back of her hand first, and the warmth of the thin water under it. Lowering one finger she came quite abruptly into the cold. A little hard thing moving along the stone went round her knuckle and on its way without coming off. Far below, in water she could not reach, there was a knock. She kept her hand there until she felt the long lift that came up from the mouth of the valley. Then she took it out. There was a red mark on her wrist where it had rested on the edge of the trough.

She sat on the sack of cement for a while. Afterwards she laid the open end of the pipe in the notch over which the water was already running. It took nearly the whole run of the notch to fill the basin below. Once the ball in the basin had risen and shut the valve, the water spilled over the lip as before. The crack went on taking its little share in either case.

This was not much of a change to make in a hillside. In the valley there was no change that could have been found by laying a finger to the stone. The shore along the ceiling wandered a little lower in wet weather and drew up in dry. The white grazers went with it.

The power went back to the room off the square. There were some empty egg skins still hanging under the valve. The mother had got as far as the lower hole and was lying with two arms outside. They had gone rather slack. When a shrimp touched one of them the suckers took hold in their old order, from the tip inward, but not hard enough to keep it.

Here the power stayed without putting a hand in at either opening. Toward slack water the arm ends lifted a little and settled in a different place. There was one more small movement of water through the room. Well into the next flood the shrimp came back, and this time it went in.

Out on the felt the young one had something under an arm. It had taken a long time to draw the thing up: each time it caught hold of a leg the other legs braced against the strands. Now there was a clear husk tumbling away and a brown morsel held close under the web. The felt tipped seaward in the ebb. For the first time since it had settled there the little octopus let go with all eight arms. It went scarcely its own length before taking hold again, on the next tuft out, where the light was better.
