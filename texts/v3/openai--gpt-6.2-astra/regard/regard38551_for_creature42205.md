There was one that came away with the stone. That is a different thing from losing hold. It was an old one, blue almost to the edges of its feet, and it had been feeling upstream in the usual way when a scale of brown rock, no thicker than the skin on boiled milk, parted from the lip. For the first few feet of the fall the animal was still walking on it.

Now there was something attending to the mountain that could have held it all quite still. It could have kept the scale on the stone and the stone on the mountain and the mountain in precisely that relation to the red river which permitted a person lying among the reeds to see the little town. It liked the underleaf very much. Among the things it liked about it was the way it went on trying the edge of the scale with one foot, although there had never, in all its long acquaintance with stone, been an edge of this kind before.

Below the lip was a shelf. Water reached it only in a wind from the south, but there was a stand of hollow stems on it, growing out of a crack that went back into the warm dark. The power put the tip of one stem in the way of the falling scale. It had to bend the stem a good deal farther than a wind would have bent it. Even so, the scale went through. The underleaf stayed on the stem, folded over it like a scrap of wet cloth.

For a while it did badly. There was air on both sides of it where there ought to have been stone on one side and running water on the other. Its longer jaw hung out. The power that had bent the stem waited there with it, not knowing whether there was anything else to do. By and by the jaw went in. One foot took hold below another. It took the underleaf what remained of the afternoon to go the length of the stem, and toward evening it came to the crack and found water going in. There was room beside the water. It went in too.

Up on the lip the place the scale had come from was the color of weak tea. In a day or two the keeper with the shell string noticed it. He put his finger there and felt a gritty surface, a poor place for anything to stick. He supposed that something might have fallen, and when he was next asked the question he said so. It troubled one of the small visitors, a girl who had until then been quite happy to lie with her chin over the water. She took her chin back. He did not tell her that five feet were enough, as he had sometimes been tempted to do. Instead he moved his basket so there was room for her to sit beside him and sort the snails. She was rather good at that. She found a live one inside a much larger empty shell and put the two back together.

There were other falls. The power was not always at the lip. For a time it went about with the idea of putting shelves under things, and discovered, by attending to the places where shelves already were, how much a shelf could change. On one there were old fruit stones two feet deep and a tree feeding on the black stuff at the bottom. Under another the rock was dry for want of the water the shelf turned aside. There were white beetles there which had no business anywhere wet. It was worth a good deal of looking, this question of where to put a shelf. In the meantime it did not regret the stem.

Late in the lean-water months, when there was scarcely enough coming over the lip to darken the rock, a keeper out looking for a strayed bean basket got down onto the lower shelf. She found the basket wedged in the hollow stems. In order to free the handle she had to lie on her side, with her ear quite close to the crack. There was a faint repetitive sound inside, too slow for dripping. She stayed to hear it again. Something yellow came partway out of the crack and settled over a snail; a little farther in there was a second foot, and beyond that the brown glint of water moving steadily into the mountain. There was no seeing how far it went. She eased the handle loose a stem at a time and went back up with her basket.
