The power that took an interest in the lip came first to the opening behind the flake. It had been a long time since it had looked at daylight from so low down. There was the wet chin, and under it the quick working of the skin over the throat; then the mother turned sideways to get in, and there was daylight again. The little mouse went in after her. It had seen enough of the outside for one day.

For a time the power stayed where it was. It could feel through the floor the many small blows of the drops, including one, well back under the rock, which made no sound on the ledge. It followed this one down. Water from the deepest hollow had found a way out through the bottom. It ran along a slant in the stone for about the length of a bird, gathered on a grain of quartz, and fell into an empty crack below. In the whole of an afternoon perhaps a thimbleful went that way. The rest went over the white sill, past the cricket.

Down the inner face of the hollow were little scratches. They went up in twos and threes and stopped well short of the rim. The power put something of its attention into one of these. It felt the push of a small foot against stone, and the much greater push that had followed when the first had done no good.

It could have let out the water through the bottom. There was very little between the grain of quartz and the floor of the hollow that could have stood in its way. But it had seen the mouse drinking there. Besides, at the spillway the cricket had finished its gnat and was washing its face in a thread of water scarcely thicker than either of its feelers.

On the side away from the spillway the white collar had grown out over the water. The power put a pressure on it, very gently, from above. A piece about as long as the mother mouse, but not much wider than her tail, gave a little click and tipped in. Its lower end lodged in the grit at the bottom. The upper end still rested in the notch from which it had broken. The underside, which now faced up, was exceedingly rough, having grown, season after season, in little crowded knobs. A foot the size of a husk of birdseed would find quite good purchase there.

The cricket sprang at the click and came down facing the other way. It took it some time to get turned round in the spillway with those feelers. The bird beside the hollow opened one eye. That was about the extent of the disturbance.

The power stayed for several mornings. It saw the lights from the liverwort travel across the birds, and learned to distinguish, in all that calling, the particular two notes which would make the bird at the waist draw in its tail and turn. The young mouse came out again. It went some way under the tail this time, and when the feathers lifted it stayed to look. The power, which could have kept the feathers shut, was glad it had not done so. There was a hawk a long way down, white at that distance, crossing and recrossing the blue space between the mouse's forefeet.

None of the young mice fell into the hollow. A beetle did, an old one with a dent in its back. For a while it went round the sides doing with six feet what others had done with two. Then one of the feet caught, and almost at once the beetle was out. It stood on the white rim for a little, passing its legs one after another through its mouth. A sitting bird raised a wing, and it went in underneath.
