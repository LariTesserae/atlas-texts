There came a winter when the snow did not wear thin. There was wind enough, but the first fall had been followed by rain, and the rain had made a crust to which the later falls held fast. At the correcting bend one could still see the print of a bird's two feet made before the second snow. The toes were full to the brim with fine loose grains. Nothing had taken them away.

By then there was something attending to the works that could have taken the water off them altogether. It could have set the slab back on its sill. It knew even the set of the lower hinge, which no man had seen since the day the stone was hung. But the slab made a good bed where it lay, and on the sill there was another good bed. It left the stone alone.

Under the bend the beasts were still in their file. In most of the little ones there was no longer any green to be seen. One had come out from under the heel in front of it and turned about on the rod, and was working its lips against the scar of a bud. It had got a little brown filing of bark loose. There was nothing in the bark for it.

The power began on the snow, at the place where the bird had stood. It lifted a piece of the crust no bigger than would come off on the end of a spade. The wind got under the broken edge. By evening there was a bare place rather longer than a punt, narrow at the windward end and feathered out at the other; the bird's two prints had gone over the ice in little white lumps. The snow came to rest over a cut. It had taken light from nothing there.

For a time the bare place seemed to have been made a little too far to one side. What light came through fell mostly on the hard crown of the bank. But the sun was still low and went a good way round in the course of the day. In the afternoon the light lay slantwise down the ditch. There were shoots there with all but the last of the work of making a leaf done in them. On the third afternoon the sheaths began to part.

It was an old beast that found them. In coming off the wicker it took a leaf down with it and let the leaf go again. Several feet along the rod loosened at that. By dark the little beast with the filing of bark on its lip was well down in the ditch, holding a sheath with both forefeet so as to get a better purchase. There was a short green stroke in it the next morning. From above, through the roughened ice, none of this could have been made out.

There were other banks where the same small service was wanted. There were also banks under deep snow where it was not wanted at all; the beasts on those rods had gone into winter well fed and were lying easy. The power did not clear a straight way from one end of the works to the other. It went by the condition of what was underneath. In one broad pan it did no more than loosen the snow along a crack. A change in the wind did the rest, though not until nearly a week later.

In spring the slab and the sill stood apart as before. A company gathered there, and for two days there was nothing long enough to catch in the joint. On the third day a blade caught. It was a poor thin blade with a split up the middle, and the first beast to try it got only its forefeet down before the two halves opened and it came back. During the night a second blade went into the split and the two held very well together. The power, which had remained to see, had no part in that.
