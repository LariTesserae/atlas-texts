The power came down into the basin in the shape of a woman with a rent in the elbow of her coat. She had other coats, if she wanted them. She had her pick of elbows too. But the rent was a real one. When she bent her arm a little pink point came out in the weather.

The woman in the seat moved her work off the threshold. She had the coat turned inside out on her knees before she noticed that her visitor, who was standing in the runnel in her stocking feet, had not so much as clouded the water.

“You could hold this,” she said.

It was the lid that gave her light. With somebody to hold it she could have the light from above, which was better for work on a sleeve. The power held it until she was told she could put it down.

“What do I owe you?”

“Have you anything dry to put on?”

The power put her hand inside her coat, which by then she had on again, and brought out a pair of stockings rolled up together. The woman felt the inside of the roll with a finger. Then she took off her own stockings. There were marks from the ribs of the wool all up her shins. The power sat on a bed lid in her bare feet and waited while she made the change.

There were a number of things she had thought of doing on the way down. Seen from a height, the basin looked very much in want of a channel. The old mouth of it was apparent even under three fathoms of shingle. It would have taken rather less effort to open it than it had taken to hold the light. But from down here she could see where the water would come. The scoops for the man's heels would be among the first things to go. She had thought too of making a spring of sweet water. Just where she would have put it, clear of the bed and handy to the seat, a thread kept twitching on the wet shell, though the woman had her feet quite still.

“Are you going back by the bar?” the woman asked.

“I can do.”

“Tell them I can't do any more black till the cloud lifts. They can send me white. And if you see the tall man, tell him Saturday, if it's fine.”

The power went by the bar. The people there had a white shirt in pieces and had been holding it back because they thought a shirt in pieces too much to send. She took it down with her on Friday. The tall man had got the message too.

It was fine on Saturday. The boy brought his own blanket, in a roll that had slipped so that the middle of it trailed behind him like a tail. He was to be allowed to stay as long as he liked, even till morning. His uncle would come for him if wanted. The difficulty was to know, up on the landward side, whether he was wanted. They had agreed on a white lid set upright at the lip of the basin. The boy had undertaken to put it there himself. It was quite a walk from the woman's seat, and when the night came he found that he didn't care to make it.

He didn't particularly want to go home either. He had got the grit right under his lid, after several trials, and he and the woman had seen the two birds at once. The trouble was a much smaller one and took him some time to tell. At home, if he woke up, there was a kettle to be heard on the other side of the wall quite early, long before anybody came in to him. Here he didn't know what there would be.

The power had come to collect the pieces of the shirt, which by now were a shirt again. She was folding it on the threshold.

“I have a kettle,” she said.

She stayed in the tall man's seat. About an hour before light she went up for fresh water. It would have been easy to heat it where it was, in the hollow in the lid, but for the noise he wanted it had to be the tin kettle and the little spirit lamp she had brought along with it. She got the wick up with the point of a needle. For a while there was nothing to hear but the slight tinny shift of the bottom as it warmed. Then came a sound like somebody rubbing a thumb slowly round a rim.

By the time the water boiled the boy was asleep again. The power made tea. The woman came round the end of the lane in the dry stockings, which had begun to take the shape of her feet, and they drank it sitting opposite one another with one heel apiece in the hollows in the wall.

“Will you be wanting those back?” the woman asked.

“No.”

“Good. I've cut the tops off the others for cuffs.”

In the morning the power took the shirt away. She left the kettle where the lamp had stood, on a level shelf a little way in from the rain. It was an ordinary kettle. In time the handle would come loose at one end, and the boy, who by then would know how, would put in a rivet. For the present there was enough water left in it for him to wash his face. He came carrying it round the bend with both hands, taking such care not to spill any that the bird with the black toe walked nearly the whole length of the lane ahead of him without his seeing it.
