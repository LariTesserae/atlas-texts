The pear was gone when the father came up. He found the place readily enough; there was a damp oval on the third stone, and on the fourth a bit of skin no bigger than a scale from a fish. The new mountain between them was smooth. He went on up and told the boy he had found where it had been. This was not what the boy had asked him to find.

I learned this some years afterwards from the boy himself. He had grown into a rather silent man with a beautiful shearing hand; the old and infirm among the pear trees liked him to come and cut their hair. He remembered the pear chiefly because of the trouble his father had taken the following morning to look for it, even going down on his belly to see under the door. It had been a long time since he had thought anybody had eaten it.

By then I had met the other visitor to the roof country. I say met, though we were never to my knowledge face to face. I had gone up in the company of the woman with the plait, who now walked with two sticks on the salt and needed neither of them on stone. The hut she had built was nearly finished. There was no longer a hut to be seen on the far table. When I asked what it had been like to carry the chipped lintel into place, she said the chip had come out of it while they were carrying it, and she had said something at the time of which the children still occasionally reminded her.

It was towards evening. She wanted a little water to wash the salt from her sticks before taking them inside. I had left the bottle at the stack, or what used to be the stack; there was one slab left, too badly bowed to be much good for a roof. I went back for it. On the way I passed a small bright patch of rain hanging in the air. It was about the size of the crown of a hat. The drops were falling quite slowly and ended at the height of my breast, as though there were a surface there. I put my hand under it and the drops went on ending an inch above my hand. It was a very pretty thing. I called the woman to come and look.

“I can see it from here,” she said. She was standing in the doorway with her hand out. The palm was wet.

She washed the ends of her sticks, which took rather more water than I should have thought the patch contained. Then she put a finger in her mouth. “That's from down home,” she said.

We fetched the bottle all the same. There were limits to what either of us cared to count on for supper.

In the night I woke to a noise I had not heard since coming up. Someone was trying, very gently, the latch of the door. This door, unlike the first, was hung on its pins. I could see the little wooden bar rise against the crack of light and settle again. The woman was awake on her mat.

“Shall I let it in?” I said.

“I should think it could come in.”

The bar rose and settled. After a while she got up. “All right,” she said, rather crossly, as one does when a caller has waited so long out of politeness that one has had to put one's shoes on again. She opened the door.

What came in was the smell of a room a long way down the mountain. There was hot iron in it, and soap and the particular smell of cloth just too dry to take a crease. We could hear two women disagreeing as to whose turn it was to clean the stove. One of them was laughing. There followed the unmistakable whack of something soft and heavy striking someone across the back.

The woman on the threshold put her sticks against the wall. “Oh,” she said. “You've got the wrong house.”

At this the smells and voices ceased. There was for a little while a great impression of attention, not directed particularly at either of us. It made me aware of the cut ends of the fibres in the door, each a little brush, and of a place inside my right shoe where the stitching would shortly give way. The woman seemed to find it quite ordinary.

“He doesn't come up now,” she said. “His breathing's bad. And they go to see him on Sundays. You needn't go to the trouble of bringing his girls up here.”

The latch stirred once under her hand.

“Well, of course it was kindly meant,” she said, and came back to bed.

In the morning there were two clean shirts folded on the bad slab. We took them down with us. The women at the laundry were glad enough to see them, as they belonged to a customer who had been in twice already.

For some time after that nothing remarkable happened, so far as anything on the roof can be said not to be remarkable. The steps came in. There were forty-one of them at first, leading nowhere that a person would wish to go; then a landing wide enough for a laden animal to turn on, then more steps. They did not reach the country below. They went down the face of the mountain to a narrow shelf where a grove was beginning, the first ever found below the lip. In the afternoon it had the sun full on it. Its leaves, instead of lying over one another, hung down the cliff, so that from underneath one saw the lavender side of every blade. The woman made a point of going there. I went with her once, carrying the sticks.

On the third step from the top she sat down with her back to the table. There was not much to see but light. She had brought a pear in her pocket and cut it in two with the small knife she used for trimming the ends of a weave. One half she ate. The other she laid on the tread beside her.

“Was it you, then?” I asked.

“Sitting here? It is today.”

We sat until the light went off the lower grove. When she reached for her sticks the pear was still there. She picked it up and ate it on the way home.

I believe the power which had noticed the mountain could have finished those steps in an afternoon. I have less idea now than I had then of where it might have taken them. For a season or two one could feel it about the place, especially at a sticking latch or when the end of a slab had to be lowered onto somebody's fingers. If asked, it would bear a little of the weight. The difficulty was in asking for little enough. Once we got a stone up so high and light that the smallest child, reaching up with both hands, walked it all round the hut before we could persuade him to bring it back. He was pleased for days.

What it did about the pear I know exactly. The barber told me. His father was an old man, in bed under the sloping roof of the house where he had been born. There was a leak above the bed which his son had come to mend. He had got the tiles off and was lying with one arm down between the rafters, feeling for the place where the lath had gone soft. From below his father watched the hand travelling about under the roof. Presently he put his own hand up and took something out of it.

It was a small sour pear, warm on one side, with a flat bruise from being rolled across a floor. The old man knew it at once. He ate it down to the pips while his son was still up on the roof, and when the tiles were back in place and the son had come indoors, gave him the stalk to put away.

“Where did you find it?” the barber asked him.

“Where you said.”
