The power that took an interest in the dipper had seen it first from below. There was a fall of nearly a hundred feet between the nest and the next place on which a bird could stand. Seen from that place, the willow rib in the mouth of the nest was a fine pale mark against the sky. Presently two feet came out and took hold of it. The rib bent, the feet went in again, and a small quantity of dirt came down.

It would have been quite possible to put a ledge there. The power went up to have a look. The rock immediately below the nest was scored all over with narrow parallel scratches. A fox had been getting down from the meadow by putting its forefeet on this place and backing up again when it could find nothing for its hind ones. One of its red hairs was held by the rough tip of the willow rib. The power left the rock as it was.

For three mornings it came there early. On the third morning a young dipper came out entire. It had a freckled front and a morsel of white down standing loose on the crown of its head. It sat on the rib while one of the old birds went past it into the hole and out again. On the outward journey the old bird had to go on its side. There was a flurry at the door and afterwards the white morsel was on the head of the old bird. The young one had hold of the moss by one foot. It let go and flew away into the cloud, making a perfectly serviceable turn to the left to miss a blade of rock. By noon there were two of them on a shelving stone a good way down, trying to take one fly from each other, though the stone was black with flies.

In getting to know the movements of the old birds the power had come upon a length of stream on the other side of the back wall. It ran down a crease in the mountain into a pool, and the pool emptied at its lower end through an iron pipe. The pipe went under a track that had once been used to bring the stone down. A boot would go into it comfortably. Just inside, an iron tongue stuck up from the bottom where the metal had rusted through. A little stick coming down lengthways went over it quite easily. A stick coming down crossways stayed there.

There was a dipper in the pipe. It was neither of the meadow birds but a bird so like them that the power had followed it all the way up the crease before it noticed its bent outside toe. The bird had gone in at the lower end after something in the shallow water. There was room enough to turn round. But in turning it had worked a wing under the iron tongue, and every time it pulled, the tongue took a little better hold. Water about the thickness of a pane of glass went over its feet. Its bill was open. From outside the pipe all that could be seen of it was the reflection of its white breast trembling on the roof.

The power put one finger under the iron and lifted. For some time the bird did not move at all. Then it folded the wing and walked out past the finger into daylight. It stood on the track with both wings hanging rather low. A drop came off the end of each at much the same moment. It flew a short distance, came down, and went the rest of the way to the pool on foot.

The power pushed the tongue down flat and ran a finger over the place in both directions. A little ridge remained that could be felt coming upstream but would not catch. There was nothing else amiss with the pipe. In particular there was a shelf of fine black sand along one side with the tracks of a water shrew on it, going in and coming out. The power took its finger away.

It went back over the wall in the late afternoon. All the young birds were out now. The smallest had got as far as the gutter behind the shelter, where it was engaged in trying to swallow a black larva that had stuck to the outside of its bill. Each time it opened its mouth the larva went down with the lower half and each time it shut its mouth the larva was still outside. The power sat on the raised seat. After a bit the bird wiped its bill on the edge of the slab and picked the larva up the right way round. The burr under the seat continued until quite late, long after the stone had lost the warmth of the sun.
