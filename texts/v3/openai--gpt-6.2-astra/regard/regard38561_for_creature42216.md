The power that took an interest in the shelf saw the water in the rock quite as clearly as the water outside it. There was an inconvenience in this. It could look at a hill on a cold morning and see which of the six pebble beds would have water in it by afternoon. It had therefore watched the birds for some time before it understood what the young ones were doing on the stones. To see what there was to learn, it had to look out through an eye past which no light had got except the light of that particular morning. It chose an unoccupied place on a knob and gave itself two such eyes.

They were a little too far apart. At first it could see both sides of a rush at once, which no bird can do. It put that right. It spent several afternoons there, and on the fourth afternoon went down the wrong runnel and had the experience of standing on dry pebbles while a quite young bird, not thirty feet away, got the white grub.

What it had not expected was how pleasant it was to have the water come round its feet, on an afternoon when it had gone the right way. It kept the feet as well as the eyes.

In the course of the winter a piece came off the underside of the shelf. Such pieces are continually coming off. Down below, among quite tall trees, there are slabs still lying grass upwards, with a little of the shelf growing on them that does not grow anywhere else in the valley. This piece was scarcely larger than a door. It went down in the dark without touching the cliff, and in falling took away the outer part of a stair on which nine birds had been accustomed to sleep. None went down with it. They were out in the moss at the time.

Eight found other places. The ninth went a considerable distance in behind the water, where two beds of lava had drawn apart. It was dry there, and there was a draught through the back which kept the feathers moving agreeably on either side of its neck. The power, which had taken a place on the next stair down, could hear the brushing of the toes until the bird had turned round and settled.

In the night the water got into the cleft above, and froze. By morning the entrance was rather less than the depth of a bird's breast. The bird could put its head out. Indeed from below there was nothing very remarkable about the sight; a seam bird will often spend the first hour of daylight looking out of a hole. Only when it drew its head in and tried coming out backwards was it clear that something was wrong.

The power could have warmed the rock. There were, however, a great many things in the rock besides this bird. In a film of water just beyond its tail lived some colourless jointed creatures which went from the egg to the grave without ever having occasion to get a purchase against a current. The ice was holding back quite a head of water. Nor was there much to be gained by putting food into the cleft. What the bird wanted was to come out, and very likely to go back in again.

There was a part of the lower sill as broad as the bird and about as thick as the grey skin on boiled milk. The power took this away. It took away another such thickness, and another. It put the stone down in the valley in the form of a rather handsome smooth pebble, for which it had no immediate use. When there was room for the bird it stopped.

For some time nothing happened. It was disagreeable to the bird to have anything going on under its chin. It stayed well back in the dark. Then a fly walked along the sill, attending with its front feet to a drop of moisture much too big to drink. The bird came forward, took the fly, and was outside. It stretched one wing down past its foot and spread half its tail, then did the same on the other side. Before going out into the moss it turned and looked into the cleft, as a bird will look into a place where it has lately found something to eat.

It was in fact back in there that night. This gave the power more satisfaction than if the bird had gone elsewhere, though elsewhere would have done just as well.

In spring the eyes and feet appeared again on the knob. They had by then acquired most of the rest of a seam bird, including the orange inside of the bill, which was a help on days when the power got things wrong. An old hen would sometimes bring it something. At first it was inclined not to take this, knowing what a number of mouths there were already on the stones. But it observed that when it refused, the hen did not take the hopper to another stone. She stood holding it across her bill and turning her head first one way and then the other, unable to get on with her afternoon. After that it took what she brought.

Quite late in the summer the two of them went down together into a dry course, the power a little ahead. There was a broken place in the light which the hen, from where she stood, could not have seen. She followed anyway. They waited so long that a small white butterfly settled on the wet-coloured part of the hen's back and opened its wings flat to the sun. When the water came it divided at a pebble and came to all four feet at once.
