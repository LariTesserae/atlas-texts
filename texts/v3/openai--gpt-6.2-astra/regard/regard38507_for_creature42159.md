The power that took an interest in the weighhouse first noticed the sister's laugh in the stone. It went a little way up the spar, much less far than the knock of the knife. To hear the end of it the power had to come down.

It stayed for supper. None of the eleven knew it was there. The one who came to complain said that a person might as well eat his own hinge, and took some more. Up in the square the weighman put away his small weights inside his large ones and locked the cupboard. For seven hours there was very little to hear on either side of the tread.

In the morning the power went up to look at the paving.

The worst slab was not, as one might have supposed from below, the slab nearest the stair. It was a long one well out in the square, where the carts turned to bring their loads level with the weighing platform. A wheel going onto one end lifted the other end the thickness of a barley hull. A wheel going off let it fall. Underneath, a rib of sound stone ran without a break from the bed of the slab to the crown of the little vault. The blow travelled along it beautifully. In Tesset's kitchen the wax jumped out of the joint in a single piece and lay on the floor like a dropped eyebrow.

The power could have put an end to this in any number of ways. What it did, on the second morning, was to take the weight of the loose end as it came down. It did this for one cart only.

The mule went on pulling. The carter went on thinking about his breakfast. In the town there was the accustomed rising roar, with a piece missing out of the middle. Tesset, who was cutting yesterday's bent growth out of a bed of amber fans, heard the small dry succession of ticks with which a fan opens. She had heard it before at evening, but never so close to her knife. She took her thumb off the pleat just in time. A little farther down the bed another gardener straightened up and rubbed both sides of his jaw.

The power tried it again on market day. It took the falls of the long slab and left the rest of the paving alone. This time there were quite long intervals in which a person at market could keep one hand on her basket. An old man who sold silver grass went on signing his prices, since he had been signing them for forty years and had no intention of shouting numbers at people like a boy selling damaged roots. People signed back. There was no difficulty.

That afternoon, when the carts were gone, Tesset found a fan with all its pleats spread and a narrow white line across them, where the edge of the next fan had rested against it. The two had grown up so close together that there was no room for both to open. She fetched her paddle and moved the younger one by the breadth of her hand. She had to do this in five places. It was more gardening than she had reckoned on for the day, and she came in late.

The power was satisfied about the slab. It had no wish, however, to spend the remainder of the life of the square standing underneath one end of it. Nor did it particularly want its work to depend upon the continuance of its good opinion of carters, which, after a wet market morning in the square, was not what it had been.

There was a woman charged with the repair of the paving. Twice she had been sent to look at the long slab, and twice she had walked the length of it and found nothing amiss. It took the weight of the loaded wheel, well out on the corner, to make it rock. The power waited until she was crossing the square behind a flour cart. When the wheel went on, it kept the far end up for a moment after the wheel had gone off. A little fan of flour slid into the open joint. The slab came down and puffed a fine white line across the toe of her boot.

She backed the carter up and made him do it again. He objected that there was a fee for a second weighing. She said she did not propose to weigh him.

That was the whole of the power's work on the paving. The woman had the slab up before dinner. She did not touch the rib of sound rock under it. She put down a proper bed of screenings and turned the slab end for end, so that the worn corner no longer took the turning wheel. On the underside, in red chalk, she put the date and her mark, as she did on all her work, including work which nobody would see again in her lifetime.

Below, it was by no means quiet. There were still all the other slabs, and the men who brought the millstones still came down the weighhouse stair with that peculiar last stamp of men who have set down something heavy and find themselves unexpectedly light. But the sound no longer had time to go quite round the saucer before the next blow sent it round again. The dust lay down. Here and there on the bare slope it kept its stripes for a long time.

The power went back once, rather late in the season. The bare side still had no track across it. Nobody had gone to fetch the bright flake. There were now two beds of amber fans where there had been one; Tesset had taken advantage of a thin run of the black seam which had never been worth the trouble of working before. She was on her knees persuading a root to lie flat. It had a kink in it like a folded tongue.

From the town came a thin, exasperating whistle, rising by little jumps. Tesset sat back on her clogs. The youngest had found that in the intervals between carts he could get a note out of the kitchen wall by blowing into it himself. Presently there was another note, lower and considerably steadier. Someone was showing him how.

Tesset turned one half of her collar towards the house. With the other half she was listening to a tick close at hand, and when she put her paddle down again she put it a little to the left, clear of the opening fan. The power, which had been on the point of stopping the whistle, went about its business.
