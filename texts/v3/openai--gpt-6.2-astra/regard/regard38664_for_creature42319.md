The power first noticed the moth by noticing the bead. It had been following the bead up the leaf, taking pleasure in the way it kept its bright surface even when squeezed quite flat between the leaf and a crossing stalk. At the six feet it became two beads. The power attended to both. When they came together again it was still attending to the place between them.

It could feel where the hooks went into the leaf. There was no feeling left in the hooks. There was, however, a good deal in the leaf, which had begun to draw a thin tough skin across each of the six punctures. The power followed the work for a while. It did not take the moth down.

At the lip there were females still walking. One had got as far as the turn and encountered a difficulty. The root she was on had doubled back under the strain of a feeding fish and gone through the space between her body and one folded fan. When the fish let go, the root straightened. She now had five feet on the sea, one foot in the air, and a length of perfectly straight root through the crook of her arm. She went on walking with the free foot. There is a peculiar largeness about the movement of one small foot that has lost its purchase. It describes the whole arc its joints permit.

The power had the means to put her on the rainward face of a leaf in the meadow. It had seen just where the others ended. But the water between the lip and the meadow was cool, with warmer streaks in it; it had little hard-bodied creatures in it which struck a swimmer on the belly and went spinning away; and no one yet knew quite how this moth would row.

It took hold of the root a little way on either side of her and brought its two holds closer together. Into the slack she drew her arm. Immediately the power let go. The root sprang straight with a note which brought three fish to the spot, all putting out their lips. It was the smallest of the three that took her in and blew her out. She opened one fan well before the other and went round in a circle.

For the rest of the afternoon the power lay under the lip. It was tempted to make the alteration permanent. A little more slack at the turn, hardly enough to show in the admirable curve of the water, and that particular mischance need never occur again. While it considered this, an egg drop swelled above it. At the change of stroke the tension came on the threads; the drop went in whole, leaving a little cold dimple in the air. Farther along, a walking female stopped and turned at a tapping so faint that even the power, with its great attention, had taken it for the settling of salt. Both these things required a taut thread. There were doubtless others. It left the tension as it was.

At dusk it found the swimmer again, more by luck than by anything else. She had learned to open her late fan a little wider than her early one and was making good headway, slantwise, with a small correction every dozen strokes. The shadow of the cloud lay ahead of her. At its edge she stopped rowing. For a time she hung in the clear green light, her fans spread to their full extent. Then she went in.

The power stayed out in the sun. It had a new thing to attend to there. Each stroke of the fans had left in the water a pair of turning rings, one large, one small. The last pair widened against its face. It held quite still until it could no longer tell them from the ordinary movement of the sea.
