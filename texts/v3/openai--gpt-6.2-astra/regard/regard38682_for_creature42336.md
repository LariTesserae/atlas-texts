The first thing the power did was to go round to the other side of the gate and listen there. A knock heard from the lower reach is a rather paltry sound. There is a little jar in the handrail. A moorhen will sometimes lift one foot and set it down again in the same place. Nothing on that side suggests an afternoon spent holding a copper door.

There was a considerable thickness of old stone between the door and the lower reach. It had been cut for a lock which was never built, and the present gate rested on one end of it. The rest went under a grass bank. It was very sound stone, without so much as a drain through it, and there was no one in it at all. The power tried it with a little of the weight from the handrail. A daisy on the bank shook some rain off. There was room there for a considerable noise.

At the next opening the power did not take the knock away. It had seen the white dust travel and the trays go in; it did not know what else in the town depended on the gate. Instead it put a joint between the old stone and the jamb of the copper door. The joint was no wider than the scratch left by a key. With the door hanging straight, nothing whatever happened. Drawn a little way back, the door could be made to touch it.

For two days nobody touched it. On the third, well on in the passage of a blow, the door went back and stayed there. Up on the bank a whole ring of daisies simultaneously lost their rain. Down below, the last of the clatter died out in the middle of a street. It was an odd sight. Half the dark undersides of the paving blocks were showing and half the pale tops, with a ragged line between, as though somebody had gone away in the middle of laying a floor.

There was a good deal of coming and going to the door that afternoon. The one from the deep houses tried it three times in quick succession. The one that attended to the glass brought door and stone together first at a corner, then along the whole edge. Someone found that by laying a chip of mortar under the door one could leave it there and go about one's business. Someone else took the chip away. By evening there were several chips in a little rack, all of different thicknesses. The power had not thought of that.

The next morning a knock went through from first to last in the old way. The morning after, it scarcely got past the square. There proved to be no rule about it that could be made out from the towpath. Even on a particularly hard day, with barges waiting nose to tail in the upper reach, the copper door might swing free for hours. Once, on a Sunday with scarcely a boat about, it was wedged back before the first pink showed. In the long quiet at the rear of the town a ceiling which had always been gray was taken down in little squares, turned over, and put up again. Its other side was yellow. Visitors went quite a way out of their way to pass under it.

There remained the question of the frost. Someone on the towpath had already drawn a chalk mark round the break, and at the lock house there was a sack of quick-setting cement on a handcart. Here the power found it necessary to do something on a rather larger scale. In the night it settled the handcart on its springs, very gently, until the iron rim of one wheel lay against the sill. When the keeper took hold of the handles in the morning, he had the whole passage of a knock through both palms.

He stood there until it was over. Then he set the handles down and got on his knees. He was in time to see a collecting tray come out. As he watched, it folded away and came out again. There was not a speck of dust in the water.

The sack went back to the lock house. Later the keeper fitted a low iron hoop in the path, of the sort used to keep a wheel off a young tree. He set it well back, having noticed where the light got in. He had no very satisfactory account of this item to give at the quarterly inspection. In the end it went down as protection to exposed work, which was accurate enough.

By the time of the next frost the rack at the copper door had acquired a small blue lamp. It was the spare bead from the niche. There was a chip so thin that it was difficult to find in the dark, and this was where the light was wanted. The niche stood empty for a while. Then the fourth light on the new street went pale twice, and a shutter at the square answered, and a little later the same thing happened at the fifth light without an answer. It took most of the evening for the fifth to discover the empty niche. A sound made there came back.

For several evenings thereafter there was a tiresome little clicking in that part of town. People going home from the square took the buried street more often than was strictly necessary. The power, which could have stopped the echo with a grain of sand, left it alone.
