The power came near enough to see what happened at the fine end of the brightness. A hair of willow root crossed the gap there. On one side of the hair the sky showed plain; on the other there was still sky, but seen through the thin brown skin of the root. In the bubble the difference was less than the difference between the two sides of a blade of grass.

For a while it looked no farther. It had come a long way, in the manner of its kind, which was to have left a great many things alone in order to attend to one.

From outside, the meadow had seemed to be waiting. This was especially so if one noticed the shrew. A shrew can go very little time without food. There was the fine machine of its jaw, the ear laid back in the fur, the neck narrow enough for finger and thumb to go round it together; and there, a little below, was something to eat. It was a great temptation to give the shrew the next instant. The power knew very well how. The slate would not have split, nor would there have been any startling light in the galleries. The shrew would simply have had its chin a little farther forward in the grit.

Before doing this the power attended to the feel of the feet through the shrew's jaw.

There was much more of the little swimmer in that feeling than could be seen by looking down the pool. One pair of feet was free of the floor altogether. Another had hold of a shred of willow skin so soft from lying in the water that it scarcely counted as a thing apart from the feet. The shrew could tell the two pairs apart. The feeling went back along its jaw to a place almost under its ear. There was no ache in it of having held still too long. That ache had been in the power, which had come from a long way off and was accustomed to arriving.

It left the shrew its whole attention. It did not put into the small head, beside the distinct feel of the feet, the feel of being watched.

There was something it could do farther down. Well below the hollow, out on the open face of the ridge, a thread of water issued from the slate. This was the other end of the low *rrr*. In winter a little tongue of ice grew out there, with a hollow underside like the underside of a human tongue. In summer the water slid along a shelf and came off it in drops. Each drop took a grain or two from a bed of red earth that had gathered on the shelf. In time the shelf would be clean. In more time the water would cut back into the seam and find the common floor beneath the bays.

The power put a finger on the shelf. It could keep the red earth there. It could keep the willow hair across the sky when the rest of the ridge had worn down to the height of the trees.

Under its finger, in the red earth, was the seed of a birch. One of its two papery wings had rotted away and the other was almost sound. The seed had begun to split. A white tip, so tender that the weight of a wet grain would have bruised it, was coming out against the slate. Here, quite plainly, was something for which it mattered what happened next.

The power took its finger away. A drop came down. For a little while it stood among the grains without sinking in. Then the red darkened all round the seed.

It was on this shelf that the power sat for the rest of the afternoon. It had a back broad enough for the sun to warm and, towards evening, for a small fly to settle on to get the last of the warmth. When the fly had gone it got up. No one coming along the ridge afterwards would have known where it had sat, except that the earth in one place was damp a little farther back under the lip of the slate than could readily be accounted for by the drip.

It did not go up to take a last look. It had seen the shrew very well.
