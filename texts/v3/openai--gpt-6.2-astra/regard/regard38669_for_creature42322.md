There came under the shelf, one afternoon, a power capable of lifting it. It came in at the lower end, where some earth had slipped from the bank and there was a fan of loose stuff on the floor. In crossing this it put back a small snail which the slip had carried down. The snail had been going down of its own accord. It was halfway across the fan again before the power had got as far as the first towers. This was the first thing it learned under the shelf.

It stayed a considerable time. It found the old ones going round the step and was glad it had not done anything about the step. It found that the little owls, whose proceedings gave it some uneasiness, lived largely on the glossy roaches, and that the roaches went into the owl houses quite freely to eat what was left of other roaches. It had rather a good view of things at this scale. With one part of its attention it could follow a grub eating the inside of a seed and with another see the blue bush bending very slowly under the weight of a gray beast that was trying to reach the seed. The result was not, as might be supposed, to make the grub seem small.

What interested it particularly was the noise in the floor. There were things to be heard there besides the heels. A builder dropping a shaving made practically no impression, but a file of builders rounding a corner had a recognizable sound. There was a faint long complaint from the roots in the clefts when the wind went over the upland. And once, well on in the dark, there came from the limestone behind the owl houses a sound so low and handsome that the power listened for its repetition with pleasure. When it came again it was not quite the same note. A piece of the roof of the sleeping crack, some eight feet long and thickest at the back, had begun to part company with the rest of the hill.

The power held it where it was. This took very little of its strength. It could have held it until there was no longer a shelf, but there were reasons for letting it down. At its thick end it had already sunk a little. The strain was passing from it into a much larger thickness of sound stone, much as the weight of a person sitting at the end of a bench may be felt by another person sitting some way along.

There were seven in the crack. The power knew the sound that would bring them out. It even knew, from listening on the oval, the slight inequality in the two strokes made by the old female whose back was nearest the light. It considered making that sound outside. But she was in there with them. There was no telling what sort of a difficulty it might be, for a beast asleep with its nose against her, to hear her calling from the street.

Rain was another matter. They knew what to do about rain. On the rare occasions when water came down a cleft and worked in along a bedding plane, they left the crack at the first touch of damp on the pink sill. A little way up in the hill there was still some water from the last such occasion, enough to fill the bowl of a spoon. The power let it through.

First one sole disappeared and then another. The old female came out with a wet streak up one side of her face. She sat washing it while the others got down, one young one going backward and feeling about with a long foot for a ledge on which it was already standing. They went along behind the owl houses and in at a narrow place previously used for keeping empty seed purses. No one tapped. It was apparently not necessary to hold a meeting about water.

When the last of the wet had sunk into the sill the power lowered the stone. It took until morning to get it comfortably bedded. An owl in the end house came out backward twice in the course of the operation, looked over its shoulder and went in again. By daylight there was only a horizontal pencil mark where the entrance had been, and below it a clean flake of pink limestone which had not seen daylight since there was a river there.

The new quarters were not quite so good as the old. They were deeper, which was an advantage in summer, but there was no sill for the young. For a few days the power was tempted to attend to this. Then it saw one of the pink-soled beasts sitting in an empty ground-floor window, watching the pieces of leaf go by at the height of its nose. There were drawbacks to this arrangement also. From time to time a builder came in over the beast's shoulder and had to be wiped off. But the beast went back to the window after wiping it.

The power did not settle under the shelf. It had enjoyed being there, which is a rather different thing from being needed there. On its way out it crossed the fan of earth again. The snail was nowhere to be seen. There was a dry track up the bank and a dry track down it, both shining in the last of the sun, and on this occasion the power was content not to know which had been made last.
