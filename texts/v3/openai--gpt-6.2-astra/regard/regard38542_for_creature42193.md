The first thing the power did was to put a finger in the water that went into the little e, and the second was to take it out again. A letter made in this fashion is not necessarily a letter addressed to anybody. In the absence of the finger it was an e again almost at once.

It stayed for a turn of the orchard. This is a longer time in winter than in summer. In summer, when the raft rides high, the spout falls nearly flat on the mint and gets a good purchase; in winter a good deal of its work goes into making a hole. The power had time to observe that, for half an hour before the branch reached the brick, the purses in that branch shut up. The noise of gravel ceased as far back as the fork. The branch went by stiff, the pearl squeaked, and then, beginning at the tip, the ordinary noise came back. Whatever the brick was for, there seemed no occasion to pad it.

There was one thing missing. The electricity man, if he wanted the belt off, could take it off. There was a loose pulley for the purpose, just visible beside the fast one through the slot in the floor. The fork for shifting the belt was still in place. All that had gone was the long iron handle. Where it had passed through the boards there was an oblong hole, made round at one end by the business of a good many mornings. A small red cord from the middle tree had come up through this hole and was feeling about under the bench. There was blue chalk on its tip.

The power spent a wet afternoon in the water below the shed. It found three pear labels, the bowl of a pipe and the iron handle, in that order. There was a mussel on the handle. It was one of the quite separate sort, with a little garden of its own about the hinge, and instead of disturbing it the power took the shape of the handle, which was not a particularly difficult shape, and slid up through the boards into the socket of the fork.

For nine days nothing much happened. The red cord lay against the handle and received from it a fine steady trembling. On the tenth day, at about the time the glazed brick squeaked, it drew tight. The belt went across. There continued to be a whir in the shed, and for a surprisingly long time there continued to be water in the mint, but it was possible to distinguish the instant at which one ceased to be the cause of the other.

By evening there was the smell of pears. Below the slot the dimpled thing came up, tipped out its pint and sank again. It did this four times. The fifth time it remained where it was, with the juice standing in it, very bright in the little light that came down past the belt. From the direction of the gate came a single gulp. The juice sank into the dimples. The cord gave a shove, rather harder than was necessary, and put the water on again.

In the next fortnight it did this twice more. The second stoppage lasted barely a minute. Thereafter there was no particular rule about it. Once the orchard lay for six days with the branch an inch short of the brick. The heron, which had got into the habit of coming for the squeak, spent much of the sixth day looking indignantly down the lane.

The power would have been quite willing to stay. Indeed there are less agreeable things to be than a handle in a warm shed, with the smell of mint coming through the wall and a pencil to be heard going into a corner whenever the floor takes a new inclination. But by November the red cord had grown down one side of it and up the other. There was a loop of hard wood through the eye of the fork. The power tried being a little less handle each day. By the first frost it was no handle at all, and the belt went across as well as ever.

There are still no pears on the trees. On certain mornings there is a yellowjacket walking about the slot in the floor, with the cautious high step of a person crossing a kitchen that has just been washed. The electricity man has seen it. He has seen the empty socket, too, and the belt running idle; and once, while he stood wondering what ought to be done about it, he saw the fork move over of itself. He shut the shed door gently. At the gate he had to wait a moment. A good stout rope of water was coming down into the mint, and the orchard, getting under way again, had laid the end of a branch across the latch.
