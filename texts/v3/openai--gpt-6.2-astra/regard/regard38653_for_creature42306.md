On the underside of the sill, just over the white animal, there was a splinter coming loose. Its free end pointed east. At rest it cleared the bank by a comfortable distance, but when the tread was down the two were very nearly in contact. A white thread of cotton had caught on the point. At every weighing the thread picked up a little black meal. By the end of the month it looked like a leg hanging out of the wood.

The power that had taken an interest in the place watched the thread through a Monday morning. It watched a young one get hold of the lower end and try to carry it away. Another came to help. Between them they pulled themselves quite off the ground. The white animal, bringing a foot home, brushed them loose without uncoupling its feet. They set off in opposite directions, one with a speck of meal and the other with nothing.

At eleven a lorry loaded rather too much on the left came creeping onto the iron. The bracket sang. The bank swelled against the hairy props, the sill came down, and the splinter entered the bank almost to its root. On the rise it brought up the cotton, two husks, a quantity of meal and one of the young. This last hung on to its husk. It had been its business ever since it got out of the egg to hang on to whatever it was carrying, against forces quite out of proportion to its size. It did not appear to have a second arrangement for occasions on which the thing it was carrying hung on to something else.

There was ample power available to hold the tread steady with the lorry on it. But the lorry had been sent there to be weighed. There was power enough to lay the young one down in the fault, too. The difficulty with that was the splinter would still be there.

What happened, as the rear wheels came off, was a sound which not even a man with his cheek to the floor could have heard. The last two fibres parted at the root of the splinter. It came down point first, very slowly on account of the cotton. The broad end struck the bank and rolled over. The young one had six feet on the rock again and still had its husk. After a short struggle, in the course of which it turned completely round under the husk, it got off with it.

There was a light patch on the underside of the sill, the shape of the bowl of a spoon. That was the whole alteration to the works of the scale. On the weigh ticket the lorry was found to be a hundredweight under what the driver said it was, and he went round to the office to have this looked into.

Below, the cotton was taken apart before noon. The splinter proved too heavy for the carriers. It lay across the mouth of the fault until a falling tread lifted the thin end and the white animal, putting out a foot, happened to let it down on the other side. From then on it lay lengthwise, with a passage on either side of it. The carriers going up took the nearer passage. Those coming down took the nearer passage also. There were the usual difficulties whenever two met.

The power stayed for the afternoon. There were losses to the west, including a well-fed bean which got all the way past the brass and then, in trying to right itself, kicked against the stone and landed between the spread forelegs. There was a new carrier, so nearly transparent that the first thing it put in its mouth remained visible a long way back. Nothing else was done.

Toward closing time a mother came by hauling a shell with eggs in it. The scratch she was making for was beyond the fault. At the end of the splinter she let the shell go, went on a little way, and came back for it. She tried the lip of the shell under the thin end of the wood. It fitted there. She went off toward the wick in the last long bar of light, leaving the eggs in a place that had not been there that morning.
