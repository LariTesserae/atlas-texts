Late that year, as the woman was feeling for the door of her house, somebody put a hand out from inside and found the slit for her. She supposed it was her sister's youngest, who had a way of coming in to see whether there was anything to eat. But it was a stranger, and had been standing in the dark, the plants in the bowls having gone out. It had not known whether she would want them picked fresh.

It was a great power. There was very little room for it in the house. The woman put the bowls one inside another and turned the largest upside down, which gave it somewhere to sit.

“I can take you down,” it said.

The woman felt along the shelf for the twist of dry weed she kept for visitors. It was in much the same state as her hands, and they sat for a while sucking the salt out of it.

“Is there a way back?”

“Yes.”

“Then come on a wet day. On a clear one I should like to be at the post.”

It came in the morning with rain standing on its shoulders. She had been thinking about the descent. She had put on two tunics, one inside the other, and had wound her hair close to her head, as she used to do to work in the weed. In the pocket of the inner tunic she had a copper leaf that had come down into the dry bed with a lot of other rubbish when a reach of the river turned over. It was rolled, and there was something in it. Every few days, held to the light, the something had seemed to be a little farther round the roll.

The power took her down by the outside of the mountain. There were shelves there, quite invisible from above. On one of them a tuft of the yellow moss grew upside down, in the shelter of a lip. On another lay the blade of a paddle. She knew the two long holes in it for the kind that used to be lent to children, that went through the water easily and splashed the person behind. She had always supposed that everything that went over went all the way.

The top of the cloud was cold. Then for a long way it was neither dark nor light, and rain went past her upwards, though when she put her tongue out for it, it was the ordinary kind.

They came out underneath in the afternoon. The falling water made such a noise here that there could be no question of talking. The power had to lay a finger against the hollow below her ear to turn her head.

There was the grey animal. It was standing up.

For all her life she had known the shape of the little clearings the animals made in the canopy, and the great grey oval of a side lying asleep against the roots. It had never occurred to her to wonder what was folded up beneath. This one stood in water to its knees. Its legs were a great way apart, to give the belly room between them. The forelegs had rubbed two smooth places on a bank of red mud. On its back the trees grew very much taller than on the backs she knew; the lower branches had died in shade and still bore the dry cups of their floats. The leaves were copper on both sides.

She could smell the hidden flower even through the smell of broken water. She began to work at the fastening of her inner tunic.

It was a bad knot for stiff fingers. The power made no move to help. She struck it rather sharply on the wrist and held the knot out, and it undid the knot at once.

They put the rolled leaf in a fork, well above the mud. If the grub came out, it would have a short flight to make. If it did not come out, there was a good depth of soil under it. All along the back there were faint blue flashes where other things were flying.

On the way up they stopped at the shelf with the paddle. She turned it over. Three little pink knobs clung to the underside, each in a patch of wet, and she put it back as it had been.

It was still raining at the houses. The power saw her indoors and got a light for the bowl. She was tired in the peculiar way one is tired after looking all day at things a long way off. She asked if it would be going down again.

It said that it could.

“There are others who'd go,” she said. “My sister wouldn't, for one. Don't have them all down there on my account.”

For some time after that the power stayed at the last post. Those who wished to make the descent took hold of it there. A good many went at first. One brought back a lump of red mud and made a cup which leaked a little, and another came home greatly taken with the long legs and for the rest of his life carved grey animals with the legs too long even for the animals below. Some went as far as the cloud and found that enough. The woman's sister went as far as the paddle. It was one she had lost herself. She had made the holes with a hot stone and one was much nearer the edge than the other. She was pleased to have this fault in her workmanship confirmed, after such a length of time and so much difference of opinion about it.

In time it became quite usual to have been down, or not to have been. The power did not require a house. In wet weather there was a drier place on the last post, about the breadth of a hand, and that was where one took hold.

The old woman went on coming to the rim on clear evenings. People who had been down sometimes came and sat with her, and people who had not been down sat there too. It made very little difference to what could be seen from the post. A copper leaf turned over in falling; the lip caught the light; for an instant, with the whole white country of cloud behind it, one could see through the thin part to the small dark weight at the fold. Then one lost it.
