The power that took an interest in Copper Yard came down by the stairs. It looked like a woman carrying a chair, a sight that occasioned no particular remark until it came to the last step and there was the question of where to put the chair. Ada moved her own a little. Len had to alter the turn he gave his cupboard check.

For six evenings the woman sat there. On the sixth she asked Ada if there was anything she wanted done.

“About what?” said Ada.

There was no very good answer to this. The woman explained that she had no business of her own in the yard. She had come because she could do things that were difficult for other people, and sometimes things that were quite out of their reach. If Ada had anything of that sort in mind, she would be glad to hear it.

“Can you carry that chair away with you when you go?” said Ada. “Only we can't have it here in the mornings.”

The woman said she could.

It was Thursday before Ada had anything else to say to her. In the meantime the new leaf on the fig had opened out. It had creases like a hand that has been in a glove too long. Ada had noticed that the woman did not touch it, though she had seen several people do so.

“I should like to go up when it's raining,” Ada said.

The woman looked at the stairs.

“I know,” said Ada. “I've got legs. The thing is to know when. By the time the coats come down you've missed the beginning, and by the time you've got somebody at the table it's generally over. I'm not asking you to make it rain.”

She wanted the kind that begins with separate drops on the paving, far enough apart that you can see the shape of each. There was a smell to it that Len brought home, though on Len it was mixed up with the smell of the school cloakroom. She would like to be there before the first drop fell. She didn't particularly want company.

It would have been a small matter to the woman to bring rain into Copper Yard. For a moment she saw quite plainly how it would look in the lamplight. Then she folded up her chair, having discovered the use of that little moment people ordinarily have between wanting to do a thing and being able to do it.

On Tuesday, shortly before eleven, Ada put her fingers into the wool and found a cool button. It was not cool in the ordinary way of bone or shell. It was as cool as the rail of the stairs where the shade ends. She knew what it meant. She lifted the canvas behind her and took off her apron in the kitchen. Then she went round to the next shop by the lane and asked the man there to stop feeding the mattresses through for a quarter of an hour.

“Something wrong?”

“No,” said Ada. “I want to go out.”

He looked at the clock. “I'll have my bread now, then.”

She was on the pavement looking at the sky when the first drop struck her on the eyelid. It was quite a hard blow. She had forgotten that about rain. She shut both eyes and the next drop hit the back of her hand, and then there were dark shapes all over the paving, some no bigger than nailheads and some as big as the penny pieces there used to be. A boy ran by with a folded newspaper over a pie. Across the street a shopkeeper stood winding in a striped blind that would have done very well to shelter under, but of course he wanted to keep it dry.

Ada walked as far as the crossing. The gutter was taking water now, carrying little rafts of the yellow stuff that comes off the city trees. She got the smell she had come for, and another one, a rather rank smell from the first wetting of a horse blanket on a cart. She had forgotten that too. By the crossing the rain was so thick that the separate marks had joined and the paving was all one color. She went back to work with the neck of her dress stuck to her.

“Well?” said the man through the partition.

“It's coming straight down,” she said.

He sent a mattress through. After a minute he put his hand in for the buttons and she tipped them into it as usual. Neither of them mentioned the quarter of an hour again, except that on Friday he asked her if she could manage the feed as well as the table while he went up to get a tooth looked at. She could, at something under half speed, and did.

That evening Len bent down to pick up his check beside her chair and paused with his nose in her hair.

“You've been up,” he said, offended for an instant, as if she had called at the school without seeing him. Then he sniffed again. “Not today?”

“Tuesday.”

“That's not rain, then. That's your soap.”

The woman did not come back for some time. When she did she left her chair at street level and stood a while at the head of the stairs. She had an idea for a bell that would sound below whenever rain was on the way. It needn't be a loud bell. Still, there would be no hearing it at the wool table without hearing it also in the room where Len slept, and Ada had asked for a morning in the rain, not for a bell.

While she stood there a hand came up out of the chute and felt about on the pavement. It belonged to the sweeper, who sometimes came this far on his break without troubling to climb the last two steps. His fingers found a patch of sun. He left his hand there. After a bit the woman took her chair and went on, walking on the street side so that her shadow would not pass across his knuckles.
