The first thing the power thought to do was to take a little off the sill of the lower socket. It was a considerable height for an animal of that thickness, and on the evening the power first watched, the animal slipped at the top and had the last half inch to do twice. It came up the second time with a grain of brown glass caught in one foot. It attended to the glass before it went indoors.

The power waited to see the place at low water. There was just enough water left behind the sill to cover a back. A split thumbnail appeared above it now and then, wiping the comb. To have made the going easier by the thickness of a penny would have let most of that water out.

After that the power took rather less notice of what looked difficult from the track. It got down to the level of the saddles. In a week it could tell, from the inclination of the paddles, which of two apparently identical pieces of water was worth standing in. It never got very good at telling which stone would click next. There was a small one to the left of the chain that it would have sworn was about to go, and never did.

In November a screw fell out of the brass plate at the back of the pump-house door. It fell into the thrift and did no harm there. But the next time a man came to read the gauge, the handle came away in his hand. He had a job getting the door shut afterwards. Before he left he wrote up both matters on a printed sheet. Under GENERAL CONDITION he put down the other thing that had bothered him for years, which was that you could drive a vehicle straight off the end of the track.

He was quite right about this. In February two men came with a steel rail the width of the track, with two feet turned down, and a bucket of quick-setting cement. They laid the rail on its side on the stone lip. The feet had been made to fit the sockets. One man went back for water while the other knelt to clean the sockets out.

The power was well able to break the rail. It could have put the bucket out in the middle of the estuary. What it did was to hold the sun on the lower hole for the time it takes a cloud edge to go by. The man had his bent bit of wire half an inch from the rim. In the light he saw an eye turn to look at the wire. Then, well away to the left of where he would have put it, he saw the other eye.

He sat back on his heels. When the water came he was still there.

‘Look what uses this,’ he said.

The other man had a look. The leather lifted and showed its feet, which settled a question between them as to whether it was something washed in dead. They took the rail back to the yard. There was a deal of difference between preventing a van going over the edge and preventing it getting within six feet of the edge, but it was all in favour of the latter. By Friday there was a stout rail across the track six feet back, with room to walk round either end. The man who had knelt down put the screw back in the door plate too. It had a bit of a green stain on its head from the thrift.

The power did nothing about the pipit. It saw it take a clear-backed one no bigger than a flake of salt from the ledge where it generally got sand hoppers. Afterwards it watched the bird for a while on the far side of the bank. The bird had a life there which was not visible from the hollow.

At the next high water the man who had found the eyes brought his dinner down and sat on the new rail to eat it. He was in time to see the pale things lift into the reflection, though from six feet back he missed the feet folding away. He finished his tea long after he had meant to and took his flask and paper back with him.

By dusk the one at the gate was home. It had slipped once on the sill. There was a little track in the wet where it had slipped, and another, sideways across the first, where it had got a purchase and gone in. The power could distinguish the two in very little light.
