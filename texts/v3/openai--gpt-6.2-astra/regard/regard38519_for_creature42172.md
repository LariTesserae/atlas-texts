The power heard the taps again towards morning. This time they came from some distance apart. Two were at the mouth of the blister and two were well out on the shelf. There was no answer from the young birds. All three were asleep, with their heads pushed in under the breast of the bird at the entrance, so that it appeared to have six small feet in addition to its own.

Out on the shelf the other bird had found the water. In the night a piece of the yellow deposit had come away below ground. Water which had been obliged to come almost to the surface could now go quite comfortably along under a roof for another forty yards. It came out at the foot of a low step. There was sedge there already, quite white and lying flat, from some earlier occasion on which the water had taken this way. As the ice let go of it it rose a blade at a time. The rail was walking about on the mat, lifting the blades that had not yet risen and looking under them.

The power could have put the piece of yellow stuff back. It lay rocking in a cup in the stone, within an inch of the place it had come from. But the bird out on the shelf had a grub already, and there was a great quantity of live root in the white mat. The power left the piece rocking and went along under the lava by the way the bird had come.

For most of the distance there was room enough. A rail, even a young one, will not put its foot down a hole merely because it is dark. It feels on ahead with the long middle toe, and if there is nothing there the foot comes back and goes out a little to one side. There were places where a bird might take some time in this way but none where it need stop. At the low step, however, there was a piece of quite smooth wall. The grown bird had gone up it with one stroke of its wings. On the face of it, about halfway up, was a shelf no wider than the back of a knife, on which the old water had begun to lay down a little rough brown stone.

Here the power did something. It let the new water have one afternoon on that shelf. For the rest of the flow it was still early morning; a bead which had been gathering on the point of a root had not yet come off. On the little shelf the water ran and steamed and left its load behind, much as it had done elsewhere along the step for a hundred years. When the bead fell there was a brown lip with a good deal of grit to it, broad enough to take the end of a toe.

It was nearly light before the family came through. The young birds went in no particular order until they reached the wall, where the first one stopped and the other two got up on its back. There was some shrill calling from the bottom of the step. Then a red head appeared above it, went down, and appeared again with a piece of white grass laid across it like a whisker. In a little while all three were out. One of them had used the brown lip. The other two had got up by treading on the tail of the parent, which had gone down to see what the noise was about.

By noon the grit on the lip had caught a single husk of sedge. It stayed there through a week of wind. There was good feeding at the step, and before the husk blew away the young rails could go up and down there without the least use of their wings. They went down much oftener than there seemed any occasion for. There was a place at the bottom where one could push one's head through the falling water and come out with a dry patch on the very top, as red and round as ever.
