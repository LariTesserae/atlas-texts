What drew the power’s attention was the eight. Ink laid over dry ink carries a little more light than ink laid on paper. In the open book, under the keeper’s elbow, that was the last figure to be legible when the lamps went out.

The calf responsible for it was asleep with one hind foot in the water. It had got out on its third attempt, by a method peculiar to itself: it set its chin on the ledge, went over on its back and for a moment had all four feet in the air. There was a hollow in the stone which just took its shoulder. Next morning it got down head foremost in the usual way. The power, which had waited to see whether the calf could manage both operations, turned its attention to the water.

Poor flow means different things above and below a wheel. Above it there can be six feet of quite stationary water, perfectly good for bathing, with a dark cool layer at the bottom into which it is pleasant to let down one foot. Below, a whole succession of little falls can lose their middle. There is water coming over the lip and water standing in the basin and between them an inch of dry stone. For the crabs this is of no consequence. To a ledge cow an inch is as good as a locked door if there are six tons of limestone the proper height for a ledge cow immediately overhead.

By the end of that summer the cows in the western wheel chamber had eaten the floor clean. They went on making their double sweep in the evenings, and now the marks lasted until morning. The keeper saw this and brought in a broken shrimp brick on the end of his sounding line. The cows would not eat it. In the night one of them drew up the line hand over hand, so to speak, with her lip and ate the little paper crab that was living in the reel.

There was a remedy quite near at hand. When the wheel had last been moved, the masons had laid a sill across the old tailrace to keep the stream in its new course. At the dry end of the sill they had left a notch to let out the water while the mortar set. Afterwards a man had stopped the notch with a stone the size of a loaf. His thumb marks were still in the mortar on either side of it. Beyond it lay a succession of pools that no cow had browsed for eighty years.

The power could have taken the stone out. It could not have put back what would have gone out with the first rush of water: three basins of warm silt full of translucent eggs, laid in strings, each string fastened at one end to a little upright tube. There were creatures in the tubes too. The drawings of the works showed this reach as an unshaded strip with the word *dry* in it. In summer the entire population of the tubes would have gone into a keeper’s cap, though it would have taken a good part of his life to put them there.

What the power did was to ease the stone forward by the thickness of the skin that comes off a scalded thumb. The mortar held. A bead of water collected on the lower edge and fell on the old paving with a sound like the beginning of a cough.

At the third grating beyond the baths the listener took the rod out of her mouth and spat. She had supposed the sound to come from one of her own back teeth. She put the rod to the other side of her jaw and heard it again.

It took nine evenings to find the stone. It was the listener’s father, a retired tile setter, who thought of going to the baths and pulling out the wooden steps. Behind them, very low down, was the former access for the men who cleaned the tailrace. A thin man could still go in there sideways. On being told what lay beyond the sill the keeper went to considerable trouble to borrow the long-handled lamp from the bearing shop. He lay under the old arch for nearly an hour. In his report he called the tubes a kind of macaroni, which was all he could think of to give an idea of their size and open ends. As for the eggs, he had seen one of them hatch. He asked to keep the lamp a second night.

In the end they put a length of glazed gutter over the sill. Water taken from above the wheel went down it, over the heads of the tube dwellers, and filled the farthest basin first. From there it backed up very gently. It was the first job of the kind the tile setter had done lying on his back since his apprenticeship. He said there had been a time when he could eat his dinner in that position, soup excepted.

On the fourth evening the cows were missing. The keeper found them on the other side of the sill. Only the calf was in sight, going along upside down under the low roof with its feet against the stone, a perfectly sensible way of travelling in a depth of water somewhat less than the height of one’s own back. From considerably farther off came the knock of a lip against the wall.

The power stayed long enough to see what came out of the tubes. It was a little fan on a fine stem, gray with a violet edge, which opened under water and shut at the passing of a shadow. There were such fans in other basins up and down the cut but nowhere else, so far as the power could see, did they have that edge. The keeper saw it too. For a number of years there was a pencil kept in the lamp cupboard, of no use for entering figures, that was exactly the right color when wetted.
