The first thing the power noticed was the spanners. It had seen the little ones under the lip and had been about to lift the iron a shade. Then it saw the short spanner lying across the long one and waited. Towards morning a fitter came with his dinner still in his pocket and tried the screws in turn. He put a finger into the water coming out at the end of the strip. There was a slip on it. He wiped his finger on the underside of the packing, waited a little longer, and finished the job. The power went down through the hall to see what else was being done.

There was rather a lot. In a room below the water line a woman was listening to the end of a stick with the other end set against the casing of a main. She could tell a loose seat from a split one that way, though she could not have heard a word spoken into her ear. In the foot of the fifth stack a boy was holding a patch to the wall with his back while his father mixed the stuff to bed it in. The boy had found that he could read in this position provided somebody came now and then to turn a page. On a high shelf above the two of them was a willow plate with a neat bite out of the rim.

For a time the power went about in this fashion, getting in nobody's light. It followed a discharge up from the low wards, and the water back down again by a number of ways, some intended and some not. At the very bottom it found the stone on which the first floor had been laid. There was a fall in the stone towards the north. On the far side of a low shoulder it fell a good deal farther. Water getting over that shoulder would come out in the open, well beyond the eight stacks. The power could have let the pond down in a night. It went out over the shoulder first. There was a narrow valley on the other side, with washing hung under the eaves of houses on account of the rain.

In the tram office the piece of packing on the sill had dried and curled up at the ends. One evening the clerk took it down to dust, and found a plan underneath, ruled on the paint as finely as the lines on the palm of his hand. It showed the bed of a little watercourse, from a point under the disused weighhouse as far as the valley. There were figures for the levels. He got a strong impression, running a nail over the last few inches, that the maker had got that far and would like some advice.

The people from the valley came up on the tram. It had not been generally known in the district that the valley people bought water in August. Nor had it been generally known in the valley what came out of the district besides water. A bottle was passed about and left standing on the table during the discussion. By the time they broke for dinner there was a blue line round the bottom of it. The valley people took the bottle home.

There is a tap now in the side of the disused weighhouse. Tap is the name it goes by, though the wheel takes two people to turn it and the actual opening is down in the stone. The cutting beyond it was the power's work. It made that part when asked, in the dimensions chalked out for it, and left the chalk marks where they were. The settling beds and the branch to the valley cistern were put in by the two sets of water people, who by that time knew one another fairly well. It took them most of a winter to agree on the gates. There is a wheel at either end; either wheel will shut the water off.

The bellows still work. There are upper streets that would get no water at all by the new cut, and seams in the fifth ward in which the water must go both ways if the packing is to keep sweet. But it is possible to rest a bank now without setting two other banks to a longer stroke. The fitters have begun on the bearings that nobody alive has seen standing still. At the foot of the twelve steps the fifth is still a dip and a rise. A rail has been put in there out of tram stock, turned over so that the part a hand takes hold of is the part the wheels wore smooth.

Most of the very young slips that go down the cut get no farther than the first settling bed. At the valley end, however, the children have discovered an animal under the wooden sill of the cistern. It is rather smaller than a shirt button. Its back has a blue heart, then a wide clear space, then a narrow band of the honey-colored grit the valley houses stand on. It has been left where it is. To see it you have to lie down full length on the flags, with somebody a little heavier sitting on your heels. There are generally two or three willing to do this, and on fine afternoons a short wait for a turn.
