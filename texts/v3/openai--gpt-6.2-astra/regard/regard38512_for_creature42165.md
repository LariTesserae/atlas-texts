All that morning something in the field attended to the fleck of green. It attended also to the little louse above it. It could feel the pull of the leaf on the six feet as the wind went over the track. Below, where there was no wind, it could feel the much slighter pull of the empty skin on the bristle that held it. The lacewing had caught it by a hind leg. The rest of it hung down over one eye.

For nine days there was nothing done in the room that might not have been done there unwatched. The shoot put up a leaf with a nick in it, of a quite different pattern from the first two. Three times a sheep took the grass on either side and left this leaf standing. The heap by the shell door grew high enough to bury the third splinter, which had never supported anything. On the white wall the red mite found a second red mite, and for the remainder of that afternoon there were two sets of dark tracks with a short length in common.

On the tenth day no bead fell. There were beads as before, and one on the nearer root grew to the usual size and let go. But the wall had come in as far as the root. The bead went quietly down the join between the two and was taken up by a cake of dust behind the shell door. A sowbug working there came out with a clean streak down its back. The springtails remained under the chalk.

The thing that attended to the field had the strength to lift off the turf entire, with the sheep standing on it. What it did, toward evening, was to take hold of the fallen grain of earth a little way down the track. It brought it back up to the opening. There were two white tracks in the roof, left by beetles going opposite ways, and where the tracks crossed there was a small soft place. It pressed the grain into this place until scarcely any of it showed. From below it looked like the end of a loaf put into an oven a great way off.

During the night the grain took up water. It was good stiff field clay and swelled as it had done in wet weather for a hundred years before the hoof came down. A split ran out from it, no wider than the mark a hair leaves in butter. By morning the split had reached the wall. The mite crossed it in passing, putting down two feet on the farther side before lifting any on the nearer. Nothing fell.

There followed a dry day and a wet one. On the wet one the shell lintel ticked, although there was no sowbug in the opening. A piece of the white wall had tipped forward at the top and come to rest against it. The piece was the size of a sorrel seed. Between it and the standing wall was a passage which admitted at first only air, then the forward half of a springtail, and at last the whole of one, which went in and did not come out on that side. Presently a second went in. From the level of the clay you could see both of them through the white, one a little higher than the other, going about their business upside down.

After that there was generally a loose piece somewhere in the room. The sowbugs got hold of the small ones. These were carried backward through the door and laid down in the dark, where apparently a very little daylight, provided it had quite finished setting, was of use. The large ones leaned where they were. Behind one the lacewing changed its skin. It came out pale as the end of a root, leaving behind a good dark coat, the empty green purse and all. Before its new jaws had darkened it had begun to furnish itself again. This time the first thing it put on was a chip of white. It reached rather too far forward with it, and for an hour or more went about with a little roof over its face.

The grain is there still. In dry weather it rattles in its socket if a sheep puts a foot down close by. In wet weather it is quite fast. About once in six days a bead finds its way clear of the nearer root and falls on the open clay. There is no telling beforehand quite where it will stand, for the root is lengthening, and the wall that once held it has gone off in several directions. At the foot of the last drop there was room for nineteen springtails. One place at the edge was left empty, being occupied by the shadow of a small white roof.
