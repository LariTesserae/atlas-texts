For three evenings the power did nothing that could have been distinguished from looking. It followed the white foot down and up again. On the fourth evening it went down a good deal farther than the swimmer. From there the crooked tree was a black brush on the edge of a light sky. There was a crescent of light under it, too. Water had picked the earth from under one great root, leaving the root stretched across the gap with all the little stones that it had grown about still fast in its grain.

The tree would go in time. The power put no earth back. There were young trees behind it, drawn a little out of the upright by the light that came in through the notch. One of them already had a branch over the stream. The black birds used it in a high wind, when the branches of the old tree thrashed so that a bird wet to the skin had difficulty keeping its seat.

What held the power longest was the time a mother spent at the rock with a young one under her chin. There were three more in the bed. Each time the water lifted it, the bed turned a little on the floor of the hole. Presently an edge would catch against the low part of the roof. The cat, for its part, had two young in a hollow so dry that dust came off their coats when they wrestled. It had been on the ridge since noon without eating. It was looking the other way, but one ear was turned nearly flat back toward the sound of water in the hole.

Under the ridge, not much higher than the bed, there was the cast of a branch. It ran off the main passage at a slant, dwindled to the thickness of a wrist and ended at a plate of stone. Beyond the plate lay the hole on the other side. In the dark the power went the length of the branch, feeling its way as a swimmer would have to feel, with the backs of the hairs on either cheek. Then it laid a little pressure on the plate. A piece the size of a palm tipped over into the upper hole. A second piece came with it and lay flat. The noise aboveground was less than the click of a shell on a leaf.

There was a draught now. It went down the branch and stirred a loose end of sedge in the turning bed. The mother took the youngster back inside. For some time nothing more happened. Then her nose appeared at the new opening, the pale tip quite grey with rock dust. She came through without the youngster, went out as far as the grass on that side, and returned. The power had not known whether she would take to it. Even when she had carried the first three up through the branch and laid them together in the dry, she took the last one over the ridge. By then the cat had gone.

The power did not go along the ridges opening ways through the stone. In this place there had been a branch, and a plate at the end of it that could be moved without bringing the roof down. It was a pleasure to have found it. For a while it stayed by the upper hole. The mother went out once more and came back with a mouthful of the old wet bedding. She pulled it apart in strips and laid them on the two fallen pieces, which held a little more of the day's warmth than the floor. The smallest of the young crept onto the strips before she had finished spreading them. Its feet hung over the edge of the stone.

Long afterward, when the crooked tree had gone and the birds were using the next one back, the two pieces were still there. Swimmers going through the dark put a foot on the first and a foot on the second. They had worn a shallow place in each, smooth enough that a little water carried in on the belly would stand there all morning without soaking away.
