The case goes into the welt of Kit's shoe and will not come out for blowing. She takes the shoe off and sets it with the toe in the break. For a little while she feels exceedingly foolish, sitting in a public place with one foot in its stocking. Then the case comes sailing out, black grains uppermost, and she is glad she did it. The car has gone. Pocket puts his chin back.

Of the things done in the city that day this is the one that attracts the attention of a power capable, among other things, of settling the ground. It could settle the whole plain between one passage of the car and the next. The houses would want underpinning afterwards, and some of the doors would want taking off and shaving, but a street laid out on Monday would still be there on Friday. It considers doing this. Under the toe of Kit's shoe the case has got hold of a hair of root. There is water enough there to turn it slowly over and show the little fan at work. On settled ground there would be no such water.

The power goes about for a season before doing anything. It is present in the baths on the day a man is brought in on a door with a broken pelvis, having stepped on a plate that was fast at the near edge and loose at the far one. It is present when a woman in Porter's Court wakes in the night and takes her baby downstairs for no reason she can afterwards give. A little later the two chimney pots come through the ceiling of the room she has left. On the other hand there is a kitchen in the same court where the rising of the flags has brought the oven to exactly the right height for an old bench worker with a stiff back. He bakes on Thursdays. The power stays to see him take the bread out. He has had the oven forty years and this is the first summer he has not had to kneel.

In September there begins to be a sound before the ground moves. It is two light knocks, one well down and one nearer the surface, like the noise a lodger makes on coming in late and trying his key in the wrong door and then the right one. Between the second knock and the movement there is time for a woman carrying a full pail to put it down and get her fingers clear of the handle. The interval is the same for a blister the size of a shirt button as it is for the lifting of half a parish. This is the best the power can manage without putting any appreciable check on what is coming up. For a week or two it tries giving longer notice, but at that distance there are so many things the stone might yet do that nobody in the city gets a night's rest.

The knocks are easier to feel than to hear. Soon there are men at the carriage works who can take them through the soles of their boots and have the pressure off the rollers before the foreman at the window sees a thing. A deaf woman who sells watercress is much in demand as a partner at the municipal dances. She gets it through the floor in her thin shoes and can turn a partner clear of a rising board without either of them going out of step. Nobody thinks to thank the power. Most people suppose the plain is getting older, as indeed it is.

Pocket takes the knocks through his chin. In November he and Kit are at the covered stop when his teeth give two little taps against the buckle on her knee. She has been reading the paper, which contains a mistake of his owner's: six hundred pairs of naval *trouts* are wanted at the clothing depot. She folds it to show Mr Vale. Under the bench a narrow piece of pavement is beginning to stand up on end. The old break opens beside it, and out comes a quantity of water the colour of weak tea, carrying root hairs and yellow cases. In another minute it will be over the kerb and down among the cinders.

Kit has the paper folded already. She gives it another fold and lays it in the gutter with its far end in the moss behind the shelter. It carries water quite well, though the naval trouts go first grey and then black, and by the time the little runnel has ceased there is nothing left fit to show anybody. A good many of the cases have gone down into the cinders. A good many are in the moss.

The power, which has not quite left off attending to Kit, is particularly pleased with the newspaper. It had not thought of the newspaper.
