The power came down by the north stair. At the end of the rail it stopped, and the woman from the fish stall, coming down behind it with the marks of her apron strings still red in her waist, took it by the elbow.

“Left a bit,” she said.

She put its hand on the end of the bench. There was room for one on the dry part if the others moved up, which they did without interrupting a discussion about the price of ice. In this way the power, which could have come down through the ceiling at any point it chose, got a seat and heard the end of the discussion.

Presently it felt the animal against its shoe. It had not had feet for very long and had been keeping them quite still, out of consideration for her. This proved to be a mistake. She went a little way along the shoe, tried it again, and was about to leave when the power let its ankle tire as other ankles do. She settled at once.

It went on coming for the rest of that summer. It got to know the time of the rain to within a passenger car. It saw a young one bowled all the way down the diagonal with a shred of wool streaming out behind it, and had already put out a hand when the small thing came up against its mother's flank and began with great application to climb over her. The power took its hand back. Once it came early enough to see the cricket. Once the woman from the fish stall fell asleep against its shoulder and missed the opening of her own stall after dinner.

The thing it eventually did concerned the south kitchen.

The people there were having a new range put in. The old one had to be taken apart to get it out, and behind the warming oven, where nobody living had ever had occasion to look, was a round patch of plaster about the size of the bottom of a tumbler. The fitter tapped it with the handle of his rule. It gave a hollow answer. He was pleased to hear it, since he had a pipe to take down and had expected to spend most of the morning with a drill.

There were five young in the bed that day. The mother was out. Between the plaster and the fish scales at the head of the bed there was rather less than the length of the fitter's rule.

The power was on the bench below. It felt the tap, which was quite distinct from anything the market ordinarily sent down. For a moment it considered making the plaster sound solid. But the fitter wanted a way through the floor, and there was nothing unreasonable in that. It went up the south stair to have a look.

The kitchen was in a considerable pickle. There was a side of the old oven on the table with the bread on it, and the people of the house were trying to give the fitter his tea in the sugar basin, the cup handles having all been put somewhere safe. Under the window stood the one piece of the old range that hadn't been moved yet, an iron foot with a screw through it into the flags. The power could feel a draught coming up the screw. It remembered that, seen from below, the south landing had two pale rectangles, one above the other, with a small rusty drop at the lower corner of the top one.

It knelt and turned the screw. It was a long screw and for the first few turns took both hands. The fitter, who had been about to break the plaster, put down his rule and came to help. Under the iron foot was a service opening, quite sound, with a brass lining that would take his pipe and room beside it for his thumb.

“Well,” he said. He drank his tea out of the sugar basin. The round patch stayed where it was. There was no reason to tell him what was behind it, any more than there had been a reason to tell the sleepers in the wool what was on the other side.

Downstairs the rain was nearly over. The mother had reached the mouth of the bore with a short white thread which kept sticking to the wet stone. She would get half of it up and go back for the rest, whereupon the first half would slide out again. The power sat on the wet end of the bench and watched to see how she managed. On the fourth attempt she put a middle foot on the part she had got up. After that it was easy.

By the time the bread flat went over, the water had soaked through the seat of the power's trousers. It climbed the north stair with a dark half-moon showing on the back of them. The fish seller, shutting her steel cupboard, saw it come up.

“You'll know another time,” she said.

The power said that it would.
