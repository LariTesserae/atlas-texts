The power first came to the high works for the sake of the animal. It had seen the clean strokes on a tile and had mistaken them for the marks left by a much larger creature at a great distance. This is an easy mistake to make if you do not ordinarily have to go nearer to a thing in order to see it better. By the time it understood about the size it had seen the feet. It stayed for some weeks.

What troubled it was the shut period. A wet tile is a country of considerable extent if you are small enough, but it is no country at all once it is dry. There was a chamber due to be opened and relined. The keeper had already put up the notice about drawing water in advance. On the last evening before the shutdown the power watched him take out the loose tile on which the animal was grazing, lay it flat in a covered dish, and carry it across the ward to another chamber. He had to go the long way round on account of some steps. In the new chamber he propped one edge up with a shutter pin until the two wet surfaces touched. He did not stay to see the animal go across. There would have been a good deal to explain if anyone had found him there with the dish.

The power was pleased to have waited. It had been on the point of providing the first chamber with a permanent spring. It went back some days later and saw the men working there on their backs with the sleeves of their shirts rolled under their heads. A spring would have been very much in the way.

It took, after that, to looking at the sides of things that people could not see. There was little use in attending to the upper surface of the basin. A crack there no longer than a pencil would have a chalk ring around it by dinner time. Underneath was another matter. Here were whole streets of inverted scenery, the roofs of the old runs hanging in pleats, with long hard drops at their edges. The heat pipes went down in pairs through this dark and came up again. At one point under the eastern wards a pair passed through a narrow place between two folds. The stone was closing on them. There was room yet for a finger on either side. Both pipes looked quite sound at the top, and would continue to do so for some time after there was no room at all.

The power could have put the stone back. What it did instead was to make the two pipes cast their shadows upwards for a minute or so, just when the covers were off for the weekly inspection. The fitter saw on the underside of her canvas awning two long dark stems with a very distinct waist of daylight between the stems and the stone. She fetched the other fitter. By then the shadows had gone, but the other fitter held the end of the tape while she went down with a lamp. It was awkward getting past the first bend. They took the loops out on Thursday, and used the occasion to put in a little wheeled seat for going down, which they had wanted for years.

The fitter made a drawing of what she had seen. In due course a copy was sent to the school. Children given the drawing and a piece of chalk were invited to show where the light had been. There were several quite creditable answers, all of which required a hole in the ground in a place where there was known to be no hole. The fitter preferred these answers to the suggestion that she had been asleep.

That summer the cup went in. It went in so readily that the boy trying it, who had braced one knee against the slab, knocked the skin off two knuckles. He let go, and the cup went down. The girl from the pump yard had brought the net. She had been trying to get a word in for some minutes about putting it underneath *first*.

There was a knock from below, then another, quite a long way to the left of where everybody was looking. Nothing more was heard. The boy sat on the sole of the pumping station sucking his knuckles. The girl spread out her net and considered it. A net which will catch a falling cup is not necessarily of much use for gathering up a cup which has already fallen. She went home for a shutter pin, bent it in the heavy hinge of the kitchen door, and tied it to the corner of the net.

By now most of the people had gone on to the pictures. A curtain tender stayed. She held the cord between her fingers and could distinguish quite well between the pin drawing over bare rock and the pin drawing over grit. Once she felt something which she said was neither. The girl drew the net back a little and tried again. It was nearly dark when they got the cup up. There was a piece out of the handle, and in the bottom, quite dry and round, was a small bead of the old black stone. For a time there were two things to pass round the square instead of one.

The power had watched the whole business. It had been tempted, as the cup fell, to let it fall a little more slowly. As it happened, it had not even had to decide against this. The time taken by a cup to fall out of a boy's hand is very short.

It still comes to the works. The fitters, who have a number of awnings up now over openings where nothing has ever been seen, would be glad of a regular day. There is none. On its most recent visit it spent the better part of an afternoon in a kitchen above the former pump yard. The net was back on its hook, somewhat the worse for being drawn over grit. A man came in, took the onion scissors out of it, cut himself some greens and put the scissors down by the stove. A little later, passing the stove on his way out, he noticed them and put them back in the net. There had been no occasion to do anything. It had been a good afternoon.
