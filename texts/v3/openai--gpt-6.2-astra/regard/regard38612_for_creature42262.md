For a winter the power that had taken an interest in the bay kept the hours of the dog. In consequence it saw very little of the far shore and a great deal of the undersides of gates. It learnt that a cow about to kick bears down on the other hind foot first, and that the farmer, coming home in the dark, would put her good hand out at a certain turn of the lane. Whether or not it had anything else in view the dog came under that hand. A few yards farther on it went about its business again.

There was one business it could never bring to a satisfactory end. On still nights it could hear a sheep crook knocking against the boards of a byre across the water. It could hear the boy lift it down, and distinguish the scrape of the byre door from the much shorter scrape of the door belonging to the house. But in a rising wind it could not hear the brindled heifer on the path until she was very nearly home. It would run down to the crooked porch and stand with its ears set forward and the long hair in them blowing straight back.

One such evening, when the boy was bringing the heifer over rather late, the power stood there too. Halfway across, the heifer set her forefeet wide apart. Her hair parted down the brindled ribs and showed a strip of soft grey underneath. There was a squall coming up the channel that had not yet struck either shore. The boy, who had felt the halter tighten, was coming up beside her to see what was wrong.

For the length of that squall the air on the weather side of the path went at walking pace. A little way below their feet the spray still travelled so fast that it looked like white scratches on the water. The heifer brought her feet together and went on. The boy had a cold ear and a warm one and was glad to turn his head when he reached the porch. He had no particular account to give of the crossing. The dog examined them both.

It would have been possible to leave the air like that. But there were small boats that came through under sail, and nobody in one of those would thank you for taking the wind out of it in a running tide. Besides, the reading on the board had been right for the porch. What had been wanting was a reading for the middle.

After that, in each of the twelve porches, there was a draught through one of the upper courses of brick. It came through a joint no wider than a grass stem. On a calm day one could put a wet finger to it and feel nothing. Down the bay a good way there might be sheep leaning on a fence with all their fleeces blowing awry; that made no difference either. But let a wind cross the path out of reach of the shelter of the land, and it came in through that joint, with the smell of the water it had passed over. The wren found it first. On rough days it went in at the low end of the crack behind the bench and stayed there.

The boy noticed it when he hung the heifer’s bell up on a nail to free both hands for the halter. The bell turned on its thong. Outside, the geese on the meadow were standing in their own breath. He put his cheek to the brick, then went out and looked. Far off, between two stretches of smooth lead-coloured water, was a patch as finely ridged as the roof of a mouth. He took the heifer home by the lane. It was a long way round and his mother had to warm his dinner twice.

By spring there was a tuft of red worsted hanging at the joint in every porch, low enough for a child to see and too high for a cow to reach with her tongue. It took people some time to learn to stay on the bench on a fine evening with the worsted standing out stiff above their heads. It took them rather less time to learn the other thing: that on a day of great uproar in the alders there might be an hour of quite tolerable going out across the bay. The farmer made use of such an hour to visit the boy’s mother. She took over a jugful of the alder twigs, the ends wrapped in wet sacking. They had tea in the yellow window. She had seen it from her kitchen for nineteen winters and had not known there was room in it for a table.

The power kept company with the dog a while longer. It was there on a morning when a strip of meadow came loose at the spring and the dog, which had been going very handsomely down the line of cows, stopped short. One black cow went on feeding. There was firm ground round by the stone, but to come at her that way meant going out of her sight and leaving her to her own devices for a minute or more. The dog stood smelling the torn place. Then it turned and ran inland, past the staple and down the other side, and shortly the cow came up at a trot with a fine string of salted grass dangling from her lip.

The farmer opened the gate for them. The dog went through close against her skirts, wet and very well pleased. There had been nothing to do there at all.
