On the fourth morning there are four handprints at the polished place. Two are very small. One of the young has come down headfirst behind the female, setting its feet exactly where she set hers, which is a fair way of going until she turns around. They meet nose to nose. It opens its mouth very wide at her. She takes it by the scruff and carries it home, its tail making very determined turns about a vine that is no longer there.

The power that has taken an interest in the basin sees this. It could put the limb back. There would be no more difficulty about it than there is for the female in picking up her young. But there are eggs now on the underside of the limb, laid in little amber combs by a fly that goes on foot through the leaf mold. Raised into the wind, they would dry to husks by noon. And what the female would do, given back the high hole in the fig, would be to go to the low hole and begin the considerable business of carrying the bedding across again.

It leaves the limb where it is.

The matter to which it does attend is some distance off, at the foot of the basin. Here a road comes up through the cane and stops at a red peg. On the other side of the peg there is to be a deep cut in the stone. Farther down, where houses have gone up among the old garden plots, rain comes in under the doors. There is a woman there who has stood two nights in one month with a sleeping child over her shoulder, lifting things off the floor with her free hand. Water that runs down a road is not much like the rain that fell on it. It arrives all at once and brings half the road with it. The cut is to take it away.

On the sheet of paper in the surveyor's case the black strips are very well drawn indeed. You can put a finger on the little division in the stone where it went either side of the fig. What is not on the paper is the country underneath. The cut will open the side of a gallery pretty much as a knife opens a cane full of water. For a day or two it will carry off a fine clear flow even in dry weather. After that there will be a thousand white root ends hanging in air.

The power has no wish for the woman to spend a third night on her feet. Nor does it think the water ought to stay where it is on account of any particular merit in a shrimp. A shrimp, set down on that sheet of paper, would at once get into the fold under the surveyor's thumb and have to be got out with a straw. It knows what to do in the place where it lives. So, for that matter, does the surveyor, provided she can get a look at it.

In the morning, while she is pulling up the red peg to drive it a little straighter, the power lets her see down through the stone.

It is an odd business. Her boots remain perfectly substantial. There is a bit of green cane leaf stuck to the side of one of them. Immediately below the leaf, at a depth she judges to be about twelve feet, a shrimp is turning a white morsel over and over in its forelegs. Beyond it the light runs a long way under the forest. She can see the little beaches of grit on the inside of the bends. She can see where a roof has fallen and the water goes three ways through the fall and comes together again. She sits down rather hard. By the time the man with the mallet comes over, she is sitting on ordinary black stone with a red peg across her knees.

They bore there before they cut. At eleven feet nine inches the bit drops so abruptly that the man at the handles bruises his chin. They send down a light. After that it is possible to discuss the matter without the surveyor having to tell anybody about the shrimp, for which she is grateful.

It takes most of a season. In the end the road water goes off in six places instead of one, into wide shallow beds of rushes in the old plots. The people who own the plots are paid for the use of them. Somebody has to go around after a hard rain and take the cane trash out of the mouths of the drains. None of this happens by itself. In the first big downpour the woman in the low house wakes, puts a foot out of bed and feels the floor. It is dry and unpleasantly gritty. She has been meaning to sweep under there for days.

Up in the basin the three young are flying, after a fashion. The trick of landing comes last. There is a perceptible interval between the arrival of the front half of a young glider at a trunk and the slap of the rest of it against the bark. The female sleeps through these noises. It is a different noise that brings her out of the hole one evening: the fine steady scratching of something stripping fiber from just above her head.

It is the smallest of the three. It has found a loose place in the bark. It works its teeth under, takes hold with both hands, and pulls until it goes over backward with a strip twice its own length. For a moment it hangs by its hind feet with the pale stuff lying across its face. Then it gathers the strip up against its chest and goes off around the tree with it, out of sight of its mother, to a hole of its own.
