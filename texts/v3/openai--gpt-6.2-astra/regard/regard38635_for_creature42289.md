The first thing that the power did was to put a finger at the end of the R. The green thing came onto it without any particular show of relief. It had found another piece of the world and went along tasting it. At the first joint it turned back. There was very little to eat on the finger of a power.

By then the wash was coming in. The power let it have the green thing and sat down on the step to see where the wash went. This took rather longer than one might suppose. Part of that very wash was running out of a woman's hair on the stair before the rest of it had left the dish. Some went into the deep rock. A little stayed in the R. It was possible to follow every drop, but it meant paying no attention to the woman, who was trying to get a wet heel into a wool stocking. The power put out the same finger and held the stocking open at the back. The woman got her heel in. She looked round, rather surprised, and said thank you to a boy who had done nothing of the kind.

In the course of the next few years the power found quite a number of things to do about the bath. There was an afternoon when a man on the ridge meant to tip a cartload of broken crocks into a hole. He had tipped things there before. From below, the hole was one of the daylight bars, which the man could not possibly have known, since from above it was a hole full of nettles. As he backed the cart the power let him hear the bath. He stood with both hands on the tailboard listening to drops fall from a fern forty metres under his boots. It put him in such a queer state of mind that he took the crocks home again. They did very well under the flags of his new yard.

There were also a great many afternoons when the power sat on the step and did nothing. It had a fancy for the crab. Once, after a long succession of washes in which the little claw had closed on water, it was tempted to set a green thing down just upstream of the fossil. But this was very much what the wash itself did, except that the wash did not have to choose one. The power waited. The crab ate that evening.

What gave it the most trouble was the name on the riser. Water had got in behind the R. The whole face of the letter, with its little company making their rounds, could be felt to give if one laid a thumb on it. A flake as big as a playing card was going to come off the step.

The power held it on for one more wash. Almost all the company went out on that one and a fresh lot came in. It held it on for another. By the middle of the afternoon it had a stiff thumb and had discovered something about the difficulty of finding a good time.

In the end it took the flake off. It took it off under water, with the wash standing at its highest on the stair, so that the change, as far as a creature in the loop could tell, was chiefly in the direction from which the light came. It laid the flake face up in the lee of a lip. There was no great virtue in this spot except that it was wet and within reach. In a year the R was full of cream-coloured stone. Before it filled, a short hem had fed its way across it, leaving a little ripple in the upright stroke which would have puzzled the man who cut the letter.

On the riser the place where the flake had been was rough and pink. People sitting on the dry step took to putting a bare heel against it; it made a capital place to scratch. Below the reach of their heels, in a hollow considerably less elegant than an R, the young collected as before.

The power still comes there. You cannot tell by the height of the wash or the fall of the bubble whether it is in the room. Occasionally somebody getting dressed on the stair has the use of an extra finger for a moment. More often the only sign, and one you would have to be sitting very still to notice, is that when a bird goes over the ridge, there are two of you looking up.
