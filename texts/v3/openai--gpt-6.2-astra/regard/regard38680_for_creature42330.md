The power, coming down into the basin, took care not to come as air. Even a little displacement, such as a hand makes in passing over the head of a child, might have gone on stirring the reeds for a very long time. It came instead as a weight in the cream stone at the southern lip, in a piece that had weight already and was well supported on its lower face. From there it felt the feet of the reeds, and the six feet disposed so variously among them, and presently the two small forefeet folded about a stem.

It stayed there through several turnings of the old sleeper’s head. There was a difference between them which from a distance had not been apparent. In turning to the left the animal went a little beyond the point at which the air lay squarely across its cheek. It stretched its neck, putting out the loose pink skin in two folds below its lowest shield. Then it drew back. In turning to the right it was content to stop as soon as all the whiskers on that side were bent.

There was a single blade, some way in front of the fork, which had scarcely begun to lift. Its point lay across the lane of brightness. From the left eye of the sleeper, with its neck at full stretch, it was possible to see past the point; from the right it was not. The power looked along the same line. There was a good deal there to see. The ridge that ended bluntly, seen end on, was no thicker than a straw. Above it the red shrubs made a fleck like the mark a berry leaves on the tooth. The sleeper, however, had very little use for this part of the view. Its eye did not accommodate for the distance. What it watched was the point of the blade itself.

The leaf had the minutest flaw in its saw edge, a smooth crescent from which three or four of the teeth were missing. Air coming round the crescent arrived at the sleeper’s left cheek in a fine thread a little warmer than the rest. By stretching its neck it could run the thread from the root of a whisker nearly to the tip. It had discovered this some time ago. On the last few turnings it had been obliged to stretch a little farther. The leaf was going up.

It would have been easy to keep the leaf there. But as the power watched, the old animal missed the thread, felt about for it, and found instead the small commotion in the air just clear of the point. This took three whiskers at a time and let them go in a different order. The sleeper shut the eye on that side. For a while it did not turn its head at all.

The power left the leaf to rise.

It might have gone then. What detained it was a pressure it felt in the stone, not from the feet about the stem but from the four that hung below the fork. They had curled in toward the belly; now and then all four spread, with the intimate assurance of feet coming down on ground they know. The power traced the work of them back along the middle ridge. There, under a ledge, another old sleeper was busy tamping dust for its bed. It put its feet out in pairs, brought them down flat and turned them a little outwards. The one up in the reed had taken the use of the movement. Each time it turned its hind feet outwards a little dust came out of the fur. There was very little left.

What if it should want to get down? The fork had grown a good deal higher since the animal climbed it. No work it could take from another would shorten the bare length of stem below. The power considered laying a slant of stone from the fork to the ground. It could have done it with one of the long upper plates, leaving all the fans upon its lower face untroubled. But the sleeper had made no movement to descend. There was a particular excellence to the fork in that no stone came up to it.

Instead the power tried a small thing with the weight it had assumed. It lifted it a little, and set it down; lifted it and set it down. It did this in the place, well known to the basin, in which a piece of work can be taken up by anyone who has a use for it. The motion was that of a foot testing its hold before the other feet let go. It had no direction in it, up or down. Nothing obliged the sleeper to take it.

For a long while nothing did take it. Then, in the crack near the southern end of the thicket, the young one that had been walking with its mouth put a forefoot down in the dust. It bore on the foot experimentally. The foot held. Its expression on discovering that the rest of its body could be brought forward over that one fixed point was worth the considerable journey the power had made to get there. It took another step, this one by itself, and fetched up against its brother.

Nearer the end of the ridge something took the movement very faintly indeed. There was a double tick, with a furry scrape between. Beyond the chipped edge a beetle stood for the first time on six feet in the light. It was a handsome creature, with a line of lemon yellow where the wing cases met. It cleaned one feeler, then the other, turned about and took a firm hold of the edge from the other side.

The old sleeper in the fork took nothing. The little disturbance at the point of the blade had moved on above its head. It had got one of its own forefeet free of the stem and was holding it up, toes spread, in the lane the leaf had cleared. Each toe put a break of its own in the current. By inclining the foot it could bring any of the five cool intervals across its nose. It was occupied to the exclusion of almost everything else by the difficulty of getting the middle one exactly between its nostrils.

The power settled its weight for good into the piece of stone. That one service, of a foothold tried and found sound, it left going there; the expense was slight, and there was no telling who might presently be glad of it. It withdrew from the roots and the lilac filaments, from the shared flower with its singular gray tenant, and lastly from the fork. It did not wait for the sleeper to succeed. There are small things that go better unobserved.

On the southern lip, if you pick your way along the sharp rim, there is a place where you will find yourself bringing one foot forward with rather more confidence than the footing seems to warrant. The stone is quite sound. From there you can see the tops of the reeds nearly to the other side, and, a little east of the middle ridge, a break in their turning no wider than a paw.
