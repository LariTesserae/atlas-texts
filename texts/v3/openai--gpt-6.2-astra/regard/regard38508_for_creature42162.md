The first person to get a good look at the power was the water keeper. She was lying on her side with her arm in the drinking slot of the upper court. Something was stopping the water there, farther in than the length of her fingers, and she had put her ear down to see whether she could hear it move. She saw, across the inch or so of daylight between the lip and the water, an eye looking in from the other end.

She drew out her hand. The eye remained. It was a dark, attentive eye, upside down as hers had been, and though she knew most of the people who were still supple enough to get into that position, she did not know this one.

“Can you reach it from there?” she said.

A little cone of rolled-up leaf came out against her palm. The eye disappeared. When she got to her feet there was no one on the other side of the basin; nor, on considering the matter, was there room for a head between the stone and the kitchen wall. She unrolled the leaf. It had the marks of her own groping nails at one end and was otherwise quite ordinary. She went on to the next court.

For some time the power had been looking at the town from about the height at which a cloud going inland clears the mountain. It could distinguish the clean water from the wash water and the wash water from the water going out among the plants; it could see where a shoulder hurt. It had been troubled by the distances. It had taken the water keeper's eye, looking back at it with some impatience through the slot, to make it understand that if it wanted to be of use it might come close enough to be asked.

Its next appearance was in the kitchen where the joints were grated. It sat in the doorway with its knees up. The person minding the baby, who had been wanting to get up for some time, put the baby between those knees and went out. The power found that if it spread its fingers the baby could get a finger in each fist. The child pulled with all its might, trying to get upright, and the power, which could have lifted it without feeling its weight, held perfectly still. There was a soft bump as the child sat down. It looked up in astonishment and began again.

“What shall we call you?” one of the women asked. “In case we want you another time.”

It took the question seriously. A name called from one end of the town would be of very little use at the other. It put out its free foot and tipped over an empty wooden bowl. The women knew the sound perfectly well. There was a discussion of how to distinguish a bowl knocked over on purpose from all the bowls knocked over in the ordinary way, during which the baby succeeded in standing up. In the end they agreed that if one wanted the power one would hold the bowl a little way off the paving and let it drop bottom down, twice.

It was the second knock that altered things. People went on dropping bowls much as before, and after the first knock there was a small, rather agreeable interval in which the whole court listened. Very often, when there was a second, somebody nearer than the power came out to see what was wanted. Once it was wanted to sit with a man who was dying and who, having had a great many callers, wanted someone he need not entertain. It sat with him through the afternoon. Toward evening he asked it to turn the mat a little so that the small shiny place came under his hip. After that neither of them spoke.

One of the sisters under the thatch screen asked whether it could do anything about the yellow dust. She showed it the cloth, which was dark blue, with a little damp constellation of pollen where the screen leaked. The power took hold of the top of the fissure in its two hands and for a moment all the flowering crowns inclined away from the house. From the mat in the doorway the sister saw, for the first time in that season, the bare gray pavement on the other side.

“Oh, not that much,” she said.

The crowns came back. Between them they moved the screen down the street the length of the cloth. It was a better place for drying things, though somewhat less convenient for talking to people in the kitchen. The sister used both places thereafter. The power had thought of an arrangement by which nothing yellow would ever fall on anything blue, but there was evidently no occasion to put it into effect.

There was one thing it did without being asked. Below the last inhabited court was a piece of old street on which a slab had broken through. The crack was narrow at the top and widened beneath into a considerable hollow. In fine weather a carrier went round it without breaking step. In wet weather it was invisible. No one had fallen there yet. The power could feel the weight of each approaching foot through the stone; it could also feel, well down in the hollow, the fine roots of the grove below the street. It did not fill the hollow. It brought up a long block from the loose stone at the foot of the mountain and set it across the break, resting either end on sound rock. This took less time than it had spent holding the baby.

The following morning the people going to cut reeds found a length of dry, pale stone where they were accustomed to step sideways. One of them went back for a pecking stone. New stone is treacherous under a wet sandal, something the power had not considered, having up to then mostly gone barefoot. By noon there were six notches in the upper end and a seventh begun. The power finished the seventh under direction. Its first six blows made little white stars of no particular use. On the seventh it got the angle and lifted out a chip.

It could have made the block last as long as the mountain. Instead it went away for a while to see about a bowl. When it came back, a child with a bundle of reeds was crossing. At the unfamiliar patch of white the child slowed, felt for a notch with one foot and went on. Out toward the middle of the street someone was waiting to take the load. The power stayed where it was. From so close it could see what it had missed from the height of a cloud: the waiting person had brought two cold cakes, and had kept the leaves separate in the fold of a shirt, so that there would be something sharp to eat with the last mouthful as well as the first.
