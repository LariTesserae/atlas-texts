In the visitors’ book there was a space for the place a stranger had come from, and a much smaller space, scarcely sufficient for the word *yes*, for whether the stranger required a guide. One morning the girl found the former filled in with the words *the other side of the stone*. The name had been left blank. She put her thumb on the writing. It was quite dry, and so was the sheet underneath, but her thumb came away wet.

She went down to see who had arrived without using the stair.

There was no one on the landing. On the handrail there stood a drop of water rather bigger than a pea. Its reflection had the nineteen streets in it upside down. While she was looking, the drop ran along the rail to the wall, against the inclination, and stood touching the stone.

“What do you want a guide for?” she asked.

There followed a delay of the kind that occurs when two people have each been brought up to let the other go through a door first. Then something spoke to her from the damp place on her thumb.

“I can tell what touches what,” it said. “I have a great deal of trouble with what belongs to whom.”

It turned out that a considerable quantity of the mountain was able to speak, although only by means of water, and until recently any water available to it had lain under such a weight of stone that there was very little it could do with its mouth. The new fork had relieved the pressure. For the first time in rather a long existence it could put out a drop where it pleased.

It had been attending to the town. In particular it had attended to the opening of a tin with a bad rim. The amount caught in the basin had been smaller than the sound it made and the person opening it had waited through a conversation for one more drop. The mountain had water enough, in a bed of gravel behind the western face, to make such a precaution unnecessary. It wished to know where water might be put without intruding.

The girl considered the washhouse, whose keeper would be gravely inconvenienced by a quantity of water which had not first been drunk. The enamel works needed water, but of a very particular sort. A wet cuff could not get the two halves of itself apart without help.

“Would you mind being in a tin to begin with?” she asked.

It did not mind. She fetched an empty pear tin from the sisters, washed out but still smelling faintly of pears. The drop got in. There was now a dark wet circle on the bottom and a perfectly dry handrail.

“Does it cost the stone anything?” she asked. She had been thinking about the surveyor's dates.

“Yes,” it said, with some pleasure at the question.

They went to fetch the surveyor.

For the next three weeks there were conferences at the landing. People who had known the girl all her life had to give their names because she was not going to begin making exceptions at the very moment when the book had become so interesting. The surveyor brought his square of white card. The keeper of the still brought a set of nested measures and a piece of apparatus with which she could make a spot of dissolved matter show brown on a white wick. The mountain came in the pear tin, which it generally preferred to have tipped a little towards whoever was speaking. Nobody knew whether this helped it to hear. It helped them to speak.

The difficulty was not getting the water out. The difficulty was what would happen to the gravel when the water was gone. Each grain at present bore some of the weight of the grain above it; the water bore the rest. There were two houses and a length of rail above the gravel, and though the mountain was able to distinguish perfectly between one grain and the next it did not know how far a rail could go out of true before a wheel tender lost her livelihood. The tender, for her part, was unable to give an answer in thicknesses of water under a grain of gravel. She brought down a spare shoe from the rail, set it on the edge of the pear tin, and rocked it until it clicked.

“About that,” she said.

“Ah,” said the mountain.

In the end they left the gravel alone. There was another supply, very much smaller, which the mountain had not thought worth mentioning. On the wooded side a cloud spent part of most mornings against a certain slab. The slab took up water on one face and gave it back on the other, where it fell down an empty crack and eventually arrived at the roots of a tree. The tree could use all of it in the growing season. In winter it used almost none. The rest went into making a stout icicle in a place where no one, including the mountain until that afternoon, had ever had occasion to take an interest in an icicle.

It took the sisters until the first cold weather to make the pipe. For most of its length no pipe was needed; the mountain could carry the water along the underside of a bedding plane by keeping one side of the drop a little more inclined to the stone than the other. At the town end, however, the surveyor would not have water travelling naked over a pencil mark. The last nine feet were consequently made of rejected lengths of instrument tubing, joined with collars of blue enamel. There was a cock at the end, and under the cock a shelf to stand a vessel on. They put the shelf low. A person who had carried a full tin up from the lift in childhood knew exactly where to put a shelf.

The first winter the yield was about two cups a day. The town still took its tins on the lift. Nobody tore out the recovery still or ceased to open a damaged rim over a basin. What changed was that if you wanted a little water for a purpose not easily explained to the keeper, there was now somewhere to get it. Someone washed the inside of a mouth organ. A man on the third street grew four long white onion leaves out of an onion bottom in a saucer. The youngest sister wet a rag with hot water and laid it across the ball of her left thumb at night, a thing she had been advised to do for twenty years. By spring she could close that hand without first having to look at it.

The mountain did not go away. This was hardly to be expected. Nor did it now consider itself at liberty to enter the kitchens through the water there. It knew enough about belonging for that. When it wanted the girl's company it put a small bead on the landing rail. If she was occupied the bead waited, sometimes until it had gathered quite a coat of filings and looked like a little grey mouse dropping. She brought it news of things that did not touch the stone.

In return it told her, one evening, that the mouse which collected the surveyor's dates was out.

“Where?”

“Against me. Below your left foot.”

She lay down and looked through the lowest opening of the grate. The mouse was on a bracket with a curl of green stone in its mouth. It could not see where it was going. Each time it tried to turn its head the end of the curl struck the bracket. After watching it knock three times the girl had very nearly put her hand down to help.

Then a second mouse came out along the bracket. It took hold of the other end. For an instant the two stood quite still with the bright shaving between them. The first let go and the second, which was facing the right way, backed into the clearance with it. Presently it came out empty-mouthed. They went down together for another.

The girl fetched the book. It was difficult to make a drawing that showed the important part, which was the little space that opened between the teeth of the first mouse while the stone stayed exactly where it was. She had two tries at it. The book was the town's, but there had been no visitors that day and she used the whole line.
