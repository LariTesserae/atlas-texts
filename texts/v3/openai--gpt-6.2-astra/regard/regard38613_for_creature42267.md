In the spring the woman who keeps the plot comes out with a fork. She is going to put in peas. There is room for two good rows if she takes up the sorrel, which she means to do in any case. She has been pulling the leaves off it for soup for eleven years and is under the impression that it owes her nothing.

The power could move the root. Indeed there is, a little way down the bank, a pocket of earth which has never been turned. The sorrel would take very well there. But the two deep tines of the fork are already in the soil, one on either side of the hollow, and at the pressure of her boot the earth between them has come down hard on the root. The lobes have drawn together for the first time in their growth. The tooth is entering the notch. Underneath it a clear back has puckered, and on the seed bed a mouth is holding fast to a neck that has begun to give.

All this occupies somewhat less of the woman's morning than the time between putting her weight on her heel and putting it on her toe.

What the power does is let the woman feel the little regular movement against the sole of her boot.

She steps off the fork. When she puts her hand on the shaft there it is again. She has felt much the same thing through a spade set too near a mole run, though hardly at this time of year, with the frost only just out of the ground. She draws the tines straight up. There is a nice question of whether to have a look. She stands with the fork in one hand and the three sorrel leaves gathered in the other. The stems feel cold and full between her fingers. In the end she takes two leaves and eats them, works the rest of the bed, and puts in two rows of peas, one shorter than the other by the length of her boot.

The holes the tines have left are quite deep. Into one of them, that evening, the rain lays a long finger of cold. On the tooth inside the root a second drop gathers by the first. Their sides flatten where they meet. For a time the seed bed and the white strip above it and the old animal upside down in its seat can each be seen twice, very small and with a bright curved rim. Then the drops run together, and the weight is too much for the tooth.

The young one on the bed is washed the breadth of seven seeds. It comes to rest against a green point, with both mouths shut. Going back would cost it nothing. It puts out a mouth upon the green, however, and finds a skin there which comes off in one piece, rather as the skin comes off a pea in the pot. It takes nearly the whole of the afternoon to get this into itself. On the wall the feeding one has gone back to the notch. The down there was pressed flat when the walls drew together. It goes among the flattened threads lifting them and letting them go, until one curls of itself and the meal proceeds in the usual way.

No one puts the neck that gave way back as it was. There is a loose seed now. At intervals it rolls a little farther than the others, with a dry click at the end of its travel, and the one under the amber lip misses a beat. By the time the pea shoots are up, it has got used to the click. The small one has not. At every click its foot goes pink all over, and there is a place on the floor, well away from where it came out of the grain, that stays warm until evening.
