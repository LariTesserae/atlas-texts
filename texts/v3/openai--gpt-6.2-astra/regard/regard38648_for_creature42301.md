The power could hear the brushing with its head up. It could hear it from the other side of the basin. There were long stretches of floor under which it went on nearly all the time, and places where a single small patch of white had to serve a great many mouths. At the clicking step there was only the one animal. It was longer than the grandmother's hand now. When somebody trod on the step it stopped eating, but it did not run. There had never been any occasion to run.

For a while the power considered letting the boy see through clay. It would have pleased him very much that first night. There was the row of feet he had not quite understood, and the two chips, and the little drift of grit the animal pushed out of its way with its chin. But in the next room a woman was taking off her shirt, and beyond her somebody had put by half a cake on a shelf for the morning. There would have to be a great many exceptions to seeing through clay. Also the boy had shut his eyes and was doing very well with his jaw.

The first thing the power actually did was turn a square over.

This was a square from beside one of the cold kilns. A dozen or so of these had been made out of a poor mixing, with a white grain here and there the size of a wick seed. The grains would swell in the first damp after firing and throw off sharp little flakes. At the time of turning there was still enough give in the clay for a fly to have walked across it and left tracks. The square lay all night with its good face down on the paving. By morning the upper face had dried rather faster than the lower and the corners had lifted.

The kiln wanted no boy that day, but there were the cold stacks to go through. He knew enough to pick out a crooked square. He put this one on the floor and pushed it to see how much it rocked. It didn't. All four corners were up. He lay down and blew underneath it and a little fan of dust came out by his ear.

It occurred to him that this was quite a good piece of work, though of no use for paving. He took it home tucked under his shirt. His grandmother, trying the clay between finger and thumb, found one of the white grains and showed him how to pick it out. It was like taking a stone out of porridge. They spent an agreeable evening spoiling the finish on both sides, and she got him the length of her forefinger to use as a measure. At the kiln next morning he asked what it cost to have one thing fired.

“What thing?”

He showed it.

“Do you want it that shape?”

He said he did. It went in over a flue mouth where a straight square would have been spoiled.

He put it down by the bowl where he had seen the animal. There was room under each lifted corner for a head, and very little room for anything else. The trouble was that it lay too close along the sides. The water, which he had counted on to go under, divided neatly and went round. After three rains it was perfectly dry underneath. His aunt, who had made several unnecessary journeys to the bowl to see how things stood, lent him her scraper, and he took a little off the paving at the uphill edge. He was careful to give the copper straight back into her hand. This time water went under and stayed.

For some days he lifted the square whenever he went by. Once he saw a thread on the paving, white at the tip and nearly clear for the rest of its length. Next time he lifted it the thread came up with the square. He tried laying it down in precisely the same place. The two ends lay alongside each other and presently went gray. After that he let the square alone.

The power, meanwhile, attended to a considerable difficulty quite a long way overhead. The clay axle of one of the great dishes had been bedded too far into its socket. As the dish grew heavy and began its turn, the end of the axle ground a little deeper. A pinch of red meal came out at every rain. There was still a good year's thickness to go through, and then the dish, taking with it two courses of roof and a good deal of the wall beneath, would come down on the early shift.

It would have been easy to hold the dish up. What the power did instead was catch the pinch of meal, which ordinarily went a long way down the dark on the breath of the kilns, and let it fall straight. It made a small red spot on the shoulder of a woman fitting pegs. She brushed it off. The next morning it was there again. She tasted a little of it on the end of her finger; it had the flat taste of fired clay, not the salt of the mortar she was using. She got the others out from under before she began knocking to find what was loose.

It took nearly a month to get a staging up there. Quite a few people had their first good look at a collector. The boy's aunt went up on a rest day and came down with both her elbows skinned and a look of considerable satisfaction. She had always wanted to know whether the insides of the dishes were glazed. They weren't. What made them shine was use.

Out by the bowl the boy had almost tired of getting down on his stomach to see nothing. On a morning of very little rain he found a damp frond leading out from one corner of the square and another, somewhat smaller, leading in. He put his cheek down. For a long while the only thing he felt was his pulse making the rim of the bowl knock softly against his face. Then there was a scrape, a pause, and another scrape that began before the first one had quite finished.

He fetched his grandmother. It was a long walk for her, and by the time he had got her settled on his aunt's folded apron he began to doubt the good of having brought her all that way to hear so slight a thing. But she did not put her head down. She held the corner of the square between finger and thumb, very lightly, as if somebody at the other end were about to tap it. Presently she held out her other hand for his.

There were two of them eating. He could tell them apart better through her hand than through the floor. One went at it in long pulls. The other made three or four short strokes and then seemed to have to think what to do next.

They left before either animal came out. On the way home she had to sit down once, on the old ridge among the finials, to get her breath. He asked if she thought they ought to make more crooked squares. She didn't hear him at first, and when he had said it again she took a while to answer.

“I should like one for keeping the cake off the ants,” she said.

He had never seen an ant. He asked her what one was. She looked surprised, then doubtful, then so amused that for a moment she had to put a hand on one of the knobs to steady herself. It appeared there had been a grandmother before her who used to say that about the cake. She had no more idea than he had what an ant was like.

He made her the square nevertheless. It stood on her shelf on four short feet, with the cake on top and the little head with its eyes shut in the dry place underneath. The power did not put ants in the basin to explain the saying. There was enough going on there already.
