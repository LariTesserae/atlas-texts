It was the white mite that caught the power’s attention. There was something about its two back legs sticking out of the dent that suggested a person trying to get a cupboard down a narrow stair. The power had put out a finger to ease the grain round before it saw, by looking through the grain instead of at it, what the forelegs were about. There was a little salt in the bottom of the pore. The mite had got its mouth to it very nicely. The finger was taken away.

There remained the difficulty of the mouse’s breath. A mouse ought to let its breath out. But the power could find no spent part of the breath in the mouse, no more than it could find a sour part in the milk light. It knew what it was to wait with a full chest, and had come prepared to relieve the mouse of that waiting. The waiting, it discovered, had all been done at the hinge hole, by the one looking in.

Still, it would have liked to know the place otherwise than by looking. Having the gift of making itself small, it entered by the hinge and went down as far as the clay. Here a curious inconvenience presented itself. To set a foot down was to require a place where the foot had not been, followed by a place where it was. There was ample clay for the sole, but nowhere at all for the step. For a little the power hung there in the attitude of somebody feeling for the bottom rung of a ladder in the dark. Then it drew its foot back into the field, where a bumblebee, which had not been there a moment before, had settled on its ankle.

It sat on the grass. The bee went in among the clover. A fine rain began, hardly enough to bring out the brown of the wood in the two round stains. By evening there was a thread of water following the old run of the gate. It went past the hinge hole without going in. The power put the pad of its little finger in the thread and felt the water divide and come together again on the nail side. It tried to imagine what the field would look like to a creature accustomed to finding its way about by where a sound was, rather than by what sound came next. A shower must be a dreadful business. No sooner had one got one’s bearings by a pat on a leaf than there was a pat somewhere else, and the first leaf, though it still had the drop, had lost the pat altogether.

It was on account of this that the power did the one small thing it did. In the wet earth outside the hinge it pressed a hollow with its fingertip, sloping the bottom a little so that at one end the water was deep and at the other end it came to nothing over the whorls of the print. The thread filled it and went on down the field. It was an ordinary puddle. It reflected a stalk of grass with a kink in it. When the stalk nodded, its kink went half the length of the puddle and back again.

From outside, one could not see much use in this. From inside, however, from the low cup in particular, the puddle lay just in the square of the hinge light. The big swimmer had always had an excellent eye. Along with the root and the foot of the ledge and the four clear gloves, there was now visible to it a piece of green with a bend in it, and the bend was first here and then there. The swimmer did not have to go anywhere to have this view. Nor had it to finish the stroke it was making. At the back of the brown eye, where things were exceedingly small indeed, there was room for the grass to nod.

By morning the water had sunk in. A thrush broke the rim of the print getting at a worm; in a week the place was level. The power had no wish to put a permanent fixture there. On wet days the hoofmarks in the field did very well, according to where the light fell. In dry weather there was sometimes the flank of a passing cow, a most extraordinary breadth of warm red, with one hair going the wrong way on it. And sometimes for the whole length of a summer afternoon there was nothing in the little square but sky.
