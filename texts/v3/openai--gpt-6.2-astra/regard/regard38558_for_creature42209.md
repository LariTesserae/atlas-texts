The lamp stays on the floor a little longer than usual. The man who is writing has put a 3 where he meant to put an 8, and is considering whether to close the two loops or strike the figure out and write it again. During this time a fly walks up the warm glass. The toad comes to within an inch of it. Here there is a difficulty of a kind it has never met before. It can see another little toad coming at it out of the laundry, and between the two of them is a fly.

The man closes the loops. When he takes up the lamp there is nothing to be seen but a wet mark on it about the size of a shirt button.

What has taken an interest in the laundry could have held the lamp there all night. It could have let the toad see through glass once and for all, a considerable addition to the understanding of a toad and one for which it would have very little further use. Neither of these things is done. The men go up. One has a red line across his palm from the handle of the basket, which disappears in the time it takes him to get into his coat.

There is, however, a difficulty at the foot of the earthenware throat. A piece of glaze has come away on the inside. It lies across the bottom of the throat with its hollow side uphill. The little water that still comes this way collects behind it, until there is enough to turn it over; then the water goes down with a gulp and the piece rocks back. A fly coming up this way can walk round it. A toad going down can get past it too. On the far side there is a fall of four feet into the closed tank.

It is not a place the toad has any particular wish to go. But in March, when rain begins to come down the old lines, there will be a sound from behind the glaze very like the sound from beyond the wall where there is open ground. The resemblance has to do with the size of the hollow, and is exact in a way few things in the laundry are exact. A toad that has sat still for hours to advance the length of its foot can cover a surprising distance when it hears that sound.

There used to be another way out. The people who built the delivery chute left a cleanout at the back, just above the yellow pavement. On the other side of its iron door a run of brick steps goes up to what was once the drying yard. The steps are broad and shallow, made for a man carrying something wet and heavy. Grass grows on the top three. At the bottom the door has rusted fast, though both pintles are good. What holds it is a little tongue of rust at the sill, no thicker than the bit of steel the men put under the straightedge.

For the rest of January nothing is done. In February the water on the cold main takes for once to the dusty bolt. It goes behind the chute. There are dark marks there from other years when it has gone that way without anybody's assistance. It wets the sill, and dries, and wets it again. One evening as the hangers are ticking there is a small sandy noise that does not belong to the succession of ticks. By morning the door stands out at the bottom about the thickness of a thumb. It need never stand out any farther. The men would have to take the chute to pieces to see that it has moved at all.

In March there is the promised noise in the throat. There is also, at the foot of the chute, the smell of a considerable quantity of wet ground. For two nights the toad sits with its head out of the brick. On the third it goes behind the chute. Going up takes most of the night. In the morning it is backed into a tussock on the second grassy step, with its chin in the laundry and the rest of it, according to the old plan of the building, in the yard.

The basket continues to be put down a palm's breadth to the left. There is no particular reason to alter this. In hot weather the lowest step is a good deal cooler than the yard, and the spoonful of brick cooler still. Nothing has been arranged about which way the toad is to go.
