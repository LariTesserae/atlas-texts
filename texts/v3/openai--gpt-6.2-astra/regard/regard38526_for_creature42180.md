At number five the knock is followed by a quite distinct *mind your toes*. The boy knows the touch of the old woman at five. She has two fingers that won't bend, and has to say most things with the heel of her hand. It has never occurred to him that you could speak to somebody's feet.

He tries *good evening* against the floor. He gets back something that might mean *good evening* or *the round kind, for soup*. By now the water is coming over the sill. He has to get his boat out.

For a week there is a great deal of talking through the floor. They establish that the little animal goes as far as number seventeen, though not what it does there. The man at seventeen, who mends accordion pleats for the trams, has been hearing it for thirty years and had supposed it was at number nineteen. Number nineteen has been an empty shaft since before he was born.

It is the tapping that attracts the attention of the power. In a city of this size almost anything you can think of is happening somewhere all the time, but a conversation conducted along the backs of eleven loose tiles is rather unusual. The power listens for a while. It has always liked the way a question sounds in this part of the basin. The last touch is very light, as though the speaker were already taking a finger off to make room for the answer.

There is a difficulty at seventeen. Behind the man's workbench the animal's run takes a turn downwards. Formerly it went straight on, but when the shaft was sunk at nineteen an end of drainpipe got broken, and successive tenants have stuffed the broken end with whatever was handy against the draft. There is a tram ticket in there from a service which went out of use four hundred years ago. A little farther in is the stiff shoulder of a blue kid glove. Beyond the glove the pipe is sound all the way to a shelf of dry sand, two acres of it, under the goods exchange.

The power could put the animal on the sand. It could take the glove out. It has a look on the other side first.

There are eggs on the sand, thousands of them, on stalks as thin as hairs. What lays them spends its grown life in the dark, flying rather badly. Even the small current from an open pipe would lay the stalks flat for a considerable distance. The glove has done very well where it is. As for the animal, it has gone down the turn after a white jointed creature about the length of a grain of rice. It is busy and in no particular difficulty.

At number three the woman is getting up off the floor. She does this by putting a hand on the cupboard and hauling, which is harder on the blue border than moving the cupboard once would ever have been. One of the tiles has come away at its upper edge. Behind it the mortar has turned to meal. In another month or so she will have it off in her hand, and quite possibly come down on the stove.

Here the power does a small piece of work. It does not fix the tile. It lets her feel it move.

She stands still for a little while with her weight on her good leg. Then she takes the tile off altogether. It comes away with a sigh and a sprinkle of gray stuff on her apron. On the back, in the finger marks of the person who pressed it into its mold, there is still some of the original blue, much brighter than the face. She turns it over twice. There is just room on the back for her four fingers, if she spreads them.

The boy comes in on the last of the ebb the following morning and finds her setting a brass handle into the solid stone at the side of the cupboard. He gets the job of washing the loose mortar off the tile. He does it in the chipped saucer, a corner at a time, changing the water frequently. When he has done, the woman sets the tile back on a little ledge without fastening it. Either of them can lift it now.

They do lift it, naturally. There is a view of the back of another tile some six inches away and of the run between, quite clean except for three fish scales and a button. The button is one of the boy's. He is pleased to have an explanation for this, though as his father points out, he hasn't one. They put the tile back.

That evening the woman gets up by the handle. It fits her hand exactly, having been put there by herself. She pours a little water in the saucer and pushes it into the usual place. The boy is at five, lying on his stomach, trying to learn the difference between a greeting and an instruction about soup.

The power stays until it has learned the difference too.
