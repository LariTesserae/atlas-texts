The power first made itself known by asking to borrow the stool. This happened on an evening when the old woman had won nearly all the grit and was getting tired of being told there was none left, when anybody could see there was plenty behind the lips of the new tiles.

“What do you want it for?”

“To see if anybody at the other end knows whose it is.”

“Well, mind the leg,” she said.

The stool disappeared from under the bench. You could tell where it had stood by the three little dry spots which took an appreciable time to fill. It was back before the street went dry. There was something on the seat like a dab of flour. The old woman tasted it. It was flour.

Down below, a great deal had changed. The pipes no longer came up in the middle of a court. What had been a court was now a bakehouse, and the mouth of the pipe, with its handsome collar of blue tiles, served to keep a dough trough off the damp floor. The woman who did the early baking had been considerably startled to see a stool come sidling out from under the trough. She had put her hand on it to keep it from tipping over.

She did not know whose it was. She could, however, tell the power something about the cord. Her brother still made that hitch. It was a good one for a chair leg because the harder you bore down on the seat the tighter it drew. He had learned it from their mother.

“Is there still water up there?” she asked.

The power said there was.

There had been some thought, on the part of the power, of bringing the upper and the lower people together. It had imagined something rather fine: the blue mouths running clear again, the little vessels filling, a face looking up and another looking down through the same round piece of sky. It had not imagined the dough trough. Nor, until it stood in the bakehouse, had it understood how far a full jar of water would go in a place where the floor was lower than the sill.

It went back up and described the bakehouse. The tall man took his hands off the plug. 

“Which way does she work the dough?” the little girl wanted to know. There was a long discussion of this, conducted largely with the heels of the hands on the bench. Apparently there was more than one way.

In the end what the power did was quite small. At the baker’s suggestion it opened a passage no wider than a quill through the side of the blue collar, below the trough but high enough to get a cup under it. At the upper end the passage opened in the paving beside the west bench. Water would not go through of its own accord. You had to put your thumb in the hollow and push. This was easy enough for the children to do and they had to be asked to stop doing it until somebody below said she was ready.

For a long time all that passed between the two places was a little very cold water, particularly welcome in the bakehouse toward the end of a shift. Then one night the baker, rinsing her cup, pushed back. A little warm water welled up beside the bench. It smelled faintly of anise. The old woman, who had been lying on the wide landing with the man she liked, sat up.

“I used to hate those,” she said, with considerable pleasure.

The power did not stay to supervise the passage. Once it came back and found a wooden peg in the lower end, the baker having gone to her brother’s for a fortnight. It left the peg alone. On another visit it found the children upstairs trying to send a whole stool through a hole the width of a quill. It nearly told them this was impossible, but remembered where the stool had come from and went about its business.

There is a change in the sound of the place now, though you have to be well below the rim to hear it. Sometimes after the low *tok* of a float there will come a much smaller tapping, generally three strokes, a pause, and one more. It is the baker knocking her cup against the blue collar. Not everybody bothers to go down. The old woman in particular will not leave a good game for it. But there is generally somebody on the red street who will put a thumb to the hollow and hold it there until, a long way down, the rim of a cup taps once.
