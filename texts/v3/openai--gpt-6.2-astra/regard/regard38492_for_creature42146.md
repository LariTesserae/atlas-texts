The power came down to the curb at the hour of the note. It could have heard the ticking from there, but it went into the room. For some days it stayed at the height of a broken stalk. From there the bare place looked very wide indeed.

One evening a beast was late in crossing. It had three feet on a foreign blade and the rest on its own when the note stopped. The two blades lifted it, pulled it quite straight, and let it fall. It landed on its back in the oval. There was no wind there now. It lay with its feet shut for a long time and then put them out one by one. Each foot had a little sideways motion at the end of its reach, the motion that ordinarily brought a tooth into the crook of a toe. It made this motion against the empty air.

The power put a single grain of mineral meal against one of its feet. The toes closed. The grain was rather too small; the foot took it up and went on feeling for something else. So the power took hold of the grain. Against this slight resistance the beast was able to turn onto its belly. The power brought the tip of a spent leaf down to the floor. The beast got on. It went up the leaf carrying the grain, and only let it go when it needed that foot for climbing.

In the course of a good many evenings the power saw two others fall. One landed right way up within reach of the foliage. The other got its own back caught on the rough underside of a blade and hung there kicking until a foot found the edge. Neither needed a grain held for it.

What troubled the power was how long the first one had lain still. It went out into the buried town and looked through the leavings of the builders. In the house above the tiled flue it found, under a fallen bench, a thin paring of black tile. One face had taken the impression of the coarse cloth on which the tile had been laid while soft. It brought the paring down. In the floor of the oval, a little below the place where the long leaves touched, it set a piece of that face no bigger than the back of a beast. The black threads and cross threads of the impression stood just proud of the stone. A toe could take hold there from any side.

For a season nothing used it. Grit went over it and came clean away. Then a young one, going out on a bridge before the note had fairly begun, came down with the first shake of the leaves. It struck the black square, caught, and stayed flattened there through the draft. Afterward it spent some time brushing the tile. The power had hoped to see it climb straight back into the red wood. Instead it walked to the other edge of the square and brushed that too. It had never been on such a thing before. When it had done with it, it caught a low tooth and went up.

There are now three of these dark squares in the hollow. They are far enough apart that they do not turn the grit into the egg stalks. From the curb one cannot see them. The note has not altered, nor has the report of the falling vane. Sometimes for whole years nothing comes down on any of the three. The power knows this because it still comes, though no longer to the height of a broken stalk. It likes to sit a little farther in, among the spent leaves, where the young ones go about with dusty feet. If it sits quite still they will try the edges of its shadow with their brushes.
