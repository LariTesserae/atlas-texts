The second time the bird comes out it is looking over its shoulder. In this way it gets quite a distance from the ferns before it notices where it is. The boy slides down the roof and holds out his hand. His grandmother puts the half gourd in it upside down, which obliges him to look at her. She has a little juice running off the end of her nose.

That evening the power that has been attending to the crown goes down inside it. There is a noise here too, after the upper air is quiet. A drop falling from a tooth of stone is followed a good while later by the sound of its landing. In one chamber there are thousands of such teeth, each with its clear drop, and only the one from which the drop ever falls. The power stays to see it fall again.

In the morning it goes about with the boy. He has a piece of gourd rind on his head. He has seen his grandmother carry things in this fashion and thinks the difficulty must be chiefly a matter of the shape of the head. At every few steps he feels his to see how it is coming along.

They go as far as a pan in the keeping of his mother's brother. The man has taken the fringe down to comb it and is sitting with his back to the boy. There are certain advantages to coming upon somebody from behind in a country where you can make all the noise you please. The boy gets close enough to put the gourd rind on his uncle's head. In return he is allowed to lift the lid. He cleans the pan to his satisfaction and sleeps there that night, his third bed. His uncle has to go quite a way in the dark to tell his mother.

It is on this walk that the power learns something it has had no occasion to know. From the height at which it first saw this place, the stone roofs lay about like the pieces of a game, and it could have put a finger down on any one of them. From the height of a man's eye a roof is very like a piece of the crown. In the dark there is nothing to distinguish the two. The man goes on his hands and knees for the last part, feeling for the bed of long moss that runs past his sister's door. He minds this less on the outward journey than he does coming back. Towards morning, when the boy wakes and asks for water, his uncle has only just lain down.

There are any number of things the power could do about this. It could make the stone roofs shine a little after dark. But the woman, for one, sleeps outside in good weather, and pulls the mat over her eyes on nights when even the moon is more than she wants. Also there is a pleasure in having a place that no one knows is yours until you take them there. The boy has two such places already, though it would be news to him that one of them belongs to his grandmother.

The power does a smaller thing. Inside the seed the woman sewed back into the mat there was once a little meat of a most tenacious yellow, the color that stays in your sight after the lizard has got safely away. The meat has dried to a skin against the shell. The power gives this skin the property of holding the last light that fell on it. Then it waits.

For the better part of a year there is nothing to show for its trouble. At last the hair breaks. The seed rolls out and the woman, who is mending the hem again and has a stone to hand for beating the fibers flat, breaks it by mistake. The boy gets the halves. He wears them on his root loop with their black backs out. Only when he goes to bed and takes the loop off does he discover what he has. There are two little pools of light on his stomach. They go up and down as he breathes.

For a time this is strictly his own affair. Then one night he leaves a half at his grandmother's, yellow side up, in a dint on the edge of the roof. He is going to sleep there on his way back from somewhere else. Coming across the rock he sees it from so far away he can hardly believe it is his. He stops and covers it with his thumb and uncovers it again. His grandmother, sitting out with the mat across her knees, sees a small figure making holes in the sky for no one in particular.

After that there is a fair amount of borrowing. The halves do not light the ground at your feet. They tell you where a person has thought to leave one, which is another sort of use. Sometimes they are both on the same roof. Sometimes for a month nobody can remember who had them last. Once the woman gets up thirsty in the night and sees two yellow eyes looking at her from under the lid of the water pan. She has a few words for her grandson in the morning.

The daughter opens another of the black seeds to see. It is quite ordinary inside. There were to have been six good weights in that mat, and now there are four. She sews up the two empty pockets with pink pebbles in them. These stay put rather better.

As for the power, it still comes to the crown. If you have attended closely to a place it is hard not to want to know what will happen there next. One evening it finds the boy lying belly down beside a vent with both shells set in the stone where the light will fill them. The bird from the ferns, now full grown, stands within reach of his hand. A last draught is beginning to stir the parted feathers on its breast. The boy could touch the gray skin with one finger. Instead he curls his fingers over the shells to keep them from blowing away, and feels through the tender skin of his elbows the note the bird has been waiting for.
