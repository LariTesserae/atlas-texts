The power came to the shelf in the interval between the third young vole and the fourth. It had been looking from below, where the few stems that hung over the break showed black against a great deal of sky. It came closer on account of a little sound. This was the mother turning at the crossing. She had set the last of the four down and was getting her hindquarters past it. One of her claws caught in the moss and let go with a small tearing noise. She took up the young one again. The power went in after her.

There was room in the chamber, though from outside it had seemed to end at the grass. On the rock side there was a hollow about the size of an egg, full of warm air. The mother put the young one in among the others and lay across the mouth of the hollow. Presently a foot came out from under her and stayed out, the toes spread a little. The power had come prepared to do something about the distance to the bottom of the mountain. It had not, from below, been able to see where the foot would go.

It stayed through the night. There were things about the chamber that took that long to learn. The handful of grass in the hole let air through even when the wind held it pressed quite flat. The root under the bed belonged to a stem growing nowhere near the break. A long way back in the dark, water passed under that root with a sound which could be felt through the floor, but which did not wake any of the five. What woke the mother was a young one nosing into the bare part of her ear. She got up, went round once and lay down the other way.

Before morning the power found something it could do. There was a stone embedded in the face below the chamber, a narrow stone with a level top, almost entirely covered with earth. Water coming down the face had washed the earth away from one end of it. The stone was quite firm. The earth at the other end was good black stuff, full of the remains of leaves, and there was a willow root hanging no more than the width of a seed from it. Each time the wind fell, the white tip came against the stone. There was already a little polish on the stone there.

The power moved a pinch of the earth along the ledge, up against the tip. It was a small business, but not quite the business it had looked to be. There was a white mite in the earth, and the power waited for it to go on into a crack. Also the earth was dry underneath. It took a bead from higher up the root and held it until it was water again, and let it soak in from the stone side so as not to wash the earth off. When the wind came up toward morning the root lifted a little way out of the earth and settled back into it.

For a while nothing came of this except a dark mark on the root. By the time the young were going about on their own feet there were fine hairs all round the mark, with grains of earth sticking to them. Later that summer a leaf fell on the ledge and was held there by its stalk. Water passed through the leaf until only the ribs were left. Other things caught in those.

The nick-eared vole came out at the broken end one afternoon with a length of sedge crosswise in her mouth. She had been trying to get it round a bend from the inside. She put her head out a good way, swung the seed end clear of the root that had been troubling her and backed in again. A few seeds came down. Most of them went turning out of sight, but one lodged in the ribs of the leaf.

There is a tuft of sedge on the face now. From the boulder above it cannot be seen at all. From below, late in the year, it has the look of a small brush dipped in gold and drawn once across the gray. The power, passing that way, knew it for the place, and went on down the mountain.
