The power first felt the weight of the slate by putting a little of its strength under it. The red end of the root gave a click. A shrimp let go of the moss and hung for a moment on its side with its legs going. Up on the roof the little limpet went on feeding. It had come to a brown island in the middle of a blue sweep, no bigger than the full stop at the end of a line, and was going round it, taking it in from the edges.

The power let the weight down again, very gently. There was a fault in the root a little way back from the red end. The wood had gone soft in a ring, and all that remained sound at the centre was bent. It would do for this tide and a good many more. When it went, the slate would come down at the door first. The water at the back would still rise and fall, but there would be no more running sound, and very little light.

There were stones in the bank left by the same water that had laid the slate there. One of them, a blunt bit of quartz, lay on its side in the peat just beyond the root. It had been a white stone once. A stain the colour of strong tea went nearly all the way through it. Between its upper end and the slate there was room for the thickness of a sedge leaf.

The power waited for high water. The peat took up water and eased its hold; the stickleback came in, turned with some difficulty, and took up its station looking out. In the dark behind the fish the power turned the quartz a little on its long side. A thread of black mud came out from under it. Its upper end touched the slate. The pressure in the bent wood slackened, though from the marsh there was no difference at all in the look of the red end. The stone would take the weight when the wood was quite gone.

It was low water again before the power withdrew the last of its strength. This time there was no click. A drop came down on the one stone showing in the floor and made its green tip black. Another gathered in the same place on the roof. The seam had not been pinched; the little run of water was coming round the brown shell as before. The power had been rather particularly concerned about that seam.

In the next few weeks the peat washed out from the face of the quartz, enough to show a pale oblong in the wall. There was no feeding on it at first. The shrimp used the narrow place behind it, and for a while a single strand of moss lay across its face, writing a green downstroke there at the flood and lifting clean away on the ebb. By the time the red end came off the root and went bobbing out among the duckweed, the oblong had dulled to much the colour of the old underside.

One of the young limpets found the join. Its brim stood clear of the quartz at first, with the front of the foot feeling about on the new surface and the back half still fast to the slate. It stayed in this position through two reports at the door. Then the back half came along. By afternoon there was a little fan of clean tea-coloured stone showing through the brown.
