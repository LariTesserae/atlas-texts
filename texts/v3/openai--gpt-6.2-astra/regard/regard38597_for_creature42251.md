In the morning the first of the young comes out backwards. There is not room under the slab to turn a tail, even such a tail as that. It backs into the white-toed bird, which has a fly for it, and receives the fly over its shoulder without having to turn round. There is a good deal to know about living here, and very little of it has to be thought out afresh.

You have it in your power to bring the voices down. It would be quite a small matter, rather less than putting a dislocated joint back in its socket. For an instant you imagine the pond with the downpour on it. You see the little pits beaten into the brown silt right out to the edge, and the two starry tracks filling up. But it is only the voices you are considering. You turn your attention to one of them, the faint continuous cheeping that goes on high above the second roof.

The bird that is doing it has a white toe. It has gone under the roof with a leaf and is trying out various positions for the leaf against the back stone. Every time it puts its head down the sound stops. On the first shelf, well out of sight of it, the three young ones are coming along the polished way. At each cessation they stop; when the bird takes hold of another part of the leaf, and the cheeping begins again, they run. At last the first of them passes into the shadow of the second roof. The others go in on either side of it. You let the voice be where it is.

It is much harder to see anything in the valley now than when you came. There used to be a shelf, a pond, a few plants. Now there is even a particular fly you know, with a hind leg so long that it trails on the water when the fly gets up. Twice a bird has missed it through striking at the disturbance instead of at the fly. It cleans the leg after each escape, drawing it slowly between the other two on that side, as if there were something on it that made it heavy.

In the course of the afternoon a little weather gets over the lip. It is scarcely to be seen against the limestone, but the down on the heads of the young birds divides into points. Drops stand out along the underside of the broken piece of roof by the path. One drop runs in under the stone instead of falling. A minute later there is a dry little report there, quite distinct from the knock overhead.

The stone has been resting on a scale of rock thinner than a leaf. The scale has given way. Next time a bird goes over the groove the stone tips a little and tips back. It is the grown bird without the white toe. It turns at the top and looks down between its feet, then goes about its business. It weighs hardly anything. A whole winter of ice lifting at the bottom of the stone would not have shifted it to better advantage for tipping.

You wait until the bird is well along the slant. Then you put a finger on the stone where the bird's foot was. At the far end a black space opens and closes. There is room there for a foot with all its toes spread out.

On the shingle there are hundreds of little wedges of the right thickness. You choose one with a dull side and a side crossed by a white vein. Holding the broken roof quite still, you push it in dull side up, so that you can see by the vein how far it has gone. You stop as soon as it bears. The groove is left at precisely the slant it had this morning. An ant comes out from under your thumbnail and goes some way up your wrist before you can put it back where you found it.

By evening the weather has gone over. The smallest bird comes down the slant with something in its bill. It is a leaf much too big for it. The bird has not pulled it off a plant; there is still mud on the side that lay uppermost wherever it picked it up. At the broken roof it sets a foot on the dragging end and is brought up short. It tries pulling, as the old bird did on the wall. Then it takes its foot off. In this way, with a number of rests, it gets the leaf as far as the fork.

Nobody comes to meet it. After a while it drops the leaf and goes to the pond to drink. The long-legged fly gets up ahead of it. High in the last light, at the height of a river deep enough to carry trees, there is the small vexed cry of a bird that has got a billful of water.

You go out by the head of the valley, walking in the long reach that is nearly quiet. Looking back from there, you cannot distinguish one roof from another. For a little while you can still see the leaf at the fork, its muddy side turned to the sky. Then one of the five, you cannot tell which, comes out and takes it in.
