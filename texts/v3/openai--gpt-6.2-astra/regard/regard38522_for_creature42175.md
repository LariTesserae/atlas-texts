The power that had taken an interest in the town had no difficulty seeing in the dark. It could see the two of them as distinctly as the boy would have seen them with his eyes open, and the little softer oval on his aunt's thumb where a stem came against it under the mallet. What it could not do, with all that seeing, was tell whether she meant the boy to take the next turning. Her hand lay quite slack in his. He took it. It was the wrong one. They went all the way round a bed of tubers before coming to the very same fork again, this time from the other side.

The power had been on the point of doing something about the rail. It could have put a little warmth in the wood ahead of his hand, not much, the difference there is between a stone the sun has fallen on and one it has just left. It was glad now that it had not. The boy had found out something about a fork that he could not have found out by taking the proper turning. He went back a little way and came up to it again, to be sure.

At the basket court his aunt opened her eyes. He wanted to know when she had first known they were going wrong.

*At the second tuber against my knee,* she told him.

This struck him as hardly fair. He was not yet long enough in the leg to get the benefit of the tubers.

For a while the power went about its business elsewhere. It remembered the place, though. In particular it remembered the sign for help, which it had seen made once that afternoon by a man who had got the back of his shirt caught on an antler peg. Three people had been within reach of him. Only one had moved, and that one had done no more than lift the stuff clear of the peg. The man had gone on talking with his hands almost without a break.

The next time the power came to the tarn it was snowing on the ice. The western court had been opened. The hinge maker was home again, with her sand in the hollow, and there was a good deal of traffic along the new rails, chiefly of people who had made some part of them and wished to go over their own part with a hand. Under the public beds the spring was still running. It had brought up two handfuls of white gravel and a flat piece of red stone about the size of a sleeping child's eyelid. This stone stood up in the current, fell over, and stood up again. In all the mountain there was nobody looking at it.

The power would have liked the sisters to see it. For several days it considered how to bring this about. It could put the thought of the spring into the same two heads at the same hour. But it had seen the hinge maker with a good stem laid across her knee, perfectly silent and about to strike. It disliked the thought of putting anything into that silence. It could send the red stone out into the street, where one or the other of them would be sure to find it. But a stone in the street is a stone in the street. The thing worth coming to see was the trying and failing and trying again of that flat chip to stand on end.

In the end it went down and looked at the stone itself. It was very good. Sometimes it got almost upright, with a little shudder in it all the way along the upper edge, before going over. At last a grain of gravel shifted and the stone lay flat. The power stayed a little longer, and then drew back up through the roots. It had not previously occurred to it that it too might go to a place for the pleasure of going.

It was on its way out of the tarn when it saw a hand move close against a chest.

This was at the western lip. An old woman was sitting there with one hand on the bare stone. She had been born in a lower lake and had come to the saddle as a young woman, long enough ago that there were now rooms in the town grown out of roots which she remembered having to step over. Her sight had been going for years. What she could see of the outlet now was a brightness, not unlike the brightness you can see with your eyelids shut if you turn your face up under the ice. She had come to sit in the sound of the fall, which was quite as good as ever. Getting up, she had put her hand back and found nothing but smooth stone where she remembered a root. She had sat down again rather abruptly. Now she was making the small sign on the chance that somebody might be going by.

The power put a hand there.

It took some care over the hand. It made it neither very young nor very old, and bent the ends of the fingers a little out of the straight. The woman caught hold of it and got to her feet. Then, instead of letting go, she turned it palm up and ran her thumb over it.

*You don't do much,* she said against its wrist.

The power had left out the hard places. There was not a callus on it.

*Not with that hand,* it answered.

She laughed. She asked to be taken as far as the first rail, where she would be quite all right. It took her there. At the swelling for the upper court she gave the wood a small satisfied squeeze; it was one of her own rods, and there was a slight flat on the underside where she had been a stroke too enthusiastic with the scraper. She went off without asking who had helped her. In a town of that size it is a little insulting to ask somebody who they are merely because you happen not to be able to see them.

The power sat for a time on the cold shelf with its new hand beside it. It could feel the fall through the heel of the hand. That was rather different from knowing what made the noise. After a while it turned the palm down, to get more of it against the rock.

It still comes sometimes. There has been no great change in the place on that account. The sisters did eventually go down under the beds again, on a day when the spring was bringing up nothing more remarkable than sand. They had forgotten how difficult it was to come out backwards, and each accused the other of having grown wider in the intervening years. The boy can go anywhere on the rails now. He has lost interest in hinges. For a time the power kept the hand ready whenever he went near the outlet, but he is a good swimmer and has never had occasion to ask for it.

There is a little roughness in the palm these days, just below the fingers. It comes from the stone.
