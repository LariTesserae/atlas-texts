The first thing the power did was to go round to the outside. It had some trouble doing this. It found itself three times at the flour sifter, and the third time the woman who lived there, supposing it too shy to ask, gave it a cake. It ate the cake sitting on the second step. From there it could see the boy’s hand under the lip of the curb, the little finger crooked and the others spread for balance. He shut the cap and rubbed a bit of shed skin off his knuckle. Then he sat down to wait for the brown animal, which had got halfway off his shoulder and seemed to have forgotten what came next.

The power went out through the pit while the warmth of his finger was still in it.

There was very little outside. There was the back of the fired clay, gritty in places where the maker had not troubled to wipe it, and there was the underside of the tray. The power put a hand to this and felt somebody chopping something quite hard for supper. It could feel the gate shift on its broad foot. Sixty floors did not amount to much between the gate and a hand held there in the quiet. Presently it felt the three quick blows of the boy coming home.

The pit was thinner at the bottom than an eggshell. A grain had come away on the boy’s knuckle along with the skin. In another year, perhaps in half a year, he would put his finger through. The power considered putting a little clay on the back of it. But unfired clay would only come away again, and to fire it, even in so small a spot, would cook the gold meal on the nearest fan. There was a clear little animal there with some of that meal halfway down.

What the power did, after a little thought, was to set its fingertip against the back of the pit and leave it there.

For eleven months this made no difference that anybody noticed. The boy grew too big to get his finger comfortably under the hook and took to lifting it with the bent end of a horn paring. He missed two evenings with a fever and one for the pleasure of being asked, when he got back from his great-aunt’s, whether he had forgotten something. His mother did not ask. She had forgotten too.

On an evening when the tassels were so full of white animals that one of the roofs had to be propped, the last grain of clay fell out. The boy felt a pad against his finger. He took his hand away at once, as he did when the brown animal wanted to get down.

After a bit he put it back.

It was not much like the brown animal. For one thing it was warmer, and for another it yielded exactly as much as he pressed, even when he pressed quite hard. He tried a little rub from side to side. The finger on the other side rubbed back, rather too far, so that a minute ridge of clay built up under his nail.

“You’ll make it bigger,” he said.

The rubbing stopped.

He went home in a considerable hurry, then had to go back for the brown animal, which was sitting where he had left it with a dusting of meal on its back. By the time he had done that he had thought better of fetching all nine households. He fetched his mother.

She felt for a long while. What interested her was that when she took her thumb away the warmth remained, but there was no pressure at all against the cap. Whatever was there had understood a latch. She opened and shut it several times to make sure.

“Is it holding us up?” the boy asked.

His mother looked down at the paving. “It didn’t used to have to,” she said.

This was a great relief to the power, which had begun to be afraid that it might never dare take its hand away. It withdrew a little. The tray stayed where it was. Through the hole, which was now a hole and not a pit, came the smell of the prop that had lately been cut for the roof, a sharp, almost sour smell, and then a very small quantity of flour. Somebody had set the sifter going.

In the end the latch maker did put a patch over the hole, but on the outside. It was a scale of horn scarcely larger than the washer, passed edgeways through the hole and turned flat by the finger that was waiting there. It could be lifted from within. From without it could only be tapped on. The power made the mistake, the first night, of tapping after the boy had gone to bed, and woke every household in the western quarter. Nobody could account for the noise. It was the first time anybody had knocked on one of the latch maker’s latches from the other side.

There is a rule now that whoever goes down after supper takes something that might be worth passing through a hole. Not necessarily to give away. The elder sister took a knitting needle and got it back with the dropped stitch she had been hunting for drawn up on the point. The woman with the blades passed a seed, the first her stand had made, and asked that it be kept somewhere dry. The boy once took nothing at all and lay with his ear to the cap for the best part of an evening. He had meant to find out what the outside sounded like. What he heard was the faint, unmistakable click of his mother setting down two bowls. It had gone out by some other way and come round to him. He has been looking for that other way ever since.

The fans still grow together by the step. The nests have not been disturbed. Sometimes the power is there and sometimes it is off on business of its own, and then the bit of horn, lifted, gives on a cool dark space no deeper than a finger can reach, though of course it may go farther. If asked what holds the tray up, the latch maker says she has no more idea than she ever had. She does know that the patch fits better with use. Its outer face has acquired a shallow whorl quite unlike the broad mark her thumb has worn on the inside. Between these two marks a root has lately taken, and put out a pair of very small leaves. One is inside the garden.
