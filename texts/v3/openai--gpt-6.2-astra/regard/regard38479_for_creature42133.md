The power that heard the moth had heard a good many louder things without being particularly struck by them. What held its attention here was that it could hear the wings from the ticket hall. It went back, as one can go back along a length of wool to find where a stitch was dropped, and found the driver's broad thumb resting beside the bell button. Farther back it found the two cuts in the brown paper. One of them had cost the florist a perfectly good corner. She had used it to put under the short foot of her till.

The power took a place on the bench by the scales. It had no weight to speak of, but a little water came out of the joint in the bench, as it does when somebody sits down after rain.

In the course of the evening it discovered an inconvenience which nobody in the town could do very much about. The eastern terminus was growing away from the east ward. The shelf on which the rails lay and the shelf which carried the ticket hall had formerly been one piece; they were still joined by a thick neck of rock under the florist's cellar. On either side of the neck the two pieces were acquiring stone at slightly different rates. This had already been noticed. The ticket clerk kept a short board with a bevel on it to lay across the sill for prams. From time to time he turned it over and took a shaving off the thick end. What had not been noticed was a strain in the neck itself. In five or six years it would give, probably in the night, and the noise would be heard as far off as the boiler house. The drop would be about the depth of a kitchen drawer. That is quite enough, where there are rooms overhead.

It would have been easy to join the pieces fast. The power tried the consequences of this, without troubling the actual stone. The strain went west under the rails, passed the end of the long green laundry garden, and came up in the floor of the hammer works. Here the consequences became exceedingly complicated. Among other things there was a man who would have to eat with his left hand. The power let the trial go. It considered moving the whole terminus a little at a time, which was practicable, but involved making a habit of it.

At half past eleven the florist carried the till upstairs. The last car went out backwards with its interior lamps still on, a succession of empty yellow rooms going off to bed. Under the bench there was a steel threepenny bit, lost for so long that the king's ear on the upper side of it had rusted away. The power took this down through the rock and set it on edge in the narrowest part of the strain. There it let go of it. That was all it did to the mountain.

On Thursday the surveyor was having his soup when there came from below a high, exasperated singing. The florist thought a pan had boiled dry. They went downstairs together and stood listening among the pots of bulbs in the cellar. Every time a car came in, the note rose by a little less than a semitone. The surveyor went out for his bag. He missed the last of the soup, but the florist put the potatoes by for him on a saucer.

By winter there were four pale pillars under the ticket hall, each in a sleeve of the sort made in the freight district. The work was rather interesting to watch. For a fortnight the passengers went in through the florist's shop, which did her no harm at all. A boy found the threepenny bit in a barrow of spoil and spent it, after some argument, on a yellow buttonhole for his mother. The neck of rock could now give whenever it liked.

The power stayed until after the builders had gone. It liked the ride back to the western sheds, particularly the part where the driver slowed and there came from all about the car the small sounds of hares changing their minds. It was tempted to let the boy in the east ward see the one that lived behind the bread bin. One evening the boy and the hare actually stood on opposite sides of the yard gate, the boy trying to lift the latch without putting down a full jug of milk. A touch would have done it. But on the other side the hare was busy washing its bad foot, a difficult business at its age, and had chosen the spot for its privacy. The power held still. Presently the boy set down the jug, and the hare heard the bottom of it grate on the stone and went away through the cabbages.

In the spring the bent flower at the terminus put up five stalks. Only two of them faced the ticket hall. The others faced out over country of which as yet there was very little evidence. On mild evenings the florist stood in the doorway with her scissors in her apron pocket, looking at them with the professional interest she ordinarily reserved for things she might conceivably sell. There was no question of cutting these. For one thing, nobody could reach them.

The power went away on such an evening. The bench, which had been a little damp throughout the winter, dried in time for the last car. In the joint between its two boards a brown scrap of wing, no bigger than a flake of onion skin, stirred in the wind of the closing doors. It proved to be attached to a whole live moth. After a while it drew the wing in.
