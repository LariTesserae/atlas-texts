The power came first to the cold band and put a hand down for a hem that was going out on a biscuit lid. The animal stepped aboard without interrupting what it was doing to its whiskers. For a little while the power held both hands still, having in one the hem and in the other the lid, and not knowing which of the two had wanted to go out to sea. On being offered the choice the hem went back on the lid. It had found something in the turned edge.

This was a useful beginning. The power followed the lid until it lodged against a rush and then went inland along the main.

At the western stair the younger attendant was taking off a boot. He had gone in up to the hip, having set his foot aside for the animal with the nicked tail. The flask was all right. He had held it high above his head throughout, a thing which pleased him when he thought of it afterward but at the time made it difficult to get out. What troubled him was that in going sideways he had come down on a roll of clay. He had felt the little roof go flat. He was working his toes into the mud beside the stair, trying to find the place again.

The power could have told him that there had been nothing under the roll. It had only to put its attention there and the place lay open to it, down to the print left by the last hair of the tail as the sleeper went out. But a voice saying *there was nothing under it* would have been a considerable event in the younger attendant's life. He would have had to decide who had spoken, and whether to tell the elder attendant, and whether on another occasion he could expect to be told. He was wet enough already.

Instead the power sat on the lowest tread and took some clay in its hands. It had noticed a difficulty about the western stair. The first step down, which had once been a very good step, now gave upon whatever water people remembered at the foot of it. There was always a bottom, but it was not always the bottom one had left in the morning. The power considered putting a stone there. This would be easy. It could put a stone there so thoroughly that the man who poured the stair would remember building over it, and might even remember, on the strength of that, a father who had taught him how to build over a stone. The man had done very well with the father he had.

While it was sitting there the younger attendant put out his bare foot and found a knee.

“I beg your pardon,” he said.

The power moved along. The attendant used the place it had vacated to get his sock on. Then he sat a little longer than was strictly required, because the presence on the tread beside him was warm and because it had not asked what he had been doing with his boot off. Presently he said that what they needed here was another six feet of handrail.

“How far down?” asked the power.

“Down isn't the trouble. Out. You lose the rail before you've found a place for both feet.”

In the morning there was no new rail. There was, however, a third person at the works, willing to take the inner end of a length of pipe while it was being threaded. The elder attendant had been meaning to do the job for two winters. It was one of those jobs for which two people are perfectly sufficient provided no one is required to run the works.

They bent the last foot of the pipe back on itself, so that a sleeve following it homeward would not catch. At the other end they fitted a stay to the stringer of the stair. Nothing went into the clay. A person coming in from the plain could reach the bend well before committing a foot to the ground below the bottom tread. A person going out could hold on until he had quite made up his mind. When they were done the elder attendant ran a twist of waste along the whole of it and, where a thread of the waste caught, took the file to it once more.

The power was asked to stay for mussels. It stayed. In the course of the meal a hem came along the new pipe, lost its footing on the bend and fell into the younger attendant's open coat. There was a little pause while the two of them considered what to do about this. Then the attendant held the skirt of his coat up against the tread, making an easy incline, and the animal went out carrying a bit of his lunch. He did not feel its tail. He had mussel liquor on his hands and it seemed a pity to put the salt on it.

The power did not undertake the vacant post. There were things it could do at a pump which would have made it very difficult, after a time, for anybody else to take a shift. It comes in now and then when they are doing a job that wants another pair of hands. There is no rule about asking it. Once the elder attendant called for it for the better part of a morning and in the afternoon discovered that the younger one had had it at the far end of the main all the time, holding a nut on the blind side of a flange.

The rail is much used. Already there are people who remember leaning on it to fish when they were small. The attendants don't contradict them. They know when they put it up, and there is room on the stair for somebody to have had a pleasant afternoon besides.

As for the hem that went out on the lid, the thing it had found in the turned edge was a narrow rind of icing, shut in when the metal was pressed and quite dry after all those years. It softened at the second tide. There had indeed been an iced one in the middle. No one at the works has found this out, and there is no particular reason why they should.
