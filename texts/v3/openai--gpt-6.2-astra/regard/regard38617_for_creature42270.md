The pump comes up to speed. You feel it first in the buckle of your watch where it touches the rail. By then the mouse is down the inside of your leg and away along the kerb. For a while there is nothing to be seen. Then, well below you, an iris stalk dips in a place where the water has not yet got to it.

The power that had taken an interest in the valley could feel a pump start from considerably farther off than that. What it could not do, without attending to a mouse, was tell what there was to be got by going to that particular stalk. It stayed for the night. Toward morning it saw the black beetle again, or one very like it, going about its business with a fine round bubble of fresh air.

There was work in hand at the upper end of the beds. The oldest of the brick races was leaking into the ground under the bus road. For years there had been a place in the road where the driver bit off the end of whatever he was saying; lately it had become necessary to take the bus over on the wrong side. The race was to have a new lining. The water would run between two smooth grey cheeks instead of between bricks, and you could take the lining up in sections if anything went wrong below. It was a good piece of work. There was no reason for the town to go on losing its road.

On the Thursday before the shutdown, the woman who had drawn the sections came up to make a last measurement. She put her folding rule down on the landing while she got the cover off a bolt. This was where the power did the one thing in the valley that could be put down to it with any certainty. It moved the rule about the width of a pencil. When the woman reached for it her glove caught the end instead of the middle, and it went down flat into the yellow iris.

It was a boxwood rule that had belonged to her father. She went down for it.

From the lower walk the underside of the old brick coping was just at the height of her spectacles. She saw the wet ledge first, and the little fan of prints going from it into a perpendicular joint. While she stood there with the yellow rule in her hand a hem mouse came out backwards, drawing after it by one corner a grey pad of bedding almost too wide for the joint. It had to brace its tail against the wall to get the last of it out. Then it let go. The bedding fell into the race. The mouse rubbed its nose vigorously with both hands and went back in.

She had known about hem mice since she was six. She had not known one of them could have occasion to throw out a mattress. She put her spectacles close to the joint. Far back, in what light there was, she could see the orange ledge, quite dry, with a little hollow worn in the middle of it. At the back of that was a turn. Nothing whatever of the sort appeared on her sections, though they were drawn large enough to show the bedding of each course of brick.

It was nearly a month before the first length of lining went in. Some of that was delay over the loan of a crane, and some of it was spent by the woman and a mason called Bell on the lower walk with a lamp and a bit of bent wire. They found that the pockets belonged to the outer face of the wall. It was possible to stop the leak without filling them. The difficulty was the turned-over top of the new lining, which would shut them off almost as neatly as the lid shuts off the compartments of a cutlery box.

What they eventually left was an interval under the turn, on the landward side. Bell could just get his knuckles along it. At either end it came out flush with one of the iron ties across the race. To someone looking down from the bridge there was no difference between this and the lining on the drawings. The bus went back to its own side of the road. The driver took a week or so to give up pausing in the middle of a sentence.

For eleven nights nothing used the interval. On the twelfth a mouse went in about the length of its head, came out and tried the identical place again. Much later in the winter Bell, tightening a nut at the far end, felt something warm pass across the backs of his fingers. He kept his hand there. A second thing passed, and a third, the last one lingering with a hind foot in the hollow between two knuckles before it got a proper hold on the brick.

He mentioned this to the woman next time she came up. She made a small correction to the finished drawing. That mattered more than either of them supposed. Years afterwards, when there was no one on the early shift who had worked with Bell, a man pricing a repair put his nail on that interval and asked if it was meant to be there. The drawing was fetched out of its long tin and unrolled on the bench, with a glove at each corner to keep it flat. It was.
