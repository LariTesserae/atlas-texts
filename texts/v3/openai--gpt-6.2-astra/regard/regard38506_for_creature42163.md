The power could feel the whole weight of the pillar. Most of it came down well to the left of the thumbprint, on a part of the bed where the combing had gone quite flat. What came down on the red doors would not have cracked the empty skin of one of the discs. There was no occasion to lift the pillar. Nor was there anything the matter with the animal on the dry afternoons. Behind its doors the loose side lay in a damp pleat. A young one could turn there as easily on the twelfth day as on the first.

There was one thing the power did not know, which was how much of the sound the animal heard. It could have gone into the animal to find out. Instead it attended to a morning on which a strip of sacking had been hung over the mouth of the passage while the rooms above were swept. The hard note went out of the sound. At the chip the damp had got as far as the second turn, and the animal was well out, with one feeler laid back along the edge of a door. It went on eating. When the sacking was taken down it lifted that feeler and laid it back in very nearly the same place. That was all the power learned about the sound. It left the tile alone.

In the course of time the broad red roof farther in lost a little of its color. There were new doors in the sill, so thin that when they were closed you could see the dark line going by underneath them. Up in the colonnade a joint opened in the white pavement. It was nobody's fault. The stone had been brought in quite green and had taken all those years to finish drying. A man came with a pot of fine stopping to do the joint. He had orders to do the little places round the pillars as well, so long as the stopping lasted.

He did good work. He cleaned each place first with the short stiff hairs of a brush and pressed the stuff in with a narrow leaf of steel. At the foot of the last pillar he had rather more than a leaf's worth left. He went down on his elbow, because from above the chip looked shallow and from the side it looked deep, and he did not care to leave a hollow behind his work.

What the power did was to take the weight of the pot for a moment. The man had set it a little unsteadily on the handle of his brush. Ordinarily he would have had to reach after it just as he got his eye down to the chip.

He saw the whorl first. He had such a one on his own thumb, broken across by a white cut. It interested him to see where the other man had borne down and where he had eased his hand in lifting it. He would have liked to get a finger in to feel how hard the old stuff had set. Then the two nicks came apart. The animal put out so little of itself that what the man saw was chiefly the change in the thumbprint: a short piece of one line was there, and then it was covered, and then it was there again with a wet edge to it.

He sat up and took the pot off the brush. After a bit he got down again, this time with his mouth tucked into his shoulder. He had noticed the breath mark. There was nothing doing now in the second turn of the whorl, but he found the old doors, and the green at the ends of the furrows, without help.

It was his own decision to use the last of the stopping on a stair where a bit had gone out of the nosing. On his sheet, against the last pillar, he put *open to passage*. This was not quite right. The lining of the passage was entire. But it was a thing a workman coming after him would understand and would be likely to leave as he found it.

The stopping on the stair dried a shade too light. For a while you could find the place by it: you came up over the pale patch and went on to the pillar on that side. Later it took the dirt like the rest of the stair. By then one of the thin pairs of doors had thickened and gone brick red, except at the meeting edges. They fitted a little better than the pair you remember. On a dry morning, even with your cheek quite flat to the stone, you would say there was nothing in the thumbprint at all.
