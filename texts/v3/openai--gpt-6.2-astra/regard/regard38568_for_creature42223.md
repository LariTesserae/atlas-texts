There came a time when a man put his weight on the rush loop coming out, and the loop kept the shape of his fingers. He had to take them out one at a time. He was a careful man. The next day he came back with a hank of good cord round his waist.

It was the root he meant to put it round. There was nowhere else that looked quite so fit for the purpose. He felt up along it till he came to the place where it entered the roof. It would have held him once. It might have held a considerable number of people, especially if they had gone through the pinch one shoulder at a time, as you ought. What it would not have done was grow much thicker inside the cord.

The power that had taken an interest in the room could feel the root as far up as the grass. It could feel the man, too, down to the slight soreness along the back of the hand which he had had such trouble getting loose. He had brought the cord for the sake of the person who would come after him. There was no occasion to frighten him.

Beside his wrist there was a piece of stone about as big as the end of a loaf. It had come down in the cold part of the turn, in a winter before there were notches, and had stuck there. Behind it the wall went in a little way and came out again. The power eased the stone out as far as the man's sleeve and let him have the weight of it by degrees. He thought he had worked it loose with his elbow. It was too big to let drop where somebody would presently have to put a knee. He took his hand away from the root and passed the stone back along his body and set it in the cold, and when he felt in the place it had come from he found that his hand fitted very well there. So did the other hand, going out. You could even turn your hand over in it if you got yourself into that difficulty where you could neither go on as you were nor remember quite how you had been.

He took the old loop away. He had meant to do that in any case. The cord came in useful later for something else.

This made a small difference to the room which the power had not foreseen. The end of the rush had lain against the root. Just where the two touched there had always been a little wet, even when the air in the turn was dry. The animal that went up the root was accustomed to go out on the rush at this point and come back on its underside. For some time after the loop was taken away it put out a length of itself into the air. It had a way of feeling round with its tip that took a good deal longer than seemed necessary to establish that the rush was gone.

The power waited. On the fourth journey the animal found the hollow. The back of the hollow was damp, and there was meal in it which had not been disturbed since the stone came down. For a season or two, anybody who put a hand in there came out with a fine crooked line across the heel of it where the meal had been taken away.

Nothing was done about the bark. It was possible to feel, from the saddle, the tuck that passed over the animals when the drop struck, and it was tempting to take the strip up and put it somewhere dry. But once, in a long interval between drops, the animal on the pebble drew its loose edge off the wood. It fed a little way along the rim. Then it came back. Whether it came for the wood or for something that grew there, the power did not know. It was quite a good place to leave a thing one did not know.

Up in the turn the hollow is a little easier to find now. There is a place just below it where thumbs have taken off the dust. On the inside, out of reach of a thumb, the fine track goes on in the dark.
