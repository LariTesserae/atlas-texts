The power came near enough to see what the vole did about its whiskers. It sat back and passed both paws over its face, the left a little after the right. Then it went uphill. There was still light at the fork when it was quite dark in the nest.

There was room for the power in there too. It could have taken the young up, grass and all, without loosening the hold of the moss. But the mother was coming in. She put her nose under the foot that lay across a neck and worked her way into the middle. The young were warm except at their small extremities, and these presently grew warm too. The power left them the dark they had made.

Out on the belt at the yard, things were not going quite so well. The high note came from a return roller, one of a great many under the long loaded stretch of rubber. A woman walked that stretch once a shift. From the iron walk beside it the noise was so much louder than it was on the heath that she could not separate the note from the rest. She had been trying for three days. When she stopped the belt to listen it was like turning out the light to look for a moth.

The power waited until she came round again. Just as she put her gloved hand on the rail beside the bad roller, it let her feel in her palm what she could hear so well from a distance: one thin tremor running crosswise to the heavy shake of the stone. She took her hand off, went back a pace and tried again. This time she could find it for herself. She marked the place with a stub of yellow chalk.

It took the better part of the next morning to change the roller. For those hours the plovers could be heard from the yard. One of the men eating his bread on the steps thought there was a child somewhere beyond the sheds, whistling the same two notes to call a dog. He waited for the dog to bark. After a while he went in to put the kettle on.

When the belt started up it made the long coarse roll as before, with a space above it where the note had been. The vole was out cutting grass. It had hold of a stem whose lower joint was still rolled tight and soft, and it ate rather farther up this one than usual. At no point in the stopping or the starting of the belt did it take its paws off the stem.

The power came back often. There was no need to make an occasion of it at either place. Sometimes it stayed by the woman as she went along the iron walk, touching the rail here and there now from habit. Sometimes it came down to the willow. By the time the young could get about, the run beside the knob was wide enough for two of them to meet and get past, though neither seemed yet to know this. They would stand nose to nose until one backed the whole way out. On a still morning you could see a line of beads go from the grass, beginning at the knob and travelling steadily uphill. Then, with scarcely a blade dry beyond it, the same line would shake again.
