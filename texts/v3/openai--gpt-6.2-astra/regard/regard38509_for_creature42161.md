The power that had taken an interest in the shelf first made itself known on a wet day, when the woman was about halfway home. She had come to a place on the path where a spring ran down three natural steps. She generally took these sideways, setting the sounder of her knees to the work. This time there was a hand under her elbow. It was quite a good hand for the purpose, warm and broad, and it let go on the last step.

She looked back. The water was running down all three steps as before. There was nobody on them.

“Much obliged,” she said, after a bit. It cost nothing, and somebody might have been standing in the bracken feeling foolish.

On her next visit she found a woman in the shelter. She was sitting at the wet end of the bench, with the drips hitting her between the shoulder blades. She had a broad face, wet hair parted very precisely down the middle, and boots of a kind the woman had not seen before, each made of one piece, as if the foot had been dipped in boot and allowed to dry.

“You could sit along a little,” the woman said.

“I've been watching through the holes.”

This was worth trying. The woman got down on her knees. Through the newest hole she could see a round of pink ground with a drop bursting on it at intervals. Through the next she saw the same drop from the side, an exceedingly small silver walking stick that straightened up and vanished. There was nothing to be seen through the earliest pair. Under there the ground had grown dark.

“Is that earth?”

“Mostly what comes off your boots.”

The woman lay down and looked under the bench. There was indeed a little brown ridge, about the length of a loaf, where she had knocked her heels together before starting home in the evenings. From the ridge grew a single blanched leaf, pointed toward the light. She sat up again with a wet knee and a considerable feeling of pleasure. For years she had been so careful not to bring anything up here on purpose.

“Was it you on the path?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

They ate the sandwich. The visitor took her half in both hands and ate from the middle of the cut edge, which left her at the end with two corners rather difficult to manage. A swimmer barked at one of the crumbs. By the time the instrument sounded the two women had got well past the stage of having to find things to say.

At the note the visitor turned her head sharply. Her half of the mugful of tea went up the inside of the mug and fell back.

“Does it hurt?” the woman asked.

“Not me.”

Out on the shelf the water came back carrying the usual small freight. The birds had stationed themselves rather badly and had to run. The youngest tripped over a wing of its own, got up with a fly on the side of its face and ate it with an expression of some surprise.

“Who, then?”

The visitor put the mug on its nail. “Would you come and listen?”

They went no farther than the upper step. The visitor knelt and laid the backs of her fingers on the pink stone. The woman did the same. For a while all she felt was the disagreeable hammering of her own blood in her knuckles. Then she felt, far down, a fine repeated catch, such as one feels on the handle of an auger just before the bit breaks through. It stopped. She took her hand away.

Under the rim of the upper step a wedge of iron had come into view. It had been driven there in cutting the step, a long time ago, when the shelf was not much wider than the shelter. All the part one could see would have gone under a postage stamp. The mountain held its blunt end fast. The other end went out into the pink.

“It wants drawing,” the woman said.

“Yes. I thought at first it wanted holding still.”

Along the grain of the new stone she could now make out a line. It ran from the iron toward the rush bed and was lost among the little white cups. A fly stood on it, cleaning one of its red-veined wings with a hind leg. When the wing was clean the fly moved along the line and did the other.

“Can you draw it?”

“I can. I don't know when.”

They waited through another note. This time the woman watched the line. Just before the water turned, it shut so neatly she could not have laid a hair along it. Then it opened again, and for a moment held a glimmer of green from the rushes.

“Not at the note,” she said.

“No.”

It was late. The visitor got up. She went a few paces down the path and was no longer on it. The woman stayed to bore her hole, which she did with a great deal of unnecessary care, being disinclined to go down three wet steps in the dusk and find out whether she still had to manage them sideways. As it happened, she did.

For the next fortnight she watched. She watched in wind, in sun, and on a day so thick that the instrument consisted of three white feet with nothing above them. The line opened a little in a wind and a little more in the pause when the swimmers had finished barking and the flies were still up. But there was always a pull on the iron. She could feel it now with a thumbnail.

It was a grey creature that gave her the time. She had been watching one carry its lid from a shallow cup to a deeper one. It went the whole distance backward, with the lid held up in front of it, and at the line it stopped. The lid was too broad to let it feel for the far side. It stood there so long that the woman, who knew perfectly well it could go round, began to itch in the palms. Then a warm bead rose beside the iron. The stone on either side of the line came level. The creature backed across without so much as tipping its lid.

The bead rolled away. The two sides settled at different heights again.

The woman put her hand on the step. “Now I know what to look for,” she said.

“I'll take this end, then,” said the visitor, beside her.

It took most of the afternoon for another bead to come up at just that place. The woman had time to eat, to dry the mug, and to reconsider twice whether she had seen what she thought she had seen. When the dark spot appeared she set one finger beside it. The skin gathered under her fingertip.

“Now.”

There was no great wrench. The visitor drew out a length of black iron as easily as the woman drew an auger from its stocking. It was much longer than either of them had expected. They had to shift their knees to let it pass. At the narrow end it was bright from rubbing, and on the very point there clung one of the minute pink collars, complete and perfectly round.

The line stayed. At the next note water welled up through it in half a dozen places. The backward flood ran on as usual, and the shelf set, and beyond its stiffened edge the next clear lip began. Only at the steps was there any appreciable difference. The upper one had moved outward by the width of the iron. In the gap between the two was a twist tie the woman remembered losing sight of some years before. She hooked it out. A black rush seed went down into the space it had left.

They carried the iron down between them. At the three wet steps the visitor took the weight of it and the woman went sideways as usual. At the village the smith gave them the price of the metal, which was enough for two hot rolls with butter. The visitor had learned something at lunch. She began at a corner.

After that she came up occasionally. She had a particular liking for the little spiders that went courting, and could distinguish several of them from below by the different ways they had of putting a fly through a floor. She did not touch the instrument. Once, when a young bird got a foot deep into one of the underslung rooms, the woman saw her lean forward; but the room gave, as it was made to give, and the foot came out with no worse loss on either side than a scrap of rush and some dignity.

In the spring there were two leaves under the bench. They were still too pale. The woman considered moving it a hand's breadth, then went down on her knees instead and sighted from the leaves to the cleared sky at the edge of the shelter. She got out the narrowest of her augers. This hole went in on a slant. Nobody looking at the record would mistake it for a drip.

For perhaps a quarter of an hour toward evening a small oval of sun now travelled along the brown ridge. The leaves got the last of it. The rest fell on a place where for the present nothing grew, and where, one fine day, the woman looking through the hole saw a pair of broad, interested eyes looking back.
