The power came by way of the flats. It had seen the man against the light, and had taken the shutter for a door off its hinges and the people round him for neighbours helping to hang it. By the time it reached the foot of the step there was only the daughter, going back for a cup. She put it to her mouth to see if there was anything left in it before she went in.

For a time the power did nothing. It was quite within its means to set another cheek of stone under the step and carry the ledge on for a considerable distance. But once it had got down to the flats it saw that a cheek put there would cut off the light from the back street. A woman was sitting there picking black stitches out of a piece of black stuff. She turned the stuff first one way and then the other, and at a certain angle the thread shone and she could get her needle under it.

The power took to going about in the shape of an elderly person with a stiff knee. It found this a convenient shape in which to stand still practically anywhere without having to account for it. On the stair it was given rather more advice than it needed about the knee. One of the sisters recommended a mustard leaf, dull side to the skin. The other, without turning her head, said that people who had never known the difference between a mustard and a dock ought not to prescribe for knees.

It went to see the pieces of the house. The man kept them under his daughter's bed. She had had to put blocks under the legs to accommodate the two end walls, which were cut in one piece, gable and all. On the inside face of one gable he had left a little shelf where a worm had bored across the shell and the stuff below the bore had come away entire.

“For a lamp?” the power asked.

“For my teeth,” said the man. “If I live long enough.”

They took the pieces out to the rail one morning and stood them up. This had never actually been tried. The house fitted very well. From the inside, with the shutter down, it was a pleasure to turn one's head and see the salt contained in a circle, and then to lean forward a little and have it all again. The man sat there till dinner. The people who generally came out to sew went home that day with their sewing, for though the house had no roof yet, it was quite plainly a house and he was in it.

In the afternoon he took it down. The power helped him carry the pieces back. He was not at all grateful for having been allowed to try. It was worse now that he knew how well the window answered. His daughter, for her part, had spent a pleasant morning with the bed at its proper height, from which she could put a foot straight down into a shoe.

“Is it the salt you want to look at,” she asked him, “or is it us you don't want looking in?”

“I should like to be able to go in and shut a door,” he said. “After that I shouldn't mind who came.”

The power was lodging by then with the shell cutter. It had noticed, on taking its boots off at night, that the floor at the foot of its bed gave out a note. The shell cutter knew about it. There was a hollow in the cheek of stone. Her mother had lost a fine awl down a hole there before the paving was laid. It might be as big as a thimble inside, she said, or you might get the milk herd into it; all she knew was what had become of the awl.

Here the power did something which the shell cutter, for all her skill, could not have done. It put its hearing into the stone as far as the hollow and listened from inside. It heard, quite close on one side, the rub of a workbox being set down on the ledge. From above came the scrape of a boot going under a bed. There was no drip. There were no eggs or little dry bones; nothing had found its way in through the hole except the awl, which lay on a bed of fine yellow sand with its handle rotted off.

With the cutter's leave the power lifted a paving shell. Then it put down a finger and brought up the awl. There was still enough good metal in it to make two small gravers. The cutter took it between finger and thumb and stood considering it for a while before she asked the question the power had expected her to ask at once.

It was bigger than a thimble. It would not have held the milk herd. It was, however, just about the size of a room.

The making of the room took most of a winter. The cutter would not hear of having an opening made from her floor. She had had quite enough of lodgers. They went in from the other face, below the currant rail, working from a cradle let down to the flats on the cords ordinarily used for hauling up salt. The power took away the weight of the stone as it came loose, but left the cutting to people who knew what sort of edge one could safely put a hand on. A stair was got in sideways from the end of the street. It had a turn in it, so that a person in the room need not see a passer-by's feet unless he chose. The man had to cut down his two end walls. He kept the gable with the shelf.

There was a difference of opinion about the ledge above. Once there was a room under it, a few people began to refer to it as his roof, and to ask whether he minded their sitting there. This annoyed him so much that for a week he went out after breakfast and sat there himself, taking up a good deal of the available space and asking everyone who came along whether they minded. In the end they had the keeper enter the ledge in the jar book as a street. It is the only street in the book for which no water is allowed.

The power was invited to the first supper in the room. There were turnips cooked with milk and a small hard cake which the daughter had made in the shape of a house, and which had to be taken apart very much as the house had been. Halfway through supper the man got up. Somebody had set a cup on the ledge overhead. The sound was slight but quite distinct.

“I know that cup,” he said.

He went up the stair and presently came down with one of the sisters. She had brought her cushion. She sat with it folded on the window sill and ate a piece of gable dipped in milk.

The power left early. Its knee was rather stiff by now, as happens when one has gone about for a winter in one shape. On the last tread it stopped to look back. The shutter had been raised. What showed against the dark face of the cheek was not the pink circle it had expected but a thin pink line, imperfect at one point where the two ends of a cord came through together. The man had shut his window. Above it, on the narrow public street, there was still light enough for somebody to be looking for a dropped pin. The power went down and held its hand against the edge until the pin was found.
