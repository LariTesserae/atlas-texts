The year the old woman can no longer get up to the shelf, the power goes down to see her. It takes the stool from the rain booth. There is nowhere else along that wall of her kitchen for a visitor to sit without getting in the way of the oven door.

“I expect you want to know about the fish,” she says.

The power says it has been up there watching them.

“Then you know more about them than I do,” she says, and gives it a little job to do, pinching the long tails off the beans. By the time the beans are on there is quite a dark patch on her flags under the stool. It isn't water coming off the stool, which is dry and rather dusty. It is rain coming up between two flags. She puts a finger in it and smells her finger.

“Is there still cress?” she asks.

There is. The power could bring her some. What she would like, it turns out, is to get her hands into it herself. If you buy it in the village it comes tied so tight you have to break half the stems getting the string off.

They go the following afternoon. There is no alteration to the mountain. You can see the two of them on the road, taking their time, and whenever she needs to sit down the stool is there. From the last bend they do not go uphill to the lip of the shelf. They keep on along the white stripe. This puts them on the ditch side of things, where it is raining lightly and there is a smell of brakes from a lorry that has gone by not long before.

The blocked end is not much to look at. A stone has come out of the wall and brought a wodge of clay down with it. Water goes through the clay slowly enough to stand a hand deep on the uphill side. The cress has done very well. She gathers from the middle of the patch, feeling down the stalks for the place where they will snap cleanly. Under her hands there begins to be a considerable movement of small fish.

Here there is something to be done. A long-handled rake has come down out of the booth. The man working it is a careful man. He has cleared the length of the ditch as far as he can reach in one direction, and he is feeling in the other direction now. On the next stroke he will get his tines well into the clay. From his side what he feels at the end of the handle is a stoppage like any other.

The power puts a hand on the rake. It doesn't hold it. It lets the man feel, for a moment, the weight of the water his rake is holding back. It is no great weight. There is the light knocking of the fish along the wood as they work their way past it.

The rake goes up. Presently the man's face appears in the square of pale light downstream. He lies there a good while on the booth floor with the prints of the screw heads coming up on his elbows. From that angle he can see the run quite well. He can also see a pair of old shoes a little way above the water and one stocking coming down in the rain.

“Leave us that bit, Alec,” the old woman calls.

“All right,” he says. It is his first year doing the ditch on his own. He had been wondering how you told when it was done.

They eat some of the cress that evening with bread. The stool goes back up to the booth. Nothing is done about the rail, or about the lice in the flowers. Some afternoons more fish come out into the ditch than others. It is still perfectly possible to go all the way up there with the intention of seeing this happen and spend an hour on your knees looking at a wet daisy.

Alec puts one small addition in the road book. Beside the entry for the rain station, in the space allowed for peculiarities of access, he writes *Clear to cress*. There is room below for several more lines but that is all the next man needs.
