It was the weight of the brass, rather than anything peculiar to the metal, that brought the salt up. The daughter tried it the next autumn with a stone of about the same weight. She had tied a bit of red thread round the stone so as not to lose it. At the end of dinner there was a crescent under the stone and nothing under the thread, though the thread had lain on the lane for precisely as long.

She left the stone there. In the course of the year its weight was taken up so completely by the bearing that very little salt came to the edge. By the following autumn there was less to eat under it than on either side. One of the animals had attached itself to the thread and drawn its tooth across a good many strands to no purpose. She unwound it, rolled the lip of its foot very gently off the last strand with her thumbnail, and put the thread in her pocket.

There was a power by then that had taken an interest in the place. It could have gone through the basalt without disturbing the fit of the three chips in the street. It had felt the little pulls on the thread, but had not known until the child took it off whether the animal was feeding well or badly. In matters of this size it had quite a lot to learn.

For a winter it kept weight on a short piece of the lane, a little way out from the nearer wheel notch. It never held the weight still. Where it felt the tooth at work it bore down a little farther along, easing off behind, at about the speed at which a person might draw a towel through the hand that was hemming it. The food came up very well. The trouble was that an animal would follow it on one foot with the other three up at its mouth. Even when it had had enough and ought to have been finding a place to fasten on for the night, it felt the fresh deposit a little way ahead and let go again. One morning the power had to catch one by the chin. It set it down in the notch and took all the weight off the lane.

The mountain carried quite a load of snow that spring. In a thaw, a piece the size of a house would lose its hold on the upper rock and come down past the underside of the harbor in perfect silence, turning over once or twice before it got far enough down to make a sound. Snow stuck to the harbor itself came off much less readily. The silver skin would often have a whole afternoon's fall hanging from it after the rock was bare.

On the next such afternoon the power made a bearing underneath the quay. It made no alteration to the cart lane. The new bearing began under the stone between the two old wheel notches and extended out a little, with a turn at the end at which it came up almost level with the floor of one notch. What it took was the weight of the snow that stuck to a patch of silver about as big as a spread hand. On a still day it took nothing. In a good updraft it took perhaps as much as the brass cover of a wheel. The power had judged this last rather carefully.

After a week it was possible to tell where the turn was by the dust on it. The animals found it in the ordinary course of grazing along the stone. There were days of plenty followed by days on which they had to go about and look. A young one that rolled off at the turn went face first into the side of the notch, a drop of somewhat less than the length of its head. This happened several times to what appeared to be the same young one. There was nothing much to be done about that.

The power went elsewhere before the autumn cart came in. It left the split shutter as it was. In the bath it had once put down something like a hand, to feel what it was that the people of the street had liked; but the current from that was long since gone.

The daughter was tall enough now to bring the cart the last few yards in, though her father kept his foot in the brake loop. She felt the new bearing through the rim of the near wheel. After they had unharnessed she went back and put her palm to it. It troubled her a little that it should seem to be carrying a load, when between her face and the far end of it there was absolutely nothing. Then a tuft of snow blew loose from underneath and she felt the three lines in her palm go slack, one after the other.

Her father had gone down to the court. When he came back she was having her dinner on the quay with her coat unbuttoned and the wheel cover still on the wheel. He asked her if there were any this year.

She shifted a little to make room. In the nearer notch a white animal had got itself settled with its heavy chin on the stone and all four feet drawn up underneath it. There was food not half its length away. For once they could see quite well, because of the light coming back off the snow. They finished their dinner there without hearing a sound.
