It was the fitter’s waiting that first drew the power’s attention. At the other six plates he gave the screw its last half turn and then took his hand away. At the seventh he waited with his palm on the butt of the screwdriver. He could not have said how long he waited. In fact it was very nearly the time it takes a pull to go round the washer. He had once been asked by a boy what he was waiting for and had put the boy’s hand in the place of his own. The boy had felt nothing at first. Then he had said, ‘Oh, that.’

The power had the advantage over the boy of being able to attend to both ends of the screwdriver at once. It could follow the tremor down through the screw to the place where the thread of ice ended, and out along a strand of wet feet; it could also feel, in the fitter’s palm, the old break in a little bone which made that particular tremor quite distinct to him. The break had been badly set when he was nine. It gave him trouble in cold weather. There was no provision in the tower drawings for either the mites or the break.

At first the power did a small thing which turned out not to be especially useful. There had been no cloud at the gantry for eleven days. When next a finger came to the seven, the power kept the air still for a little while after the finger had gone. All the animals on the wool had time to drink. But they met the returning fan too far down the twist, where ordinarily only two or three would be caught by it. Some of the full collars rubbed against the wool. A considerable quantity of salt was left in the wrong place. It was fetched away in due course by the smaller mouths. No great harm was done, but the power let the next finger make its own weather.

For a time it did nothing more than attend. It discovered, among other things, that the gold rib had not been lost. It had worked into the pile crosswise and formed the lintel of a room. Each animal going in under it rubbed the fluting a little brighter. It also heard the heads of the bubble worms knock. The sound had scarcely any duration. There were generally two knocks, since both worms, on meeting, backed away about the same distance and tried again.

In the office at the foot of the tower there was a drawer of new number plates. They were to replace the stamped ones at the next long stoppage. The figures on them were black enamel on solid nickel, a considerable improvement as far as legibility went. There was a paper band round the stack, on which somebody had written that they were interchangeable with the old type. This was true of the screw holes.

Here there seemed to be something for the power to do. It considered making a flaw in the enamel of the new seven. But a flawed plate would merely be sent back to stores. It considered letting the fitter see the six feet moving behind their windows. He would certainly take care; he might also, out of the very same care, try to get the fibre into a matchbox. The power had seen enough of the buried end to know how much of a country one would have to put into the box along with it.

On the day of the stoppage, therefore, it did no more than turn over the old paper band after the fitter had slipped it off the stack. On the underside was a drawing in pencil. A man in stores, years before, had made a rubbing of an old plate to show where the holes went. The screw holes were two little white circles in the graphite. The seven was white too.

The fitter put the new seven over the rubbing. He looked through the screw holes, then took it off and looked at the white figure. Up on the gantry he put his thumb over the stroke as he had often done before, only this time, instead of feeling for the water, he felt for the draught. He left the old plate where it was. In the book downstairs he entered, against the upper condenser, **Keep existing plate. Opening required.** There was space for a reason, but he had six more bearings to attend to and left it blank.

That entry will not last for ever. The book is kept on a shelf under a pipe, and the pipe sweats in summer. For the present, though, a new fitter reading down the page comes to the seventh line and puts the solid plate back in the drawer.

The power has not made a standing arrangement about the clouds. There are still afternoons when only the first animal gets its collar full. It has, however, acquired a habit of listening at the buried end when the man oils the bearing. Before the comb starts again there is a moment of perfect quiet there. In that moment the amber lintel, relieved of the weight of the water, lifts a little in the wool. The animals coming and going underneath it need not stoop.
