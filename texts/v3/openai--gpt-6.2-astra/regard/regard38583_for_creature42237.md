What the power did was to put a small run of water in a place where nobody would think of stopping it.

There was an old mooring ring on the harbour face of the sill. The lightermen did not use it. A lighter riding empty had its deck too high for a man to reach the ring, and one riding full had the ring under its rubbing strake. Behind the plate that held it there was a space about as big as the crown of a hat. On the street side of the sill there was another such space, washed out round the foot of the last pier. For all the years the pumps had been running there had been the thickness of a man's palm between the two.

The power took away that much. It left the roof up and the bottom rough. At low water a little fresh water went out under the plate. At high water the salt came in. Neither made any difference that could be read against the pencil line. A piece of red wool caught on the mooring ring would lie now on one side of it and now on the other, and in the slack between would hang down very straight, with a clear bead of air in the fray at the end.

For two years the principal use of the new way was made by a shore crab with a barnacle over one eye. It sat under the plate and took what came. The eel went on living in the fill. There was no call laid on it to go anywhere.

Then on a wet night at the end of October the attendant came down with his coat over his head. There was a leak in the roof of the receiving bay, and somebody had put a bucket under it on the grating, where it did no good at all. He could hear the bucket from halfway down the steps. It had a mean tin sound quite unlike the note of the switch box. He was considering whether it was worth climbing back to move it when he saw the eel clear of the stones.

It was lying the length of the kerb, in the ordinary depth of water, with its head towards the bars. The eyes showed up very well. He had been looking at that head off and on for years and could not have said where the eyes were, exactly, in relation to the tubes. Now they were the first thing he saw. There was a little cold light along the lower jaw too, such as you get on the edge of a spoon when you turn it over in a dark sink.

He sat down on the sound tread. In a while he felt the pulse through his trousers change sides. The eel went on lying there. Far overhead a man picked up the bucket, said something about the weight of it, and emptied it through the grating.

That put the eel into motion. It did not travel the sixty paces in anything like a straight line. It tried a joint under one door number, went back nearly a third of the way to try another, and lay for some time with the middle of its body round a pier and the two ends out of sight. Once the attendant thought it was coming back to the tile. Then something pale turned over down by the bars. He went as far as he could on the kerb and put a hand against the concrete. From there he saw the nick in the tail slipping under the foot of the pier. After that he saw a button crab come out in a hurry, moving as usual sideways but with no particular side in the lead.

On the other face of the sill the tide was low. The eel came out behind the ring and lay a moment in water that was rather too fresh on one cheek and quite salt on the other. A lighter moved against its fenders. Underneath it was a depth of soft black mud with a few worm mouths standing open in it. Beyond the lighter there was more depth and a set to the water that could be felt along the whole length of the skin. The eel went that way.

The attendant, when he got above, took the bucket off the grating and set it on the asphalt a foot to the left, directly under the leak. He waited for three drops to make sure. He could see the mooring ring from the end of the bay, but by the time he got there there was nothing to be seen except the wool, drawn out fine in the last of the falling tide.

He did not tell the new man that the eel had gone. He had only seen it go under a pier. When they let the water up for inspection he still told him to mind his boots.

Under the tile the sand kept the shape of the chin for the better part of a day. A prawn got into the hollow and stood there working its small hands. By morning the water from the pipe had combed the place level.
