The power first noticed the knock. There was one in the upper country and one in the lower, and for a long time it took the second for an echo of the first. Then one afternoon the hand at the fallen chimney lay over on its side to ease a kink out of its ribbon. All the weights lay still. Out of the well came the second little knock, as punctual as ever.

It would have been an easy matter to draw the fields together. The power could have done it with rather less effort than a hand expends in getting over a sill. But there were things out in the bright interval. Looking across it instead of down, one could see long skeins of creatures going east on the draught from the lower chimneys. They went slack at the approach of a train and drew themselves up into almost invisible knots. The power let two more trains go by. It saw them tighten in good time for each. There was no knowing what notice they would have of a movement of the fields made by anything other than a train.

On the third afternoon it went down into the workings.

The trouble was local. A siding beyond the fallen chimney had been kept to accommodate a stand of silver moss that grew nowhere else in the furrow. The bank parted as usual to let the wagons through, but it put them back on the runner a little further on each year. The effect on the length of a day's haul was too small to show on any switchboard. At the well it amounted to the width of a door, with both trains going at a fair pace. The old hand on the rim was coming as close to the one below as two people passing on opposite sides of a door. Both had taken to letting out an extra pleat. This nearly did it, which was perhaps why they kept coming back.

In a rack six galleries down there was a folded length of track with its black ends plugged with white buds. The power took it out. The thumb notch was on the wrong side for the use to which the power meant to put it, and for the first time in a great many years there was a thumb on the other side of a coupling, feeling rather foolishly about for the hollow.

It made a loop above the siding, out on the bare felt clear of the moss. It did not splice it in. It laid the two ends alongside the runner, where the bank could take them up or not, as it did with the elbows of old corridors. For a whole morning the loop lay pale at the joints. A parcel hand no bigger than a pear got out onto it, found no nap and got off again. Then the west front came through. When the blades had turned and there was light on the loop again, the buds were gone. The next load of backing felt took the long way round.

At the well the two fields came snug in the ordinary manner. The old hand was not there. It was halfway over a sill at the time, with its forward ends already down on the gallery floor and a disposition to sing coming over it that it did nothing to resist. The power, which had been waiting at the well with considerable interest, heard the note come up through the galleries. After a moment a door answered. 

It was eleven days before there was a hand on either rim at the proper time. By then the power had learnt to watch from the top of the bank. One could see very little of what happened at the well from there. What one could see was the shadow of a wagon go out in a fine new curve over the felt, and the skeins in the interval gather themselves up, and presently the old hand coming home on two ends, making much better time than usual. There was nothing knocking behind it. It went straight past the first sill and over the second, and was asleep on the third before the last of the daylight left the wall.

The power stayed until evening. Two of the loose beads rolled together as they opened, so that the little animals issuing from them got hold of the wrong ones. This seemed to make no difference. One went down and the other stayed up. For a while a minute cream end kept appearing over the lip from below, feeling about for a hold, until the bank put out a bit of pile for it to catch on. The power had been on the point of putting a finger there.

It did not go round the country setting right the other wells. At one there was a balk in the runner, and that it eased out. At another the lower field had gone dark for reasons of its own. It left that one dark. It took to travelling on the trains, usually with the new doors, and acquired a good ear for a loose hinge. In time it could tell the rattle of one from the knock of a hollow bead even when both were going on together under the same petal in a high wind.

Beside the fallen chimney the old hand still goes down to the well. Its new weights are cloudy amber all through; it will be a long while before one can see a blade of moss in any of them. Where its hollow bead used to come bumping along there is now a bare soft end, a little lighter in colour than the rest of the ribbon. The moth tried twice to settle there and was tipped off both times. It sleeps these days in the thumb notch of the new coupling. At midday the track gives a little under the train and turns it out, quite pink and cross, into the light.
