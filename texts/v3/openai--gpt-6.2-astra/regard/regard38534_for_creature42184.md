There was one crossing for which Bette could remember neither the fare nor the change. A woman had come aboard at the last minute with a suit on a hanger, holding it at arm’s length over her head. The ferry was crowded and the suit had evidently just been pressed. Bette had held the hanger while the woman got her purse out, and all down the strait she had watched the two empty trouser legs walk a little way apart and come together again. She had never found out what the suit was for. It was one of the things she would have liked another crossing to find out.

There is something in the strait that can give you another crossing. It is quite capable of putting the water back up to the red line. It remembers the pressure of the ferry on its back, and the curious lightness of the return trip on mornings when the shipyard whistle had blown. For it the difference between then and now is rather like the difference between the two sides of a leaf. Either side is within reach.

It had been aware of Bette for a long time as a spot of warmth coming and going along its upper margin. Lately the spot had begun to stay still. A small quantity of water would come down from above, already warm, with an anemone in it, and afterwards the warmth of the two hands would persist on the coping. There came an evening when it followed her home.

It went by way of the bowl. There is always a little water left under the turned foot of a bowl, however briskly you empty it. On the way it got a good view of the ground at the height of Bette’s knee. It had known the place when a knee there would have been a drowning matter. Now there were dust and the husks of figs, and a dropped ball of green wool which Bette put in her pocket. At the door she changed the bowl to her other hand so as not to knock it on a bicycle bell. All these adjustments interested it. It had never had to get anywhere by the use of two sides that took turns.

It stayed on the draining board that night. At some distance through the house a man was trying, with long intervals between attempts, to play the opening of a tune on a comb. The thing in the strait listened to hear whether he would get it. Presently a small hard object rattled in the sink, went round twice, and settled over the hole.

In the morning Bette found a crab there and carried it to the spare room for Lena. By the time they had established its color, found the pencil and moved the tally on by one, the strait had given up the idea of another crossing. It had known about the houses, of course. It had not quite understood that to put the ferry back, even for the length of one trip, it would have to interrupt so many things that were partway through.

It went out with them instead. Lena carried the stool by a different leg every twenty paces, to give each leg a rest. When they got to the new land there were more anemones than usual. Bette attended to the ones in the sun first. Some came away readily; others had their feet so far down in the clay that she had to work a forefinger round them and bring up a plug of earth. The little girl ferried the empty bowl back from the wall. She was not allowed to carry it the other way. They went on until the school bell rang and then Bette had to take her in, with a print of the seat of the stool on the back of one bare thigh.

By noon the ones they had not reached were dry. There were little rings where they had been, like the marks left by glasses on a table. The strait lay offshore considering these. It could have lifted the whole edge in a wave, but there was a boy asleep against the warm side of the wall, his cap well down over his nose. Besides, a wave goes back again.

What it did in the end was very slight. Along the next strip that rose, and only where the clay was still soft, it left the tracks of its smallest currents a little deeper. They were scarcely wider than the channels you make with a stick. The last water to leave the new ground ran out through them, taking a loose shell here, a little silt there, until what was left was a succession of pools held together by wet threads. At high water the threads ran the other way.

Bette saw the good of it at once. She had been carrying things half a mile that now need only be carried to the end of her reach. Better still, a thing with a good hold on the bottom could be left where it was. There were shrimps in the pools within the week. The school put out a notice about not walking in them, which was observed very well by the children and not at all by the gulls.

For several mornings the strait watched Bette go by with the bowl under her arm. It was a little disappointed. It had supposed it was giving her the use of that arm again; there was a stiffness in the shoulder now that it could feel from well out beyond the buoys. Then it noticed that she was carrying her breakfast in the bowl, bread and half a copper fig under a twist of paper. She had always eaten before setting out, standing up in the kitchen while there was still almost no light. Now she ate at the wall. Sometimes the girl came too, in which case there was a whole fig and Bette was particular about the division.

By the end of the summer the channels were taken for granted. One of the new streets had to bend to miss a pool. Its two rows of houses looked into one another’s end windows, a thing not provided for in the plans, and a pair of neighbors who both did a good deal of sewing had a narrow board fitted between their sills so they could lend the iron without going down. In winter they took the board in. The strait, which had once been inclined to think of streets as places where it could no longer go, liked the bend very much.

That winter Lena found the sixpence. She sucked it clean and took it to the sink, where Bette was washing the pudding bowl, and asked what it would buy. Bette dried her fingers and laid the coin on the side of her forefinger, steadying it with her thumb. Something in the old movement brought back the feel of a wire hook riding on the joint of her wrist and the woman fishing in her purse with both hands free at last.

“I believe,” said Bette, somewhat to her own surprise, “it would have got the two of us across.”

Lena put it away against the possibility.
