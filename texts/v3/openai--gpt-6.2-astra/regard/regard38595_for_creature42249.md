The first thing the power did was to put an ear to the glass. This was not quite so simple as putting a bill to it. The power had to leave off hearing elsewhere. There was rain going into a lake in the older country, and for twenty breaths the rain went in unattended.

What it heard was a great many things finishing. A drop finished its journey down a tooth; a root drank the drop; the skin of a pear gave way under the pad of a browser’s tongue. A long way down, a pair of wheels were coming to the last of the seam that had grown them. One had already lost a tooth. Its fellow went on turning through the empty place, and it was this interval, rather than any noise in it, that brought the grey heads up all along the ridge.

The power very nearly put the tooth back. It could feel the shape of it as plainly as one feels with the tongue the shape of a tooth no longer in the mouth. But the syrup was already running through the gap. Three levels down it had loosened a mass of old glass, and the glass was coming away in a long curl with a hollow in its face. In the hollow were the black beginnings of mud. It would have been a pity to lose the pool.

There was, however, a bird in some difficulty.

It was a young one, quite ready to come out. Its blister had travelled past a recumbent herd five times. Five times a foot had come out, caught in the belly hair and pulled. The head had got as far as the eyes. Then the foot had let go. From outside there appeared to be nothing the matter with the hole. It was round and handsomely polished; the rasper had done rather better than usual by it. The trouble was at the back. A thread of glass, finer than a fern hair, ran from the wall through the loose web at the bend of the bird’s wing. The wing had grown with the thread in it. At rest the bird felt nothing. When it pulled, its wing opened behind it in the blister like a little grey fan.

Its mother still came to feed it. She was getting old. There was a bare place under her chin where one could see the water she carried moving like a bead of quicksilver. When she had fed the youngster she generally remained a little while with her bill in the hole. The youngster felt the underside of the bill with its tongue. Neither of them could reach the thread.

The power took the weight of a gnat off the surface of a pool.

A fish rose. For an instant the bright country lay open, not only in the water but in several hundred empty blisters along the cliff. A little of its sunshine fell through a clean blister, through the drop in which a yellow rasper was asleep, and onto the thread. One could have put all the warmth employed on the eyelid of the bird without waking it. The thread drew up into two green beads. The wing settled against the bird’s side. The gnat, which had got its wings dry, went off among the ferns.

There was no herd within reach that evening. The power found this rather hard to bear. It could see a strip feeding out on the far side of the grove and knew exactly where to press to bring the two abreast. Instead it stayed by the hole. In the night the bird stretched, discovered a space it had not known of under its wing, and spent a long time getting its bill about in there, putting the feathers to rights.

Toward morning something brushed the stone. The mauve foot went out and came back with a piece of fern. A little later it went out again. This time it closed on hair.

For several days afterward there was a grey bird riding backward under a browser with a green bead in the web of its wing. The bead was no inconvenience. In certain lights it threw a round green spot on the belly of the animal, which the other young birds tried to pick off. When the herd lay down, an old bird had to hold its head to the stone like anybody else’s.

The power went back to the lake. There had been a considerable fall of rain. In the shallows the grass blades stood each in its own clear sleeve of water, and out on the lake the rings were so thick together that there was scarcely an inch of level surface left. It listened there for a while.

Up in the glass the empty blister went on its way. Its rasper came in and cleaned it, working carefully round the small green bead on the wall. When the light was right one could look through it at a long, easy slope running down to a lake. Usually there was nothing much to see. Once there was a shower ending, the near half of the water quite smooth already, the far half still dark with rain.
