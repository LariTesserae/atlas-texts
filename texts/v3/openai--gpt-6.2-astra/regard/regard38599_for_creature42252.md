It was the noise that attracted the attention of the power. At the distance from which it first heard the place there was no separating the three notes; they made a single rather pleasant sound, considerably fainter than some others in that quarter. Coming closer, it was surprised to find people living in it. Closer still, it found the girl doing something which it could not do. It could attend to the quiet or to the sound, but it had no ankles to lean from, and could not by its own efforts arrive at her particular mixture of the two. It stayed with her until she had finished. This was quite a long time. Her father won a small wager which her mother had not known they were making.

The power could have taken away the noise. There would have been no difficulty about leaving the air to drive the turbines. It had done rather more curious things than that. But already it had seen one use for the noise, and on the way down to the fourth street it saw a man at a lathe put the end of a wooden rod against a bearing and the other end against the little bone in front of his ear. He was listening through the noise, and knew what to listen for. The power began to have some notion of how long it would take to go round the town finding out what it ought to leave alone.

It went home with the girl. That evening she stood the saucer on the table and sat with her chin on her arms looking at it. There had been a change at the chip. What was ordinarily a single edge was now two, with a space between them the shape of a grain of rye. While she watched, the space shut up. She got a pencil and drew what she had seen on the inside of a laundry wrapper. Then her mother wanted the table.

The power was interested in the hem. From where it was, it could follow one of the small folds all the way round the saucer, including the part at the back which the girl had missed. By a further adjustment it could follow what happened inside the fold. It spent the night at this. In the morning it knew what a dry spill was like to a hem, which was not in the least what a dry spill was like to anything else. It also knew the difference, quite appreciable to the hem, between the two sides of the chip.

For the next few days the power did very little. It watched the girl take her turn at the laundry. In the course of a morning seven hundred packets went past her; she picked out two. One was quite all right. In the other the projecting thread was so short that even after she had put the packet aside she had trouble finding it again to show the woman at the belt. On the strength of this she was asked to come back the following week. Going home, she bought a pencil of the sort that writes on glass. She put two marks on her saucer, one at each end of the place where the hem had divided. Nothing of the sort had happened there since, but now she would know where to look.

It was about this time that the power noticed a length of hem on the underside of a gutter which was due to be replaced. The new gutter was already up, hung on short stays outside the old one; on Friday the fitters would draw the stays in, and the old gutter, which was in eight-foot sections, would go down to the furnace. The hem had grown along it for about the length of three houses. At two places it had gone up under the paint and come out again. There was no knowing how long that had taken.

On Thursday night the power brought a drop of water to the point where the two gutters came nearest together. It let the drop increase until it touched both pieces of metal. This made a sort of neck, very narrow in the middle, with a free surface all the way round. The hem went a little way out on it and stopped.

The power waited. It could feel the water wanting to come apart and had to give some attention to keeping it as it was. Towards morning a fine edge appeared on the farther gutter. By the time the men came with their spanners there was a piece as big as a thumbnail on the new metal. Most of the hem was still on the old. The power had thought the rest would follow, as a person follows a hand already laid on the banister. Apparently this was not the arrangement. It let go of the drop when the first man took hold of the gutter.

On the Friday after that, the girl, coming up from the laundry by the outside stair, saw a fitter sitting on a stage with his lunch. He was rubbing the underside of the new gutter with one finger.

‘There's a hem there,’ she told him, leaning out a little. ‘You'll never feel it like that.’

He said he knew there was a hem there. What he was feeling for was the burr on the joint. He moved his lunch tin so that she could get past. Then it occurred to him to ask how you *would* feel it.

The next time there was a gutter to be done the men put the new one up touching the old, with a bit of wet lampwick squeezed between. This had its inconveniences; the wick tended to get into the bolt holes. After a few trials they used a strip of the stuff the laundry people cut off the sheets when they turned the sides to the middle. You could pull it out by one end. The girl tried a piece between her saucer and a tumbler and found that it worked very well. She had two hems now, or a hem in two dishes. On the laundry wrapper, underneath her drawing, she put down the time it had taken.

The power was by then a good way out along the line. It had not quite got over the business of the gutter. It had been prepared to carry the whole length across, even to the two little runs under the paint; it could have put the paint back afterwards. What it had not been able to determine, in the time at its disposal, was whether this would amount to taking the hem to pieces. The small surviving patch was doing well. That was something it could tell even at a distance. It was not much help with the question.

At eleven minutes past the last station it stopped at the shower. There was indeed no hem there. The drops struck with considerable force and the tall plants went on nodding long after each impact, so that a drop falling through them seldom had the same passage down twice. The three sleepers were black with water. Sitting on the end of one, well clear of the track, was the inspector, eating a pear with his coat drawn up over his head. He had come for another look.

He finished the pear on the wet side of the shower and stepped out on the dry side to put his coat on properly. Before going on he held his empty hand out, palm upwards, to the rain. The power, which by now knew him fairly well, understood that this had nothing to do with the inspection. It gave its attention to the place where the rain crossed his wrist. For a moment, by staying quite still and letting him do the moving, it got something of what the girl had got that first evening by the tracks. Then he shook his hand and went on, and the drops went all the way down to the grass again.
