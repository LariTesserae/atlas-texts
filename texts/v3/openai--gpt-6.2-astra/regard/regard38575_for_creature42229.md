At the other end of the pipe the bell can scarcely be heard. The engine stands in a shed on piles, and even when it is not running there is the slap of water under the boards. The power that has had its cheek to the ladder all evening comes down to the shed in time to hear the note. Here it is two sounds: a thin iron tick and a much rounder sound from a tumbler left upside down on the bell years ago.

There is a board on the wall with a slot for every gauge the engine once supplied. Through one slot a brass counter shows a row of figures. The note from the summit moves the last wheel on one place. All the other counters are green in their slots. On a nail beside the board is the long key with which somebody used to wind them back to zero at the end of the month.

Under the shed the tide is well out. The intake lies in a pool of its own, among mussels so old that the points of their shells have worn through to pearl. With each stroke of the engine the pool goes down. Water comes over a sill of shingle to make up the loss. A piece of bladderwrack across the sill is by turns a dripping black rope and a fine spreading tree. At the very bottom of the tide it stays a rope for sixteen strokes together.

The engine has a sound for this. A cup leather goes up the cylinder with no weight of water on it and comes down with a slap. Far up the mountain, where nobody has ever thought to listen for the sea going out, the little new shrimp folds its fans at the slap and drops a leaf or two toward the floor.

The power stays through another tide. At high water there is depth enough over the sill to float the shed door if one were to take it off its hinges. Nothing is amiss with the engine. The grease still comes down a red worsted thread to the bearing; a spider has ventured one stay of its web on the walking beam and no farther. It is the sill that has been coming up. Stones thrown over it in winter lie where they fall. On the inside of the pool the oldest mussels stand pointing out of the gravel like the corners of buried books.

In the next ebb the power takes a stone off the sill. It is a flat one the length of a hand, with a white limpet on the under face. There is some difficulty about finding a place for it. Set down as it was, it will come down hard on the limpet. Turned over, it will leave the limpet all afternoon in the sun. In the end it goes across two stones a little farther out, white face down, with room underneath for the limpet to put out its foot.

A runnel no wider than the red thread comes through the place it has left. On the last few inches of the fall the runnel goes the wrong way. The power watches a quantity of perfectly good water go out of the pool. Then the sea turns. There is very little to see of the turn except that a skin of golden dust, brought up against a pebble, unpleats itself and goes back through the notch. For twelve strokes this time the bladderwrack hangs still. The engine slaps twice.

It takes five tides, not all of them alike. At the end there is a crooked passage through the sill that a child could stop with a heel. The water in it is never quite still. A prawn from outside walks in against the run, its long feelers laid straight back along its flanks, and spends the low water picking at the hinges of the mussels. The cup leather goes up heavy and comes down soft. The shed fills with the ordinary smell of wet boards warming.

There are larger things the power has seen how to do. With the long key it could have started a very great number of wheels. It has been inside the hollow post and knows where the copper might be drawn tight to the rim again, and how little would have to be taken off the float to let it travel the whole height of the chamber. It knows the depth to which the three green bights would be lifted if the packing were made fast. Before going back up the mountain it puts the tumbler right side up beside the sink. The bell, when it sounds, is a surprisingly sweet one.

Up at the cap the new shrimp is missing part of a fan. There is no knowing how that happened. It can keep its place quite well if it puts the short fan against a leaf and works the other in the open. The leaf swings a little under the unequal pull. On the next leaf down, the one-eyed fish lies with its mouth open and its blind cheek just clear of the copper. Water goes in at its mouth and out under the two fine doors at its throat. The beat at the top of the pipe is as it was before, except that at the very bottom of the tide there is water in one or two beats that used to carry nothing but the sound.

The power comes less often after that. A winter sea puts a stone back in the notch; a freshet takes it out. The limpet works round the end of its slab to the upper face of its own accord. When next a cheek is laid against the last rung there is quite a different shrimp on the saddle. This one puts up both hands at the shadow and holds them there a long time after the great warm face has gone away.
