There was a house farther south, beyond the last sheepfold, which had been a town. It consisted of one room now. The stairs to the lower tiers were full of grit, and the lintel over the oven mouth had come down and was lying neatly inside the oven. The room still in use had a round hole in its roof for smoke and a square one at the foot of the wall for a fox. A traveler could close the square one with a stone. It was considered civil to take the stone out again on leaving.

The woman at the stair head knew the house by report. People who had slept there spoke of it with some affection, even those who had got up in the night to put the stone in. What she had long wished to see was the approach to it. There were three ways, according to the men who brought salt, or one way, which looked different in different weathers, according to a woman who had been bringing salt a good deal longer. She had heard so much about a certain low ridge that she sometimes saw it in her sleep. She would have liked to go up to it awake and see what came next.

There had never been a very good reason why she should not go. The boys were big enough now. They could do everything except get rid of a man who wanted to tell them how to unload his goats, and at this even she was not always successful. She had good shoes, and knew half a dozen people with whom she would have been glad to walk. What she had not been able to do was to imagine coming over the last rise on her way home and finding nobody there. In her mind she was always sitting by the trough.

The power whose attention had been drawn to the plain had no difficulty in seeing the house. It could see under the fallen lintel, where a field mouse had made a store of seeds with the wings still on them. It could see all three approaches. It could not tell by looking which of them the woman would like to take.

It came to sit with her one afternoon, in the shape of an elderly person with rather tender feet. She moved along the shelf to make room. For a while they pared down the hard edge of a piece of felt to put inside one of the shoes. When the shoe had been tried on again, the stranger asked her where she had been.

“Today?”

“Any day.”

She mentioned two or three places. Then she told it about the house, taking some time over the question of the ridge. It listened. It had not known before how much of the plain could travel on the back of a goat in addition to the load.

“I should like to go there with you,” it said.

She looked down at its shoes.

“Well, not in those.”

They arranged to leave in six days. The stranger took the shoes down to the man who had made hers, and the woman told the boys. The younger one wanted to know whether they were to be paid her share as well as their own. The elder said of course they were, and on the strength of this undertook to settle with the four households about the carrying of hot drinks. By the fifth day they were getting along so well without her that she had a disagreeable afternoon. The mason, eating his lunch beside her, gave her the heel of his cake to throw at a goat that was worrying a curtain.

“Mind you look at that oven,” he said. “I can't see how a lintel comes down *inside*.”

In the morning the stranger was waiting on the white paving in a pair of good shoes, somewhat darker at the toes where it had been standing in the dew. They took bread and a skin of water apiece. At the edge of the first long reach the woman turned round. The boys had backed a goat against the shelf the wrong way about and were having to lead it away and begin again. She watched until they had done it. Then a little inequality in the stone took the whole town out of sight.

They had a good walk. The stranger knew when to talk, which was on the long easy stretches, and when to let her hear her own feet. Once it pointed out a patch of very small yellow flowers growing in the print of a sheep's foot where the hoof had broken a crust of grit. Once she stopped it from stepping on what it had taken for the shadow of a ledge. There was a considerable depth below. It thanked her without saying that a fall would not have hurt it. A good deal of the pleasure she was having lay in being responsible for the two of them, a thing which up to then she had chiefly done sitting down.

The low ridge was lower than she had expected. There were indeed three approaches, but one had been used quite recently by sheep and would have been most unpleasant in wet weather. This accounted to her satisfaction for something the salt woman had said. On the far side, which in her sleep had always been as bare as the near one, there was a hollow full of grass. She stood looking at it for so long that the stranger sat down.

“Did you know it was here?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I'm glad you didn't say.”

The house was a little below them. They ate by the oven. Afterward she lay on her back and put her head and shoulders in under the broken arch, getting her hair full of old soot. The lintel had two pivot holes. It had been a doorsill first, and it was too short for the use to which they had put it. She was pleased to have something definite to take home to the mason. There was a great deal of the afternoon left. She spent some of it doing nothing whatever on a step in the sun.

That night there was a scratching at the square hole. Half asleep, she reached down to put the stone in place and felt a light cold nose against her knuckle. She drew her hand back. The fox came in, went about its affairs at the foot of the opposite wall, and went out again. In the morning one of her shoes was missing. They found it outside, not appreciably the worse for its journey.

On the way home they talked of other things. The stranger had a question about the split basket. It wanted to know whether the woman had put the stone there for the sake of the birds or for the sake of people who might be coming up the stairs below.

“For the basket,” she said, a little surprised. “It's a good basket yet.”

From the last rise they saw someone sitting beside the trough. It was the younger boy, with a girth across his knees. He had put his stool in just the place where she put hers, since from any other place on that stretch of paving the afternoon sun came up off the white stone into one's eyes. He lifted his hand. He did not get up; he had got the end of the girth tucked under his foot to keep the work tight. She had not known, in all the time she had sat there herself, how pleasant it was to be met in this fashion.

The stranger stayed for a cake and went on down the stair in its good shoes. There were places below that it had seen very clearly and had never been to. As for the woman, she made the journey over the low ridge twice more in her life, once with her friend the mason, who took measurements, and once alone. On this last occasion she went a little beyond the house. There was no particular reason to turn back at it, except that until then it had been the place she was going to see.
