The first thing the power did was come down the steps. From a considerable height it had seen the shape of the garden and understood the use of the hollow bricks and even the arrangement by which the little carriage borrowed its motion from a descending length of cable. It had supposed the lowest terrace quite a dangerous place for a woman to sleep. At the height of a woman's cheek it discovered the cool back of the tile. It stayed there till the lorry came round the bend.

After that it was content for a while to go where Nel went. It liked the winding house, where the men knocking wedges out of packing cases had begun to knock them out from the thin end, which took rather longer but didn't split the wood. It liked the works school rather less. The boy's account of the sill had come back to him with the word *warm* struck out, on the reasonable ground that he had taken no thermometer. He had put it in again in smaller writing.

There was very little to be done about these things. The power had it in mind, however, that when Nel came to fit the long side to the box she would need someone at the other end of it. It could have held up the board. It could have held up the mountain. It wasn't sure she would care to find a board hanging in the air just where she wanted it; there would be the business afterwards of living in a room where that had happened once and might happen again. It decided to go up and ask.

Anyone who has done a job behind a shut door knows the difference between a knock that means something has gone wrong and a knock that means there is a pair of hands outside if wanted. The power made the second sort of knock. It had hands by the time she opened to it, and enough of the rest of a person not to put her to the trouble of asking where it had gone.

“From below?” said Nel.

“I've been down there.”

“Have they sent my wedges?”

“I didn't bring any.”

“Well, there's a thing you can do without wedges.”

It wasn't the long side. She had rigged a sling for that from the valve-wheel, and could raise or lower either end a very small amount, which was better than having a person tell her it was level when what they meant was that their arm was tired. She wanted to get the empty box round the turn in the path before putting in the last screws. They took a handle apiece. Behind the ginger it was necessary for the power to go backwards. At the turn she said, “Mind my tree,” and for a moment it had all it could do, with the whole range of its faculties, to place a rather large foot among the scallions without doing harm.

When the box was down she went back for the screws. The power sat on the step trying the unaccustomed sensation of a splinter. It was in the ball of the thumb. One could bring it quite distinctly into view by pressing the skin on either side of it, but as this took two fingers there was nothing left to pull it out with. Nel did it with the needle from her loose ends.

“Good handles,” it said.

“Yes,” said Nel. “I thought so.”

They worked till the bright bead stopped at the brass mouth. There was no need to fill the box all at once. Nel put in the first basket of earth because she wanted to see how it would lie, and they had tea. She drank the cup without the saucer and gave the other to the power. By the time it had finished, she had found an occasion to tell it about the heel of the blanket. They lifted the wet corner and looked. The hole was a little off the drain now, the weight of the meadow having pulled the wool downhill, but you could still see what a fit it must have been. The power, which had seen the transit of one star across another from the only point in the heavens where their edges exactly coincided, had no difficulty understanding her satisfaction.

There was one thing it did without asking. On the way out it noticed that in coming round the turn it had bent a scallion after all. It put the bend right. As for the pear, it could see a number of ways in which the matter of the pear might turn out, including one in which a hard little fruit was cut into eight nearly transparent slices with a cable knife and pronounced very good by seven people out of eight. It kept these to itself.

“Will you be up again?” said Nel. She was at the scraper, working a plug of blue wool off the visitor's heel with the toe of her shoe.

“I'd like to.”

“Bring a bit of earth, then. Not a sack. Everybody thinks it has to be a sack, and then they put off coming.”

On its next visit the power brought earth in a tobacco tin. It had taken some pains in choosing it, going by the smell, which was a pleasure about earth it had previously known very little of, having generally had to do with it in quantities sufficient to make a world. Nel knocked it out on the downhill side. A small red worm shortened itself in surprise and went in.
