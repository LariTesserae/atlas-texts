The power, when it first put a finger to the loose staple, felt a very slight pull in the direction in which the staple was already turning. It took its finger away. There had been a wind for three days from the lower country. A grain of the hard almond stuff was going round and round under the shoulder of the staple. At each turn it took off a little gray flour. The flour lay in the lee of the shoulder in a fan as white and fine as the mark made by a moth against a window.

It would have been the work of a moment to put the staple back as it had been when it was driven. There was even a mark to go by, a dark waist on each of the two shanks, now standing a little above the stone. The power waited through a shower instead. With its finger, which was still quite close, it could feel the dry wind going one way and the wet wind the other. Between them was a space scarcely wider than the edge of a coin. Here the small ferns grew. They had roots like the brown hairs on the rind of a coconut. Some went down the socket. During the nine breaths the hairs on one side were dark with water and on the other quite silver. To drive the staple home would take most of them down with it.

At the next turning of the staple the grain came out. The power caught it. It was a rather pretty grain, striped through the middle, and still angular enough to cut. After a little consideration the power put it down where the shower had left a film of mud in the bottom leaf of the cupped plant. There it lay without doing any more work until an eyelash, already trailing a considerable house, took it up and spent half the afternoon fitting it in with the stripes inward.

Removing the grain did not, of course, mend the socket. The staple went on turning. In the course of a winter it came up the thickness of a fern tip. It was the going up that mattered. By turning, it accommodated the roots; by going up, it would presently come out altogether. There was a moonless night toward the end of winter when the power held it very lightly down and felt, far down in the dark, something come up against the end of one shank. It was the back of a rasping animal. The hole had gone right through to a parting. A moment later the back withdrew and a tenant came up the hole, combs first, much too large to get out at the top. The power had meant to put a foot on the shank down there, as one puts a washer on a pin. It gave up this idea too.

What it did in the end was to stay. It took up no room to speak of. There was still room for the six people, provided two sat on the lip. In ordinary weather it let the staple alone. When an upward gust came, as happened chiefly in the moment before a hard rain struck the heated floor, it put upon the shoulder just the weight that had been lost in gray flour. This was less than the weight of a full mite. The staple turned under it and did not rise.

For some years that was all. Then a root, one of the silver ones, passed through the eye and thickened, and another passed over it in the other direction. The power tried taking off its finger in a gust. The roots held nicely. In a dead calm, too, the staple could still be turned; it gave a little rubbery squeak, quite lost at midday but audible in the evening when the males were down to single strokes. The power had acquired a liking for those strokes. There was one, well out under the lip, which invariably came in a little early and had to make up for it by waiting a long time for the answer.

It no longer had business in the hollow, but it came there sometimes. On one such visit there had been a hatch in the uppermost cup. A young hooked animal was on its way over the bare floor, carrying on its back, by a thread it had failed to bite through, part of a grit case. This made a bad business of walking. Each time it got over an almond the case lodged on the other side and presently drew it back, hindquarters first, into the hollow it had just left. There was a black lizard quite near. The power knew the grain with the stripe in it, now showing its stripe, and felt a certain responsibility. It pinched the thread in two.

The little animal went on. The lizard took it about an inch short of the fern. It stood a moment working its jaws to get the long leg in, a job for which a lizard's mouth is poorly designed. Then it went back to listening with its chin on the stone.

The power sat on the lip awhile. Beside it lay the bit of empty case, open at both ends, with the striped grain built in as firmly as a boss in the roof of a church. It did not undo the work to get the grain back. When it went away that evening the case was still there. With the wind in a particular quarter it would roll a little way, stop, and give a thin and unexpectedly agreeable whistle.
