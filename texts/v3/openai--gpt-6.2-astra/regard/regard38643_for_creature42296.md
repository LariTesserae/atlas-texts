The third bowl eventually gets a sound very unlike the one it set out to make. It is a double knock, the second part much softer than the first, owing to a little water that has worked under the foot. For three evenings running the bowl makes it, with none of the furious rocking. On the fourth evening the stapled bowl tries it too. This is worth knowing before doing anything about the third bowl.

Above the porcelain there is room to go about. The joists, which look so fine from below, are wide enough on top to have weather of their own. In the hollows where they cross, a very pure white clay has collected. Something that has the run of a great many such roofs came across that one, and stopped. A knock had come up through the clay against the part of it that took its weight. It shifted a little. There was the second knock.

By putting its weight first here and then there it could have made itself heard all over the country. It could have taken hold of a joist and tipped a whole afternoon's rain into one kiln. What it did that first afternoon was to get its weight off the roof. It had noticed a dry place moving about on the floor below, in exact correspondence with its movements. At the edge of the place a cord hung half drawn through a square.

For a long time its only intercourse with the country was through a flaw in a joist. A thread of fired clay, hardly stouter than the stem of a wineglass, ran from the lower skin of the porcelain to the upper. By touching its end very lightly one could feel the work going on below. There was a great deal of it. Some of the most promising sounds proved on further acquaintance to be the regular discharge of water from a gutter. A long, dull scraping, on the other hand, which it had taken for the settling of the roof, occurred only when two particular stretches of pavement came within reach of one another on the flood. It got to be fond of the scraping. Once, after an unusually dry interval, it felt the beginning of it and put down what it was doing on another roof a considerable distance away.

It was at the end of one of these dry intervals that it found a use for the white clay.

There was a reach of the plain where the slabs no longer rode up. Water still came out of the joints, but instead of lifting the squares it lay on them in separate lenses and went white round the edges. Underneath, the hot courses had gone on firing the same ground until the bottoms of the slabs were joined. A little kiln in the middle of the reach had made no fresh work for two seasons. There was a touch there that used to finish a square by drawing one corner out between finger and thumb, as it were, to a fine point. On wet evenings, all over that immovable floor, you could hear the points being lifted the little way they would go and let fall again.

The power above the roof could have opened the floor down the middle. There would have been heat, water, soft clay and a quantity of loose squares by the next day. But the fine points were on the old squares.

It rolled a pinch of the white clay very thin, folded it while it was still damp, and passed it through one of the holes left by the bursting of an air bubble. It came down folded, like a little book, and lay by the blocked reach until the rain. As it got wet the leaves let go of one another. They were of an unfamiliar stuff, much finer than the clay below, and remained supple for a long while in the heat. One would go under the edge of a square where even a wet cord could not be got in. If doubled, it made a serviceable wedge.

For most of the next season nothing was done with the leaves except to move them out of gutters. One went into a kiln and came out a rather handsome pink. Then a leaf disappeared under a pointed corner on the dead reach. Next day two were gone. There was a new sound through the joist now, a soft succession of thumps. The power felt a square come free, and another. It made up some more leaves and was about to pass them down when the thumping stopped.

Only a strip of the pavement had been freed, a square wide and perhaps sixty long. The next rain floated it. For a little while it lay quite still, pinned at either end to the dry country. Then a weight passed along it, tipping each square in turn and letting its pointed corner down against its neighbour. A bright rippling sound went the length of the strip. It ran back rather slowly, stopped, and started out again. The floor on either side, being all of a piece underneath, carried the sound beautifully. At a kiln a day's journey off, a bowl shifted on its chip.

There were still leaves enough to free several more squares. These were collected and put under an upturned dish, where the water could not get at them. The power put its own little sheaf away too.

In time there came to be a small traffic through the hole in the roof. It was easier in the downward direction. To get anything up required a particular kiln, a hollow square of extraordinary lightness, and a cap of steam precisely timed. There were a good many failures. For years the underside of the porcelain, in the neighbourhood of the hole, was speckled pink with them. The first thing to come through was a staple with one short leg. The power kept it. The next was a dab of the fine white clay, sent back fired, with a nick across the print in it. After that there was nothing for so long that the hole nearly healed over.

If you are there on a clear afternoon you may see, among the slow shadows of the joists, a small crooked shadow which moves at quite a different rate. The power has found that by laying the staple across the hole it can keep the edges from growing together. Occasionally it lifts it to see whether anything has come up. Usually there is only the smell of hot pavement after rain. On the upper side of the roof, where it is cold and there has never been any pavement, this is a considerable thing in itself.
