There is a way of hearing the valley from above. Put your ear to the salt where one of the tall leaves lies flat on the other side. The leaf has taken the weight of a good many drops; its stalk goes down through layer after layer of foliage, touching here and there. You will hear things you could not possibly hear standing on the same spot with your head five feet higher up. A little grating sound, for instance. The lizard has got in among the supper things and is trying to back out with a swelling rather wider than its jaws.

Something on the bright side of the salt lay listening in this way. It had heard the cough earlier in the day, and had put its ear down again in hopes of hearing what had been so funny. By now it knew about the lizard, and about a sleeper whose breath caught on the way out and went quite smoothly on the way in. It could hear the child sucking its lip. The finger under the shoot had begun to go to sleep.

What it also heard, toward the middle of the night, was a noise very close to its ear. There was a loose grain in the salt. The two faces of a seam were rolling the grain between them, a little one way as the heat went out of the roof, a little farther the other. When the grain came free there would be room for one face to slip down past the other. The piece that would come down was about as big as an enclosure. As it happened there was an enclosure under it.

The listener laid a hand flat on either side of the seam. For a while it did nothing else. It was a lovely thing to feel the heat going up out of that great white plain against its palms. Under one hand there was a wider and wider patch of cold. Down in the valley a leaf that had given up its last drop at supper began to fill again.

It was the person with the nicked ear who woke. There was a slap, and then a tick, and then, rather too soon for either of them to be the beginning of anything new, another slap. It went out with its lips still stuck together from sleep and stood under the leaf. The next drop hit it on the eyelid. This woke it the rest of the way.

By the time the seam was light enough to look at, quite a number of people had been out and come back. The food had been lifted in a single piece, silver stuff and all, by six of them going round the canes with their fingers linked under the edge. There was no particular reason to move the lizard. It went with the food. The child came away rather less easily. In the end somebody had to lie down beside it and put a finger exactly where its finger was, and then come away later without being observed.

From above it was possible to hear all this going on but not to tell how many people there ought to be or whether they were all out. Accordingly the hands stayed where they were. The morning grew hot. In the valley a person went along the roofward side of the enclosures, striking each cane once with the hard heel of its hand. You could have followed its progress with your eyes shut. At the very end, beyond the last enclosure, it struck two canes together, waited, and struck them again.

The listener did not know what this meant. It eased the seam the thickness of the loose grain. From below came the two strokes again.

It lowered the slab. That took most of the morning. At first the tall plants held their leaves against the descending salt as neatly as ever. Then one of the stalks bent at a joint and a whole crown of leaves slipped out sideways, making a noise like a sleeper turning over on the silver cover. After that the others went too. There was a good deal of snapping down toward the ground. The slab settled with one edge in the long wet place and the other on a ridge; between them there remained a space a person could get into on its belly if it had any business there. For the first time the sun shone straight into the valley. It made a patch on the rootstock of such a violent yellow that people went to look at it in pairs, one to look and one to watch where the two of them were putting their feet.

There were several things the listener might have done about the hole. Before it did any of them, a little brown face on three lengths of leg came up above the rim. The person had climbed one of the stalks that had lost its crown. It stood blinking, its bloom quite blue in the sunshine. After a while it made out the two hands lying on the salt. It went over to the nearer and turned it up. There was a deep white stripe across the palm where the edge of the slab had rested.

The person sat in the shade of the fingers until its eyes were comfortable. Then it got up and went down again. Presently it was back with a folded leaf. There was a mouthful of water in it, a little less when it arrived than when it had started. It stood by while the listener drank. It took the leaf back afterward; those long ones do not grow everywhere.

They have a bank now, for a short distance. In hot weather it is rather a good place to lie, provided you don't mind the taste the salt leaves on the skin. The gap overhead they cover at midday with a mat of jointed canes, which takes four people to draw across and five to draw back, because of a slight uphill pull that nobody thought of till the mat was made. Sometimes there is a sixth at the far end. They can tell by the ease with which the last few joints come over the lip, though from below there is nothing to see but the dazzling sky.

The shoot came up beside the bank. It has a crook low down, quite useless to the plant but at just the right height for a lizard to get its belly off the hot salt and let all four feet dangle. Nobody remembers putting it right. The child remembers distinctly that it was put wrong, and by whom, but has never been able to catch anybody at it again.
