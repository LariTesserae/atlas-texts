The power that had come to the mountain heard the knocking first. It was down in the dry riverbed, where the red stone was still giving off the heat of the day. Very little of the sound came so far. To hear it at all you would have had to be attending to the mountain already, and the power was. It went up to find what part of so large a thing could make so small a sound.

It took no shape on the shelf. There were eyes about, and almost any shape it might have taken would have meant something to those eyes. Even the shadow of a leaf, crossing a certain amount of open stone at a certain speed, would send all the little brown bodies flat against the ground. It stayed to hear the knocks repeated. By the time it had made out which throat belonged to which knock, it had been there a good while.

In the lowest fork of the uppermost tree there was a pocket of water with one tadpole left in it. The others had gone over the side some nights before. This last one had the full equipment of a little frog except for its mouth, which was still a tadpole's mouth, and the great business of its day was to go round the inside of the pocket eating the green film off a blade of fern. The blade lay in the water in a loop. The little animal worked its way down one side and came up the other and seemed to find the underside of the loop quite as easy to go round as the top.

There had been a wind. A dead cane lodged higher in the fork had come down a finger's breadth, bringing with it a quantity of the handsome black earth. The water which had run down that cane into the pocket now ran past on the outside of the tree. By morning the top of the loop was dry. The tadpole came up to the place where the water ceased to cover the blade, hung there eating for a while, and went down backwards.

Here was a thing the power could do. There was no need to put the cane back. Under the weight of the wet soil it had found a good firm seat, and two new white roots were feeling their way into the pith at its broken end. The power put one bead of water on the bark, in the dry space between the trickle and the rim of the pocket. The bead stood quite high. When the next water came down the cane, some of it took the wet way.

It was a very small division of the stream. Farther down the trunk, where a froglet had been sitting astride the run of it with its legs agreeably wet, the water still went by on both sides of the froglet. Up in the fork a fleck of dirt, stranded that morning halfway up the loop, floated off. Presently the tadpole came round the top of the blade, mouth to the green, its pale belly turned for a moment to the sky.

The power stayed through another turning of the shadow. It had not known until now how a frog gets out of water for the first time. In this instance there were several occasions on which it would have been possible to suppose the thing accomplished. The little frog got well up on the blade and sat there with the water clear of its chin. Something in the air moved against its skin. It went in again with such dispatch that the blade, relieved of its weight, came up and slapped the cane. A little later it climbed out backwards, a surprising proceeding which left it with a good deal of tail on dry land and its nose still under water. It spent the warm part of the afternoon like that.

It was dark when it went. A knock sounded from the lip, and another a long way from the first. Between the two the frog stepped off the blade into the earth of the fork. The earth yielded a little under each of its feet. For a time it stood taking a foot out of its print and putting it down in precisely the same print, as though the yielding were a property of the foot and required looking into. Then it got under a dead leaf. There was a slight lift to the leaf as it passed and no means afterward of telling which way it had gone.

Toward morning the slate bird came. It took a cane in its foot and leaned so far over after a fruit that a little slide of earth went down the trunk. The trickle divided again, this time of its own accord. Nothing remained on the bark to distinguish the place where the power had laid the bead from any other inch of wet wood.

Down in the valley later that day a cloud broke against the mountain and there was rain for an hour in one red fork of the riverbed. The power went down to be there for it. On the shelf it was a dry afternoon. In all three crowns the orange fruit shone, and in a fork well below the fruit a small damp foot came out from under a leaf and took hold of the bark.
