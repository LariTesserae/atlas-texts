The following spring a fine red stick appeared beside each of the sleepers. They had been put in while there was still snow in the hollows. Each had a number down one face and, on the other, a black line showing where the top of the new track was to come. The man stood at the sleeper where the voles had been and held his hand level with the black line. There was room below his hand for his whole boot, standing upright.

The new track was partly on his account. In November he had put a foot through a sleeper, gone down to the hip and skinned the front of his leg getting out. He had been a long way from either red roof or hut at the time. It was his own report of the matter, with a little drawing to show how the wood had been undercut, that had gone down to the valley. In the drawing he had made a good job of the hole. He had left the paper white on either side of it, as one does when drawing a hole.

The power that had taken an interest in the pass could have kept the track from being built. It could have done this quite cheaply, by its reckoning, with three wet days at the proper time; the money would have had to go back at the end of the quarter. But it had seen the man in November, lying for a little while with his cheek against the wood before the first sickness passed. It had no particular wish for him to go through it again.

On the morning the engineer came up, she and the man had just such weather as would have made them glad to finish their business in the hut. There was rain on the window in big drops that kept their shape on the greasy glass. She had brought the drawings in a tube. They unrolled them on the table and set the sugar tin and two mugs on the corners. There were to be pipes at intervals under the track. She showed him the blue pencil ticks where the water went through.

What the power did was to give them half an hour of sun. It took no rain out of the weather. That fell a little later, on both of them.

The first thing the sun lit in the hut was a photograph opposite the window. There came off it the same confused shine there was on the pass outside. The engineer got up to see it better. She had to stand a little to one side on account of the varnish on the print.

“Where was that taken?”

He put his boot on the sill.

She looked along the row. She was soon able to find the same stone in four of the pictures, although in the third there was nothing to go by but a little boiler of white water where its top ought to be. She went outside with the drawings rolled loose in her hand. He took the mugs to the sink.

When he caught up with her she was at one of the blue ticks. The pipe they had allowed for there would have taken the whole run easily. What interested her was that there were already two runs, and the red stick stood on a piece of sod between them with its hairy underside showing. She set off uphill to see where they parted. At the first willow bed she lost them both. A little farther on, putting her foot down on what looked like a particularly dry piece of ground, she brought clear water up over the welt of her shoe.

They went most of the way across. He showed her the bad sleeper. He showed her also, some distance on, the split where the green had gone in. There was nothing living there now that either of them could see. A few stems had lodged crosswise in the split. Pushed with the end of his finger they moved the whole length of the timber, being held together underneath by something finer. By now it was raining on the drawings, and she slipped them inside her coat. They had tea at the valve house, where there was nowhere to sit but a box of flanges. The man dried one of her socks for her on the motor cabinet.

The track they eventually made was a footway, wide enough for a small wheeled carrier. In places it went on the gravel as before. Over the wet ground it went on feet, with considerable lengths of daylight between. It cost more per yard and there were fewer yards of it: for nearly a quarter of a mile at the upper end they used a grey back of moraine which had been there all along, rather out of the direct line. From the hut the bend looked extravagant. On foot it amounted to very little. They left most of the old sleepers where they lay. There was no saving in taking them out.

Nothing was done about the voles in particular. That first summer there were not many. In the second, the man found cut grass in the toes of the spare boots at the valve house and took to hanging them by their loops. By then one of the new runs had gone under the footway at a place quite unprovided for in the drawings. It went through in a broad sheet, leaving little brown combings on the uphill faces of the feet. In hard frost these stood out from the metal as stiff as bristles.

Once, crossing late, he stopped halfway over. A vole had come out below him carrying a piece of sedge so fresh that the crease down its middle still held a bead of water. Through the narrow space between two planks he could see her quite well. She sat on the old grey timber under the new and drew the blade through her paws. It was raining lightly on the pass. Down there, for the width of two boards, it was dry.
