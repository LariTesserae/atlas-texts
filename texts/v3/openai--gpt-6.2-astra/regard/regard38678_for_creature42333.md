The young one comes as far as the grain. On the side away from the shell a little of the skin has gathered into wrinkles. The young one gets a hook into a wrinkle and eats for some time without having to go anywhere. Its mother passes twice. The second time she takes no more notice of it than of the grain.

There is a power attending to the mountain. For most of its life the mountain has been of interest to it on account of the slowness with which the red stone cools. It was there last winter when the split went through. For a while there was light all the way down the split, a fine red light by which, if there had been an eye in there, it might have seen the two faces of the break before either had lost a crumb. The power watched until the light went out. It has not had to go away in order to attend to other things. This is fortunate for the shell, whose fan is now the most interesting thing on the mountain, and would be easy to miss.

By noon the grain is sticking quite far out of the water. The strokes of the fan have begun to show at the surface. Each makes a low ridge which goes as far as the young one's hooks and shakes them loose from the wrinkle. Presently the fan itself strikes air. The hairs come together in a wet point. The shell draws them in.

It would be a small matter to send the pond back into the main hollow. There is even a track for it, a faint darkening of the red along the way the drop came yesterday. The power attends for a moment to the place where the track enters the hollow. A clear beast is standing there with its feet wide apart. In the middle of the space enclosed by its feet are two smaller sets of feet, still rather uncertain of their footing. One slips; a broad yellow-spotted ankle bends and the little foot gets up on it again.

High on the outer lip there is a flake of stone with daylight behind it. Mist has been collecting behind the flake all morning. When it falls, as it will in a week or so, the water behind it will have two ways to go. One is straight out over the ice. The other goes by a succession of small irregularities to a place just east of the shell.

The power puts a little of the night's cold into the water behind the flake. There is a tick. The flake falls spinning, showing alternately a dark face and a face so bright that its fall can be followed a surprising distance. The power would once have followed it all the way. It stays to see which way the water goes.

For a while it goes both ways. Then the supply from behind the flake gives out. There is only what condenses on the freshly broken surface, which is rough and takes the mist very well. This goes by the irregularities. It arrives at the eastern pond without a splash, a dark toe of wet advancing under the dry grease. The mother feels it first. She puts her hooks out beyond the edge of the pond and pulls. A whole length of the crescent comes off in her grasp and folds over her.

It takes her some time to eat her way out. By the time she does, there is water over the grain again. A shred of grease is going round and round in the current made by the fan. On each turn the shell gets a little of it. The young one, pink now except along the seam, hangs on to the other end and goes round with it.

Not every day on the mountain will be misty. Nor will the new face of stone take water quite so well once it has weathered. The power leaves it rough as it is. That evening, when the pond has had all it can hold, a bead sets off from it to the west. It carries no animal. It leaves on the stone, in a place that has been dry since the mountain split, a fleck of the grease. Long after the bead has gone, the fleck keeps its wet edge.
