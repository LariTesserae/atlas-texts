The power that had taken an interest in the rail could have lifted the crossing by the breadth of a hand. At low water the young birds could then have gone across dry. So could the fox. Indeed, on the second evening of a spell of east wind, when the water had been blown off the back of mud, a fox came as far as the stick. It stood there with its forefeet together and its hind feet rather wide apart, listening to something in the western bed. The power left the crossing at its old height.

There was something it wanted to know about the pumps. It followed the water back from the screws, which was a considerable undertaking even for a power; a great deal of the water was on the move without apparently changing places with any other water. The western screw drew from a deep cut. Into the cut came, among other things, the water from a row of houses on the river road, the lower rooms of which had stone floors a step below the level of the gardens. The eastern screw kept a culvert free under the railway embankment. But the middle screw had been put in for the fields. For a good part of the summer it lifted water out of the basin to make room for water which came in at the next rise of the river. It did this very well. There was nothing the matter with the engine.

In September the engineer came to read the levels. He let himself into the hut, took down a board with a sheaf of forms clipped to it and sat on the step to get the date right. In the square for the middle pump there was a little rule of thumb, printed in violet ink, for converting hours run into acres drained. He had made this conversion every month for eleven years. His pencil went as far as the word *acres* and stopped there.

This was what the power did. It gave him the full use, for perhaps half a minute, of a doubt he had had before.

He did not see the rail. What he saw, looking up from the board, was a long streak of by no means level water between himself and the middle house, and beyond it the white bands on the caps, some of which were already in need of paint. He entered the hours. In the space for acres he put a small neat question mark. It caused him more trouble than a figure would have done. Before the end of the month he had had to go out twice in the skiff, once in the rain, to show another man what he meant.

The middle pump is still there. There are days in winter when all three squares of glass go cloudy with the heat from below, and the watchman can put a foot on the floating drum without going down a single step. But for most of the time now the flywheels in the middle house stand with the same spoke uppermost. A wagtail takes flies off the brick apron. From the bench you can hear them ticking against the glass.

The change in the water was not altogether to the rail's advantage. The stick went under. For two evenings the bird came to its old place at the edge of the eastern bed and went back in; on the third it swam a little way and got out on the same side. There is a crossing again now, farther up, where the rushes from the two beds grow in among one another, though their roots are still quite separate. At about the time the last light comes through the stems there is a call from one side and occasionally an answer from the other. They are not particularly sweet sounds. A man sitting on the middle apron to eat his supper can hear both of them quite distinctly, and go on eating.
