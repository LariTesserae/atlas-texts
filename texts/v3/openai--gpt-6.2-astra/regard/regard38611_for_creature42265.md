The power that took an interest in the block did not at first distinguish the ramp from any other street. It could go up it as easily as down. What drew its attention to the difference was the grocer’s wife, who, going up with an empty basket, stopped halfway to change hands. She did not change hands. She stood for a little with the basket resting against her skirt and went on with it over the same arm. There was a place in the brickwork there, about the size of a saucer, from which the nap had been worn by people leaning one shoulder against it on one pretext or another. On the downhill side there was no such place.

For a while the power considered raising the block. It was no great weight. The objection was the fig, among other things; there was no height to which the fig could be raised without being taken out from under its rivet. And there were four streets’ worth of people to be consulted, counting the one that bent. The power let the block lie. It had noticed something else in looking at the ramp. Behind the worn patch, where one would have expected solid packing, there was the cool flat side of a stone stair.

It had belonged to the old carpet warehouse. The warehouse had had its feet in the lower lane and its hat, so to speak, in the standing town; the hat had been pulled down to widen a yard, and the feet had become a number of quite small premises, one of which sold eels. The stair, being nobody’s premises and of no use in widening the yard, had been left in. It went up by short flights, with a turn and a broad landing to each, inside the standing wall. At the bottom a door had been papered over with advertisements for what could be done about the feet. At the top there was a blank wall.

The power put a landing at the top where a landing ought to have been, and a door in the wall of the yard. It tried the stair with the weight of a person coming down rather heavily on one heel. Two of the treads rocked. It attended to those. Then it was a little at a loss. One could hardly expect the eel man, who had a chalked price and a very creditable likeness of a skate on the bottom door, to take a scraper to it without encouragement.

On Wednesday Ellen heard, in the middle of an account of a difficulty about getting good screws, a second account of the same difficulty, much fainter and a little late. She put her palm over the bare place at the six. The first voice stopped; the other finished saying *brass*.

“Did you hear that?” she asked.

“I generally can,” the lining cutter said. “If I put the basin down. It’s the wall at my back.”

The ledge where she ate her dinner had once been the sill of a stair window. Ellen got out beside her, which she had never done before. The woman had rather unexpectedly light eyebrows. They shifted the basin, and a piece of old matting the woman had brought up to sit on, and found behind it a panel of three narrow boards. The middle one had a knot missing. Through it Ellen saw, perhaps a foot away, the nose of a stone step, with a lozenge of sunlight on it that could only have come from an opening higher up.

The eel man was quite willing to lose the skate provided Ellen would do him another. It took the better part of the afternoon to free the hinges. They went up together, Ellen and the lining cutter, with the little blue hand lamp from the barber’s, though after the second turn there was no need of it. There were iron sockets at intervals where a rail had been. On one of the landings someone had done a sum in red chalk and got it wrong. The lining cutter, who checked the dressmaker’s bills, stopped and did it again. They came out behind a pile of iron bedsteads, in a yard with a weighing platform sunk flush with the flags. A man in his shirtsleeves was oiling the pivots of the scale with a feather.

“Well,” said the lining cutter. She had a spot of eel chalk on her cheek. “If I’d known it was as near as this I’d have brought your clean collar.”

The iron man did not come down that day. He walked to the first turning and had a look and said that going down was all very well. The following week he had a rail put in, a handsome one made in lengths of different patterns, with a smooth wooden cap over the joins. He came on Sunday. It took him twenty minutes, including a sit on the landing with the sum. He went into the dressmaker’s and saw the table at which the linings were cut and the long shears with one bow bound up in flannel. He had imagined the woman sitting at her work. It troubled him rather to see how high the table was and to understand that all the hours he had pictured her sitting she had been on her feet.

They had their tea there. On the way back he took the basin, which it had not previously occurred to either of them he could carry. They went past the chair on the barber’s step just as Ellen’s father was getting up from it to go inside.

After that there were people on the stair at most hours, though not so many as one might have thought. The ramp was still better for anything on wheels and quicker for a person with good wind. The grocer’s wife preferred it. There was a view at the place where she stopped, and she had been keeping an eye for some years on a householder who was attempting to grow a pear in a square gin bottle. The eel man did very well out of the stair. Ellen painted him a skate with the eyes on the proper side, which his own had not been.

She herself used it chiefly at noon. In the yard there was a clock on a bracket over the scales, a plain round clock with no glass, its face gone almost to the colour of breakfast tea. The iron man had given her the job of doing it up. She could have taken the dial down to the loft, but she liked standing on the weighing platform to work, with the yard going on about her and the little weights travelling to and fro on the beam whenever she shifted her feet. From there she could see over the crooked hedge. It was an odd thing, after so many years of knowing exactly where to set a knee or reach for a cup, to see how little of the place there appeared to be. Her loft was a strip of zinc no wider than the brush she held up to measure it by.

The power, which had stayed to see if the two doors were going to stick in wet weather, saw her there. She had come to the foot of the dial. There was a flaw in the metal at the six and she was working the white carefully round it. From the yard it looked as though she had missed a bit. From the other side, should anybody ever have occasion to sit there, there would be a speck of day.
