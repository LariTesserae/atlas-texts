The first person the power spoke to was washing a plate. It had waited until the others were gone, thinking this considerate. The woman had both knees in the hollows and a plate in either hand, and when something spoke out of the valley she lost a good plate, the one with the little drain in the rim for eating curds.

After a while it came back up. It came edge first through the curtain, hung there streaming for a moment, and lay down on the table.

“Thank you,” said the woman. “Can you do that with anything?”

“Anything I can find.”

“There is a boy's left boot. Not now,” she added, as the plates began to tremble. “He is thirty-six.”

The power wanted to know how a person got to the pass.

It turned out that this was quite easy. Going up, the freight sledges were empty except for the curd skins and whatever the people of the city had contrived to mend for people on the other side. There were handholds for eight passengers. Coming down was another matter. A passenger took the place and, more to the point, the weight of two sacks of meal. You could pay the carriage if you had it. The letterer had been putting a little aside for some time. The woman knew because it was she who kept the accounts, in blue pencil on the cupboard where the good plates went.

“Could a child ride down with me?” asked the power.

“How do you go?”

It showed her, with a drop of water. The drop went out through the curtain and came back from quite another direction, cold enough to smoke on the warm table.

“Not mine,” said the woman.

The letterer, when asked, said not hers either.

In the end the power went to work for the freight people. There was no difficulty about giving it a job. The difficulty was reckoning its pay. It could lift a laden sledge clear of the roof, which was of no use whatever, since the men at the front steered by the feel of the runners. It could have brought the meal from the mill without a sledge, but there were tolls to be paid at two bridges, and the upkeep of the bridges would have fallen on a very small number of people who kept goats. What it finally did was walk behind on the descent, with a hand, or something that answered for a hand, against the back rail. The brake shoes lasted much longer. It took its pay in passenger weight.

There was enough for the letterer too. She had not been to the pass since she was small and had forgotten about the dust. At the top she took six or seven steps slapping it furiously, turned to say something to her daughter, and found her doing it on purpose. The freight people had gone indoors. There was nobody to laugh but the two of them.

The lip was not red that day. A storm below the shelf had driven cloud up past it, and what showed was a narrow purple mark, rather like the place on a plum where the skin has split without coming away. The child held the lump from her pocket out at arm's length. It hid the city, the storm, and a considerable part of the low country.

“Which bit is ours?”

Her mother got down with her cheek very close to the child's and sighted along a finger. Even so she could not be sure. The water went over white in three places, and a house ought to have been under the middle one, but the place she recognized as the baths was on the wrong side of it.

“It is a good deal longer than I thought,” she said at last.

At the inn they had an onion apiece, baked in the ashes. The child ate the middle first. They went back to look before the sledge left, and this time the cloud had got up altogether. A man was leaning on the rail looking into it. He sold straps and buckles, came that road twice a month and had never seen the city at all.

On the way home the power felt a succession of small, deliberate taps through the back rail. The child had worked her hand down between two meal sacks. It put something there for her to tap against.

“Did you make it cloudy?”

“No.”

“Could you have taken it away?”

“Yes.”

She considered this. “Next time,” she said, “I should like to stay till it goes by.”

The power had been intending to ask what next time she meant. Then it felt the rail begin to press hard against its hand and attended to the descent.

For some years after that there were two passenger places on most of the downward runs. Sometimes they were taken by people who had business. Sometimes somebody went up to do nothing in particular, a thing for which there had never been much of a name in the city, though there had always been the polished stone under the lip. The letterer spent what was left in the cupboard on a new brush. The woman who kept the accounts got the boot back after all. A very small foot had worn the lining through at the heel. She had forgotten how small.

The child went again in clear weather. It was red. What she had not expected, and afterward had trouble explaining, was the sight of the water leaving it. From the table it was the water that stayed where it was. People got up, plates went sliding down from the end, somebody went away and came back grown, and there it hung. From the pass she could see the wind take the whole fall and lay it out sideways in the air. There was the little dark notch where they ate, quite bare for as long as she watched.

That evening she climbed up behind the clock bracket. She set the brass hand toward the pass, then moved it back a little toward her bed. There was a place between the two where the nail had worked loose and the hand would not stay. She took a white button, put the nail through one of its holes and drove it home with the red stone. After that the hand would stay wherever she put it. There were six buttons left, which was still a great many buttons for a person who did not have to fasten anything.
