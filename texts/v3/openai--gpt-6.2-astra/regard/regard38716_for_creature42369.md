The first thing the power did was to take its shadow off the water. It had leaned over the square in much the same way as anybody else and had put nearly the whole fan in the dark. For a moment there were yellow edges everywhere. Then the light of the bosses came back, one patch at a time. The power found that it could attend quite as well from underneath the paving. There it stayed for the summer.

It learned to tell a loose stone from a sound one by the tread of the tenants. On a loose stone there was a little answering tap after the sixth foot had gone. It learned the weight of a tram with nobody in it and of one that had come from the swimming baths. At a quarter to five on Wednesdays a great many children rode on the back platform for three stops, and the third pier took the weight twice, once when the car went over and once when it rocked back on its springs. Tenants on the side of that pier used the interval to get a somewhat better hold. Very small ones did not know to do this and occasionally arrived at the bottom riding on somebody other than the animal with which they had set out.

There was a matter about the middle pump. The mechanic was already aware of it. On Thursdays he now put his thumb to the bearing housing, took it off, wiped it on his trousers and tried again. What he felt was a knock so slight that it was possible to mistake the pulse in the end of the thumb for it. The power could feel it from any stone in the square. It could also feel, a considerable way off, a bearing of the right size lying in its original paper in a drawer. There would be no difficulty about bringing the two together. The mechanic in fact did this himself, on the last Thursday in August, by means of a requisition which had to go to a man on the other side of the tram square for a second signature.

What neither man knew was that the housing would have to come off toward the water. There was just room on the plinth for a man to stand and draw it toward himself, provided there was somebody on the step below to take the weight. There was seldom room on that step for even the heel of a boot. Along its outer edge, where the cream-colored deposit made a little undercut, there were eleven unoccupied beads. Even at noon only four of them showed from above.

The power considered putting the eleven somewhere else. It could have put them back afterward to within the thickness of the skin on the water. But in the course of one Thursday, while the mechanic sat eating his bread, forty or fifty tenants had occasion to turn there. A bead put back in its place would not be much use to an animal that needed it while it was away.

In the music school next door there was a stool with legs of unequal length. For years it had stood on a sloping floor in the cloakroom and been quite a good stool. That summer the school had the floor leveled. The caretaker took the stool out to the pavement and set it beside a bundle of worn bass strings and a notice concerning a lost mitten. The power turned the stool over. This was its whole expenditure of force in the square, apart from the unfortunate business with the light. With the stool standing up, the mechanic had passed it twice. With its feet in the air he could see what he wanted it for. He carried it down on his shoulder.

Two feet went on the paving and the two short ones on the bottom step. The seat made a dry place for the housing, which was all the second man had been wanted for. While the mechanic worked, tenants passed to and fro under the seat. One went up a stool leg and spent much of the morning attending to a fleck of green paint under the rim. When he lifted the stool the tenant was in the water again and the paint was still there.

He left the stool beside the disconnected pump. It is not much good for sitting on there, but it will hold a cup if the cup is put well back. On the underside of the seat, in the part that was over the step, there are eleven little silver lights on a fine day. These are only reflections. They travel up the wood as the sun goes over and disappear into a screw hole one after the other. It was by watching them that the mechanic came to amend his count of the tenants on the step.

The power still comes on Thursdays, though there is nothing the matter with the new bearing. It generally gets there a little before the mechanic. There is a pleasure to be had, from just under the fan, in feeling the first foot come down on the stair: the heel first, then the long, slow giving of the weight to the sole, on a stone quite dry and a good twenty feet yet from anything he might hurt.
