What first took the power's attention was a coat in the middle of the road. Seen from above, it seemed to have been put down there for somebody to come back for. There was a woman under it. She had folded it twice and was sitting on it with one leg out in front of her. Her basket stood a little way off, the pears having gone on after she stopped.

There were already three people coming. The power could have brought any one of them to her in the time it took a pear to turn over on the grit. It came down itself instead, in the shape of a woman with the broad back and rather short legs of one who has spent much of her life carrying things uphill.

“Have you hurt yourself?”

“No,” said the woman on the coat. “That is to say, no more than when I set out. If you wouldn't mind standing over there, I could have my leg off a minute.”

The power stood where she indicated. Behind it there was a small noise of buckles. The three people approaching on the roads saw a back turned towards them and went about their business. Presently the woman asked for her basket. She put the wooden leg across it and set to work with a scrap of fleece, trying for a better fit. It was sour work doing it on the road. At home she had a seat high enough to let the end of the leg hang free.

They walked together as far as the first garden. The power carried the pears. At the gate it asked what she did when there was nobody to stand in front of her.

“Oh, there's always somebody,” she said. “That's the difficulty.”

The power stayed in the lowest quarter for a while. It was useful at lifting things which had got themselves into corners when there was more room than there was now. People soon got in the way of sending for it. In return it asked the loan of a handcart and the right to pick over the heaps. The taker-out who kept the cart went with it, partly because one of the pins was apt to jump its socket on the downhill run and partly to show it which ovens had wrens.

What it had in mind was a little place to sit, halfway along the longest road. It had thought at first of six such places, then of one very good one with water in it and a roof. By the time it had spent a week in the town it wanted to see somebody sit in the first one before it built the next.

They found two sides of a wool bin, nicely matched, and a long sill with a groove in it that would take the lower edge of either slab. For the seat there was a choice of stones so great that the power went and fetched the woman with the wooden leg. She chose a piece of an old draining board, for the height chiefly, though she was pleased with the little flutes in the underside. One could curl one's fingers into them to push off. When they had it up on the cart she stayed to help with the pin.

It took about a morning to set the stones up beside the road. The grit was quite shallow there. The power set the sides at an angle to keep the wind off the seat and left a good wide entrance facing the mountain. Then it went up to the rim and looked down. The new work was hardly visible. One could see the white of it against the grit but no detail at all.

The woman used it on her way home. A boy used it to get a thorn out. By evening somebody had already had a meal there and left a pear stalk in the groove of the sill. The power, very well pleased, went down in the morning with a second load of stone.

On the road it met the inspector from the upper channel.

“Was it you put up the sitting place?” he said. “My wife's father went by there yesterday. He would have been glad of a hand getting up.”

“Did he fall?”

“No, he sat down. That's why we couldn't see him.”

They left the second load on the cart.

The inspector had in fact gone out to look for the old man. It would not do to have to make a search of the six roads every time a grandfather was a little late to supper. Nor did the woman with the leg want a gap cut in the stones. As she pointed out, if a person wanted people looking in, there was the whole of the mountain to sit on already.

For some days they did nothing. Then one of the dye-house brothers, eating his dinner in the shelter, found his cart had run back a little on the road. To hold it still he passed the end of the shaft in through the entrance. From well across the shelf his brother saw the shafts sticking out, one of them higher than the other, and came over to see what had gone wrong. This gave them the idea of the arm.

It was a piece of split pole, whitewashed at the end, on a pin in the outside corner. Sitting on the draining board you could put a finger through the wall, where the sides of the wool bin had once been pegged together, and trip the catch. The arm would swing up against the sky. It took some arranging to make it fall up instead of down. Three small children and a mason spent most of a windy afternoon at it. When they had finished, the youngest could work it with the little finger of either hand. To put it back you had to come round the outside.

For a while it went up altogether too often. People came off the roads to find all three children sitting on the board, looking earnestly at one another as though one of them might yet be taken ill. But the walk out to the shelter was a long one to make for the pleasure of pulling a catch, and in a week the novelty wore off. The white arm lay along the top of the wall, ready for use. No rule was made about what sort of trouble you had to be in to raise it.

By then the power was being asked for a sitting place on the north road, where the carriers changed shoulders, and for one on the short steep road from the well. It helped with these. On the north road the carriers wanted a shelf, rather than a seat, so that a person travelling alone could get under a load again. The well people wanted no stone at all. There was a particular view of a pear tree against the sky that they disliked to interrupt. They put in two posts and a hide strap between them which took the weight off your feet very agreeably if you leaned your rump against it.

There were other things the power might have done. It could feel in its hands how easily it might give the mountain a thousand ordinary winters in a night. There would be sand in the river then, and the first little beaches; the water would come out milky below the bridges. It went once to the well and looked in at the red cup, thinking about this. A girl beside it was having a tooth out on a bit of cord. The keeper made her spit into a cabbage leaf, not down the well. Afterwards the girl looked over the curb again, with the tears still on her cheeks, to make sure the cup was there.

The power let the mountain be. When it left, it took the handcart back to the taker-out, with a new pin in the socket and the old one in the tray in case there had been a reason for the bend in it. Nobody saw it leave. To get to the rim it had to pass the first of the sitting places. There was a basket just visible inside, and on the top of the wall the white arm lay flat. The power went by on the grit, looking straight ahead.
