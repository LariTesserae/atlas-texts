The hand that felt this could have held up the western face. It had been laid on the ridge with some care, since it was the first hand there had ever been on the plateau and there was no telling what provision might be made for a second. Sure enough, when it was lifted there came up on the ridge five little pads of differing heights, the tallest under the hollow where the palm had been. They stayed up for three circuits. On the fourth the lip that looked after the reels appropriated the tallest pad, and the other four went back into the ground.

This was useful to know. The hand had meant to steady the old freight incline on its way down. It had appeared a pity that anything which had kept its appointment with a hill for forty thousand years should go over a cliff. But there were sockets under the incline, as under the station, and far below them roots were already putting out the fine perishable tips by which a socket is first explored. To keep the incline up for so little as a season would be to send those tips feeling about in the cold for something that was not there. The hand let the matter alone.

It stayed, however. It was able to stay a long while without resting its weight on anything. It watched the hot ridge harden and the fields open about it, and saw that what was coming up inland was not quite another freight incline. There was a place on its upper side for a reel to lie at ease. The teeth were much too fine to take the load of a freight belt. The first thing they engaged with was the selvage of a sheet of tow that had got under the ridge by accident. They brought it through without breaking a thread. Subsequently a good deal of tow came that way.

In the season of the fall the old incline travelled west with a nursery of cork bushes on one side and a piece of flawless pavement on the other. At the very edge a last little crop of corks ripened. The pavement rolled over on them and they went down quite snug between the two surfaces. The incline followed with its teeth uppermost. For a time, as it turned, the teeth faced out into the gulf, free of everything, and in that interval a tremor passed along them from the bottom tooth to the top. A little oil came out at the top and hung there in a bead. Then the roots took hold below and the teeth went into the face.

The hand would have missed the bead had it been occupied in saving the incline. It caught it on a fingertip. There was not usually anything to catch what fell outside the works.

Oil in so small a quantity ought to have set hard at once. The bead remained clear. Across its surface went a minute steepening, which ran to the edge of the fingertip and stopped. After a considerable wait it came back. It had very little strength. When the hand turned to look at it the steepening disappeared altogether on the uphill side; it had not the means of lifting its own oil. The hand levelled itself. Presently the steepening came by again.

It was tempting to carry this up to the new teeth. The distance was nothing. But the hand had felt those teeth take the tow through, and there was no particular reason to put a hill under a machine that combed floss. Instead it brought the bead just under the eaves of the nearest buried station and waited for a root tip to come out. One came, touched the oil and thickened. In a little while it had sucked the fingertip perfectly clean.

For two seasons nothing followed. The hand spent them at the inspection pit. It became quite good at telling when the shutter would rise and, what was harder, when it would like to rise and could not, owing to the passage of a load overhead. On those occasions there was a faint double pull at the catches. Once the hand took the load. The shutter rose on a magnificent view of the underside of a belt. Several gauges tested themselves in quick succession and the shutter came down. The hand did not do it again.

At the end of the second season a root under the buried station put out a round clear drop. There was a steepening in it. Beside it, at about the distance a finger might conveniently reach, another root put out a dry tip.

This time the crossing took very little oil. The dry tip drew off a film and the drop stayed where it was. By the next circuit there were three tips, not all at the same distance. The hand attended to two. The third turned in at the end and made a socket, on which in due course a little bush of insulating cork established itself. There were no more tips on that part of the eaves.

So there began to be, outside the close-packed substance of the plateau, a small traffic for which the works had hitherto had no fitting. Not much went by it. What could be sent by a belt went by a belt. What had a way through the cliff, even a way that took several seasons, was generally entrusted to the cliff. But occasionally two things which had worn smooth together in the works would go over the edge a hundred yards apart. Or a pressure, coming at long last to the end of a rib, would find that the socket it required had closed before its arrival. Then a drop might stand out on the face. There was not always a tip to receive it. The hand kept such drops level until they cooled. Some set quite plain. In one, no larger than a seed gauge, there remained distinctly the mark of three unequal flanges and the little sideways turn at the end.

In time a pad grew on the cliff, rather broader than the one provided for the heavy reels. The hand supposed at first that it marked a place where a quantity of oil would shortly have to be carried. It waited there through the fall of two stations. Nothing came out. Only as the hand moved away did the pad lift a little at one edge, in advance of the movement, as it used to lift under a reel about to lean. The hand came back and laid its wrist on it. At once the gooseflesh ran away on either side and the tow beneath lay quiet. A considerable standing expenditure of heat ceased.

It had cost the plateau something, all this while, to keep a rest ready in precisely the right place. The hand had been at pains not to weigh upon the works. It allowed a little weight to settle now. Far inland, under a shutter, the floss filled out and took its time about sinking in again. On the face, a mile below the wrist, a clear bead gathered, waited, and was taken up by a second root which had grown just far enough to reach it.
