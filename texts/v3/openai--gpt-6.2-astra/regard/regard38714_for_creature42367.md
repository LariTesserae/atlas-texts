The first thing the power did was to open its foot. It had gone to some trouble to have a foot of the right sort, and it seemed a pity to disappoint the animal. There was not much of a draught between the two toes. The sails filled, however, and the little thing got as far as the knee of a searcher sitting on the step below. She took it off with the back of her comb.

On the collar beside her there was a left foot with four marks in it. The power put its elbow down and felt three people knocking. It could feel a good deal further into the mountain than the searcher could. It could feel the fourth person, for instance, lying with his cheek against his arm, quite asleep. His mother was doing the knocking for both of them.

The power put a finger against the joint of the leaf. The searcher struck it smartly across the knuckle.

She took it round the back of the house. There was another leaf there, no thicker than the lid of a workbox, with its lip resting on the one the power had meant to raise. On its underside two people were lying on their stomachs cutting a passage. They had made such a neat job of the entrance that from the stair it looked like one of the paths in the fleece. One of them was passing little packets of spoil backwards with her feet. The searcher took a packet and passed it to the power. For the next part of the morning this was what the power did.

All four came out very well, the boy still bearing on his cheek the weave of his sleeve. The people who had been digging came out backwards. One had to be eased out of the entrance by the ankles, on account of having put her comb in an inside pocket and then forgotten how she had turned to get it in.

At the collar the searcher drew a right foot. Then she rubbed out a left foot some way off. It had two marks in it.

The power asked about this. By now it could speak a little with its fingers, though its pointed knock was much too loud.

*That was yesterday,* the searcher said.

It waited for the rest.

*If I leave it there,* she said, *somebody will spend today looking for it. We haven't even got the right crease. All we know is that there were two.*

The searcher had felt them from inside a house which had itself been shut at the time. On getting out she had gone to the place where she supposed they must be. There had been nothing there but an old store hollow and a grub pushing a ball of buttery fluff up the wall, losing it every few inches. She had worked out afterwards that she might have been feeling the knocks through the roof instead of the floor. Once a leaf was up there was no recovering just where it had lain on the one below.

The power had the names of a great many things at its disposal, but not those two. It went and lay in the hollow where the searcher had lain. It fitted badly. Its newly made feet were excellent; it had been less particular about its shoulders. For a while all it felt was the digging party knocking meal out of their coats. Then the leaf settled a little, and against the point of its left shoulder there came two very faint, quite separate blows.

It could have spoken to the searcher from there. Instead it put its finger to the roof and tried a remark it had learnt that morning. It meant *Have you room?* and was used when passing a packet back along the line.

After a considerable interval somebody said, *For what?*

That was how the two were found, a full day's walk from the old store hollow by the paths, though the distance the knocks had travelled was scarcely the length of a comb. They had food. What they were rather badly in need of was for somebody to go down the outer stairs and fetch their daughter, who was due home and would get into the empty half of the house and find nobody in it. The power went. It was an agreeable walk. On the step with the worn corner it met a person carrying a bundle of new combs, who listened to its message and made it repeat, with some exactness, what the voice in the roof had said. Then she gave it the combs to carry and went on ahead.

Afterwards the power was often asked to go down the outer stair. There was no mystery about its being a power. It could carry as many combs as you liked, and in the worst of the wind it could put a shoulder out on the empty side for somebody to walk against. It might have put a parapet there. But on asking where a parapet ought to go it was shown, in one afternoon, eleven places where it would be very much in the way. In one of these the grey animals came ashore in such numbers at certain times of year that there was a grey hem to the step, all the little square sails slack and drying in the lee of the riser.

It did make one lasting alteration. At the request of the searcher it put a second band of brass just inside the first, with a little space between for the sweepings. On the inner band went the feet for which there had been no answer. You could come back to one after a day, or after a season. There were generally very few. The brass took the dust beautifully, and when a left foot was cleared away there was room on the outer band for a right one of quite extravagant size.

In time the power had a hollow of its own. It chose the abandoned crease above the outer stair, partly for the prospect and partly because the extra excavations on either side of the head made excellent places to keep things. In one it kept its comb. In the other, after a good day's carrying, it generally found a little meal. On mornings when the roof was slow to lift it would lie quite still and feel, somewhere about its ear, the delicate business of a sail being made ready.
