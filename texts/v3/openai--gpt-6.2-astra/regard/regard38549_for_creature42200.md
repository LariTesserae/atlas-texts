There is a noise a lift makes when someone below wants it stopped. The handle in the basket draws a little shoe against the moving cable. In the lift house this is felt in a porcelain stud which the hand at the brake keeps against the inside of one knee. A good lift hand can feel it through two pairs of wet trousers. There is no provision for feeling it in the engine house. The lift hands have been trying to explain this to the machinists for years. A machinist puts a hand to the cable housing and says, quite truthfully, that there is nothing there.

The power which had taken an interest in the country could feel it. It could feel a great many things through stone. At first it mistook the rub of the little shoe for a defect in the cable, and would have smoothed it out, except that on one occasion the rub came twice and the second time had a distinctly impatient quality. After that it attended less to the iron and more to what people had their hands on.

At the second western wheel the old machinist had got as far as the governor with his daughter. Here it was possible to speak by putting one's lips to the bone just in front of the other person's ear. He had been looking forward to this. He had a question about the fish in the pool. It had been an old fish when she went away.

“Still the one,” she told him. “It has a spoon in there now. You can feel it if you put your hand in.”

He understood this to mean that she had lost a spoon and knew where it was, and that the difficulty of getting it out had proved, over the years, just a little greater than the difficulty of doing without it. It was the first piece of news she had given him that brought home how long she had been gone. He stood holding her elbow. The wheel went on turning under their feet.

Down at the crossing a lift hand was sitting with his legs over the side of a stationary basket. Something heavy had stepped aboard and had not stepped off again. He had run the backs of his fingers down a flank and found the trouble: a strip of old basket wicker had got itself worked up between two of the closing toes. The animal would not let him have the foot. Each time he tried, all four feet shut harder. He had asked for the stop and got it. There was salt to be got up and the wheel was overdue for service, but a lift hand at the brake will not start a basket until the rider has asked, if it takes until the next rain.

The power could have taken the wicker out. It had, however, a difficulty which it had first encountered in listening to the cable: it did not know what the handle was for until somebody used it. There were a great many handles in this country. A foot closed on a stick might be a foot in trouble or a foot asleep. It settled for making its attention perceptible, very slightly, to the man in the basket.

He felt something take a little of his weight. He put a hand behind him, thinking that another of the animals had come up under the wicker. His hand went out into empty air. For an instant it was rather like leaning against the outside of a bath full of water while somebody moves in it.

“Anybody there?” he said.

The power had heard speech but had never had to bring it off. It made an attempt with the air. The result was so like the noise a goat makes before bringing up iron water that the man drew his feet into the basket.

“Well,” he said after a while. “If you've got a hand to spare, hold this.”

He had been trying to support the belly with one arm and get at the foot with the other. He guided the attention to the belly by pressing up with his palm and then gradually letting go. The power took the warm weight. Released from the business of holding itself up, the animal relaxed one hind foot and then the other; the bit of wicker came away with a little wet stick to it. A moment later the power was holding nothing. There were a few movements of branches a surprising distance out from the rock.

The man sat for a time running the strip between finger and thumb. It was a piece of the rim, not of the floor as he had supposed. He found where it had come from and pared the place smooth. Then he signaled to go up.

At the lift house he said there was somebody at the crossing who could be got to hold things. He did not say it with any expectation of being believed. The woman at the brake said, “What, all the way down there?” as if the unusual part were the distance. She had lately been having the impression, when she lifted her youngest out of the wash pot, that somebody was ready to take the child if she lost her grip. It had not occurred to her to let go to find out.

In time the power learned to make a serviceable voice. It learned it from the leaf tubes, which have among them nearly all the bores required, though for a while its speech was affected by how much rain there had been. In dry weather it was quite easy to understand. People asked it things. Where a lost island had gone was a common question and one it could generally answer, provided the questioner knew something useful about the underside. The names of birds were no help to it whatsoever. Once it followed the wrong piece of turf for nine days on account of a spoon, and thereafter asked what the spoon was made of.

There was an early sickness among the goats which it did not know how to cure. This surprised the engine folk more than it did the menders. It could hold a sick animal in an easy position practically indefinitely, and did. There were children who got their first whole night's sleep in a week that way. There were also goats that died. To the people whose goats these were it never mentioned the night's sleep.

What pleased it most, though it took it some time to recognize pleasure, was being asked to attend to something there was no difficulty about at all. The machinist's daughter, who went away again and came back several times, got into the habit of finding it before she came to the house. She would lie down on the great divide with the side of her face against the grooves. It knew her by a little hard touch that came separately, the long nail of her first finger. She would ask whether the western wheel was running.

For years the answer was yes. Then once it was no. She kept her face where it was.

“Is my father at the house?”

“Yes,” it said. It had learned not to use more air than was wanted. A tube no bigger than the end joint of her thumb was enough for the two of them.

“Is he all right?”

“He's sitting on the lower step with a bowl. He has been scraping the bottom for some time.”

“That's all right, then.”

She stayed a little longer. It could feel through her cheek the small changes that passed over her face at the sounds in the rock. Presently she asked if it could hear the water going over in the south. It said it could.

“Not down there,” she said. “Here. Put your ear here.”

It had no ear to put there. For that matter it was already in the south, where masses of warm water, thick as turf for the first few lengths of the fall, came to pieces on the way down. But by now it knew something about holding what it was given to hold. It drew its attention in from the lip, and from the woods rooted in their long shelves below it, and from the innumerable small disturbances where the rain met the iron water. It attended to a piece of rock about the size of a woman's cheek.

For a while there was nothing. A jointed thing went over her wrist and down into a groove. Then the stone gave, not enough to stir the water in the groove, and from very far away there came the long low sound, worn smooth in traveling. It was quite unlike the noise at the fall. There was room, before it came again, for the woman to draw breath.

“There,” she said.

They listened until her father came up the back of the divide, tapping his empty bowl with his spoon to let her know which way to come.
