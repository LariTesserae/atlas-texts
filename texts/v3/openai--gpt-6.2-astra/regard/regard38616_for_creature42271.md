What first engaged the power was a small error in the sound of the place. A shell dropped from a fork and struck a palm fan, as it might do twenty times in a morning. There was a pause in which it ought to have been travelling on down to the next fan. Instead there came from under the ground a knock so like the knock of a frog that the frogs along that bit of bank answered it.

The shell had gone down the hollow stem of an old palm. The foot of the stem stood in one of the long rooms under the bank, where the air had lately become rather bad. There was quite as much of it as ever. A climber putting up its face there found the accustomed space, drew a breath and went on. A little way farther on it felt the need to breathe again. The older animals had taken to passing that room by. They went with a good deal of speed through two rooms and breathed in the third, a matter of no great difficulty for an animal that knew where the third room was.

The power passed some time in there. Little things went on which could not be heard from the forest floor. A claw plucked at a root and the root continued to tremble after the claw had let go. A bubble came up from the black stuff on the bottom and hung for a while in the hair of a passing belly. The dropped shell lay on a ledge above water. Now and then a shrimp sprang high enough to touch it.

At the upper end of the palm stem, just where the light gave out, there was a fan which had failed to unfold. It had grown part way out of the hollow, doubled on itself and dried in that position. There was a passage beside it wide enough for a little shell to fall through, though the loose dry folds closed again as soon as it had passed. The power put a finger there. It felt on one side of the finger the warm close air of the room, and on the other the small ordinary wind which was going through the forest, lifting the hair on the backs of the lizards the wrong way. It turned the dry fan about a quarter of a turn and left it. The stiff midrib held the folds apart.

For most of that afternoon a thread of air came out of the stem smelling of mud. Towards evening it went the other way. Down in the room the first that was known of the change was that the shell on the ledge dried white. Then a climber, a young male with one ear nicked quite through, came up in the old place and took a breath. He hung there a moment with the water drawn snugly in under his chin. He took another breath, turned over and attended at some length to the fur of his stomach. In a day or two the room was in ordinary use again.

The power did not undertake to keep the passage open. The palm was nearly done with standing; already there were mornings when a fine brown meal lay in a circle round its foot. By the time it came down there might be daylight in the roof, or the room might be full of white shell and a good place for a tree. For the present, though, there was air. The small ones coming along behind their mother could stop where she stopped. On going away the power heard one of them discover the shell on the ledge. There was a brief industrious tapping in the dark, and then the little double splash of an animal setting out again with income enough to occupy both hands.
