The first thing the power noticed was the adze in the hill. It had lain there a long while. The cord through its eye was gone, but the stone still had an edge, and it seemed a pity that anyone should go short of a blade with so good a one lying idle. A little pressure from below would have brought it into daylight. Before applying the pressure the power looked to see who was short of a blade.

It found a woman working with a poor one. She had a boy kneeling beside her, and between them they were cutting a step in the floor of a house. To get a purchase on the stone she had wrapped the end of her belt around it. Every few strokes she had to stop and wind the belt up again. The boy wanted to do this part. She let him; it took rather longer.

“It would be quicker with yours,” he said.

“Yes,” she said.

In a while he asked where hers was.

“Behind your foot. Don’t kneel on it.”

The boy looked. There was a very good blade there, lying on a pad of calf hide with its edge turned toward the wall.

“Then why?”

“Feel.”

She gave him the poor blade. He took a stroke or two, cutting very little. On the third stroke he felt a soft bump come up the belt into the web of his hand. He put the blade down and they lifted the shaving together. Below it lay a hem with four young ones, bald except for the little brushes at the ends of their tails. When the light fell on them the tails all curled up. The mother did not wake.

“A sharp one goes straight through,” said the woman.

They went outside to finish their meal. By the time they came back the mother had got the little ones away. She had taken them one at a time, and in the dust on the step it was possible to see how on the first three trips she had hurried back and on the last had had no reason to hurry. The boy got down on his hands to look. The power, which had a good many places to attend to, stayed to look too.

It put the matter of the buried adze by.

What it did do, some time later, concerned the forked limb. There was a season when rain fell twice too close together. On the second occasion the deep layers had no room for more water. For the better part of a day there were streams on the shelf, clear streams running between banks of floating cork, so that old people sat up in the doors of their houses to see a sight they remembered their grandparents sitting up to see. At the meeting of the troughs the water turned in a slow wheel. It took away quite a thickness of rind from one side of the limb and uncovered the dead foot of it. There was not much foot. For a long time the limb had been held up chiefly by the tight fit of the surface around it.

When the water went down it stood a little out of true. This could not be seen from near by. But a party coming up from the western edge kept losing it behind a rise off which, in the ordinary course of walking, it ought to have been lifting steadily. At evening they spoke about it. By then the people under the limb had noticed something too. The aprons in the hole had begun to smell. A spoonful of water, perhaps less, had got past the lip that had kept the hole dry for so many years.

They took the aprons out. Some came to pieces in the hand. There were knots in them nobody knew how to make, which was a greater loss than the thread. A girl spent most of an afternoon undoing one with a bird bone, stopping at each turn to lay a bit of new thread alongside the old. By evening she had a length of mesh the size of her palm. She tried it on the end of her foot and found that you could pull it tight from either end without narrowing the openings. There was a use for that quite apart from catching meat. She went looking for somebody with a baby.

Meanwhile the limb went on leaning. A discussion began as to whether it should be propped. There was no particular harm in its lying down. Birds could drink at a seam without ever having seen it; people who had arranged to meet there could meet there just as well if the highest thing on the skyline were a person looking for somebody else. But there were the peeled handholds, and the old people liked to see them white in the morning. It is as well to say exactly what one wants. Otherwise much time can be spent establishing that a thing is unnecessary, and at the end of it one wants the thing just as much as before.

They decided to try a prop. At the nearest pool a tray limb had died standing up. They cut it loose, keeping a good broad pad at one end, and rolled it for two days along a trough. At night the hems slept under the pad. On the last morning there was a calf beside it too, a late one that had not got the length of its legs yet. It had evidently followed its square of shade a good way in the wrong direction. One of the party had to take it back.

The power had by now learned a little about the fit of one old surface upon another. It could have made that fit fast under the forked limb. It could have held the limb at its old angle until the people who wanted to see it there were dead, or until their children were dead. There was no particular point at which that undertaking would come to a natural end. The people, on the other hand, had gone to considerable trouble to get a prop.

They set the pad end down to spread the weight. There was a difficulty about the other end. While it was being worked into place, two people had to stand directly under the lean of the limb. They went in quite cheerfully, having first had somebody lie down and listen for the tearing of the rind around its foot. There was no tearing. What was giving way was a layer much farther down, turning to paste in the trapped water without making any sound that came to a heel or an ear.

The power held the limb for the time it took them to come out.

It did not take the weight of it altogether. The people at the prop felt a considerable push, enough to tell them that they had set it too upright. They got clear and called the others clear. Presently the pad began to slide. It went very slowly at first, throwing up a little ruck of rind ahead of it. Then the ruck split, the pad shot off down the trough, and the fork came down with a noise like a hand clapped over an ear. Dust stood up on both sides. For a while no one could see anyone else. In the quiet afterward a child said, quite audibly and with considerable interest, “Are we all flat?”

No one was. The prop had gone farther than seemed reasonable. They left it where it was, pad uppermost; later someone made a house against it.

As for the limb, they had never known how big it was. There was room down the length of it for everybody to sit without touching shoulders. The upper two white patches were not peeled at all but worn by the feet of birds, with a fine stippling that you could feel with the ball of a thumb. They found a second hollow high in the fork. It held nothing of interest to people and a great deal of interest to a hem, which had to be drawn out backward by its hind legs and sat washing them for a long time afterward.

At the next meeting a few people wanted to raise one end of the limb again. Most preferred to sit on it. The old ones in particular liked a seat from which it was possible to get up without accepting a hand and making the little noise that went with accepting it. Nobody supposed that this settled the matter for good. Already the damp was getting into the underside. In another generation it would be a soft, comfortable thing to sit on; a generation later children sent to find it would be told to look for a long hump crossing the trough, green on the north side first.

The power went about its other business. It had kept no one there who wished to go, and the people had come away with what could be had of the old knots. Once, much later, it thought to look for the adze. The hill had opened of its own accord along a seam, and there was the eye of the stone, just visible, full of clear water. A bird with its feet braced on the rim was drinking out of it.
