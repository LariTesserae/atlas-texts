The power that had noticed the shelf could distinguish, in the noise of the works, the sound of a glass being set on a table. It could hear the spoon settle against the inside of the empty lunch tin when the woman changed hands. For a little while it attended to what the girl was attending to. From where it was, it could see the mouth perfectly well. It had not known until then what was to be seen from the paving.

Presently the girl got up with a red place on her cheek. Her mother caught the tram. There was room on the front platform that morning and she rode the two stations looking at the back of the driver's head, where an energetic attempt had been made with water to lay down a tuft of hair. The girl went in for her breakfast. Nobody had been made late.

At the laundry the last of the summer linen was being packed for the farms. By noon the bales were gone, and the boy, who had come to fetch a cord he had left in the yard, stood for a moment in the place where he had been able to hear. The works were particularly loud there now. Two iron rings in the wall, formerly held still by the weight of sheets against them, were jumping on their staples. He took his cord and went home to dinner.

It would have been an easy matter for the power to quiet the shelf. The difficulty would have come afterward. The girl would have slept on past school time beside her full glass. In the galleries a man with his hand on a bearing would have had to find some other way of knowing what went on inside it. Besides, it was not at all clear that the boy wanted a quiet city. He had liked taking somebody to the place behind the sheets.

Under the laundry stair there was a room which had once been used for weighing soap. It had no window. The scale had been taken out, leaving a shallow pit, quite dry, and a ledge at a convenient height for sitting. The room stood on the rock itself. With the door shut, one heard very little there except for a tremendous rattling of the latch against its keeper.

On the morning the linen went away, the power moved the keeper the width of a fig seed to the left. There were two small screws to be turned, and it turned them. It did nothing to the door on the yard side, where the lower panel was shiny from the backs of people resting in the shade, or to the long slant of pencil marks inside, by which a soap weigher had once kept track of the height of his sons.

For nearly a year nobody went in. This did not trouble the power, though once in the winter, when a new coat of paint was being put on the laundry doors, it was interested to see whether the painter would paint this one shut. He was a better painter than that.

The following summer the boy came back much taller, with two apples going soft in his pockets. The children found the room in the course of looking for a place to cut the bad parts out of the apples without having to give an account of what they were doing. They sat on the ledge and shut the door. After a time the girl said, quite unnecessarily, “You can hear yourself eat.”

They brought the grandfather there on a Sunday. He sat down with some care, his knees being troublesome, and listened to the boy eating an apple for his benefit. Then he asked the girl to say something. She could not think of anything to say. At last she told him that the mouth of a limpet was square.

“Is it?” he said.

She had heard him speak, of course, in the upper part of town in winter. But it was a long time since they had been up there together, and the voice she remembered as belonging to him was chiefly the loud voice a man uses to a child at his knee when there is a power works under the floor. This one had a little catch in it at the start. It made him sound interested before he had got to the words.

“Nearly square,” she said.

On the way out he examined the latch. The old screw holes showed at one edge of the keeper, two black crescents in the otherwise unbroken paint. He ran his thumb over them with the absent approval of a man who has spent a good part of his life correcting errors of about that size. Then he held the door for the others, and the noise of the shelf came in.
