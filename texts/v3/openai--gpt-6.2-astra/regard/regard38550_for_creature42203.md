The first thing the power did in Olt was to put a little wet grit back where it had been. It had wanted a better look at the fine tail below the boy’s shoe. The grains had come up at its attention as filings come up to a lodestone. The boy was looking at his aunt and did not see. It took the power some pains, afterward, to learn to look without doing this. There were places where a grain or two made very little difference; it could not at first tell which places these were.

For some years it frequented the bridge. It was able to watch the welt through the thickness of the sill, but preferred to wait until the water drew off and watch it from below. In this way it learned, among other things, the considerable difference between an hour in the life of the welt and an hour spent crouching on one’s heels in the grit. It came to know the woman by a small sound she made in getting up, audible just before her head came into view over the bank. In time she had a stick with a crosspiece on it at a good height for getting up by. The boy had made it rather high on purpose. It could be shortened, whereas if he made it too short, he said, what was he to do then?

There was a flood in his fifteenth summer which brought, instead of the laundry, the interior of a house. This happened occasionally where a channel on the other side had been built over in a long run of dry years. A table stood with two legs in the water. On the table there were a lamp, a stack of thin yellow cakes, and one of those blue cups made in the south by pressing clay over the end of a thumb. The woman who lived in the house was on the stairs with her skirt gathered in one hand. She had come down to see what the noise was.

The boy asked if she wanted help with the table. She looked past him, through the arch, and said a name. His aunt, who was on her way down with the first pair of trays, set them on the bank.

It was a twelfth voice for the roof call. There had once been a girl among the eleven, one who could go up the outside of an oven stair almost as fast as the others could go up the steps. She had gone visiting on a flood with her mother’s consent and a good coat, meaning to be away a winter. There had been several crossings since to the district she had set out for. She had not been at any of them. Here she was on a flight of wooden stairs, broader than her mother had been, and with the particular long upper lip that in her family generally came on with the loss of the back teeth.

The boy went for his aunt’s sister, who remembered the girl well. By the time he got back the two women had drawn up to the edge on their respective sides. They had got past the first difficulty, which was that each thought the other must want an account of the time. The woman on the stairs was showing his aunt a cake. They were made in the pan, without an oven. You could make as few as two, she said. Her husband liked them very brown.

The pebble under the bridge swung half the breadth of its notch. The power felt the woman on the stairs gather herself to jump. She was looking at the boy’s aunt. Her bare toes had gone white where they held the lip of the tread.

It would have been a small thing to hold the districts together. It would not have been a small thing everywhere. Already, on the far side of the house, a man had brought his barrow down to a strip of yard which ought presently to come dry. Farther off there were other yards, and flags being lifted over a conduit, and a woman half across a gutter with a hot pot in her hands. The power let the water go.

What it did hold, with rather less force than it had once used to lift the grit, was the blue cup. The table had begun to rock. The cup was travelling toward the edge in little starts, and the woman on the stairs had turned her head at the sound. For the two or three breaths the districts still had in common it stood quite still.

“Shall I come?” the woman asked.

“If you like,” said the boy’s aunt, who had never been able to answer this question any other way, even when it was a small child asking to go with her to the oven.

“I think I shall stay here.”

“Well,” said his aunt. She put up a hand; across the dwindling strip of water the other woman did the same. Then there was a low bank there, and the two trays waiting to be brought down, much too late to do the seedlings any good.

All that afternoon his aunt was very poor company. It was her sister who took the trays to the conduit and soaked them, carrying the water up in a baking dish because the bucket was in use. Toward evening the boy found his aunt sitting on the oven stair with her stick across her knees.

“I would have liked one of those cakes,” she said.

In the house on the other side the woman put the cup back on its shelf. The power, which could have followed either woman with equal ease, stayed a little with this one. She had let the cakes burn cold in the pan and was scraping it out with a bit of wood. After a while a man came in wheeling an empty barrow. He took the wood from her and went on with the scraping while she told him. When she came to the part about being asked if she liked to come, he stopped for a moment, then tipped the pan toward the window to get a better light on what he was doing. She ate the top cake off the stack. The next one she left for him.

The cup remained for many years in that house. It was lost in the ordinary way, in washing up.
