It was at the dish on the oldest knee that the visitor first tried putting down a foot. It had no feet of its own, but to lay a little weight on one side of a skin rather than the other was well within its means. It chose a place a short way ahead of the grazer. A pit came, the grazer felt for it, and the visitor took its weight off at once. It had felt, from below, the hurried reaching-about of an amber sole. What it had meant as a greeting had been taken for a step by both animals, in different directions. They were a little while getting comfortable again.

For some days after this it confined itself to looking. There is a good deal to see on the oldest knee that cannot be seen elsewhere. In particular, the green scales at the fold are so far apart that one can watch the milk-colored stuff actually going round the bend. It goes round very slowly. A speck caught in it at breakfast will by evening have moved about its own width toward the shade. The dish was narrowing because the fold was taking it in. By the end of the summer the black leaf would be on a wall too steep to hold water.

There was no lack of lodgings farther up the field. The next dish had a wide lip, a floor so nearly level that even in the driest weather the leaf kept soft, and, on its underside, a lovely short fur of salt where the water sweated through. Between the two dishes the field was sound. The difficulty was a strip of old stitching, thicker than the rest and very slack on the underside. A foot might go down halfway to the belly there without making the least impression on the ceiling. The visitor found this out by trying it well away from either animal.

The simplest thing would have been to lift the two and set them down again on the good lip. But on the morning when the last water spilled out of the crease the lower animal did not want to go up the field at all. It went down round the knee. Its companion followed until it stood almost on its head in the sun. There was a trickle there, the last of the spilled water taking quite a long way round a scale, and the white coat filled up from it so handsomely that for a while nothing could be seen of the animal but its feet. It took the better part of the morning to comb the water out. The visitor, which would have missed the trickle altogether, waited.

Toward evening the grazer turned uphill. It went as far as the stitching and put its first foot in. Underneath, the amber sole felt forward in the right direction but considerably too far to one side. This time the visitor did not put a foot down. It took a little of the slack up between two stitches, as one might take up the loose stuff of a sleeve to feel a pulse through it. The dent came through quite plainly. The sole found it. As the two went forward the visitor let the stuff out behind them and gathered a little more in front. Once they went back three steps for no reason it could discover. It went back with them. By dark all twelve feet were on firm ground.

They did not settle in the excellent dish. They passed it in the night and were found next day at a mean little saucer not much bigger than the one they had left. It had one advantage. A narrow tongue of its leaf had grown out over the rim and adhered there, so that a grazer need not lean down to eat. The old animal, which had spent most of its life leaning, stood quite level in the sun. Below it five amber soles held fast while the sixth went unhurriedly through the white hairs.

In the slack strip the stitches had settled back, all but one, which remained a little drawn. Rain ran along it that afternoon and fell from a new place on the ceiling, a matter of an inch from the old. The visitor stayed to see where it went.
