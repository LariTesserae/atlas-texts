The power first did something about the steps. It had sat on the top one through a watering day and the day after, and had noticed that although the fern in the joint grew dark all the way out to its tips, the clay at the foot of the steps stayed pale. A leaf came down there from the narrow stocking. By the following morning the animal on it had got as far as the riser. There was a little heap of grit where the riser met the floor, and the animal had gone up the heap and come down it twice. By noon it had drawn itself into a button.

The power put it back. This took considerably less time than finding out where to put it. On the first leaf it lay drawn in exactly as before. On the next, which to the power looked much the same, it let down the edges of its foot. The difference seemed to be that the second leaf had an old split in it, the two sides of which lay one over the other. The animal went between them. As the edges parted to admit the shell there was a momentary view of the heart.

Behind the bottom step, where no one sitting on the steps could have felt it, the power broke off a long sliver of stone. It laid this slantwise from the heap of grit to the joint, with the broken face down. Water from the handful of earth went a little way along the underside at once. By the time the fern was green again it had gone the whole way. From the front it looked as if a rather thick book had been put under the step to stop it rocking. The steps had never rocked.

For the better part of a year nothing used the sliver. Then, one afternoon, there was a track on the clay which ended squarely at its lower edge. There was no track coming out again. The power was pleased, though it did not lift the stone to make sure.

There was a difficulty on a different scale with the main. The wedges of earth under the jacket were getting smaller. One of them fell while the power was there and broke on the floor with the peculiar hollow noise of a dropped flowerpot. Above the empty place the pipe sagged by rather less than the thickness of the enamel on the valve-house sign. A length of hot pipe has to be allowed to grow and shorten. Simply holding it still, which would have been quite easy for the power, was not the answer.

There were people whose business this was. The power found one of them upstairs on a November morning, trying the nursery gate with a key on a long bunch. She had come to find out why the valve house was not where it was shown on her plan. The power did no more than hold the dry stems of a cotoneaster aside while she looked about. Through them she saw the first tread of the stair.

She came down as far as the landing that day. A week later she came back with two other people and a lamp that stood up by itself. They took measurements by its light. One of them went over to the steps to sit down and, finding the sliver at the back, put his hand to it in just the way a person will try a loose tooth. Then the woman called out a number to him and he let it be.

The work they eventually did is quite plain to see. There are two new supports under the main, each with a broad foot on the clay and a curved shoe at the top in which the pipe can move. They brought the parts down the stair. For getting from the landing to the floor they used a ladder, which went away with them. They also put a date on the brickwork inside the valve house, in pencil, following the old practice; the previous date, much higher on the wall, had been put there by someone standing on a floor that no longer existed. The new work made no difference to the *tock*. It made a considerable difference to what happened after the *tock*, although this consisted, for the most part, in nothing happening.

The power had thought of joining the five stockings. A root no thicker than parcel string would have served between each pair, and an animal on a failing fern would then have had the choice of four others. For a while it even held a loose root out across one of the intervals, to see how this would do. Before it had got halfway across, the end nearest the main was dry. There were other ways it might have been done. But the stocking from which it had borrowed the root had turned slightly under the pull, bringing into the warmth a face of the bundle that had always been in shadow. There were white young there under the bark. The power eased the root back into its twist.

It still sits on the steps on occasion. In the joint below there are sometimes seven animals and sometimes two, and for one whole spring there were none to be seen. The little fern put up a fourth frond that year. It came out sideways on account of the stone, a queer low frond with its tip almost on the grit. In late summer a slug could be seen on the underside, taking water at a notch. Its shell was still so clear that the green showed through it. When the bead was gone it stayed with its mouth there a little longer, and got the beginning of the next one.
