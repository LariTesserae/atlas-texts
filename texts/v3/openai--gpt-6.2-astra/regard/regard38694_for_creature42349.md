When they came to take the shoes off, the engineers put a saucer of water on each of the three courts. They had other instruments, considerably more sensitive. The saucers were for people who had been asked to stay behind a rope all morning and were entitled to see something. In the north court a sparrow got into the saucer and had a remarkably thorough wash. They had to do that reading again.

The first shoe came away in the engineer’s hand. It was black on the outside and quite bright where the lintel had borne on it. There was a little screw for adjusting the distance, with one side of the slot chewed up. It interested Emil that the people who had put a school over open water had sometimes had the wrong screwdriver.

By midday the south court was standing on its own ground. The change was first noticed in the classroom, where a pot of pencils walked to the edge of a shelf during the spin cycle downstairs. The teacher put a folded map under it until something better could be found. Under the court, a mole backed out of a run it had been using for years. For a time the whole roof of the run had felt like the inside of a throat when it purrs.

There was something out beyond the bay that felt the washing machine too. A disturbance of that size would not ordinarily have drawn its attention. It had known the coast when the granite was soft enough to take the impression of a falling stone. What interested it was that until that morning there had been no weight there, and now there was a south court, with its burden of people and furniture and a sparrow shaking water out of its ear.

It attended for a while. In its experience a weight arriving all at once generally meant a fall, and there were things it could do about a fall. But the court was well built. Even the pencil pot was a good four fingers from the edge now. It let the court be.

The trouble was a little farther down. The brown layer under the laundry had stopped coming in just short of the brown layer under the school. Between the two was a hand’s breadth of the white stuff, already hard. A mole working along the side of it could smell the worms on the other side. It would dig a little, come to the white face, turn, and dig a little farther along. Already it had gone the length of a sheet. Beyond the sheet was the sump for the laundry waste. Its cover was an excellent fit from above.

It would have been possible to lift the mole out. There was good ground on the mainland, enough for more moles than had ever lived under the ward. But the mole had eaten here and slept here and had gone to quite a bit of trouble over a chamber with dry grass in it. Besides, there would be the next mole.

In the white layer was the shell they had watched through the glass. There had been a winter, before there was a laundry or a sea in that place, when a slight difference in the way the shell lay would have let water in along its lower lip. The power gave it the difference. It was about what the weight of another grain would have made. The water had a long time to work in. By Thursday there had been a soft place in the white stuff for eleven thousand years.

The mole found it shortly after the sheets went in. There was a certain amount of hard pushing to do even there. It came through with white dust on its nose and ate three worms in succession, beginning with the middle one.

Emil was in his chair beside the machine. Since the shoes had come off he could feel the spin cycle very distinctly through the soles of his feet. He found he could also feel the little knock that meant somebody upstairs had let the cloakroom door go instead of walking it back to the catch. Presently there was a rasping just under the chair, so faint he thought at first it was a shirt button going round with the sheets. He put his hand down to the frame. The rasp came again. It went from one back leg to the other and away under the wall.

When his daughter looked in he still had his hand there. She asked if he needed something picked up.

“No,” he said. “Sit down a minute.”

She sat on the tiles with her back against his chair. They waited through the rinse and a good part of the spin. Nothing else went by. Then the load upstairs came out of the schoolroom all together, and for the first time in her life she heard, through the seat of her trousers, what two dozen children on their way to put their coats on sounded like from underneath.
