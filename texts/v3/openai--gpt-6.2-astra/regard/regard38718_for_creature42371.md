The second time they put the bowl down they put it on its side. A bowl upside down is a very good roof, but there is nothing to see under it. On its side, with a flake of shell to keep it from rocking, it made a little white room open to the light. The boy put three streaked animals in the room. Two came out over the flake. The third went to the very back and began to eat something which had escaped the washing up.

From the stair the girl could see the door on the river. There was a black line across it which had not been there the day before. She went down to look. The man with the grinding stone had put his foot through the door on his way home. He had left the stone beside the hole so that nobody coming along in the dusk would put a foot through in the same place. It took up most of what was left to walk on.

There was by this time something else looking at the door. It had come a considerable way to see the city, although distance was not much of a difficulty to it. A greater difficulty was finding out what the door was for. It had seen the people walking on it, and the girl lying on it, and had supposed itself to know; but that morning, before anybody was up, it had seen the clear fish lying quite still in its shadow. As the light moved they moved too. By noon there was a strip of them no wider than a finger, all that remained of a much broader gathering in the dark. The power had stayed to see what the fish did when there was no shadow left. This was how it came to notice the hole.

The first useful thing it did was to take the weight of the grinding stone. It did not lift the stone. It merely held it at the height at which the man had left it, so that the wet fibres beneath could come apart at their leisure. When the girl put her ear to the door she could hear them going, one at a time, like hairs pulled from a seam.

She went for the man. He came back with her somewhat reluctantly, having understood her to say that she wanted him to carry the stone the rest of the way home.

By evening there were eight people at the crossing, with as many opinions about what ought to go there instead. There was an unused door in the lowest enclosure. There were slabs in the old hide market long enough to span the water, but nobody knew how much sound stone there was under the chalk at either end. The file maker, who had been brought down on his grinding seat and was rather enjoying himself, wanted to know why they could not walk through the water. He had done so for the first forty years of his life. One of the women who gathered the dew asked him if he remembered carrying a hot oven shelf through it. After a little while he remembered that too.

It would have cost the power very little to put a good slab across the river. It could feel the sound bed of the channel as easily as a person feeling for the bone in his wrist. But the girl had gone a short distance downstream and was standing with her feet apart, looking down between them. When the others called her she held up one hand. This was the sign a person made at a drain when there was something the matter and it was important that nobody pull a pin just yet. They came to see.

In the milk of the chalk there were two eyes. Presently there were two more. A fish came up out of what appeared to be solid ground, turned so that the last of the sunlight showed green in it, and disappeared head first. The girl felt about with her toes and found the edge of a ledge. There was water running under it. All the way from the door to where she stood, what looked like the bank of the river was only a crust a little thicker than a drinking bowl. Beneath it, in the dark, the fish had a river of their own.

The power, which had felt for the sound stone and found it, had not thought to feel for the water in between.

They fetched the unused door. They laid it a dozen paces upstream where the banks were low and hard, and left the old one where it was. The man took his grinding stone away. It gave him a curious start when he rolled it against his belly: for just an instant, while the power was letting go, he had the strength he remembered having as a young man. Then he had a heavy stone to carry uphill in the ordinary way, with a girl at his elbow asking him to turn his chin up a little, as she was not quite sure who he was.

In a few weeks the old door settled. One end rested on the chalk and one in the water, and the hole, which had been a danger to a foot, was just the right size for a child to put her face over. It was a better place to fish than before. Nobody walked across her back. In the heat of the day, if she tired of fishing, she could lie with one eye to the hole and see the clear bodies coming and going where the chalk shelved off into green. She never saw the end of the shelf.

The power stayed on. It was of use once to a bird with a loop of wet wool drawn tight about its foot, and once, in a rain so heavy that nobody could see across the street, to a woman who had reached for a child's hand and got the empty sleeve. Most days there was nothing of the kind. It grew to know the small preliminary lift of a shell before the animal changed direction. It learned that the old man sometimes said the horn was too dry because he wanted another day to think about the file.

On the upper stair there was still a bowl lying on its side. The third animal had laid eggs in it, a pale rough patch that the boy took at first for something wrong with the glaze. He had his thumbnail under one edge of the patch before he saw, in the wet his thumb had cleared, a great number of exceedingly small horns drawn in and then very slowly put out again.

He lay down on the stair and called to the girl to come and look, and the power, which was down at the baths, came too.
