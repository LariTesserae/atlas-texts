The power first attended to the bearing. A line of oil had been running down the outside of the bearing cup for eleven months. The man who filled it worked by touch, the cup being on the far side of the shaft, and felt oil where he expected to feel it. One morning, as he withdrew his hand, the power let a drop fall on the polished toe of his new boot. He fetched a mirror. The repair took him until dinner.

Below ground the effect was considerable. A sound which had lain over the others like a sheet of tin was gone. It was now possible to distinguish the two blows of the flap, one when it struck the seat and one, much less, when it struck the stop. The power waited through three changes of shift to see whether anything in the room had had a use for the missing sound. The aprons shut at the usual instant. The man at the shaft found he could hear the girl at the next machine, who had been obliged to whistle to get his attention and had come to think him rather surly.

There was a great deal more that could have been put right. The power had only to lay a finger on the main to take the kick out of the cover. It could have made the floor as good as the day the masons laid it. But it had seen what came to rest in the worn place. It left the floor alone.

In November the wash houses received a new boiler. There was to be no return water for four days. The notice was posted on the inside of a door generally kept open against the wall. Plenty of people knew of the stoppage all the same, including a woman in the blanket shed who put by enough hot water on Friday to wash her father's hair on Saturday. Nobody had considered the room. On the plans for the boiler it was a small rectangle with a diagonal stroke across it, meaning existing work.

What troubled the power about the stoppage was not that the room would empty. It had held water before there were wash houses. What troubled it was the start on Tuesday. The north pumps would already be running. There would be no tightening over the main, no half minute in which to get the aprons in. There would be the whole head of the return, let into a room in which for four days the creature had been putting out its edges farther and farther to get a living in the slack.

The power could have shut those edges for it. On Monday night it tried, on a single apron scarcely wider than a shirt button. The track closed at once. A little grain of something red, which had been travelling steadily inwards, fell out and lodged in the nap. A perceptible time passed before the apron opened again, and then it opened first on the side away from the touch.

At six on Tuesday morning the boiler fitter stood with his hands on the wheel of the return. He had been up much of the night. He opened the valve a quarter turn and would have spun it the rest of the way had he not heard, quite distinctly, three knocks on the pipe from below. On the works pipes that meant wait. He waited. A bucket or two of warm water went down the stone throat. On the far side of the room a loose end lifted from a brass cap, tried the water and took hold again lower down. All along the pipes the aprons came in. Presently there was a single knock, and the fitter finished opening the valve. He never inquired who had been below. It was a large job and there were men on it whose names he did not know.

A piece nearly as long as a pencil went up the shaft that morning. The power followed, ready to put it back. At the underside of the cover the piece spread flat, took hold where the raised letters had left hollows in the casting, and stayed. A very fine brown meal collected there. By Wednesday there were tracks in it. By Friday the piece had made a circuit of the last letter of the foundry name, working from the inside, in the dark.

This was a place the power itself would never have thought to put it. After that it came less often. Sometimes, when it happened to be in the district at ten past two, it stood on the cover for the pleasure of the kick. It stood to one side of the keyhole so as not to take the light.
