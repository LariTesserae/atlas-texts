The first thing the power did for a plate-bearer was to put it down again. It had taken the thing for a stone, and wanted to see the weather underneath. The foot came up with a quantity of white thread sticking to it. The two ends drew together. For a while after it was set down there was a pale crescent showing on the uphill side, where the plate had been put back a little out of place. Presently the water end felt its way into the crescent and the rest followed. It was a better fit than the power had been able to make.

After that it did most of its looking from below. It could be in the rock without taking up room there, a considerable convenience in getting to know small animals. Seen from below, a plate-bearer at rest was seldom quite at rest. Now one side of the foot would shorten and now the other. A mite coming up against it went on walking; the flesh gave way in front and closed behind. The bladders, which looked so much alike from outside, were very seldom full to the same degree. Water taken in on one side might spend a long time getting to the other. There was a great deal to see in what, from above, had been six months of a stone lying still.

In the course of this looking the power came to know of a change deep in the western rim. One of the old beds along which water travelled had begun to open into a fault. As yet, at each knock, only a little water went over the new lip. There would come a knock after which all of it would go. The water would appear again a long way south, in a valley with a floor of clean, jointed stone. It had been there before. There were yellow grains under the dust, and in one place the mineral cast of a fan so old that its comb was of a pattern no longer found on the plateau.

It would have been possible to close the fault. It would have been necessary to go on closing it. The country to the south was settling under its load of new stone, and to keep water in the old bed would in the end have meant holding up a fair part of a continent. The power had nothing against this on the score of the work. But it had looked at the open comb of the buried fan. There had been weather in the southern valley too.

Between the last damp slope of the old country and the first slope of the new was a saddle of bare rock. Fog crossed it; very little fog stayed. The power went there in the hard wind and did much what a plate-bearer does in feeding. It lifted a flag, took a little loose material from underneath, and let it down. The upper edge lay a shade lower than before. Water running off the back at the next fog would go under instead of away. It did this again farther on, and again, making no two hollows quite alike, since it did not know which would be any good.

At the first fog nearly all of them filled with grit. At the second, a hollow the power had thought particularly ill made kept its water. There was a split deep in the flag, too fine to admit dust, up which the water went a little way and from which it came down again very slowly. Mites were living there when the fog came back. The power learned something about the setting down of a stone. By the time the old vents began to fail there was a scatter of such places across the saddle. From the height of a hunter's jaw nothing about the skyline had changed.

The crossing took much longer than a journey. A little naked foot got as far as the second hollow, acquired a plate there and in due course went up to a crest from which the southern fog could be felt. Its young went various ways. There were failures on quite hospitable ground, and a flourishing patch of white thread in a place which by all accounts ought to have been dry. The hunters found the saddle. So did a leather grazer with three young still clinging to it. Nothing was shut out.

Long afterward, where the first gray fans were coming up out of the new stone, a plate-bearer let its edge down over company brought back from the crest. One fly went round twice without finding a way in. The power, which had been there all along, very nearly lifted the edge for it. Then the fly put its front feet together, backed a little way downhill and slipped under at a place the power could not have distinguished from the rest of the rim.

Under the plate it was warm. The fly hung up among the others. In a little while it began to work on a bead of its own, and from one of its feet there came loose a yellow grain.
