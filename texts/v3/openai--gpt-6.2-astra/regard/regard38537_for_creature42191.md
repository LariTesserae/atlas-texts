The power came first to the window at the western works. There was a dead moth in the angle of the frame and a brown animal working at it from the wrong side of the glass. It put up one hand, took it down, and tried a little farther to the left. The power could have put the moth outside. Before it had done so a woman came along with a chamois, wiped the length of the sill, and shook the chamois out of the next window. The animal went after the moth by way of the gutter.

The power stayed a winter. It had not known, for instance, how much of the hanging wood depended on a difference in height of rather less than an inch. Where two edges were level, the night damp struck them much alike and ran down out of reach. Let one edge fall a little and a lip of cloud rolled over it into the grass on the other. Next year there would be roots there. To have steadied the mountain at any particular hour would have been to choose the woods of that hour over a great many woods to come.

In February there was trouble at the western works. A worker opening a cupboard found what looked like a length of green cord laid very neatly along the shelf. It was the covering of an electric cable. The copper, still in use, was in the wall. The furnaces had to be shut down, and one casting, which had taken most of a night to pour, cooled with a fault in it. Held up to the light, it showed a flight of fine silver stairs going nowhere.

New cable was ordered in a metal jacket. In the meantime the works intended to close the holes where the animals came in. A man went round on the Friday afternoon putting a chalk ring wherever he found a hole. By the time he had done there were twenty-three rings. Some enclosed a space scarcely wider than the chalk line itself. He was to come back on Monday with a bucket of mortar.

There were young in the wall. The animals used a cavity under the cooling room for a winter nursery; it was dry, and by going down to the bottom of it one could generally find a little warmth, though not always on the same side. The way in was through a chalk ring behind a cupboard. The power might have taken the young out. There were nine of them in three pockets, and there was no difficulty, so far as the power was concerned, in taking out a pocket entire. The difficulty would have begun afterward. A mother returning with her mouth full of moth and finding a piece of bare lath had no particular means of learning where a wise power had gone with her children.

On Monday the man came with his bucket. He moved the cupboard and put his trowel to the hole. At that moment the power let him hear what was on the other side.

It was not an especially pretty sound. One of the young had got a hind foot into the ear of another, and there was a good deal of complaint and a small damp knocking against the lath. The man took his trowel away. The knocking ceased. He put the wooden end of the handle to the wall and his ear to the blade, as he did when he wanted to hear whether water was running in a pipe. By then the foot had come out. He heard a sort of snuffling, and farther off, quite distinctly, the tearing of cloth.

“There's the rest of your apron,” he said to the woman who had come to help him shift the cupboard. She put her ear to the blade in her turn.

They left that hole and four others communicating with it. A patch of whitewash over each of the other rings made a tidy job, except that the traces of chalk which showed through the wash led several people to suppose that all the holes had been done twice. When the metal-jacketed cable came, the woman asked for a short piece left over from fitting it. She slipped it through the handle of the cupboard and screwed both ends to the wall. The cupboard could now travel a little on its slab but could not travel far enough to stop the hole. In the accounts the short piece was charged to mice.

The power did nothing further there. The animals continued to take things, including, that spring, a soft pencil from the surveyor's open case. He found it in June when he went down the maintenance stair to take a sight along the underside of the meadow. It lay crosswise in the grass just out of reach, its yellow paint scored to the wood in pairs of little parallel lines. While he was considering how to get it back, a hand came through the flowers, felt about for the pencil, and drew it in another inch. He finished his figures with the spare.
