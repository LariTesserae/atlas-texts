The power that had come to the basin stayed for the meal. It had expected a quick business. In fact the little hook had to turn the fry lengthwise, and there was not room in the hollow to do it. For a while the tail stuck out into the current. Each time it fluttered the white point slipped a little nearer the gill that held it. Then a foot came down upstream, making an eddy in which the tail swung quietly round. The whole bright length went in. A scale remained on the lip of the hollow. You could see the lines on it, very fine, like the lines left by water on a shore.

What the power had noticed, coming from a long way off, was a bird walking backward. Its companion had let go of the frill some time before. The bird had eaten its end, cleaned the pleats of skin out of the corners of its mouth, and gone on walking backward with its head down and its wings a little raised. It backed into a sitting bearer, fell over the far side and got up, still backing. Something had come amiss at the bend. The two or three paces proper to the tearing of a frill had caught there. Each time the bird put a foot down it found another of its own backward paces coming up under it. By the time the fry was eaten it was a quarter of a mile from where it wanted to be. There was blood on the knobs of its ankles.

It would have been quite easy to pick up the bird. But the paces were still there, and the little gray creature, which had begun to draw itself together, lay directly in their course. There were feet enough in the passage by now to open it out for the length of the sill.

The power went down on the gravel behind the bird. It put out a foot rather like the bird's own, with a thick pad behind and three long toes in front, the inner one a little crooked. At the next tread it took its share of the backward step. Then it put out another foot. For a few yards there were two birds walking backward, one of them visible only as a pair of dry places coming and going in the gravel. The hurt bird began to go more slowly. It looked round over its shoulder. When at last it found it could stand it did a curious thing: it squatted down quite flat in the water and put its bill under one wing. The water came well up over its eyes. It stayed like that until it had to breathe.

By then the other feet were going briskly. There was no getting rid of the paces by wishing them finished. A backward step is a fairly complicated affair. There is the grip, the lift, the feeling about behind for a place to come down; every one of these had to be gone through, and the bird in its fright had made a good many. The power walked backward all that night. It went over the sill in the dark with sheets of thin ice breaking against the backs of its legs. Toward morning it came out on a bar which the falling water had left bare. The last pace was quite short. It ended with the three toes spread in the mud and the crooked one sunk rather deeper than the rest.

In the basin the hurt bird had slept through two blows. At the third it rose with the others. For some days afterward it came down to feed on one foot, with the worse ankle drawn up into the feathers of its belly. It did not give up eating the gray creatures. Before the week was out it and another bird had an end apiece and were walking apart with the usual care. This time, when the frill tore, they both sat down.

There was a change on the bar below the sill. In each of the deeper prints a little water stood after the river had gone down. When the young hooks came over, one washed into the mark of the crooked toe and lodged there. All summer it was no more than a white speck in a cup of black mud. In autumn the bar went under again and the prints filled level with grit. The next time the power came that way it could find no sign of its passage, until it set a foot down in the shallows and felt, very precisely, where not to tread.
