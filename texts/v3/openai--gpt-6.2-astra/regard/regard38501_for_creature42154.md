What the boy hears at the wall is not the whole of the sound being made at the other end. At the other end it is a considerable undertaking. A pocket of air has to be eased out from under a ledge, divided round a tooth of lime and brought together again at the lip of the shell. If the two halves arrive separately the result is the preliminary blurt. There are mornings when the maker of the sound would like to be able to put down its instrument and go home.

It was in the bay before the bay came up. In those days, if it wished to move a little warm water into a bed of spawn, it had the weight of the tide to do it with. It could turn a grounded whale sufficiently far on its side to let the weight off a flipper. There are still whales swimming in deep water with a strip of white down one side where their grandmothers lay on the beach. Now what it has to work with is chiefly the difference in weight between salt water and water that is not quite so salt. It has got very good with this. For the purposes of a horn lesson it would sooner have the whale.

It likes the boy. It likes particularly the small intake of breath, quite audible through the shell, at the end of the sixth note. At first the boy took this breath in order to play. Now he takes it and waits. There is very little in the experience of the old inhabitant of the bay to correspond to having a place kept for it in a piece of music.

One morning the four loose tiles are taken up. The boy comes down with his jar and finds them standing on edge against the wall, much thicker than anybody would have supposed and a handsome blue on the side that nobody sees. Two men have a bucket of setting paste and a board with NOT THIS WAY on it. The back of the board says NOT THIS WAY too, which makes it a difficult thing to set down anywhere in a public place.

Under the tiles there is a shallow basin. All these years the market carts have been going over it and grinding a teaspoonful of lime out of it at a time. In the bottom of it lie eleven pink fish with their fins laid over, and one very small one whose fin still stands straight up. The men have put a wet copy of the threshold regulations over the hole to keep the light out. They are decent men. They have been looking for somebody with a jar.

The boy is allowed to lift a corner. One of the fish is lying with its eyes against the printed paper. He can see the seam of its mouth delicately opening and closing on the word *otherwise*.

His jar will hold them. The difficulty is what to do when they are in it. There is not a well in the city that goes to the right water. Nor will it do to send them through the threshold; there are four perfectly sound tiles at the far end, and beyond them a woman selling ribbons.

What lives in the plain could take them back. It could draw the water down through the floor of the basin with force enough to fetch the fish along after it. But it knows these fish a little better than anybody upstairs. The very small one would go through nicely. The others have little stiff bones now, in places where once they could have folded. It would be like pulling a hand back through a bracelet that it has worn since childhood.

Toward noon the grandmother comes along to see what has become of the boy. By this time the older of the two men is holding the thick end of the stick they use for stirring the paste. She sits down beside him. The noon interval begins, the thresholds fall quiet, and from under the wet regulations comes a soft, inexpert blurt.

‘Oh,’ says the boy. ‘I usually go up.’

He has his horn with him. He plays sitting down, rather softly on account of the fish. At the sixth note a bubble comes up from the basin, followed by another some distance away, from under the skirting of the ribbon shop thirty miles off. The woman there puts down her scissors. All along the line between the two districts water is rising, not in the great travelling hump that goes before a cart, but with the almost imperceptible steadiness with which a tear fills an eye.

There is a way round for the fish. It goes by something like eight miles of gallery. To get them over the sill at the entrance requires the whole head of water as far as the ribbon shop, and the old inhabitant has been waiting since the tiles were lifted for the passing of the last cart. What it has not reckoned on is the setting paste. A white dribble from the stirring stick has gone down the joint and is hanging there, thickening in the salt.

The boy hears the seventh note begin and stick. It is precisely the noise he makes himself when the inside of his upper lip has dried on his teeth. Without particularly thinking what he is doing he scoops up a handful of brine and sluices it along the joint.

The seventh note comes out. It is nothing like the note the mistress has been trying to get out of him. It is low and beautifully rude and lasts for as long as it takes twelve fish to go sideways over a sill. The men laugh so much that neither of them sees a fish go. The grandmother sees the last one. For an instant, in water deep enough to swim upright in, it turns its two eyes away from the rock. Later she has to say six or seven times that there was nothing remarkable about them. They looked like eyes.

They put the tiles back on little feet of blue offcut, with room beneath for a hand laid flat. The repair takes less paste that way. After some discussion the older man writes this down as the reason.

Up in the school yard the mistress has had a curious experience. At noon the little shell in the wall played six notes in quite unmistakable imitation of her pupil, down to the catch in the third where one of his front teeth is coming in. Then there was a pause. She has taught for thirty years and knows perfectly well when it is her turn. Having no instrument at hand she sang the seventh.

This is a thing the old power had not until then understood about the boy. He cannot be in both places. If he is down among the tiles with his knees getting wet he is missing the woman singing in the yard. To the old inhabitant, for whom the two places are the opposite faces of a shell, it seems at first a very poor arrangement. It considers whether anything can be done.

But that evening the boy goes to his lesson, and the mistress has to tell him about the shell, and he has to tell her about the fish. She fetches down a blue enamel dish to be the basin. They get the grandmother up from the shop to show them which way the last one turned. Long after everything has been made perfectly clear they are still sitting there, finding other things to put in the dish.

The old inhabitant lets the arrangement stand. Next noon it is back at the tooth of lime a little before it is due, practising with a very small bubble so as not to be heard.
