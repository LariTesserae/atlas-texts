The power that took an interest in this household had some difficulty getting acquainted. It had been concerned before now with a river, and with a very large stand of beech. Neither had minded its coming straight in. Here it had got as far as the underside of the floor before it remembered to give the water a slap.

The old woman put down the slipper she was working on. The rings reached the wall. Nothing came up.

“Well?” she said.

A sort of dimple appeared in the floor, the wrong way up for a dimple, and burst.

“You haven't got a head,” the old woman said, after a bit.

This was the difficulty exactly. She took one of the scraps left over from cutting a sole and set it on the water, rough side uppermost. It was about the length of her thumb. It crossed the floor to the opposite wall, came back and stood quite still under her hand.

“That's better,” she said. “Do you mind if I finish this? There's a boy wanting it for tomorrow.”

The scrap went to and fro while she worked. It was very pleasant to the visitor to be in a room where somebody was working on so small a thing. From its previous acquaintance with the mountain it understood very well why the boy wanted something between his foot and the white stone. It had not known about the bit at the back of the heel that must be turned down smooth or it would raise a blister.

It came again. Sometimes the daughter was there and sometimes she was not. The people of the house got into the habit of leaving the scrap on the floor as one might leave a stool handy. They called the visitor Backside, from the stamp on the leather, which had been cut from a piece already marked out for another use.

It was the daughter who thought of asking it to do something about the middle house.

“I don't mean keep her out,” she said. “It's a long way down to go for nothing. Could you keep a little air in it?”

The scrap went under, which it did when Backside wanted to go and look at a thing. The old woman went on picking a knot out of a length of gut. It was a perfectly good length if she could get the knot out.

In the middle house there was still a dead wasp stuck fast in the white of the ceiling. Backside found the notch, and the old cane socket, and the narrow bed that the daughter had slept in until she was grown. It would not have been much trouble to furnish the room with air. There was a seam quite close. But the woman had lived a whole house farther down than this, and the one who had taught her to put away the key had lived farther down still. Backside went back up to ask how far they wanted it to go.

“Oh, leave the houses be,” the old woman said. “I know where I am as soon as I feel for the notch. But there's a thing I'd like down there, if you're willing.”

She had got the knot out. She wound the gut on two fingers and put it away.

“Coming back up, about halfway, there's nothing to take hold of. There used to be a rail there. People say, Kick off good and hard and you'll be at the top before you know it. Well, I know it quite a while before I'm at the top.”

She showed Backside what she wanted. It was only a grip, with room behind for the ends of the fingers, and set on a slant so that with the arm straight the wrist lay easy. She made it of clay against the side of the shelf. The daughter tried it and said it was upside down. The mother got her to slide into the water and try it from below.

“Oh,” said the daughter.

In the morning there was such a grip in the white face halfway down to the middle house. It was white too, and would have been rather hard to find except that the daughter took a length of red cord down and tied it round. Other people used it almost at once. An air keeper with a load of cane was particularly glad of it. He came by that evening to say that whoever had put the handle there ought to be told it was a good one. He had hung his whole bundle on it while he got a splinter out of his palm.

Backside was in. They passed this on.

After a time the clay model was wanted by the potters. There were stretches like that on several streets, and it was as easy to fire half a dozen grips in the waste space round a bell as to leave it empty. The old woman had to go to the work bell to show them how to set the first batch. She went by way of the middle house, with a good strong pull on the grip coming back, and got there rather late and in an excellent temper.

Backside continued to visit. It had never before been in a place where there was any particular way to come in, or any difference to the people inside between its being there and its being gone. One afternoon it arrived to find the scrap missing. The old woman had finished a pair of slippers for a very small child and had wanted just that much leather to stiffen a heel.

“There's your stool walking about,” she said. Through the floor one could see the two little pale soles going along the rail, hand over hand on the underside of it, in the way peculiar to children wearing something new on their feet which they wish to keep in sight.

The old woman set the lid of her workbox afloat instead. There was a little jar in it as it took the visitor's weight. She pushed off from the shelf and put her head down through the floor to see how the slippers did at the turn of the street. Backside stayed with the box. There were three needles resting in the groove of the lid, and by now it knew enough to keep them dry.
