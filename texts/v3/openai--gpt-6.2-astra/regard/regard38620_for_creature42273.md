The first time the power lay down in the sedge it did so rather badly. Its chin came down across two stems, and the tip that hung in the entrance moved perhaps the width of a snail. It was enough. Six or seven bladders went off at the near end, out of turn. By the time the run from the far end reached them they had nothing in them. There was a patch on the floor where the springtails went on grazing in the midst of the most tremendous noise.

After that the power lay a little farther back. It liked the view from there about as well. It could see one of the liverworts entire, with the thin place in its flat head where the light came through. It could see two of the eggs under a knob and, by the shadow on the white skin, tell when the animal was there attending to a third.

It had come with some notion of being useful. In particular it seemed a pity that the last animal in a drinking row should have such a poor share. The power watched one of these for the better part of a dry morning. At about noon the middle animal, having had enough, walked away. The little one took six rather hurried steps down the bank and put its mouth to the wet hairs from which the other had just removed its own. Its sides, which had lain in a crease, filled out. Presently it had had enough too, and walked away with a gait considerably less tidy than the one it had come with. For the rest of that dry spell the power was content to keep its chin off the stems.

There came a night, some summers later, when a willow limb fell into the marsh. The butt remained caught high in the fork of the tree. Most of the weight lay out in the sedge, but a short broken branch underneath had come down upon the arch. It made a dent you could have covered with the ball of a finger. Underneath, the roof had descended into the well. The water stood at the same level as before but there was no sky in it. On one side the brown seam had come apart, its two ends turned out upon the bank with the fans opening and closing in the air.

The power put a hand under the fallen limb. It did not lift it very far. It lifted until it felt the short branch come free of the arch, and moved it sideways until its broken end rested on a piece of the old hard root. The butt stayed in the fork; the twigs out in the sedge scarcely stirred. By morning there was a little water in the dent on top. A midge stood on it on four feet and washed the other two.

Below, the roof came back rather slowly. For several days there was only a bright sickle in the well. When the first run got through, the drops striking the low part of the roof ran along it instead of falling and collected at the end in one drop of a quite disproportionate size. This let go without any warning click. It turned a flat snail completely over. The snail put out its foot, a pale broad thing in that light, and took hold of the felt and righted itself. A little animal already on its way to the shell had to stop and take its bearings all over again.

By autumn there was room for the breeze under the whole roof. One row of bladders never worked again. You could hear the gap in the clatter if you knew where to listen. The fallen limb remained where it was, and in the course of time went soft, beginning at the broken end. The power came back now and then. It had learned to find the little animal without waiting for a snail to tip. The trick was to watch a shed shell in a dry place between the flutes. Sometimes, for no more than a moment, the shell was brown instead of white. Then it was white again, and if you looked a little way beyond it, there went the six legs, holding the narrow belly well clear of the ground.
