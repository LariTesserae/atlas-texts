The first thing the power did was to go down the steps with the attendant. It could have seen the whole yard at once, including the undersides of things, but it wished to see what he saw. At the last turn it saw the place on the fern where his fingers went. The stem was polished there, for about the width of a thumb. On the far side of the same stem a frog was sitting with its forefeet up against its mouth. The attendant did not see it. His hand went round the polished place and the frog went up with the frond and came down with it again.

The power stayed for a season. There were things it would have got wrong on a shorter acquaintance. The tilted bench, for instance, looked as though it ought to be shored up. Yet it had gone down as far as it was going to go; the foot that had rusted through was resting now on the brick course beneath the mould. At the low end of it, on a single night in April, seven young frogs walked aboard. There was a loose pane, too, which admitted quite a lot of cold air. It admitted most of the gnats.

What troubled the power in the end was a little book in the attendant’s coat. When he had oiled the pumps he took it out and put a tick against two printed words, *Pumps, circulating*. The book had a page for each place he had to visit. There were six such places. In June he was to give the book back, together with a square key and two ordinary ones, and a younger man who had nine places of his own was to take on these six as well. The two men were allowed a morning to go round together. The morning had already been fixed. It was going to be fine.

The power took the rain that would have fallen early that morning out at sea and brought it in over the town at half past ten. It did nothing to the amount. In the yard the two men had to wait on the landing while the younger one took off his spectacles and wiped them, having looked up at the pots.

They were at the third stopcock when the drain began. The attendant put a hand out, and the younger man, who thought he wanted a spanner, had the bag half open before he understood that he was being asked to stand still. There was a frog quite in the open on the iron wheel. Its toes were folded over the edge where the red lead had worn away. As they watched, it drew one hind foot up under itself and put the other out, a slow and rather comfortable adjustment, like that of a man on a stile. The younger man could see the throat working.

‘Do they get in the strainer?’ he asked.

The attendant said he had never had one in the strainer. He showed him where they went instead. It took some time to get the younger man’s eye in. Once he had seen the first pair of feet under a pot rim he found two more pairs for himself, one of them belonging to a frog so small that he took his spectacles off again to see it better. They missed the bus that would have taken them to the recreation ground. There was another in twenty minutes.

The power did not put anything into the book. It had considered doing so, but there was very little room on the page, and whatever it wrote would in time acquire a box beside it in which somebody would have to put a tick. The younger man did write something, on the inside of the cover. It was the number of the older man’s house. He wanted to know where to drop off a few tomatoes later on, if his plants did as well as they promised.

In July, coming down alone, he found the fern across the stairs cut back to the stem. For a moment he thought someone else had been at work in the yard. Then he saw the whole frond lying below, brown at the break, with the new one still wound up hard beside it. He could walk straight down now without putting out his hand. It was odd how quickly one had come to expect the weight.

He left the fallen frond where it was, except for drawing its tip clear of the door. By the time it had dried, the new one reached to the iron handrail. There was a morning in September when he had to lift it. He took hold quite low down.
