What became of the small ones below the lip was of some interest to the power that had taken notice of the range. It went down with one. The animal had been over the edge for six days and was by this time on a nearly vertical face of limestone. There was food on the face. Twice in the course of the descent the little hem had crossed a bed in excellent condition, with fish working both ways from the spot where the sulphurous water came out. It had eaten quite well as it went through. The difficulty was in getting a second meal.

It came to rest at last under an overhang. For the first time since leaving the pass it had a good indication, in the movement of the grains on its back, that the water was slack. It put down its forward pad. There was nothing there. The grains had given it perfectly good news about the water and none whatever about the stone.

The power put a finger there.

A finger, in this case, was a little shelf of the same limestone, about as long as the animal and just wide enough to take the pad. The hem went round on it, found the wall with its other end, and stayed all night. The power had not known until then how a hem slept. The fans came out singly, at long intervals, without much regard to the stroke, and were put away quite empty. Once, all along the belly from the narrow end to the nursery, there went a very slow wash of colour. There was nothing the matter with it. In the morning it fed.

There would have been no great difficulty in putting the animal back by the boot-shaped flint. The power could even have put back the six days. But the little hem had got somewhere which no other hem had got to. Just above it, running under the overhang, was a line of flour as thick as the edge of a blanket. A blunt-headed fish went along it upside down, scattering a considerable breakfast. On the first stroke the breakfast went out into three thousand metres of water. On the return a little of it came in under the shelf.

The power left the shelf.

For a while it made a great many such places. It was possible, by attending to the underside of the passes, to know just where a descending animal would next put a pad and miss. At each of these points it brought out a little stone. There was a season in which scarcely a young hem went down the face without finding, in the very place where it reached, a purchase. The animals collected in surprising numbers below the gravel. A camera sent down at the end of that season showed, at a depth where nothing had previously been recorded but white threads, a sort of broken pink stair.

It also showed an ivory ray going down the stair. The ray had discovered that the new shelves made good corners in which to put a crab. It took perhaps one in five of the crabs it drove there. The others wedged themselves behind the hems. By the end of the next season the face was littered with loose skins, and the bacterial beds immediately below the crowded places had been scoured quite white by the fish. These were getting fat on the flour that the crowded hems, opening into one another's fences, failed to take. The young hems were no longer doing particularly well. Nor were the crabs. The ray was doing splendidly.

The power did not take it out on the ray. It lay for a considerable time on the dark side of the mountain with a wing on either side of it and felt, through the body of the ray, the small hard knock that a running crab transmits to stone. It was an agreeable faculty. So was that of the crab, which could taste with a foot whether the hollow into which it was about to back already contained a crab. In one such hollow there were seventeen. From inside, the white underside of the ray passing the opening had the appearance of a shutter being tried from time to time by a patient customer.

The power left the occupied shelves alone and stopped making stairs. It had been attending rather too exclusively to the instant at which a foot came down.

What it did after that was less easy to photograph. Along much of the east face the limestone beds were set on end. But there were places where the range had folded in its making and a bed turned, in the course of fifty or sixty metres, from a wall into a floor. A hem that reached one of these places might go on down, or might be swung well in on the floor; either way there was stone within reach of the loose pad. The floors were a long way apart. They differed greatly from one another. Some were swept too hard to hold flour. Some ended in bowls where the water hardly moved and the sulphur accumulated. A few had fish and crabs and a good clean exchange of water, and no hems.

At the pass of the loose slab, one of these folds came to within the breadth of a hand of the gravel. Between the two was a split in the flint. Nothing much could cross it at the turn of the water. The power fitted one of the chips from the bottom of the split back into the hollow it had come out of. The fit, as has been said, was still very good. A little triangular piece stuck out. This was all it did there.

It was nearly four years before a hem went that way. In the interval the piece of flint was used chiefly by the transparent six-footed animals, which came out on its upper surface at noon. The first hem to cross was not a young one. It was the old animal by the boot. Something had altered in the seam above its station; for several months the fish had been feeding farther north. One morning, in a photograph taken for quite another purpose, the boot could be seen entire, with a pale narrow patch beside it that had not blushed at noon for at least fifteen years.

The old hem took most of a summer to get down the fold. It spent three weeks on the piece of flint. For part of that time its nursery hung over the split, and two of its young set off along the old gravel and one went down the fold ahead of it. No one kept an account of the others. Below the fold the old animal came to a place where the bed broadened and a thin stream of flour went curling out from behind a knob. Here it put its tucked edge into the stone. The knob was of no particular shape. It would have been a difficult place to find a second time.

It is there now. At that depth a boat makes no shadow and the loose slab is felt chiefly through the tucked edge, a double touch too slight to interrupt a meal. The old stripe of flint still crosses the animal's back, though there is no flint within several metres. A small ray sometimes lies on it. When the ray gets up there is a moment in which the white underside, the apricot fence and a quantity of flour all hang out together in the black water; then the stroke takes the flour, the wing folds over, and the mountain shuts.

The power still goes about there. It does not keep every loose pad from missing. On the last descent it found the little hem with which it had begun, or else a daughter of that animal, on the underside of the first shelf. The top was quite bare. It had never occurred to the power, in making a floor no wider than a finger, that what might be wanted in that place was a roof. Under it the water eased a little earlier than on the face. First the small pink fans came out; then, in the shelter they had found for themselves, the larger ones.
