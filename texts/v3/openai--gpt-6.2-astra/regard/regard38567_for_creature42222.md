The first thing the power did was to listen from the other side of the ceiling. There the cricket made a quite different sound. There was very little of the vessel in it. One heard the separate catches of the wing, and, a little after each, a dry sound a long way off to the left, where two faces of stone that had been fast together for some thousands of years were touching now in only three places. On a cold night there were two places. In the hour before the sun reached the ridge there was one.

It would have been possible to take the weight of the ceiling. The power did take it, experimentally, for less time than a bird takes to draw its white lid across its eye. The cricket went on working its wings. Nothing came of them but the little noise they made themselves. The mites went by its hind foot without stopping, and the water lay level about the swimmer. Then the power let the weight down again, so nicely that the note began in the middle, without the stroke that ought to have begun it. Several birds looked up.

What the power had felt in that instant was chiefly the weight of the ridge *behind* the ceiling. There was no holding the one up for good without undertaking a considerable part of the other. Also the white line was not the only white line. There was one beginning in the fallen wedge, in the upright trough where the bird sat to attend to its feathers. A very small weight indeed would be enough to part the lip of that trough. Below the lip, buried to a depth of two fingers in snow, there was a ledge. It was broad at the back and had an overhang. There were six pale crickets under it now, all the issue of the one that had stepped off the foot. The noise still came from one cricket only; the other five were not yet furnished with wings.

On the next warm afternoon the power put the requisite weight on the lip. A spoonful of stone fell away. Snow water, which had hitherto filled the trough to the point of running over on the exposed side, came out through the notch on the sheltered side instead. It cut a round hole through the snow. At the bottom of the hole was black stone, warm to the foot even with snow all about it, and into that convenient place a bird putting its feathers in order presently tucked one foot. When it had done that it had only to let its weight down to be sitting in the dry under the overhang. It sat there till evening. The water went on past it in a little glass rope that did not quite touch its tail.

That was all the power did there. It was not a second hollow, nor would it do for one. There was room for perhaps eighteen birds, and the water ceased at sundown and began again uncomfortably late. But a bird that had spent the night there came up from below the next morning instead of dropping from above. Several birds, seeing it come, put their feet out. By the end of the summer there was scarcely a ledge on that side of the wedge that had not been tried with a pair of feet. Some were much better than the one under the trough.

Up in the hollow the young bird no longer had to go out backward. It still slept on its side, although there was room now to stand upright in the middle of the floor. One afternoon its open eye saw the little rye-grain chip come out of the ceiling. The chip came quite slowly, at first, with a fringe of rubbish clinging to one end. A mite on the fringe got as far as the edge and stood there working its forelegs in the air. Then the chip turned over and fell. It landed among the last white threads behind the bird's ear. The bird gave its head a shake, but the rubbish was soft and went in deeper. All afternoon there was a small going and coming on the skin behind its ear, of which it took less notice than one might suppose.

When the ceiling went it went at noon. The birds at the sill fell outward into daylight and the ones on the floor went with them as if the whole quantity of silk had been pulled out by a corner. There was a moment in which the basin, with the swimmer in it, stood full and quite uncovered on the face of the mountain. The power, which had been attending to the great mass of falling stone, noticed this. It cupped a little of the spray from the falls. For a time, invisibly among the visible drops that went up beside the cliff, there was a drop that went down. The swimmer went round the inside of it with its bubble on its head.

There were a great many places to put it. The power tried the water of several with the tip of the drop before it let the two waters run together. The place it chose was neither on the wedge nor very far from it, a pit worn in the rock by a pebble which was still there. The swimmer encountered the pebble on its first circuit. It had never encountered anything in the middle before. It backed off and went round again, this time keeping its belly hairs in touch with the rim.

For some days afterward the power stayed by the new scar, listening. There were losses. Under stone so thick that no wing could ever set it sounding there were little rooms that would not be heard again. But on the fourth evening a bird came to sleep on the broken lip of the trough. It put its head under its wing, exposing a patch of warm bare skin behind one ear. Out of the feathers above it came a speck carrying something almost too large to get between the shafts. It was a clear egg with two red points in it. At the very end of a feather the mite had to wait a while. Each time the bird breathed out there was a gap. Each time it breathed in the feather just touched stone.
