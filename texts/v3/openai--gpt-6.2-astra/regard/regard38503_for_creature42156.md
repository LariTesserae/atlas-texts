It was the moving of the foot that the power noticed. From the lower air this was a rather considerable event. The bench stood across a band, with three of its legs on the band and the fourth on a bit of ordinary ground. When the woman took her weight off one heel, the bench rocked. Far down among the hanging skins a strip no wider than a finger turned its other face to the light. The bird felt the turn through the sole of its free foot and got a grip before it had to pull the crooked claw loose.

The power could have straightened the claw. It had it in mind to do this until it saw the bird asleep. The birds slept with one foot shut and the other hanging open, and changed feet several times in a sleep. This one slept on the crooked claw, with both feet open. There was a smooth hard place on the skin it used, polished by the inside of the hook. It would have been a pity to have straightened it at that particular hour.

On the next slack evening the power put a little of its weight on the fourth corner of the bench. It did not take much. The woman had been obliged to move her foot rather farther every evening, and this time when she moved it the bench stayed still. She put her foot back. The little third tap came again, very distinctly.

She bent down to look at the leg. There was a gap under it big enough to take the tip of her finger. She called across to the youngest inspector, who was on her way home with her apron bundled up against her middle and the deer walking so close that it kept putting a foot on her shoe.

“Would it trouble the street,” the woman asked, “if this went up and down?”

The inspector put her heel on the band. The woman rocked the bench. They tried it at three places a little way apart. At the third the inspector could feel nothing, though the bench was rocking quite freely.

“Do you want it to stop?”

“Quite the reverse,” said the woman.

They moved the bench there in the morning. The power took its weight away as soon as they had hold of it. Under the short leg the woman put a piece of an old floor covering, nap down, which kept the leg from going down with a bang. There was still room enough for quite a useful movement. For the first few evenings she did it whenever she heard the third tap. Then she discovered that the better time to do it was just after the first. The bird got its foot free without a tug. She could tell because there was a pause where the tap ought to have been, followed by the ordinary traffic of two sound feet going along under the trays.

This was satisfactory, though it left her with no way of knowing the bird from any other bird once it had gone past her bench. She mentioned this to the inspector.

“You could wait for the third tap once in a while,” the inspector said.

“I could,” said the woman. She went on doing it after the first.

The power stayed for the good carrying weather. There were things it wanted to see that could not be seen on a slack evening. It saw the woman send a coat with a tooth sewn into the hem, not in the expectation that the tooth would travel, but because the owner was a child who had lately gone to live in the east and who had never seen the exchanges work. The coat went and the tooth dropped into the tray and the child, home for a visit, put both hands to her mouth in delight. It saw a bridge house let go of the ground all by itself, at noon, with a noise that brought the two inspectors out from under another house looking rather affronted. It saw the birds come over the rim so thickly that a deer had to stand for some time with one forefoot lifted before there was a safe place to put it down.

Late in the year it looked in under the exchange again. There were two birds sleeping there. One had both its feet open. Above them the woman was asleep too, with her head on her folded coat and one ear lying across the tooth tray. In her sleep she shifted on the bench, and the strip turned, and the sleeping bird swung out a little and came back. It did not wake. The power went on along the underside, taking care, where the fibers hung thick, to pass between them edgewise.
