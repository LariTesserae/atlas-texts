The power that had taken notice of the bar was an old one and knew the undersides of islands better than the upper sides. It knew, for instance, what became of a cold thread when it came up through warm water. It had not known that a man might dry his hand in his armpit for the pleasure of feeling it twice. For some time it did nothing whatever but ride in the ferry with him.

It went along with the sandal, too. The sandal turned out to draw more water at the heel than at the toe. It backed under the first crossing, swung round in a little fall of sweet water from a laundry yard and came out with the moth still on it. At the last crossing there was a woman lying on her belly with her arm in the crack up to the shoulder. She brought the sandal out. The grandmother thanked her and put it back.

When they got to the shrimp pools the child took the moth off and laid it on the water. The sandal she rinsed and slapped against her thigh before giving it back. She had seen this done. Her grandmother put it on, though there was quite a lot of grit left in it.

There was something wrong at that end of town. The power felt it particularly when the old woman stamped her heel to settle the sandal. A shelf of gray lava under the last landing had parted from the sound rock. There was a space under it ample enough to take a house, with a little beach of ground shell, very white, that no one had ever seen. Each time the ferry nosed in, the bottom step gave a little on its bed, not so much as the thickness of one of the scales on the girl's leg. Presently it would go down with whoever was on it. There would be no more notice than that.

The power could have held it up. Indeed it did hold it up for the rest of that afternoon, when the ferry came in with a load of jars and twelve people went down together to help. But it was not a power that stayed always in one place. There were long stretches of coast beyond the haze, and once away from the bar it might have to stay away a hundred years. It wanted the people who used the landing to know what was under it. This proved quite easy to arrange. It let a handful of the white shell come up in the cold thread.

The boat mender, who had taken his hand out to give the master his three inches, put it in again. When it came up he had a few dazzling grains on the cushion of his middle finger. He tried one between his teeth. At the next landing he got off and fetched a length of line.

It was late before they had the soundings to his satisfaction. By then nearly everyone living on the backwater knew there was a hole under the steps. A great many of them had known for years, to hear them tell it that evening. Even so, when a fish seller came along with a basket on her head next morning, she found the steps gone. The slabs had been carried off in the night, and the master was waiting for her a little way along the shore, where she could hand her basket down from the lip of the rock. He received it very handsomely with his stomach held in. The fish seller, with both hands on the basket, did the hitch of his hip so well that he had to set it down.

The shelf went three days later. It let itself into the backwater at a slant without putting up much of a splash. The people in the houses nearest felt their stools jerk under them. Outside, the water cleared before noon. There was the white beach, open to the daylight now and about the size of a sleeping mat. A gray bird had already been along it from end to end.

When the power went away, the girl and her grandmother were on the new piece of shore. The girl had walked some distance backwards, setting her feet very carefully in her own prints, and was trying to get back onto the rock without leaving a last pair pointing the wrong way. Her grandmother reached down and lifted her by the armpits. Then they stood looking at the tracks of a child that had come up out of the water and gone nowhere at all.
