On Sunday the draughtswoman brings the watch back. Her husband has put a little collar on the peg that carries the minute hand. He has put it on upside down, so that the broad part shows, and the old man is rather taken with it. There is now something in the middle of his watch like the brass boss in the middle of a good front door.

“Mind,” he says, “I shall still want my March.”

The draughtswoman says it is his March and leaves him the drawing to sleep with. He has thought of something he can do in the patch of light when there is no need to mend the watch. He can look at the watch.

In the kitchen at the Low Hinge, Linn puts a fresh dock leaf in the pillbox. A mealworm kept overnight with a piece of dock has a particularly clean underside. She is making ready a second box, lined with the cotton out of her grandfather’s ear bottle, when he asks what that one is for.

She tells him.

“Well,” he says, after a little while, “I never kept one.”

“Where did yours go?”

He has to think back. There were very few spans in his time at the works, and for those there were mostly inanimate articles. The cap, of course. Once a lemon. He remembers the lemon very well because they had understood it was to be supplied by the works and the works had understood otherwise. But before his time they used a swift, and he believes the swift is in the rates office, in one of the shallow drawers where they used to keep the seals.

Linn puts a lid on the second box. It seems quite an unsuitable place for a swift.

That night something comes to the meadow. You could not tell it from a person coming home rather late, except that the dew does not come off on it. It sits on the top step, which is a little worn already where people sit to do up a lace. From there it can put its hand on the roof of the town.

It has a great deal in its power. It feels the sleepers through the turf, and the small irregular pull of the pumps, and it can feel, as a sleeper cannot, the difference between an inch of living wood and an inch of wood that has merely kept its shape. Somewhere directly under its palm, in the dark of a hanging cuff, there is a bat cleaning her ear. She gets the hind claw into the nick and draws it out very carefully. The nick itches as it heals.

The works people know practically everything about the flight except what it is like to make it. The thing on the step knows that too. There is a moment when the little heart goes on in two places, and then in only one. It is no worse than a missed rung in a dream for the one that goes on. For the other it is a missed rung.

It stays with its hand there until the bat has finished with her ear.

On Monday morning Linn finds, among the official instructions on the back of the drawing, one she cannot remember seeing before.

*The box may also be brought back unopened.*

It is in much the same small hand as the note about not leaning on the scales. She reads it to her grandfather, who has his good shirt on, and he says he should hope so; it is their box.

There is quite a gathering at the dispatch window. The oilman is there with a clean print of his thumb on the front of his cap. The draughtswoman has made a little model out of rind and is showing the old man how the light will get through. She uses a knitting needle for the light. Linn would like to stay and see, but the walk to the other end takes seventeen minutes as the town is now arranged, and she has to go.

At the far chalk mark she opens her hand. The lid of the pillbox has taken a pink ring out of her palm. Across the hollow her grandfather is no bigger than the figure of a man on a farthing, but she sees the change in the line of his shoulder when he lets the bat out.

It comes to her then that if she were standing beside him she could have the bat just as easily. She would not even have to take the lid off. The creature likes the warm place between her first two fingers and will stay there while she shells peas, provided she does it one-handed.

She puts the box in her pocket.

It is an awkward business stopping a municipal proceeding from the wrong side of an expanse of air across which it is not advisable to wave a handkerchief. Linn has to walk the seventeen minutes back. Several people have taken their coats off in anticipation of the work and presently put them on again. The oilman goes back to his bearing. When Linn arrives at the window the bat is in the spectacles and her grandfather is sitting down.

“I should like something that hasn't got an inside,” she says.

The draughtswoman, who has been up since five, says a rather sharp thing about when it would have been convenient to learn this. Then she rubs the bridge of her nose. There is a little hollow there that fits the wooden end of her pencil exactly.

“What did you have in mind?”

Linn hasn't anything in mind. That is the worst part. She had rather thought that once she had walked back there would be an end to the difficulty, and instead there are all these people who have still got a difficulty, and she is standing among them with a worm.

It is the old man who thinks of the lemon. Not of using a lemon, but of how the lemon was got across. His sister did it. She was a good underhand bowler and for years afterwards could not buy a drink in the freight canteen. He thinks that with a little practice he could do as much himself over a considerably shorter distance.

By dinner there are seven people out on the meadow throwing potatoes. They have had to come up: there is nowhere below to practice a bad throw. The postwoman is very good indeed. The old man is not, but he knows what a good throw ought to look like and can tell one quite a long way off. Linn sits in the grass with the bat asleep inside her stocking top. Every now and then a potato comes rolling down among the buttercups and she gets up on one knee and rolls it back.

It takes them another fortnight to get the gallery. They make it in three short stretches, with a change of level at the second pier which was in nobody’s drawing and which turns out to be exceedingly convenient for unloading a milk cart. For the throws they use bobbins from the laundry, good weighty things of glazed earthenware. Their husks lie where they fall until the works cutter picks them up. They are like the thinnest imaginable china, with a blue number on each end, perfectly legible from the inside. Linn has the middle one.

As for the thing that sat on the step, it has gone about its business. There are new pencil marks on the grandfather’s cuff, a charge for the dock leaf among them, though it grew free in the meadow: Linn had to go and get it. Under the other cuff an old nick has stopped itching. When March comes the square of light moves along the counterpane much as before. On the fourth morning it lies directly over the watch, and on the fifth just clear of it, so that a very small bright reflection from the brass boss runs up the green wall and enters a hole where nothing of the kind has happened in living memory. Two long-haired heels appear, and stay there till the sun moves on.
