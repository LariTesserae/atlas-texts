The grey hem had been like that for nine years when the hollow first came to the notice of a power able to do something about it. For a while there was nothing to see in particular. It was late in a dry summer. The lowest grain hung clear of the floor by about the thickness of the grain itself. In the course of an afternoon it went up and down a little with the warmth of the roof.

The power could have closed that distance. What kept it from doing so was a clear thread it had taken at first for a scratch on the underside of the grain. There were two elbows in the thread. The first was where the moss had met the leaf, and the second where, growing out over the crimp with the leaf for its ceiling, it had come round on the grain. A bead of water lay in the second elbow. It had come a fair way to get there. The power waited to see where it would go next.

In September there was rain. The grain touched down and rocked a little in its bed, and the bead went out from under it into the shale. For eleven days the leaf lay with its edge in the light. On the twelfth it came up. There were two more grains on the hem and nothing green on the floor. By the time it was dry the leaf hung a trifle crooked, and a crimp farther along the edge was now the lowest point. Under the flakes at the back a white leaf had opened enough to take the kink out of a moss stem. These things were all of the same September.

That winter a man came along the road with a tin of yellow paint and a stick with a wheel at the end of it. Where water had stood on the carriageway he measured to the verge and put a short yellow line on the nearest stone. There were going to be drains. At the tipped stone he had trouble making a line that could be seen from the road, and put a second one across the top. It was quite a good place for a drain. There was a dip in the road there, and on two mornings already a van had gone sideways on the ice.

The power followed the water back. Well above the bend it came down the landward bank in a fan of little runs. At the foot of the bank, under a mat of grass, it crossed the mouth of an old earthenware pipe. The pipe went under the road and came out on a shelf of thrift some distance down the coast. All but the first two feet of it was sound. The power put those two feet in order. It took the grass off in a piece and bedded it in the raw place beside the outlet, where some earth had washed away. Before morning there was water going out onto the shelf in a brown trickle, then clear.

The man came back after the next rain. He walked up the dry dip, found the mouth, and knelt down and put his hand in. He knew the make of the pipe by the lip on it. His father had laid miles of it. He set a white post there so the mouth could be found again when the grass grew tall. In the spring the road was opened in four places. The yellow lines at the bend were still there for some years, fading to the colour of the lichens, the one on top going first.

None of the water in the pipe had ever been much use to the garden. The rain that got in under the stone came off the sea almost level, darkening one face of the little white post and leaving the other dry. In the sixth spring after the drains were put in, it rained like that twice in a fortnight. Between the rains the crooked leaf had time to lift at one end but not at the other. A few hairs held in a fault in the shale. By summer there was a scallop there, no bigger than any of the others, except that for a minute or so at noon the light came through it.

Seen from underneath, the hairs in the shale were dark and the veins of the leaf were dark and the shale itself let through a little amber light at its thinnest edge. A low clear forest stood between these things. The power came back from time to time. It had a liking for that minute.
