The power that had come to the shelf had been attending to the little one. It had felt the catch in the new belts and had been on the point of easing it. Then it felt, through the roof of the calf's head, the settling of the mother's chest as she shifted her weight. She could give the calf room without taking its weight for it. The power let the belts run.

It stayed by the bed well into the dark. It was a considerable thing to it to have heard that one small sound. It could hear the whole shelf at once if it liked, including the grinding that went on a good way down inside the lime, where there had never been a foot. The more of this it heard the harder it was to hear a calf.

In the night there was a fall at the western scarp. A long piece went, with a stone on it. The power felt the stone strike far below and presently felt the blow arrive back at the rim. For a little while every listening brush on the shelf lay flat. When the beasts lifted them again there was one place where a brush went down, lifted, went down again. It was a mother feeling for the return of the two shocks at the end of six. Her calf had stopped walking.

The power went there. This was another calf, appreciably smaller. It was standing with its face to a wall of fresh lime about as high as its chest. The fall had not taken this part of the shelf away. It had let it down bodily by a few inches, without so much as spilling the grit off it. The mother was on the high side. She could have stepped back down easily enough but at each shift of her feet a little of the new edge crumbled into the calf's eyes. She had backed away and was standing where the ground held.

There was lime enough in the lower country to build the shelf out past its old edge. The power could feel, however, along the underside of the shelf, the places where a fall some morning would open beds that herds were feeding at already. It left the scarp alone.

Instead it lay down on the low side of the break. It took the weight of the calf's two forefeet on its back. This was not much weight. The calf's feet were cool in the fork of the toes, hot at the tips, and one of them trembled with the effort of keeping all six legs stiff. For a long time it did not appear to know there was anything under it. The power had made itself smooth and quite hard, like the lime. It had not thought to give itself any grit.

A breath came along the break and remedied this. The calf got a purchase, bent in the middle in the surprising way of a very young beast, and went up. Its hindmost foot kicked quite hard against the power. A chip of lime rolled after it down the place where the power lay.

There was no great rush of other bodies to cross. In a hundred of the evenings visible there the break had not yet occurred. In a hundred more the wind had banked it full. On that particular night two old animals came down over the power's back; going down was more trouble to them than going up, and one stood for a time with its forequarters low, working out what to do about the rest of itself. Toward morning the grit began lodging on the lee side. The power made itself a little smaller as the bank grew, until by daylight there was just the bank, with a long groove in it where the old animal had let its belly slide.

Out on the bed the first calf was feeding. The power knew it by a hesitation now, rather than a catch, toward the end of the run. By tomorrow it might have no way to know it at all. It lay a while longer with the last of its weight against the bank, listening to the traffic overhead. When it withdrew there was no settling of the grit. Under a small walking foot the bank gave just so far and held.
