The power came down by the carrier’s stair. It had meant to come down where the light came down, into one of the little courts, but from a long way off the court had looked empty and from nearer to it one could see the pieces of a coat. It put on a pair of shoes and used the stair.

The sack mender saw the top of its head. It was a quite ordinary head except that the hair lay two ways on it, like barley where there has been wind. She was in the middle of a difficult corner. She drew her thread through, looked again, and saw the stranger waiting on the third step from the top while a man came up with a jar.

“You can come up here and let him by,” she said.

“Thank you. I should like to see the town.”

“Well, you can see a good deal of it from here.”

It stayed with her most of the morning. Twice she had to ask it to move its feet. It was her habit, in turning a sack, to give the far end a kick that carried it clean over the edge of the table, and a visitor could be very much in the way without being anywhere near her hands.

It did not tell her what it was. On the other hand, when she said that a person who had sat so long might as well be useful, it put out its hand for the torn corner and held it exactly as she wished. This was so unusual in a person who had never mended sacks that she looked at the head again.

“What have you come for?” she asked.

“To do something for the town, if there is something to do.”

She took several stitches before she answered.

“There’s a woman coming for a parcel who can tell you what it is I want. I’ve never managed to tell anybody.”

The woman who came was her sister. There was nothing mysterious in the parcel. It was a little box of hooks and eyes, ordered by three people together to save the carrier’s charge. The sister counted out her share on the table. She had forgotten to bring anything to take them away in, and the mender turned up the hem of her sleeve and put two stitches in it to make a pocket.

“Tell our visitor what I want,” she said.

Her sister considered. “You want me to come up oftener.”

“I can tell people *that*.”

“Oh. You mean how it used to be at home.”

When they were girls they had worked on either side of a partition. Their father had put it up after the elder girl got to taking in work; there was room to spread a sail on one side of it, provided the family sat rather close together on the other. It was possible to speak through the boards, but the sisters seldom did. If one of them came upon something to make her laugh, she knocked once, quite low down, with the toe of her shoe. The other knocked back. Sometimes at supper they remembered to say what it had been about. More often they did not. It had happened on occasion that the one knocked back in her sleep.

“Is that it?” the sister asked.

“That’s near enough.”

The power could have made a way between the two houses. It looked at the way while they were talking. A stair would come down very handsomely from beside the worktable into the sister’s room. It would take away the only place in the lower room where a bed could stand out of the draught. A ladder would take less, but for whose convenience would it be, a dozen years hence? It could have made the women hear each other as if the clay between them were a thin board. But there are things a woman will say in her own room, with her sister five minutes away, that she has no wish to say in her sister’s hearing.

“Would a knock do?” it asked.

The mender got up. There was a place under her table where the heel of her shoe had worn through the lime. She knocked there. Her sister, who was still standing beside her, knocked back on the roof with the toe of her own shoe, and they both laughed at the foolishness of it.

The power went down with the sister. She showed it where she sat to do the accounts for the lime yard. It measured from there to the place under the worktable. It was a little farther than either woman had supposed; the rooms were not quite one above the other. Between them, too, was a cupboard belonging to a third household, full of onions.

What it did took it until the carrier came again. It made two small pieces of wood, one for the floor by the worktable and one for the floor under the accounts desk. The lower one had to be notched to fit round a leg of the desk. A knock on either could be felt, very lightly, by a foot resting on the other. Neither wood nor clay was disturbed in between; even the onions stayed where they were. The time was mostly spent in getting the lower piece not to rock. The power could have levelled the floor in an instant, but the sister had a good deal of trouble with small change as it was, and knew just where to feel for a penny that had rolled.

They tried it. It worked better without shoes. The mender said that in summer this was no objection. Her sister said that in winter, if there were anything very funny indeed, she supposed she could take off a shoe.

The power went away by the eastern end, stopping to let a young man with a barrow cross the sill ahead of it. No one asked it to stay. It had not got on particularly intimate terms with anybody except the two women, and they had things to finish before the light went.

For some days they were rather too attentive to the pieces of wood. Each was afraid the other might have knocked without her noticing. This wore off. There were whole weeks when neither thought of it. Then one morning the sister at the accounts saw, on a bill for the repair of the lime-yard gate, a charge for straightening the iron hook her husband had charged for bending the year before. Without looking up from the figures she slipped her foot out of her shoe.

On the roof the mender was at work on a sack so old that at one corner she was sewing one of her own patches to another. She felt the knock and answered it. By supper her sister had forgotten what it was about.
