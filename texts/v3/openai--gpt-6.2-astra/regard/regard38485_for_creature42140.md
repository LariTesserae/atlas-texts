The power first noticed the root on a day when the bench had been taken away for varnishing. There was a light patch on the floor where the bench had stood and in the middle of it, at a quarter past eleven, a green oblong with one remarkably sharp edge. The other three edges were woolly. At the sharp edge the sheet had come away from the glass and was hanging by the little root. It had pulled up a scale of paint. The underside of the scale was blue, a colour the station had not been in anybody's memory.

It was an awkward place to be growing. Behind the paint there was a bead of hard putty, and behind the putty there was glass. All the good wet wood was a quarter of an inch away, on the other side of the bead. The power could have put the root there. It waited for rain instead, to see what the water did.

There were sixteen fine days. During that time it went up to the tower. It had expected, from the weight on the four legs, a considerable stillness. In fact there was so much going on that it missed the closing of the press, being occupied at the time with the screw hole in the door. The two little leaves had shut before a cloud came across the sun. It took another six afternoons to establish that what shut them was the shadow of the handrail, which arrived a little before the cloud had happened to arrive on that first day. On the seventh afternoon the power was elsewhere and the leaves shut just the same.

One thing it did in the tower. There was a brass scale on the landing, with a pointer connected through the wall to a float in a narrow pipe. The pointer had stuck. The man who came on Fridays had put a slip of paper behind the glass saying STUCK, in a very small hand so as not to cover any of the figures. In the pipe a root had grown through the eye of the float. It was a handsome length of root, with laterals as fine as the scratches on the brass. There was no getting the float free without breaking it.

The power gave the pipe a second bore, alongside the first, and in it hung a float of the kind the old one had been before the root found it. There was room enough in the thickness of the wall. On Friday the pointer stood at a figure and trembled slightly when the pumps changed over. The man watched it through two changes. He took the slip out and put it in his waistcoat pocket. He had cut it from the margin of a photograph of his daughters and had never quite liked leaving it there.

At the station, when the rain came, the wet came in much lower down than the power had supposed. What fed the root was not the leak. The leak ran along the putty and dripped off a screw. It was the breath of the people waiting on the bench that clouded the lower pane, particularly on a wet morning when they sat close with their coats steaming. As the pane cleared, a last narrow crescent of mist persisted under the lifted scale. By the time the late train came in, the root had a clear tip again. It had begun to turn along the bead.

The power left the putty alone. Where the new bore went through the tower wall there had been a little white ceramic dust; this it collected on the end of a finger. For a while it considered making something of the dust. Then it wiped its finger on the underside of the landing, as any workman might.

In November a second root came out at the station. This one went upward, into the dry. It got no farther than the blue paint and for the rest of the winter made no growth that anyone could see. But the scale, which used to lift and settle with the opening of the station doors, was held now at both ends. Under it was a space the shape of half a lentil. Long after the last passengers had gone home, there was water there.
