A little way beyond the last kiln the sole comes to a place where somebody has taken a jar out of the ground. The hole has kept its shape very well. There is a lip where the rim was and below that a neck and below the neck a considerable belly. The bottom is wet. One corner of the sole goes down to try it and the other stays outside, folded back so that the light comes through it twice and it looks quite blue. By the time the first corner has found the limit of the wet the greater part of the animal is in the hole.

It can get out of most holes. In this one the wall comes away in little plates, each about the size of the mouth that has hold of it. After a time there is a ridge of these round the bottom and the sole is going round the ridge. Toward noon it has made one rather good piece of wall perfectly smooth.

What has been attending to the country under the salt is aware of this difficulty. It is aware also of the difficulty of a lizard on the lip, which has got a fly crosswise in its mouth and cannot shut either its mouth or its eyes. The two difficulties might look much alike from a height. It stays long enough for the lizard to rub its face against the ground. The fly comes off. The lizard takes it again the right way round.

There is a potsherd in the loose stuff at the lip, a long piece from the side of a water jar. On the inside of it are six of the little dents made with a thumb. A woman held the pot against the turn of the wheel to make them; at the bottom of each dent there is a little slew of clay that tells which way the wheel went. The sherd is quite long enough. What watches the sole eases it out of the gravel and sets it in the hole with its foot well down in the wet and its other end just over the lip. That is all it does there. It does not put the animal on the sherd.

In fact the sole goes past the foot of it several times. There is something very much to its taste in the wet at the bottom. It is evening before a loose corner goes up over the first thumb dent and down into the second. The other corner follows the edge of the sherd, which is rough and offers quite as good a purchase. For a while the animal is folded lengthways like a leaf that has not opened. Then a gather travels up the clay. A second goes up. Out on the flat the flies have risen, and the sole goes off across the lovely unoccupied floor with a cargo of eighteen or twenty little plates of salt and one live shrimp it did not have when it went in.

The boy comes on the hole some days later looking for a cool place to put his feet. By then there is quite a path up the sherd. Things go both ways on it. At the lower end a white crab is standing to one side to let a rather wider white crab by, or possibly to catch it by the leg; in the poor light at the bottom it is hard to tell. He tries the sherd with his finger and finds it firm. He puts his feet somewhere else.

After that, when they take a jar out, they put a broken one in slantwise. It takes a moment if you have the piece to hand. They are potters; they generally have the piece.
