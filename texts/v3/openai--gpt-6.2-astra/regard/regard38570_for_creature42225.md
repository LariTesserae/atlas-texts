In August the woman was asked to give back the key. They had found nearly everything else that belonged to the shelter, including the little blue stool she used to stand on to get at the cistern. The stool was wanted at another stop. The key was to go on a board with a number under it, so that whoever had the cleaning of the lower square could let himself in.

She went down with the man the first morning. He had a stiff broom and a tin of something for the green on the steps. She showed him where to use it and where the water he washed it off with would go. He put the tin back in his barrow. The steps would have to be done by hand from the bottom up, wringing the cloth into a pail. He was quite agreeable to this until he did the bottom step and found there were thirty-seven more.

“What did you do when it was a street?” he said.

“Sat on my stool and sold tickets.”

It was while they were there that whatever has the keeping of such places took an interest. I can’t say how much it knew already. It waited through two fillings. It did nothing about the animal that lost its hold near the brass mouth, though it could have put out something no bigger than a pin to catch it. The animal turned twice in the draw of the water and went in backwards.

We found out subsequently that there was a lip on the inside of the mouth as well as on the outside. Behind it was the old cistern. Nobody had thought to look there because the shelter was gone and its boards and stool had been accounted for. But the cistern had gone down with the street. On the inside it was brown to the depth of a winter glove, very soft at the surface, and full of animals. The place the woman had wiped every winter was still quite plain, a broad belt as high as she could reach from the stool, with a little rise at the right where she had been able to get her shoulder in. The animal we saw go backwards had come to as good a place as any under the interchange.

What the power did in the end was quite small. It concerned the key.

The man had to sign for it at the depot, which opened at seven. It was a quarter of an hour by tram. If there was a shower before he came, somebody from the upper square was meant to put the chain across the stair. Sometimes nobody did. The woman lived over the baker’s, with a view of the first three steps, and had offered to go on seeing to it. But you couldn’t have a key both on a numbered board in the depot and hanging by the door over the baker’s. The man explained this to her as they went up.

That evening she found she had forgotten to give it to him. It was in the little pocket in her skirt. She put it on the hook by the door so as not to forget again. At the depot in the morning the man found the key where he had hung it the night before.

I have had both keys in my hand, though not together. There is a nick near the bow which the woman made opening a bottle of orangeade the day the first rails were laid. It catches a little at the lining if you carry the key loose in your pocket. Both keys have it. Neither opens anything the other doesn’t, and if one is in the lock the other won’t turn. As far as I know this is the whole of what was done.

The woman puts the chain across in the early showers now. She seldom goes down. The man does his steps in batches of six or seven, which keeps him more or less even with the green. He has had the stool brought back. He sits on it to wring his cloth out, and when it rains he takes it up to the landing and sits with his feet on the rung. From there one can see the animals without seeing very much of the water. He says they look like the scattered bits of paper a conductor used to have at his feet after punching a whole tramful of tickets.

This morning I went by before seven. There had been rain in the night. The woman was taking the chain down; the water was already below the arrow, though one could still hear the knocking. She waited with the loose end of the chain in her hand until it stopped. Then she let me through, and went upstairs to put the kettle on.
