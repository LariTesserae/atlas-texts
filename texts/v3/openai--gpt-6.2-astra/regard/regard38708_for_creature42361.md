The first time the power came over the pass it stayed until the pavement had got the measure of it. This took rather longer than it had expected. It could have stood there without weight, but there would have been very little in that for either of them. It put down much the weight of a woman with a basket on one arm. After a time it was possible to change the basket from arm to arm without the least discomfort in the knees. The power went away by the gap, taking care not to knock the basket against the wall, although of course there was nothing in it.

It had felt something under the last pier which a person with less time to spare would have put down to a pulse in the fingers. When the pavement had quite finished with the basket, the thickening under the old wedge had come along the wall as far as the pier. There had been two short pressures and a long one. They had not been quite regularly spaced. The power knew the fit of the wedge and could have calculated to a nicety the interval at which it used to chatter. These were not the intervals.

On its next visit it brought a piece of tile from the sheltered course outside the eastern wall. There is a good deal to be said for that old tile. You can strike a corner of it against the rest and get a clear note from a piece no bigger than the corner of your nail. The power laid it down beside the last pier, with one edge on the pavement and the other on its finger. It waited again for the running pressure. Each time it felt one it let the tile down. The touch of the edge on the paver was quite distinct, even through the soles of the feet; but there was nothing to be heard in the room beyond what had always been there.

For the better part of a summer nothing came of this. The pavement learnt the finger, which was a very steady one. It learnt the tile. At length it was letting go of the loaded paver just as the edge came down, so that the power had to be very attentive indeed to feel the contact. The tile might have been lowered on to the surface of a full cup without spilling any.

One afternoon the power mistook a beat in its own finger for the beginning of a pressure and let the tile down too soon. The paver was not ready. There was a little dry knock. Before the finger could lift, a second knock came up through the piece of tile. The pavement had taken the weight off one end of it and allowed it to fall again.

The power took its finger away.

Nothing further happened until well into the autumn. By then the tile had lain so long on its two uneven edges that someone coming on it might have thought it a bit left over from the building. In fact one could already have told the difference by lifting it. There were two minute bright places underneath, one at either end, and a grey line between them. The line ran a little aslant. In the course of the winter the tile turned on it by about the breadth of a grain of wheat. It was enough. The next time the pavement bore down at one end, the other end rose clear.

People still speak of a loose tile in the room at the pass. One of the mule men went so far as to take up a little bag of sand to bed it in; his father, who had been putting his hand on that tile every winter for nine years, emptied the bag on an icy part of the path. There is no danger of catching a foot on it. It lies well in behind the pier. Set two fingers lightly on it and presently there will be something like the passage of a loaded mule, beginning far off in the ball of one finger and going out at the tip of the other. Quite often there is the rocking. Sometimes both ends come up against the fingers together, which is a thing no weight that ever crossed the saddle could have done.

It was this last that brought the power back. It had been elsewhere a long while. It came in sideways with its basket and was recognised at the first turn. At the tile it sat down and put its hand out. Both ends rose, then one, then both again. The power was about to make the answering pressures when it felt the tile turn a little under its palm. It lifted its hand clear. There was a pause of much the length of time a person will keep a foot in the air on finding a stair where there ought not to be one. Then the two ends went on, very distinctly, without the hand.

The power sat with its back against the warm side of the pier. It had meant to stay an hour. For once there was no particular call upon it elsewhere, and by the time it got up the light in the roof slots was coming from the other side of the sky. In all that time the pavement had done nothing at all about the weight of the basket. There was a red mark in the crook of its arm when it left.
