The finger stayed for the second knock. Under the pad it was hardly a knock at all; most of what could be felt was the settling of salt into the whorls of the skin. The one whose finger it was took the weight off it, down to the weight of the salt that had been displaced. At that pressure the two blows, near together, could be told apart.

It would have been easy to get nearer. There was salt enough above the seed to take up in a pinch, after which the dark seam of the hull would have been visible. But the finger could feel the drawing back of the heels as well as their fall. The salt was part of the load against which the upper leaf drew them back. The hand stayed where it was.

Below, the middle prong had begun to do something rather expensive with the chip. There was a way through the fork for a piece that size: the other two parts had stiffened already to send it down the eastern grade. Instead of letting it go, the middle one gave a little more on the side toward the stair. The chip rolled over in the bed. The underside was pale where it had rested against the ceiling and had not yet taken the dark polish of a piece of meal. A flake of this came off against the prong. The prong let the chip go. It kept the flake.

There was no grade from the fork to the landing keeper. As things stood, anything let fall there must go east. The middle prong began to put out a lip on the high side of its bed. It had done this before, to turn a load that came in badly. The other prongs made room for it in the usual way. But this lip went on growing after the chip was well down the grade. The flake lay on it, perfectly still, and went uphill.

Over the course of that interval the hand above the salt learned something of the time it took to go the breadth of a blade. At first the finger had been warm. It drew the warmth back into the hand. At first, too, it had attended chiefly to the raised grains, along which a knock went with such beautiful distinctness. Now it could feel a much less distinct thing in the part of the floor under the fork: a giving on one side, an answering strain on the other, neither of which ceased altogether between blows.

The flake was not brought quite to the pleat. The lip could get as far as the low end of the scraper, but the handle, bound down with rind, stood in its way. The flake lay against the handle through two blows. At the third some of it went under. What came out on the far side was powder, on which the grit made no purchase at all. It went over the end of the blade in a little fall onto the smooth inner face.

The landing keeper turned that face up. For a while it did nothing more. Then a blunt end of the pleat went down on the lip that had brought the flake, in the place from which the flake had gone. There was room for it there. Neither keeper had to take its weight off its work.

It was about then that the hand had something to do. A run of brine had come out on the flat, a long way off by the measure of the seed. It came out there most years, lay in its own shallow pan, and went back into the salt. This time a rim of the pan had sunk. A thread scarcely wider than a hair was feeling its way along the low bed. Where it went it dissolved a little more room for itself. Given the length of one interval it would have found the hull.

The hand lifted. Where the thread had not yet come to the lowest bed, it drew a nail through the damp salt, out to an old hollow on the side away from the seed. The thread took the furrow. It spread in the hollow to a skin so thin that before the next knock there was a white edge to it all round. Nothing was set over the hull to mark the place.

Under the stair the two soft parts remained together. There was still the rest of the crumb to come down, though not much likelihood of its coming soon. When a load reached the fork the middle prong drew in, and the landing keeper could feel, through the blunt end of its pleat, just how far the bed had yielded. At the next load it let its end go with the prong instead of making the prong draw free. This cost less. Presently a little oil began to go back into the lip.

By the time the hand went away, the ridge beside the blade had risen to the height of the lowest tread. On its working face it was rough, as a guide for meal ought to be. On the side toward the fork there was a place kept smooth.
