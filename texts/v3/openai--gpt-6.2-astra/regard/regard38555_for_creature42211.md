By the time the scrap is gone the chip has ceased to look very well seated. One corner is down. The youngster puts a foot against it and discovers that it can make the other corner come down instead. This occupies it for a considerable time. What is wanted is a second grain, small enough to go in above the first. Several of the right size are actually passing, but on the far side of its body. It has not yet learned which feet it can afford to take off the ice.

It is the water coming through the mitten that brings the youngster to the attention of the power. The plate has come loose at one end, and the power, kneeling on the shore, has put a hand under it to turn it up. A spoonful of bay water runs in at the cuff. For a moment, in the little space between the wool and the ice, the amber chip shows almost yellow. There is a thread of something pale standing over it with one end crooked like the handle of a cup.

The power takes the hand away. Its name is Ruth. She takes the wet mitten off with her teeth and lays it on her knee, where it begins at once to stiffen. She would very much like to see the thread again. On this first account, if on no other, the little district is fortunate in the power that has found it. Ruth knows the difference between wanting another look at a thing and having a reason to turn it over.

Beside her boot there is a square of green net from a box of onions. One corner has frozen into the slush on shore; the rest goes under the plate. She came down here to fetch it. On the ebb it lies slack. On the flood it makes a tidy little fence across the space between the ice and the cliff, and the bits of bladderwrack that ought to come and go with the tide have begun to collect against it.

Ruth gets out her scissors. She cuts the net close to the slush and then, instead of pulling on the part in the water, puts her bare hand in and gathers it a little at a time. There are three places where it has caught. Two come free if pushed farther in first. At the third her fingers meet the ragged edge of the hollow, and something quite delicate goes tapping down the side of her forefinger. She holds still until it has gone. The last snag is a mussel no bigger than an apple pip, fastened across two of the green squares. She cuts out the little bit it is using and puts the mussel, with its property, in a crack below the waterline. What is left of the net fits inside the wet mitten. It makes a very poor job of a hand, with green ends sticking out between the thumb and the palm.

Under the ice a certain amount of rebuilding has become necessary. The shears closing in the water have made a movement like the shutting of two large doors, though without anything like the noise. Five red swimmers have been put through the old quarter backward. The milk worm has withdrawn so briskly that for once the tenants have seen the end of it. And at the back of the hollow the youngster is standing on bare ice again. The amber chip has gone.

It goes past three occupied porches and is caught at the fourth by a tenant with a use for exactly that shape. For the rest of the winter it will form the lower half of a windowsill. There is no way of giving it back. Ruth, who would have felt quite badly about this, has gone up the bank with one hand in her armpit and does not know.

The youngster goes a little way in the direction the chip took. Where it stops, the two ribs of ice are closer together. Something is lodged there already: a grain so black it is difficult to distinguish from the hole behind it, except for a fine white vein running across the face. It has come off the rear end of a house. The youngster takes hold and gives it the usual turn. It does not turn. For a long while the youngster works at it, first with the dignified hands and then with a surprising assortment of the other feet, before arriving at the happy discovery that there is no need to get it out.
