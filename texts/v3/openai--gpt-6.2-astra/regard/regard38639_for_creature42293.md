The power came first to the patch of rock where there was nothing to be heard. From inside the limestone it could hear the peel, the balance, a barleycorn jumping on the skin of the sieve. From outside, with a cheek against the patch, it could hear none of these. It stayed there a while. It had never before had one ear cooler than the other.

It came again at the end of a long dry summer. The cistern was low. A person drawing water had to let the little bucket down past the last knot in the cord, and it came up with a fleck or two of grit in it. The woman upstairs had taken to keeping the water in which she soaked her willow. She poured it on a vine at the foot of the steps. The vine had small sour grapes, but the leaves were good for wrapping up a bit of cheese.

There was still plenty of water in the rib of the mountain. A little above the elder spout it went two ways. One way led to the groove, and the other, which was by far the easier way, went down through a bed of broken stone. The power could have closed that way with a stone no larger than the end of the girl's thumb. Before doing so it went down to see where the water came out.

It came out below the footpath, well under the lip of the town. There was a bed of mint there, trodden flat in one place by an animal with a divided hoof. Farther down, a man had put a piece of curved tile into the bank and set his jar under it. He was asleep with his hat over his face. There was a dark line up the side of the jar where he had dipped a finger in and run it down the clay to see how much longer he had to sleep.

The power came back up through the broken stone without moving any of it.

That evening the girl took the bucket by the bottom to tip the last of the water into her jar. A thread of light showed between two of its staves. She held it up. The thread disappeared. She filled the bucket again and stood it on the shelf. There was nothing wrong with the bucket until the weight came on the handle. When she lifted it, water ran out quite freely against her shin.

Her father was taking the pans off the balance. She went in with the bucket and stood on his deaf side while she explained what she had found. Then she came round to his other side.

“Lift it,” she said.

He did, and got his shoe wet. Between them they took the hoop off the bucket and drove it on again from the narrow end. For this they used the heel of his other shoe. Afterward he let her take the pans down and put them face to face. They were much lighter than she had supposed from the care he took with them.

The power had had nothing to do with the thread of light or with the girl's seeing it. There was, however, a thing it did that summer. A swallow came in at the bright end of the walk just as the bakehouse door opened. It turned away from the door into the wool room. There was a high window in there which had once looked out on the steps and now looked into the back of a cupboard in the house above. All afternoon the bird kept trying for that window. By the time the last bale was weighed it was clinging to the lime below the sill, its narrow breast going very fast.

The girl was at home. The man who weighed the wool was hanging up the pans. The power put out what would have been a hand, if there had been anybody to see it, and made a little dark about the bird. After a while the claws let go of the lime. It carried the bird as far as the parapet. For some time after the dark was taken away the swallow sat quite still, with one long wing crossed crookedly over the other. Then it dropped below the lip of the town. Well out over the pale slope it came up again, on even wings.

In the wool room the father swept a pinch of lime off the lid of a bale. He shut the door and went home in his one wet shoe. Nobody in the four lanes had anything to tell that evening, except about the bucket, which now held water all the way from the bottom of the cistern to the top.
