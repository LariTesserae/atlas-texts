The thing that was wrong with the step could not have been found with a straightedge. The higher piece still had its share of the weather. The lower piece had none. If a little scale fell back, it fell onto dead ground, and whatever spring was left in it had to serve until the weather came again. On the third day there were seven left at the bottom. One went up in the morning. The others did not.

What took notice of the house took notice of them. It could feel, separately, the six slight weights. It could also feel the old roofs, including a great many that had long since come down and were going about the country a grain at a time. A piece of one such roof lay over a fan eleven miles to the east. There would have been no difficulty in getting it back.

For a while nothing was done. The fan under that piece had a splendid set of ribs. Along one edge, in the dark between two of them, a blue foot let go and took hold a little farther on.

The next slip came from a long way north and was a small one. At the bottom of the step the grit tipped, not all together in a line as it did outside, but in a little crescent about as wide as the mark a cup leaves. All six scales stood on edge. Five fell back. The sixth landed flat on the lower floor, opened, and disclosed an animal busy getting its feet the right way up. It walked into the shade of the step. Apparently to go up was not what it had been trying to do.

After that the lower floor got a little of the weather at every slip. A tongue of stone a good way down, which for several thousand years had borne hard against its neighbor at one end only, now bore against it at both. This made very little difference anywhere else. At a house to the north a survey glass that habitually walked to the edge of a shelf and had to be set back once a winter now took a winter and a half to do it. The man who lived there put it down to the new piece of felt.

When the woman came again it was possible to get in by either door. The jammed one had not been raised in its frame. Somebody had been there with a plane; there were curls of wood behind it, very old dry wood, as fragrant inside the curl as if it had only lately been cut from a tree. She sat on the high side of the step to take off her pack. In the angle by her boot there was a dark line. She put a finger to it and for a moment believed she had found the old hem. But this one went the other way, and its back, when she stroked off a little of the dust, was smooth from end to end.

She slept in the hollow. Early in the morning she woke with a collection of small feet on the inside of her elbow. The hem had come down off the high floor for a drink. There was no telling whether it would be able to get back, so she left her arm where it was, though the hand had begun to prickle and she would have liked very much to turn over.

Presently a lift came. She felt it first under her shoulder and then, quite distinctly, under her hip on the far side of the break. In between there was an interval just long enough for a soft thing to get a purchase. The feet went off her arm in pairs. She could hear them on the face of the step, a minute brushing noise that stopped halfway up and began again. When she was sure the last foot was off she turned over and went back to sleep.

By noon the hem was under a stone outside. A little garnet had got into the bed of the stone and kept it from coming quite down. In the gap, if anyone had been there to see, the row of stout ribs moved over by one place. The garnet turned a quarter of a turn. No stone was lifted.
