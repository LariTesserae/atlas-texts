The power that took notice of the hollow took some time to find the vole again. It had seen the pale root go in. Now there were several pale roots against the dark, and only by going over the face of the island quite slowly could it find the one that had a cut end. Inside, the vole had laid its new root along one wall. It was asleep with a hind foot sticking out of the sedge. The foot was quite pink and the toes, which had been so busy on the root, were slightly spread.

The power could have stopped the noise. Before doing so it followed the line off the moor. At some distance there was a school where a man was letting the air out of the radiators, beginning in the infants’ room and going on round the building with a key and a chipped cup. By the time he got back to the infants’ room there were six pairs of wet mittens laid out on the radiator, giving off a smell of sheep. It was the one by the north windows that still did not heat properly. The power left the pressure in the line alone.

It came back in the dark. The weasel was halfway up the stair, investigating the joints between the treads and the bank. In the beam of the maintenance man’s torch its eyes showed green, one well above the other, since it had its head on one side to get under a tread. The man lowered his torch. For a moment the beam lay all across the floor, low enough to light the sides of the moss cushions instead of their tops. The power saw where the seed had fallen. It left that too. There was a good deal more than one creature abroad in the hollow with a use for a seed.

What it did in the end was for the man, on a Tuesday toward the end of February. There had been a request from the road office for a place to tip the peat they were taking out to widen a bend. The hollow was on the form as *old workings*, which was right, and the distance from the bend was given as a mile and a quarter, which was also right. There was a box for the man to say whether access was required to any part of the site. He had drawn a line round the compressor yards. He was standing on the top step with the form folded lengthways against his thigh, looking to see whether he ought to include the drains.

The power gave him one good look. It did not lend him the eyes of a hawk or bring the island any closer. It tipped the bit of birch bark at the lower entrance so that the white inside showed. The man saw it move. He had thought it a scrap of the tape they used on the valve stems, and went over to pick it up.

He was there for some minutes. The vole did not come out. He saw the berry, though, from the side that was missing, and the little runs roofed over with living moss. Getting up off his heels he put a hand on the newest slice and felt it rock. He went the long way back rather than step on any more of them. In the box he wrote about the drains. Under the box, where there was very little room left, he wrote, ‘Come down the steps before deciding. I can meet you any morning except Thursday.’

It was not a prohibition. The woman who had to find a place for the peat had six other forms to deal with and a little boy at home with his arm in plaster. She came on Friday. There were things she saw that the man had missed, among them a clear brown trickle running the length of the lowest terrace, which would have been directly under the toe of the tip. They spent most of the visit on their knees trying to make out which way it ran. In the end the road peat went back onto the bare margins of the bend. It made those margins look curiously upholstered for a year or two.

There was no part of this that the vole could have distinguished from an ordinary February. The weasel came back. The lower entrance froze up and had to be opened from inside; a flake of peat the size of the vole’s face stood out on the moss with the marks of its incisors on it. One morning the sedge in the nest was damp and the vole took it out, ribbon by ribbon, to a place under the heather where the wind got in. The power, which had stayed to see what the root was for, saw it at last. It had served to keep the bedding from slipping down the slant of the stone. With the bedding out of the way the root hung loose, caught at either end on a roughness too small to see. The vole ducked under it on every journey, even on the last journey out when there was nothing left to carry.
