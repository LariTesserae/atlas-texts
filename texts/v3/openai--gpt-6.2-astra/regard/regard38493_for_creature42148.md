The power that took an interest in the horn first made itself known in the room where the old spares were kept. It did not take anything. It wiped the dust off the end of a box. Under the dust, in the purple handwriting that had once been taught to all the fitters, were the words CROWN, TRIAL PIECE. The woman who kept the stores had passed that box going to and from her supper for twenty-seven years. Coming back this time she stopped to read it.

There was half a yard of the horn in the box. There were two pieces of cream ceramic, a length of hoop, three screws and the original black packing, with the chalk marks still on it to show which way it went in. It had been put together at floor level to see whether a man could get a screwdriver to the screws without standing on a part that was not yet there. Nobody now remembered that this had once been a question.

It would have been in the power's gift to put the wool at the crown back into its original condition. This would have entailed a fairly exact separation of what was wool from what was not. Looking closely at the second knot, the power had some difficulty seeing what good the distinction would do the snail. It left the knot alone.

The woman in stores sent the box upstairs on the little chain lift. For most of a winter the fitters had it on a bench. They found that the hoop could be held from below while a short length of packing was eased out; that a screw one could not turn with the hand was sometimes quite easy to turn by putting the screwdriver in first and the handle on afterward; and that the piece of apparatus drawn up in the office for holding the packing, though otherwise excellent, occupied precisely the space required by the fitter's left ear. These were useful things to find out in a warm room. They made a second apparatus. The first went back in the box.

What they could not learn on the bench was how much of the old felt they might leave. For this they took to asking the gardener to mark on a strip of paper, after a wetting, the places that dried first and last. She kept the strip rolled up in the handle of the shaving mirror. It took three springs to get a good account of the far side. Once she waited two hours for a swift to come out of a hole before it occurred to her that there was another swift in there sitting on eggs and that between the two of them they had a good deal more time than she had.

When at last they did some work on the crown, it was not near the burr. It was at a place where for seven years the fig had made a sound new leaf every spring and lost it before it was the size of a penny. There the wool came out in hard little cakes, dry in the middle even when the iron was running with water. The gardener sat beside the fitter and picked the cakes apart in a tin lid. There were white roots in the last one. At her touch the fitter stopped pulling. They finished that length well before the change of shift and went down with a good appetite.

In time the box acquired a new inscription. It said TO BE TRIED HERE FIRST. There was no order to that effect. A fitter who had been up once in a wind wrote it there for his own convenience, and the others found it a sensible arrangement.

As for the snail, on the next occasion when the daughter looked, there was nothing at the second knot. She turned the mirror this way and that until a spot of sun off it went traveling round the inside of the horn. The blades were there. One had a yellow tip. She drew the knot as well as she could, which was rather better than she could have drawn it eleven years before, and left the space beside it white.

The power knew where the snail had got to. It was on the back of the lower blade, eating the yellow part. There was no particular occasion to bring it round to the front.
