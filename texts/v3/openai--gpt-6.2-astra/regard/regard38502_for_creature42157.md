On the third hump in from the old shore there is a hollow with a fault across its roof. The fault is full of salt. When the wind wets the hump the salt goes clear, and one can see a little way into it, as into the thick edge of a piece of glass. A mother has lived under it for eleven seasons. There is a smooth place on the roof where she puts her head to turn round.

The power that had taken an interest in the gulf could feel the weight on either side of the fault. It knew to within a few grains how much more salt the roof would bear. There was one wet wind still to come before the high tide; in that wind the roof would settle. It would settle scarcely the thickness of a hunter's plate. There was that much room above the mother's back, but there was not that much above the joint of her left plow. Once the plow was caught she could neither go forward nor fold herself small enough to go back.

It would have been easy to hold up the roof. The power considered doing this. On the other side of the fault was another hollow, little more than a blister in the stone, in which the buff stain grew a quite remarkable purple in the dark. The two hollows had begun as bubbles in a single bed of mud. They had been coming together for longer than there had been a gulf. The power rather wanted to see what would grow on the broken edges when the wind got in.

It put a little grit into the fault instead, at the dry end, where grit would have gone in of itself if the wind had been from the east. At the next change of temperature there was a small click above the mother's head.

She opened one eye. After quite a long time she put a plow out and drew it in again. Dust came with it, and at the hinge of the plow, where she could taste things, the dust tasted of the purple growth. She had lived in two other hollows before this one. At the second she had tasted that taste on the way in and had had the greatest trouble getting turned about. She began scraping the young up under her.

There were seven. Six went into the usual places. The seventh, which had fastened itself to the smooth patch on the ceiling, stayed there while she got herself out onto the fan. For a moment the power supposed it would have to do something about the seventh. Then the mother reached back with a plow and rubbed the smooth patch once, quite roughly. The young one let go and landed across the bridge of her nose. She went down the fan like that, looking out under its belly.

The wet wind found them in a hollow a little farther inland, not as deep as the old one but with a turn in it which gave a tolerable shelter. The mother spent most of the dry hours widening the turn. Out of the old hollow there came a noise very like two peoples' teeth meeting by mistake over a kiss. A short way down the hump a few fliers rose and hung in the air, then let themselves back down into the warm places they had left.

At the high tide the mother went to the same basin she had always gone to. The new way home took her across a tongue of shingle where the stones were particularly loose. She had to set the plows deep, and the hunters coming back from their molt found two excellent grooves there, still damp at the bottom. By morning they had worn them almost level. The family had got indoors. A blue sliver showed at the turn until the mother had done settling her ribs.

In the crack in the third hump the purple dulled at first. Then, some days later, there appeared along the lip of it a row of minute clear knobs, each with a purple speck suspended a little above its base. Nothing in the gulf had yet had occasion to eat one. The power stayed to see what would.
