It was damp under Ena’s sill. The power that had taken an interest in the town knew this, and knew which of the two branches of the drain had stopped running. Each morning a spoonful of water more than the wall could give up in the course of a day came through the joint behind the dresser. In another month the back feet of the dresser would be soft. It would have been very little trouble to set the water running again. On the other hand there was a flat blue button in the stopped branch, lost when Ena was nine, and the drain crew would have had considerable words for anyone who put such a thing through into the common main.

For some time the power had been at a loss what to do with its good intentions. It could have made the two approaches meet, down to the pretty change in the paving where the bridge keepers had stood to stamp the cold out of their feet. It could have brought the bridge back for an hour each morning. There was something particularly agreeable about this last arrangement until it considered the person who would arrive at the end of the hour, carrying, perhaps, two cups on a saucer and a chair.

What it did in the end was to come and take the empty kitchen.

Ena asked two pennies a week, with the use of the bedroom thrown in, since it was hardly possible to get into the kitchen without going through it. For the purposes of paying rent and so forth the power had taken the shape of a rather broad woman with a parting in her hair. She had a letter of reference from a man in another town for whom she had once done something about a horse. It was an excellent reference except that the date was a hundred and fourteen years out. Ena, who had left her glasses at her sister’s, saw nothing amiss with it.

“It is damp,” the woman said when they got inside.

“Oh, good,” said Ena. “She owes me a pear.”

They had the dresser out together. The woman could have moved it with one hand, but the things on top had to be taken down and put somewhere, a matter on which her wisdom was of less use than one might think. Six cups went into the oven. The next day the crew came, and that evening Ena walked home with the button done up in her handkerchief. It was not one she had been particularly fond of. Still, it was odd to possess a piece of the dress in which she had seen her mother last, and to be able to take it out of a handkerchief and show it to somebody else who had seen the dress.

For a living the woman took in small jobs that required looking after. There were plenty. A shutter that banged only in a west wind had defeated three joiners, none of whom had been able to spare a whole windy afternoon to sit in a bedroom. She found that it was the shutter next door. She sat with a pot of yellow glaze while the potter went to have a tooth attended to. She took a turn at the floor window when Ena had a stiff neck and reported two birds in, two birds out, and something that might have been a third but was more likely the end of the second bird seen through a flaw in the glass. She was quite capable of knowing which it was. She found she liked being asked to look.

One day the boy from the dye yard brought her the yellow bowl. There was a little case of grit stuck to the inside of it, with a head as fine as a pin coming out of one end. He had had several of these before. There had always been some difficulty about being present at the moment anything occurred; there was school, there was work, there was the fact that his mother wanted the bowl. Could the woman keep it for him and let him know if something seemed about to happen?

“How soon would you need to know?”

He looked out of the window at the distance to the dye yard.

“I suppose yesterday,” he said.

They set the bowl on a slant, with a flat chip of pipe in it that came up above the water. For six days nothing happened. On the seventh the creature left its case. By that time the potter’s son had made a bowl that would do quite as well, and had brought it round every day to see if it had dried sufficiently to be fired. The boys were at school. The woman sat at the table with her chin in her hands and watched the skin open along the back of the small wet creature on the tile.

It was a troublesome business and it took the best part of the morning. The wings came out looking very little like wings. One stuck. The woman had got so far as to take a needle out of her sleeve when a drop of water from the folded stuff ran down to its tip, fell, and took the fold out with it. The wing stood clear, shaking slightly. There was another skin on the tile and a fly on the rim of the bowl.

The boys arrived together shortly after it had gone out of the window. The dyer’s boy took the empty skin on the end of a hair. The potter’s son asked which way the fly had gone, a question no one had expected and to which the woman was happy to have the answer. She told them the rest of it as well as she could. They had brought bread and a very hard pear and they ate at the table, passing the pear round for each to cut a piece, while the boy drew what she told him. He had the wings coming out flat until she folded the corner of her apron and let it fall.

The new bowl fired a fine even yellow except for a thumb mark underneath. It stayed in the kitchen. There was nearly always something in it after that.

In time the woman’s services became well enough known that there was a slate beside the door for people to write on if she was out. Some of the things on it she did and some she put a line through. When the crew asked her to listen at a drain mouth for a tapping from under the oven, she went at once. When a man asked her to find out who his daughter was walking home from the baths with, she rubbed the slate clean with a wet finger. There was no great change in the look of the town. The stone went on coming out of the water. The swifts came up. In wet weather the curl shone in its different colors and the inside walls of the shaft were white enough to hurt the eyes.

Across the hole the sisters had got into the way of looking up at breakfast to see what was going on in the kitchen where Ena used to live. It was a good window for the purpose. One morning the woman appeared in it holding something up between her finger and thumb. There was no earthly possibility of either sister seeing what it was. They could see from the care with which she held it that it was alive, and from the shape of her mouth that she was pleased.

Ena’s sister put down her cup and made the curl with one finger.

The woman nodded. They watched her put the thing down, bend to attend to the latch that had to be lifted rather than turned, and come out into the street. For a little while, as was necessary if she meant to get to them, she walked in the other direction.
