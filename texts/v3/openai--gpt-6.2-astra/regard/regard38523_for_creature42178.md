For the first few days the power that had taken an interest in the place supposed the eel was old. It was the care she took coming out of the drain that gave this impression. Then it watched her go back in. She went the length of the sump so fast that the grit she disturbed at one end was still going up when she reached the other. The care was on account of the motor. About her age there was very little to go on.

It could have held the motor still for her. This would have made a difference on the far side of the wall, where a pair of redshanks were walking down a ledge as the pump uncovered it. Each time another inch of ledge came clear the birds went along it, one from either end. There was a good deal of crossing over in the middle. By the time the motor cut out of its own accord the eel had eaten something in the drain. Two blue crab legs came out past her head and lay on the grit.

Most of the things the power thought of doing were like that. It stayed to see what happened of its own accord, which was how it came to be there on the morning they replaced the step.

The great hollow on the right had finally gone through. Under the stone there was a cavity big enough to take the head of a shovel, hollowed out by years of water going in and out of the sump. The crew lifted the step in a sling and laid it down on the roof of the pump house, there being nowhere else dry to put it. For the first time in eighty years the sun got to the foot of the wall. A little thicket of translucent things drew itself down into a layer no thicker than paint.

The new step had been cast with a grille in it. It was a good piece of work, with the edges of the slots rounded off so that weed would slide clear instead of snagging. All the slots were the width of a man's little finger. The eel was out in the wards.

What the power did was wake the man in the lorry. He had been asleep for eleven minutes of his dinner break and had another nineteen to go. He woke with his chin off his chest and a vivid recollection of the weight in his sleeve. For a moment his arm felt quite tired with it.

He went down to have a look at the hole. His mate was eating an apple on the edge of it. They could see both pieces of drain quite plainly. The man had never understood till then where she went when she went in under the step. He told his mate about the eel, indicating a thickness on the handle of the shovel, and his mate listened, turning the apple round to find the last of the good side.

In the end they took a piece out of the bottom of the grille. It was easier to do while the step was still on the lorry; the bed was just the right height to work at standing up. They filed the two cut ends smooth. At the height at which the step was going in, the opening would be under water except on the lowest springs. There was no need to make it any bigger than that. The man got back into the cab and had the last six minutes of his break.

The power would have liked him to see her use it. It had some notion of bringing him down there at the turn of the tide, perhaps by putting it into his head that he had left his file on the step. But the file was in his box and he had a long drive home. It let him go.

For two nights she did not use it. There were little soles on the scullery floor, so nearly the colour of the mortar that when one went from the middle of a tile to the joint it appeared to go out. On the third morning she came up along the curb with a bulge well down her length. She felt across three of the narrow slots before she found the opening at the bottom. Her head went in and for a second the bulge lodged against the iron. A slow turn on her side took it through.

On the roof in the hollow of the old step there was half an inch of rainwater. A sandpiper had found it. The bird stood up to its ankle joints in fresh water, dipping its bill and running the wet bill in among the short feathers of its breast. From inside the drain the eel could hear its feet.
