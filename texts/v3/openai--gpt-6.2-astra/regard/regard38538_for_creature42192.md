The power that took notice of the town did so on account of the laugh. It had heard the water often enough. There were falls all through that country, most of them in places to which no one had ever brought so much as a cup. But it had not heard anyone laugh inside a jar. It bent down over the stone to hear it again, and the woman inside the jar stopped laughing.

She had heard it bend.

This put the power in something of a difficulty. It had been a long time since it had had to consider where to put its elbows. It drew a little way off. Presently the boy helped his aunt out of the jar, and for the rest of that day she did her work as usual, except that before she put her head into a vessel she struck the lip with her nail. One of the jars she returned unmended. It was quite sound; what the owner had taken for a crack was a hair of his own, caught under the bottom of the handle when the handle was put on. He was an old man now. He took it home rather pleased.

The following day the power came back. The woman was at the bowl by herself. She had a jar with a piece missing from one shoulder and, beside it, on a rag to keep it from slipping into the water, the piece. She fitted it into the hole, put her ear to the mouth and lifted the piece away. She did this several times. Then she laid it on the rag and spoke into the jar.

“Was that the top of it?”

She meant the space above the stone. The power, which had supposed her to be asking who was there, was glad it had waited.

“No,” it said.

On the other side of the bowl a man fitting a handle to an awl put the awl down and looked into his paste pot.

The woman considered. “Could you let me hear the top?”

There was no top in quite the sense she meant, but there were things a sound went up to and came back from. The power knew a place up the street where the eaves of the houses nearly met, and a yard beyond where they did not, and beyond that a long open stretch of water with nothing over it but weather. It could have brought the sounds of all three to the jar. Instead it asked her which she wanted: to hear a sound from up there, or to have one of her own back.

“Oh,” she said. “One of my own, if you please.”

She went and fetched her nephew. He came with his hair up, and was rather annoyed to find that nothing was the matter. All the same he knelt at the bowl, and when she had got her head inside the jar he closed the hole in its shoulder with the missing piece. It made a much better fit under his fingers than under hers. He had still got the soft skin at the sides of his nails.

The power took the little sound she made and let it out at the level of the paving. It was not much of a sound, just the click you make by letting your tongue go from the roof of your mouth. It went up the face of the house opposite and came back promptly from the underside of a sill. A thinner click came from higher up. There was a scatter of very small ones from the ends of the tiles. After that, though the woman listened until she needed breath, there was nothing.

She took her head out. Her face was the color of the seam in a freshly broken pot.

“That last part,” she said. “Does it go on doing that?”

The power said it did.

She sat for a bit rubbing the bridge of her nose where the rim had pressed it. Then she asked if it would be a great deal of trouble to leave the jar like that until her nephew had had a turn.

It was a tight business getting him in, on account of his ears. She had not realized how much he had grown. Once inside he made no sound at all for so long that she began to wonder whether he had understood what to do. In fact he was trying to think of something to say which he would not afterward mind having said. In the end he whistled. Somewhere above, on a sill too far along the street to have sent back any of his aunt's click, a bird whistled after him.

The boy got his head out without help, taking a little skin off one ear.

“Who was that?”

But by then the bird had gone about its affairs, and the power, which could have fetched it back, let it go.

They mended the jar. It belonged to a woman in the clay street who needed it for settling the grit out of her drinking water, and there was no sense leaving a hole. Nor, when it was mended, did it have any quality the other jars lacked. The power had seen the boy's fingers at the patch and understood that a thing could be held for a little while without having to be made fast in that position forever.

It comes by sometimes, generally in dry weather. It has learned to wait until the woman taps twice on the lip. Once she wanted to hear rain before it went into the stone. Once she had the quarryman up to the bowl with one of his flakes, to hear what such a fine edge would sound like struck out in the open. He was disappointed. It made a poor little tick, with none of the running sweetness he got from it with his cheek against the cut. It was a satisfaction to the power, though not at first, to have been the means of settling this.

The boy has never asked to whistle a second time. He has tried to give the girl from the upper fall some idea of what he heard. Behind the quarry he puts his lips close to her ear and does his part and then the bird's, but she says he draws breath in exactly the same place both times. This is true and spoils it rather. Lately she has taken to doing the other part herself. They are getting to where they can do it quite a long way apart.
