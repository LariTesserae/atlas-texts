From down in the nap the approaching gust looks like a row of hairs combed the wrong way. The red arches come apart. The young one still has hold of its hair when the first of the seed scales goes past, end over end, and the old one puts a foot on its tail to remind it where it is. Then they all go in under the curl. A little while later you can put a hand into the vacated heap and feel, quite distinctly, the several places that are warm.

The power that had taken an interest in the plain came to it first by way of this warmth. It could attend to a very small difference without losing track of the things on either side of it. It could feel, for instance, the warmth left by the young going out through the scales into the wax, and at the same time the passage of the deep stroke that would presently shake the scales apart. For it these things had something of the interest that the two halves of the lip have for you. It stayed for the stroke. Afterward it stayed for another.

There was a place well down the scarp where it had some difficulty following the beat. Here a shelf had fallen away in the course of the winter. For half its length a rib lay open to the brown air below. One could have walked about inside it. Its tongue still swung, but the air went out through the side before the tongue could be driven up against its pad. There was a polished place on the pad as broad as a pond. On the polished place, undisturbed now for some months, lay a fledgling's tube with the mark of its foot across the flare.

The effects of the break could be felt far inland. Over a strip of country scarcely wider than the shadow of the curl, the oil had ceased to rise. The ground there was particularly handsome at sunset. The ends of the dry hairs split up the light, and an animal crossing them went red about the feet on one side and blue on the other. Seeds blew into this strip and lodged readily enough. When a seed put down its little rasp, however, all it could fetch up was a twist of pith like the dust from a drilled tooth.

It was possible to put the shelf back. The power could feel its whole weight where it had come to rest, a long way down, tilted up out of the brown murk. But there were things going on there too. Along the upper edge, still in the daylight for an hour at noon, the first tubes had appeared. Below the reach of daylight something with a broad soft sole was going over the fallen stuff, leaving clean lanes a hand wide through the minute growth on its surface. Once in a while two lanes crossed. The power had not yet seen what made them.

Instead it went into the open rib at a lull, and for one beat held the air in.

The tongue went up slowly. It struck a rather poor blow, a little to one side. The sound was quite unlike the sound of any of the working ribs. It was answered by a single pop some way along the ceiling, and a bead of oil came out, swelled till its neck grew thin, and fell on the polished place. Then the wind dropped. For the next hundred beats or so the power did nothing. At the next lull there was still a shine where the bead had fallen. It held the air in again.

In this way it found how much of a stroke the rib could stand. At first the old pad shed flakes, some of which the wind brought round and laid on the sunny edge of the broken shelf. The tube fringes took these in and shortly let them go again. But under the flakes the pad was sound. There came a morning when the tongue struck square, and on the plain, a day's walk in from the rim, a seed lying rasp downward in the dry strip took up a speck of oil. By noon its two cases had drawn a little apart. A red grazer got into the gap and was carried about on the growing lip of the seed leaf for the rest of its life.

There was no intention of keeping that tongue at work forever. Its pad would go in the end, and the sheets it fed would grow blind and serve in their turn as floors for other sheets. What the power did was to come back in the lulls. It came less often in the second season, when the young folds began to take the air for themselves. By the fifth or sixth there were days when one could have stood on the polished place with both arms full of loose wax and not lost a grain of it. The fledgling's tube is there yet, fastened down by its crease in a little skin of dried oil.

Up under the curl there was no particular account taken of any of this. The dry strip had been a good place to find unopened seeds, and for a while the seed eaters had to go farther for them. Then the new growth began to bear. Its scales were rather long, with a kink at the end that made them troublesome to pick off. They made excellent heaps, though. The kinks caught in one another, so that even when the margin came down quite hard the top of a heap would sometimes lift in one piece and settle again scarcely disturbed.

In one of these heaps a young animal woke before the others and lay with its face turned up. There was light coming in through a kink in a scale. After a time it got one half of its lip into this opening. It worked the scale loose, let it fall on the nose of its neighbour, and put its mouth to the next. The old one, lying breast down a little way off, felt the deep tongue slacken. It had time to smooth the skin out with its wrist. By the time it got to its feet to uncover the young, there was one sitting out on top in the cold, a scale still in its mouth, looking about.
