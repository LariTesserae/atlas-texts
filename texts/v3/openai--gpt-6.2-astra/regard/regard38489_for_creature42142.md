The first thing it did was take its weight off the mountain. It had come to rest in the western hollow in the form of a patch of mild weather, no broader than the back of the old wader. There was nothing to be seen except that the sleet, which lay like salt on the nap, lay there in drops. Within an hour the grazers had begun to bring their young up toward the warmth. Half a dozen broods were already above the depth at which they could spend a night. It took the better part of the afternoon to withdraw that little warmth, a leaf's thickness at a time, with the grazers going down ahead of it.

After that it kept no weather of its own. It looked out through such drops as happened to be there. In a drop on the end of the wader's bill the whole western sky swung up and down while the bird worked a moth to the back of its mouth. Then the drop fell and for a time there was a very good view of the underside of a leaf.

The old wader was not, as it happened, in much difficulty. There was an ache along one shin when the water ran cold. The joint had set crooked a long time ago and would have ached a good deal more if it had been made straight. There were fish quite near the left foot. Occasionally one went in among the fringes and stayed to clean them. The wader could tell these fish apart. One worked in little furious bouts and then lay still for so long that the next bite always came as a surprise. Much of a morning could go by in this way.

It was the chin that was in trouble. The seam from which the bird fed was lengthening uphill. Each time the eastern water came through, the split ran on another inch or two at the upper end and healed a little at the lower. The head was having to come farther up to reach it. For nine afternoons the power watched the bill go out over the gum. On the tenth it saw the head stay down although the wader was awake and there were moths enough to make a ridge along the split. The bird had worn a raw place under its chin getting over the cut edge of a leaf.

Lower down there was a second seam. This one had shut on a strand of woolly root and had never quite knitted across. By evening the gum stood in a clear line under the skin, no deeper down than the thickness of an eyelid. A grazer had been along the line twice, trying it with its implements. It gave up each time where the skin thickened over the root.

When the grazer had gone, the power finished its work. It parted the skin for about the width of a moth. A clear button welled out. Nothing came to it the first day. On the second there were the marks of small feet all over it and a narrow furrow where a wing had dragged. By the end of the week the wader was feeding with its chin laid comfortably in the nap. It did not have to lift its head at all except to swallow. The opening went on uphill eventually, as openings do, but not for two summers.

There was no particular change in the conduct of the wader. It did not become sparing of moths. On a fine afternoon it could put away several hundred, resting a little between mouthfuls to let the ones that had risen settle again. There were also afternoons when it lay with its bill open and let them go about on the pink ridges inside. The distinction seemed to be one the wader made for itself, on grounds that varied from hour to hour. The power came to be able to tell, from a slight slackness at the corners of the mouth, when a moth might walk the length of the tongue in safety. The moths never learned this.

In the third autumn the fish came out from under the left foot and spent a day in the open, very pale and rather at a loss. The head of the wader was down. There was still a good supply of gum well within reach of it. For some time the skin over its throat went on lifting and falling with the strokes of the drainage; then a flap shut somewhere below and the skin lay still. The power stayed about the hollow. When the first hard weather came, the bill was full of moths packed side by side, all facing out. They had found a dry way in at the corner, where the slack had been.

The following summer a yearling came across from the snow. It went down on its knees a little way off, in the posture proper to meeting the wader of a place. In front of it was a low green hummock with two holes in it out of which moths were coming. The young one stayed on its knees until well after the waters had parted. It was raining. A bead gathered at the heel of its raised foot, grew heavy, and ran out along a fringe still so short and scanty that the light showed through it red. Then the bird got up, with some awkwardness, and put its foot down among the fish.
