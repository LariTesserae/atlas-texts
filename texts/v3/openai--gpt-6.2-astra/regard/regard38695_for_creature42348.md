The trader's new house has a difficulty which does not become apparent until the roof is on. There is no room to kneel at the door. What looked like a continuation of the stone patch turns out, on being swept, to be the back of a grazer. It gets up rather stiffly and goes away, leaving a space about the size of a bed, across which the trader and the boy regard one another.

It is possible to enter the house by going round to the back and taking three steps along a root. But the post is in front. The trader is unwilling to move it. It is one of the grandmother's best, and before putting it in he has cut away a little of the rock to let it stand with its grain quite upright, as she showed him. He goes round by the root and puts a hand to it. The boy can see the fingers clearly through the open door. He can see the trader's lips move. This is a peculiarly unsatisfactory way of being offered something to eat.

That afternoon a shadow stops on the ceiling. It has the long legs people have seen before, but it is much larger than they had supposed. What they had taken for a hind leg is a foreleg; the rest of the animal comes on for some time after that. When at last it is all there, it kneels down. A little dune goes spilling off its back.

It has been round this way a number of times. Where it puts its ear to the stone it can hear the people through the posts, the grazers through their grinding plates, the small black builders through practically everything. For some years it has been coming to hear the names of the grandmother's daughters. There is a fifth sound at the end of the four, made by her finger coming down on the tooth to stop it going off the wood. It would miss that sound if she left it out.

The animal has it in its power to press the yellow stone down until it touches the floor. It has done so elsewhere, in a valley without light, and stood with its back bent for the better part of a winter while the inhabitants carried their eggs up its neck into the sun. It could put a foot over the unfinished acres here and leave it until there was ground. It is considering doing this when it feels, very distinctly, the tug of the little householder taking the trader's sandal apart. There are a great many households on the side of the ground on which the animal has not been proposing to walk.

It lies for a while with its jaw on its crossed forefeet. Then it puts one nail against the stone above the grandmother's house. It says the four names, very badly. The last is the easiest for it. It adds the little stop at the end.

Below, an amber tooth travels the length of a drying post, goes over the end, and lands in the husks. The grandmother retrieves it. She sets it upright again.

This time it travels toward her.

She has the boy turn the post end for end. The tooth still travels toward her. She has him carry it into the house next door, where there is a handsome post but a rather deaf man. The tooth comes out of the house next door in the deaf man's fist. He wants to know who has been telling him the names of four women he knows perfectly well.

By evening there are people with their chins to posts all along the older ground. Each hears the names. Nobody hears anything anybody else says. The animal is good at making itself heard; it has yet to learn what part of a conversation is not its part.

The grandmother considers. She sends the boy to the trader's house with the testing tooth. Then she puts her chin to her own doorpost and gives the first three names. It costs her a little effort to leave off there. She keeps wanting to finish.

Overhead a lifting of weight lets a slip of daylight into the impression made by an enormous ear. The fourth name does not come. After a time, from far out by the join, there is a light irregular chatter, followed by a round hard knock. The boy has tried the tooth on the trader's post and dropped it. There is another knock as he bumps his head on the lintel coming up.

The grandmother laughs. On the ceiling the ear comes down again, but very lightly, and the laugh reaches the trader. He knows whose it is. He puts his chin where the boy's head has been and asks if the boy might stay to supper. A request of that length loses something in the passage. What the grandmother gets is chiefly supper, with a sort of courteous space on either side of it. She gives her assent.

For a good part of that night people are waiting at their posts to speak to people they have not heard from since a path went somewhere else. There is a woman who has walked out to the shell midden every nine days for two years to ask her brother how his foot does, and walked all the way back with the information that it does very much as before. Tonight she has this information before the grazers have finished their evening feed. She stays at the post with her finger and thumb about it. After a while her brother asks if the hinged ridges have opened yet at her end. They have. Neither can at first think of anything else to say. It is a considerable pleasure to have time to think of something else.

Toward morning the posts go quiet. A considerable stretch of the valley ceiling has gone pink. The animal has lain with its ear on one spot rather longer than is good for it.

It might go on lying there. The traders in particular have seen advantages in this, and one of them has already got as far as a proposal about the hours for taking orders. But there is a place beyond the dunes where the animal's young are waiting for it to come back and lie down in a particular attitude so they can get at the ticks in the creases of its knees. It enjoys this nearly as much as they do. It gets up. The light comes back a leg at a time.

Some nights afterward, at no very dependable interval, the tooth will walk the wrong way on a post. People who happen to have something to say say it then. A person who would rather not be reached can put a folded husk under the post, although it takes the deaf man some time to discover that this is what he has been doing for years.

At the trader's house the people coming to talk go round by the root. The grandmother has cut him a second post, rather short, which he has set at the back at a slant; a person can sit quite comfortably on the root and rest a cheek on it. In time it becomes customary there to sit for a little after one's business is done.

The front post he leaves where it is. He sometimes holds it when there is no shadow on the ceiling and no prospect of a caller. Down at the very foot of it, where the cut in the rock is just a shade too wide, something is pulling in loose fibers to keep out the flour. The work goes forward by fits and starts. Now and then the trader takes off his sandal and lays it against the wood.
