The first thing the power did was to find out how much of the shingle the worker wanted held down. The bit under the grit was quite firm. At the other end there was still a little play. In the small hours two workers came through there with the soft belly of a moth between them. The shingle rose over the moth and settled on its back and was lifted again from the inside. By morning there was nothing to show where the moth had gone in. The power had been thinking of making the roof sound and was glad it had waited.

It stayed through the dry weather. At that time of year there was a great deal one might have done with a spoonful of water. There was also a great deal one might have spoiled. The grain in the upper tier was alive; once in a while a seed put out a white hook in the dark and the workers took it outside. The power followed one of these as far as the tip. The hook got caught in a loose skin there and the seed hung over the drop for two days before a finch took it. There was little to be learnt about the right disposition of water from any one afternoon.

The clouds came late. On the first grey morning the creature in the snail shell came out backwards, having no room to turn. It had a second, smaller pair of feelers, scarcely visible against the shell. It spent the morning under a damp husk, and in the afternoon, when the sun came out and the husk began to curl, went home by a rather long way round. A new ant road had been laid across its tracks.

Above the cleft the cloud had wet the pavement to the colour of a pigeon's breast. There was water in the shell print. The goat came up with a kid, very round in the middle and narrow at either end, that put its nose in the print and snorted the water out again. Where the goat set down a hind foot the stone sounded hollow as before. Where the kid came down on its two forefeet together there was a second sound, high and quite short. A flake of the ceiling, white on the side that had never seen daylight, landed in the street. A worker went round it. Another, coming out with a husk, went round it on the same side.

Here was a difficulty quite outside the practice of the inhabitants. They could shore a length of chaff and move a pebble. They could even move the town, given a few warm days and a place to go to. But the flaw ran in the hard bed above the ceiling. There was no way up to it from the street. At its narrow end the two faces still fitted one another down to the grain; at the other, air was beginning to get in.

The power took the weight of the stone. It did this gradually, for on the underside a flat spider was sitting over her parcel of eggs, and the parcel was attached to two different flakes of mica. Then it took a little of the stuff of the stone, less than had been worn out of the shell print by the noses of goats, and made it fast across the narrow end of the flaw. The stuff was from deep in the bed and of the same age and hardness. There was no peg to work loose. When the weight came on it again, the stone gave a little, as a roof so thin ought to give. The two flakes under the spider kept their distance to the breadth of a hair.

Down in the street the white chip lay in everybody's way. The power left it there. By evening it had been got on edge against the polished corner below the store. It was too steep on one side and on the other made a good step. The broad-headed worker came down over it with a piece of grain in her jaws. Going up empty, she went the old way and slipped.

The kid grew lean and sure-footed. There were other clouds. In time a root from above came upon the wide end of the flaw and turned along it, putting out hairs finer than the dust that blew into the grass. The power did not stop the root. There was room on that side, and a root takes a long time about anything it does to stone.

Once, much later, the goat stood with all four feet on the grey slab to crop the seed heads that had sprung up in a patch of dung. At the first footfall the ants stopped. The goat stood there a good while, grinding the seeds, its lower jaw going a little sideways at each stroke. Long before it stepped off, the town was at work again.
