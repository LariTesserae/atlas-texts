In the morning the girl went down to the boy's door. It was a good fifty paces from the slit, and she had to go through the bath to get there. She knew him by his arm. The rest of him was rather longer than they had allowed for, chiefly on account of his boots, which had been made for another boy altogether. They left them at the door. In his stocking feet he fitted very well, with her heels under his armpits. There was a short dispute about whether a person with somebody's heels under his armpits could properly be said to have the front.

It was in this arrangement that the power first saw them. It had come down as far as the pass to look at the green light on the cliff. From higher up it had taken the light for a living thing of considerable size, turning over in its sleep. It was pleased to discover its mistake. It stayed on the pass for most of a day, learning what made the light. By evening it could tell, from a notch that came and went in the green, when the woman at the head of the stair had got the seventh tread back in.

It could also see the other side of things. Under the rock the springs came up in long threads at a considerable distance from one another. One thread had lately picked up a grain of sand. The grain lay against a flake of green stone smaller than a child's palm, and the flake moved a hair's breadth each time the court above it emptied. In another winter or two it would turn broadside. The spring would then have an easier way out on the uninhabited face of the mountain. It was the spring that warmed the joints where the black birds laid their eggs.

The power put out what served it for a finger. It could have held the flake in place with very little trouble. Then it noticed that the joint nearest the bath fire had once been a smoke hole. Someone had plugged it with a stone spoon, of which the handle still stuck out into the flue. There were egg shells in a dry joint farther down. The birds had moved before. It took its finger away. It did not particularly like the prospect of those round wet heads being gone from the bath, but it had been there only a day.

In the meantime the boy and the girl had had a disappointment. With both of them on the sled there was nobody to receive anything. At the first gap a woman had given them a covered pot to take to the hall, and as there was nowhere to put it the girl had had to get off and walk. By noon they had decided to take turns. It was the boy's turn when he came to a place over the covered lane where there was a brown patch in the snow. He lay looking at it so long the girl began to think he was quite as bad a carrier as she was. Then he took a shrimp comb out of his sleeve and set it down. It went straight through, without so much as turning over.

The man who tended the bath fire was asleep underneath. There was a small sound as of somebody combing a beard. He woke up.

They stripped that piece of roof before supper. It had been beautifully eaten. When the girl held up a corner it let the lamplight through, every hair standing out dark and separate and the pale animals going sideways among them in dismay. The boy fetched a scrap of hide from the pile by his father's door. They laid the old piece upside down on it and by morning most of the animals had gone across into the dark. The man from the bath nailed up a fresh roof. He asked for the comb, which was just the thing, he said, for getting the soot out of his eyebrows.

For several days the power went about looking for a thing to do. It could have made the children a longer sled. But it had seen the boy coming back on foot, in the big boots, trying the roofs with the butt of the comb before he gave it away. He had found a place where his father would have to put in a patch before the thaw. On the other hand, it seemed a poor sort of kindness to keep a boy walking merely because he was useful on his feet.

At length it went to the slit between the two roofs. The girl was lying there on her stomach. She had brought back the red wool in which the tooth had come. The boy had hold of the other end and was teaching her the knot he knew how to tie in a hair. His voice came up quite distinctly, though his words were lost whenever he had to take an end between his teeth.

“Is there anything you want?” the power asked.

The girl thought it was somebody at the next chimney. “A light down here,” she said. “I'm doing it all by feel.”

The power bent a little of the last sun into the slit. The boy's fingers appeared, pink at the ends where he was pinching the wool. There was a callus on one finger. The girl put hers against it and felt the hard place ride over the soft whorl of her thumb.

“Now take yours out,” he said.

She did, and the knot held. The power kept the light there while she undid it and tied it again by herself. Then the boy asked for the light on his side, since he had never seen what the knot looked like from underneath. This took a little more doing. A man coming home across the pass was surprised to see, long after the green had died off the cliff, a single red stitch shining low down in the town.

When they had finished, the power let the sun go. It passed the rest of the evening at the bath, where there was room for it in the steam. On its way out it drank from the rock. It tried at first to do this with a finger, but the water ran the wrong way along it and dripped off its elbow. An old woman sitting by the door held out one of the little cupped spoons. She did not look up from the toggle she was shaving. She had heard that sound before.
