The power first noticed the basin on account of the sandwiches. It had been attending to the passage of sap through a fork some distance below Ina's left elbow, a difficult fork which had divided and joined again several times in the course of its growth. Her knock came down both branches. A little later came the sound of Dav sucking mustard off his thumb.

It took the power the better part of the afternoon to work out what the knock had meant. It was accustomed to knowing things from the inside, which in the case of a sandwich is not much help. Once it understood, it went back over the day's survey with considerable pleasure. There was a particular place where Dav had stepped across a vein rather than on it, without interrupting a bite. Ina, immediately underneath, had not known he was there.

There was very little in the basin that the power wished to alter. The slope of the schoolroom troubled it less than it did the furniture man. It knew, within the width of a finger, where the old rib would be in twenty years, but it did not know what sort of furniture people would want by then. The tackers needed no assistance. The smallest had discovered that by hanging from the smooth part of the peg with four feet it could use the other two to pull a much larger relation slowly downhill. It was learning something, though possibly not what its relations would have chosen.

What did trouble the power, when it came to look, was the gold-seamed room. On the drawing at the winding works the room was quite correctly entered, together with its bench and its two approaches. The space beneath it was blank. Ina had never been able to get into that space. Nor was there ordinarily any occasion for doing so. The lower leaf had taken to the upper one all the way round, enclosing a flat cell a little larger than the room, full of air at the pleasant temperature of the winter walk.

At one end of the cell, where no one could see it, a root had begun to turn.

It was not a very big root. In the ordinary way it would have turned through half a circle in eleven years and gone off about its business. Here it had come against the bottom of the cell and was pushing it up. In about a month the two skins would meet under the bench. After that the root would have the whole thickness of the floor to push against. The first thing anyone above would notice would be a raised nail head at the polished end of the seat. The last, some years later, would be the necessity of going down on hands and knees to undo one's hair.

The power could have turned the root aside. But on its outer curve it carried the fine feeding hairs for rather a fine stand of ceiling scales, and these had their own business in the town. It could have stiffened the floor. This would have put the difficulty off for thirty years and delivered it, considerably enlarged, to somebody who had not yet been born.

Instead it let a little of the air out.

It chose the place with care, at the back of the room just above the skirting, under a scale which had already begun to curl. The opening was no bigger than the hole left by a stocking needle. On the following afternoon, when the woman from the press sat down at the end of the bench, the floor went down with her and the room gave a low A.

She stood up. There was a much less satisfactory noise in the other direction.

By the end of the week the bakery girls could do it at will. They had to sit down together for the full note, and one of them had to get up very gradually if they wanted to do it twice. It was the woman from the press who fetched Ina. She had noticed that the note was getting sharper.

Dav had the pleasure, for once, of seeing Ina put her hand flat on something and get no information whatever. She had him sit on the bench. Then she felt along the skirting with a loose thread from the lining of her coat until the thread stood out into the room.

“Well,” she said. “There's a downstairs.”

The works lent them a listening rod, and the school furniture man, who had once made organs, supplied a set of pipes. It was possible, by fitting these in turn to the little opening and having Dav sit down, to arrive at a fair notion of how much air there was under the floor. They did it again a fortnight later. This time the furniture man brought his own lunch. He and Dav stayed on after Ina had gone home, trying to find a pipe that would give the same note when Dav got up as when he sat down.

The remedy they settled on was to move the bench. This sounds inadequate, but moving it left them room to lift a strip of floor and make a loose hatch immediately over the root. The root could now come up into the room if it wanted to. There was headroom to spare along that wall, where nobody stood on account of the glider. For the present the hatch lies almost level. The girls have asked to be told before it is raised, as their lower part depends to some extent on the shape of the place.

On the works drawing Dav put in the cell in dotted blue. It was the first part of the basin he had surveyed from indoors, and the first for which his weight had been reckoned a professional advantage. He put in the little air hole too. Ina generally knew the difference between a hole made by an animal and one made by a tool, but she could not account for that one. After some consideration she told him to draw it the size it was and leave off the arrow saying how it got there.

The power was satisfied with this. It had not particularly wanted credit, and it was glad they had kept the hole. There is hardly any note from it now, with the hatch fitting as loosely as it does. But if you sit quite still on the bench when a loaded tram goes by, the loose end of a woman's hair will sometimes lift from the back of her collar and lie down again.
