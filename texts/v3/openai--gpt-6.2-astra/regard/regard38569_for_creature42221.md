The power that had noticed the lake could have put a hand from one shelter to the other. What took it some time was to feel a knock as the animal felt it. In the granite the knock arrived a little before it did in the water. The filled bag received it along its whole length. By the time a pebble stirred on the floor the lip was already drawn in, except at the place where it touched the animal below. There the two skins parted quite slowly. Each waited for the other to pull first.

At the end of the line of wood the joint was rather wider than it looked from the door. A chip pushed into it from below would turn over and come out again. The old animal had got to this place three times. Each time it took out the last chip that held, put the two in together in a different order, and lost one. On the last occasion it lost the longer of the two. It felt for it for a while along the underside of the shelf, though it was on the floor.

At the next running the power held a grain of grit still in the wide place. It did not need to hold it very hard. The chip the animal put up struck the grain, turned a little, and caught by its two ends. When the lip moved away the grain came away with it. It went through the gutter and down to the tip of the right-hand cone.

For a long time the animal did nothing with the space it had gained. It even took the chip out once. It was able to put it back. The ends had taken a little set, and would now go in one way only.

Part of the peg came down that winter. It did not come into the shelter. The current carried it past the door and lodged it against a stone considerably farther up the road. For several seasons the supply of wood was worse than before. What came in was mostly black meal, which went out with the sand. Then the lower animal brought up a piece with an elbow in it. The old one took it by the elbow, which was the only way it could have taken it without breaking it, and the work went on.

When the line reached the wall there was no animal on the wall. There was a shallow hollow there, damp in the days of the travelers, in which some small article of iron had lain and gone entirely away. The old one could just get the tip of a thumb into the hollow. It cleaned out a surprising quantity of rust. In the dark it could be seen, if one had the means to see there, going back over the whole length of the wood, pressing each piece home, then feeling in the hollow again.

It was a little one that went across, many runnings later. On its first journey it got halfway and came back. There was food in the wood by now, not in the wood itself but in the skin that grew on its exposed face. The little one ate its way along and was quite full before it came to the chip that had turned in the joint. On its second journey it passed the chip. It rested in the hollow with its lip out on the wall, and when the knock came it filled its bag there.

The old animal could touch it or the one on the floor, but not both at once. In slack water it generally touched the one on the floor. When the water ran, the end that had been used to draw back under the shelf went instead to the wall. There was a small place there which the current struck very hard indeed. For the first few runnings the old animal covered it. After that the little one had the fit of the hollow and could keep its own hold.

The power did nothing to the peg. In time the shell of it went too. For a week the road above the bend was strewn with those thin wooden facets, each with the cut of the same hand on one side and the black work of the water on the other. A good many came in at the door. Most passed straight through the lips of the lower animal. One it took up to the shelf. The old animal passed it on to the wall, where the little one held it nearly to the end of slack water and then let it fall.
