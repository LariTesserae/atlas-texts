The first time the man was asked which way a shelf would go, he had been carrying his father's mallet less than a week. There were two boys in a shelter out toward the tip of the tongue. From where their mother stood you could see their bedding hung out on the forks. He listened a second time for her, though he knew quite well the second listening would tell him no more than the first. By the time he raised his head she had taken off her coat to run.

She got the boys. She lost the coat, which had a good comb in it, and for the rest of that winter she did her hair with a forked peg. The man happened to see her at it one morning. Of all the things that might have been seen on the flats that morning, this was the thing that brought the attention of the power down to the place. It had been attending chiefly to the ponds. A fork is quite a poor implement for combing hair; by the time the woman had got one side smooth the power had seen a good deal.

It could have held the tongues together. Indeed, for part of that morning it held two of them, well to the west where there were no houses, to see what the work would be. Along the seam the new leaf, which ought to have been slipping out as easily as a blade from its sheath, began to gather in little pleats. Water stood in them. By noon the pleats had frozen hard enough to cut a mitten. The power let go and warmed the seam between its hands until the leaf lay smooth again. Under one of the pleats it found a grazer, warm and rather pleased with its lodging. It waited for this to get out of the way.

For a long while after that it did nothing that could have been distinguished from listening. There was time. Its attention did not go away in the night, or when a shelf went under another shelf, or when the man put up his mallet and sat down to mend his sister's scoop. She had split the bowl getting a lid off a frozen cup. He ran his thumbnail along the crack and she, sitting some distance off and looking out over the same floor, felt the little catch of it almost as plainly as he did. They had used that scoop between them since before either could remember.

What the power finally attended to was the grain. A thing laid lengthwise on a leaf will sometimes creep a little across it, especially in a thaw. Pegs do this. You come out to take in the bedding and find it lying neatly spread on the floor, with the two pegs on top. It would require very little to let a loose thing feel, a little beforehand, the pull which the whole floor was presently to feel. The power tried it first with a shaving, then with a row of ice lids it took care to put back. There was no need for it to alter a single living cell of the leaf.

The man was in a house with a low red line when his mallet first turned. He had it laid flat to listen. The sound was the old sound. Against his temple, however, the haft gave a small insistent push, until he lifted his head and it was able to come round. It came to rest pointing almost straight at the door. He set it crosswise. Again it came round. Outside, the field of cups went by the door from right to left; then he understood that it was the door that was going by the field of cups. He got down on his hands and knees and looked along the haft. What he saw at its far end was where the house was going.

For some time this was believed to be a property of his father's stone. People made quite long journeys to fetch him. He enjoyed this, especially in houses where a little of the cut edge had been put by to sweeten the water. It was a child who spoiled it for him. While the grown people were laying out the mallet, she was on the threshold laying out the six-legged figures she had made. Being all feet on one side they lay over very readily on the other, and as the mallet came round the two little animals came round with it, on their sides, their feet sticking out in a row. After that it was found that a comb did quite as well. You could even spit on a flake of ice and set a bit of reed across it, though naturally no one cared to do this on a drinking cup.

There were still accidents. There is a considerable difference between knowing where you are going and having time to take in the bedding. But a mother going out along a tongue could now put a peg down at the seam, and often enough she put her coat back on and waited, and the tongue brought her boys to within a few yards of where she stood. People learned to set a scoop down before stepping across to trade. Sometimes the two scoops on either side of the crease pointed the same way. Then there was time to visit properly, to take one's mittens off and turn the cuffs inside out and do something about the flies.

The man never saw anything of the power. Once, very late in his life, he came out on the sunward side beside the smallest pond. It was summer. He lay down to drink and a fish came close under his face, a brownish fish, with one almost transparent place behind the gill through which he could see a reed on the bottom. Its shadow lay a little to one side of it on the mud. He could have put both into the bowl of his scoop. He lay still until the fish went away; then he drank, and filled everything he had, and went down to tell his sister.

She listened with her mitten in her lap. When he came to the transparent place she asked him whether the reed had looked bent there or straight. He had not noticed. He had expected, somehow, to have seen everything. They sat a while longer, looking across the floor. Between them the scoop stirred, quite gently, and neither had to interrupt the other.
