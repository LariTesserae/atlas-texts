In walking out of the tussock the tortoise took a loop of grass round with it. One end of the blade was caught above a heel and the other passed under the belly plate. For the first few yards there were three tracks in the dust, the marks of the feet on either side and between them the fine wavering line of the grass. Then the blade pulled free. The power which had come to look at the plain had been on the point of taking it off.

It stayed to see what else it might have been too quick about. There was the bank, for instance. In one place a foot or two of the lip projected over an undercut, and the weight of the earth and the hold of the roots were so nearly equal that a beetle landing on the grass above set the whole shelf trembling. On a plain so level, a fall of a few inches looked a considerable affair. Yet the undercut was the only place along that stretch of bank where there was shade with a little air going through it. There were moths in there, soft gray ones, their folded wings hanging down like the blades of so many little spades. The shelf held all that day.

What the power did was done rather late, when the tortoise was on its way back into the grass. A roof that looked sound gave way under one forefoot. The foot went in up to the shoulder and the shell came down at a slant, its lower edge knocking a second piece of roof into the hole. The tortoise tried going ahead. It tried backing. Each time it backed, the loose piece tipped up against the leg; each time it went ahead, the piece fell flat again. It rested a while with its chin on the earth. A lizard came as far as the break from inside, looked at the great pleated leg that filled its doorway, and went back.

When the tortoise next went ahead the power held the loose piece flat. It held it for some time. The tortoise had begun to eat a little of the purslane within reach of its head, and seemed in no hurry to try backing again. At last it did. The foot came out gray to the first row of scales. For a moment it stayed lifted, the toes spread; then it took the weight of the animal as the other forefoot went forward. The power let the piece of roof lie where it was.

There were workers at the break before the dust had settled. They came out under the fallen piece and began along its edge, so that what had been part of a roof in the morning was by evening the sill of a low door. The lizard used it once on its way out. By the time it came back the door was too small for it and it had to go a little farther along the mound.

Nothing new was to be seen on the plain from the bank. To see even the place where the foot had come out you would have had to go down on your hands and knees, and by then the light was failing. The tortoise had a little clay left between two of its toes. Well after dark, in another lane, it set the foot down on a dry stem, which bent and slipped between the toes and came up clean on one side and plastered on the other. The tortoise walked on in the cool.
