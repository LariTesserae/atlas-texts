A power capable of attending to the whole tableland at once would have been at some disadvantage on the shelf. It could see the far side of the moon as easily as the near one, and both sides of the child's pointing finger. There was no face in so much information. It had to get down to the breadth of one of the old person's pupils and leave nearly everything out. Then it saw the face. The slight inequality in the length of the stalks gave it an expression of having been told something it would keep to itself.

The power would have liked a second look. A little way along the shelf there was the child showing the old person how the face looked if you shut the other eye. It went that far, but did not go looking for the last visit the child paid to the shelf. There was no particular kindness in knowing which one that was.

On getting up it noticed something about the place which had escaped a more considerable view of it. The old person had rather more use for a third hand than most. A sole has to be held over a knee to be pierced, and the awl sticks on the return stroke. There are brown crescents on the tender face of the leather where the old person has had to hold it down with the nails. A little farther on, the two sisters have come to be fitted with new soles. The one with the whole hand is holding the leather. The other, who has some acquaintance with the difficulty, is examining the rock at the back of the shelf.

What she is looking for is a crack. There is a good way of holding a sole by putting an edge of it into a crack and tapping in a wedge of bone beside it. The sister has the bone. One end has been cut square to take a tap and the other is dark and polished from use at her own house. But the rock here is all of a piece. She tries the space under one of the amber animals, gets a look at what she is lifting, and puts it down again with great care.

It would be a small matter to give the rock a crack. The power considered doing it. There was, however, the question of where the other end of a crack in that particular piece of rock might go. The place where the child sat was the roof of the old person's house. Besides, the sister had not finished looking.

There was a loose stone near her knee. She turned it up, and there on its underside were the toes the child had drawn, rather too many of them, in a fan. The stone had a split in one end. The sister tried her wedge in it. It did very well, except that on the return stroke the whole affair came up with the awl and hit the old person under the chin. There is a patch of quite unsuitable language on the shelf, fortunately well above the height of a child.

The power could have made the loose stone heavy. Instead it put a little of its weight on the end with the toes on it. The next hole went through nicely. The sister turned the stone over to see what had caught under it; finding nothing, she set it down and tried once more. This time the power waited. She put her palm where the weight had been, lifted it a little, and put it down again. It took the power two holes to catch on. A palm laid flat meant hold. Two knuckles meant let go.

There is quite a length of shoemaking. The sister has gone home with one new sole a shade thicker than the other, to suit a difference in her legs. The old person can manage the signals with an elbow when both hands are occupied. Not all the holes are well placed. In one stretch of stitching there is a wide slant to the stitches on account of the old person attending to something the child is saying. They are perfectly good shoes.

This is not very much employment for a power of that size. It is possible to stand on the flattened range and see the white reaches where the grass gives out, and the rent in the clouds with the dim country showing through, and imagine a number of larger uses for it. But employment on the shelf does not take up any time. Nor is there a great weight required. If you pick up the stone when the two knuckles are on it, it weighs about what you would expect of a stone. If you try when the palm is flat, there is a modest resistance, about as much as if somebody on the other side of a table had put out a hand to steady the work without interrupting the talk.
