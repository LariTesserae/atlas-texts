Of all the things the power could see from above the hollow, the one it liked best was the top of the girl's head. There was a crooked parting in her hair. Her mother made it straight enough in the morning, but by the time the eggs were delivered there would generally have been something worth putting her head in to look at.

It came down to see what she was looking at now. It had time. It could have spent the whole of that spring afternoon between one knock on the wedding ring and the next, though the duckling, which had been at work most of the morning, would not have thanked it for doing so.

They tipped the basket. The duckling went over the lip breast first and landed running. The bit of shell came off. The girl picked it up before her mother could brush it into the yard.

‘You needn't keep that,’ her mother said.

‘I know.’

That night the shell dried on the windowsill, and the little clams lay in the saucer with their hinges towards the middle, except for one the girl had left in its lump of clay for the boy to do himself. The power went three shores down to the house of the aunt. It had understood by now that it was possible to see a great deal more of the town than anybody in it could see, and still to have missed the shrew.

The aunt was alone. A lamp stood on the table. She had the boy's pad open on her lap and was easing the stuffing out with two fingers. The cover would do again if she let a strip in at either side. There was a greasy patch where he had taken hold of it every day with the same hand. She turned the cover inside out and the patch disappeared.

Under her chair the shrew came out and went in again. The aunt went on with her unpicking.

Here was something, at any rate, the power could do. It considered making the shrew come out in daylight and sit on the flag. But it had seen the kestrel's view of a shrew sitting on a flag in daylight. There was no sense in teaching a little animal a thing like that. Besides, a shrew made to sit still would give the aunt a very poor idea of a shrew.

It went about the kitchen. On a nail by the door hung the little round glass the aunt used to see to the hair at the back of her neck. There was another nail, driven long ago for a shelf, in the leg of the table. The power took the glass down by its loop and hung it there. It turned it a little. Then, since the new arrangement did nothing whatever for a woman looking at a seam on her lap, it waited.

The aunt dropped a pin. In reaching for it she saw, where she had been accustomed to see the dark knee of the table, a small round room with water going through it. For the first time she saw the mouth of the groove without her shadow in it. A bit of oat husk turned slowly in the water against a hair. Nothing else happened. She got her pin.

Next morning she asked the girl why she had moved the glass. The girl said she hadn't. They had a look at it together. The girl wanted it turned a little more towards the opening, but from the aunt's chair it was right, so they left it alone.

It was six days before the aunt saw the shrew. By then she had finished the pad and fitted it and got it back again to take out half the stuffing on the left. The boy had one shoulder a little higher than the other, which neither he nor anybody belonging to him had known. She was rolling the spare wool between her palms when something went the length of the glass so fast that what she chiefly saw was the tail. Presently it came back on the dry lip of the groove. Its snout worked along a crack. The aunt could see the separate whiskers bend against the stone. Its feet were pink. She had expected them to be dark like the rest of it.

When the girl came with the eggs the aunt told her about the feet. She did not say that she had seen the shrew at last. What she said was, ‘You never told me its feet were pink.’

‘I haven't seen its feet,’ said the girl.

She got down by the chair. Her aunt put a hand on the top of her head and guided her a little to the left, until both of them could see into the glass, one face just below the other. There was only the oat husk. After a time the aunt went back to her work, keeping the girl's head against her knee, which was as good a place for it as any, provided she didn't presently want to get up.

The power had gone by then. It had left the glass on an ordinary nail. In summer the light would come from quite another quarter; the aunt might want it moved, or want it back by the door to do her hair. She could lift it off with one finger.
