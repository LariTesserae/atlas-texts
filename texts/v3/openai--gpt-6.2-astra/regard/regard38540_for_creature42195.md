The power that came to the mountain could feel weight in stone. In the slab overhead it felt the long pull towards the open air, and at the lip of the throat the answering pressure, confined to an area rather smaller than a man's two hands. There was no occasion for alarm on that account. The slab had been bearing down there when men came in out of the wind with their clothes full of snow. It had borne down on the same place all the while they slept.

One of the men had woken with a cold ear. It was he who had got up and laid the third wall. The power was pleased to know this small thing about him. He had been gone a very long time, and had had, as it happened, no other hand in the making of a nursery.

There was a weight less well disposed of in the wall with the old dish on it. One end of a long stone in the second course rested on a chip no thicker than a flake of horn. The chip was parting down the middle. Each time the outer face of the mountain cooled, the sound part of it became a little less. When it went, that end of the stone would come down into the joint. The old dish could have taken the weight well enough on the crown of its shell. It could not take it across the belly.

For three evenings the power kept company with the dish. It was a queer thing, to feel so considerable a body move so short a distance. First the young came up under the rim, knocking away a little of the yellow deposit. Then there was a long interval during which the old feet, set crosswise to the flute, held their ground. One pair gave, and caught again; the next pair did the same. By morning a grain which had been well under the belly was out in the open with a wet side to it. Before the wet had gone a round lodger came to the grain, went the long way about it, and arrived at the fringe of a hunter which had not been there when it set out.

On the fourth evening the chip parted. The power took the weight at that end of the stone. The two halves of the chip remained in place, lightly touching. A grazer got its head into the split and spent the better part of the night there, to very little purpose.

It would have been easy to set the stone up square. It would have been easier still to lift the dish, which had only to be moved the length of a finger to be out of harm's way. But it had taken the dish a season to get such a seat upon the joint. The inner crease of its belly fitted even a little fork in the seam, and gas which would have gone to waste up one arm of the fork came away instead as a particularly rich meal at three notches in the outer lip. The power left the feet to their business. It had other things to attend to, but was under no necessity of taking its hand away to attend to them.

By the time the last of the sound white rim was clear, the dish had acquired a small train. Two young ones, now quite heavy enough to keep their feet in the evening current, had set their bellies down on the fork. For a while it looked as though the stone would have to be held up for good. Then the longer arm of the fork went cold. One young dish left in the middle of the day. The other stayed on until it had been picked quite clean of meal, and went up the wall, lifting an edge which no one who had watched its arrival would have guessed to be its front.

The power eased the stone down. The two pieces of the chip fell out and lay a little apart on the floor. The still pocket behind the wall was somewhat narrower after that. It remained quite sufficient. Indeed the young that blew in on the following evening had rather less trouble than usual in getting to their feet. One of the pieces lay rough side uppermost, and the first dish to strike it turned a complete somersault and arrived on its feet with a sleeper's supper still on.

As for the old dish, it had come to a bad bit of going, where the flute petered out on the face of the stone. It tried the bare rock with several successive edges. At last it got two feet into a pit left by a bubble when the stone was hot enough to run. The rest of the feet drew in towards these two. There was a small sound, quite distinct in a lull of the wind, as the worn outer lip came free of the flute.

The power had already let go. It stayed to hear the sound.
