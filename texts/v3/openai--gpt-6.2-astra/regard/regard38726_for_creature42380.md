The first difficulty the power had was that it could see the whole of the country at once. The girl holding the frog could turn the other girl's head readily enough, if she hit upon the right lie, but there was very little she could tell the power that it wasn't already in a position to verify. The green place on the stalk stayed green. At dinner a spoonful was taken from a dish and the hollow in the dish filled up with gravy, which was what gravy ordinarily did but by no means what these people had counted upon. All along the runnel there was a slight falling off in the conversation.

The power took the hint. It is worth mentioning that when it looked away it really did look away. It didn't go on seeing through the back of its head, as certain of the mothers claimed to do. For the rest of that afternoon it confined its attention to the basket man's left knee, where there was a good deal worth learning about the management of a knife.

It was there that the old woman found it. She knew somebody was looking because she took a curl and for once there was quite a clean patch of trouser where she had taken it.

“Are you wanting one?”

It had not supposed it wanted one. Now that the offer had been made it found that it did.

She chose a broad curl with a little of the green outside still on it and set it on the bench. “You can do that one. Have you a mouth?”

After a little consideration the power said that it could have a mouth.

“Well, have a dry one to begin with. There's a great temptation to get them too wet.”

The basket man moved over. There was no great change in the appearance of the bench, except that the damp at one end of it took the shape damp takes round a person sitting down. The old woman showed the power how to fold the rush with the grain outside, and how to put a nick in the fold with a lower tooth. Its first whistle was a very good whistle indeed. All the bats on the nearest fan let go together, thought better of it, and went back. The old woman was delighted with the bats but not altogether satisfied with the performance.

“Did you know it would do that?”

The power admitted that it had had a pretty fair idea.

“With this one,” she said, “I'll do the folding.”

She put both hands behind her. There was a longish interval in which the power attended to the saucer. One of the wasps was washing its face. Presently there was a whistle held up to the power's mouth, and the old woman blew in at the other end just as the power blew out. It made no note whatever. A little wad of pith shot across the bench and stuck to the basket man's shin.

The old woman laughed so hard she had to sit down on the damp. The basket man went on working for a moment, finished the difficult part of his corner, and reached down and touched the wad with his finger. “You'd have trouble doing that twice,” he said. It was high praise from a man in his line of work.

After that the power came fairly often to the landing. It was useful at a funeral, not on account of anything it could do for the person on the slope, about whom it was quite properly not consulted, but because the fans were heavy. To keep a fan moving for two nights at the same speed, without once letting the draught get into the little lamps, was well within its capacity. It learned to ask where people wanted the air. Some wanted it on the eyelids and some could not bear it there. The family went home when they were ready. The power generally stayed a little to rub the creases out of the fans.

In time the old woman had some trouble getting up to the landing. It was the height of the walking rims rather than the distance; her knees would take her a long way provided they didn't have to bend. The power offered to bring the basket man down to her door. She pointed out that he sat where he sat for the sake of his customers. It offered her a knee that bent better, which was rather more to the point, and she tried one for the length of a tile. She came back walking very oddly.

“It's a fine knee,” she said. “But it goes first. I'm accustomed to go first myself.”

In the end they settled for a hand at the rims. This left the level stretches for her to go along in her own fashion, stopping when a whistle interested her and sometimes going back to try it on a different leaf. The power had been under the impression that she wanted to get to the basket man. It took several journeys to discover how small a part of the expedition this was.

One afternoon on the return trip she asked it a question which had evidently cost her some thought.

“Before the tiling,” she said, “did they know what it was going to be like?”

There was a perfectly good way of finding out. The power could have looked back along the joints to the first joint, and seen what was in the mind of whoever knelt there with the lime. It explained this to her. They were sitting on the rim by then, with her heels against the warm side of it.

“Could I see too?”

No, that was the disadvantage. It could tell her afterwards.

She considered this. “Then I'd rather try something.”

She had it take out one of the small slabs. There was the other slab underneath, hair and all. She had it set the loose one down at a slant from the rim to the tiling. It made a neat little ramp. She walked down it and up again, bent over with her hands clasped behind her, and was so pleased with the grade that she did it twice more. Underneath, in the pleasant triangular shade, a fish had already got its eye up.

“There,” she said. “I was thinking about my knees. I don't suppose the fish would ever guess.”

The power agreed that the fish would be quite justified in its own opinion of the matter.

They put in three more slopes on the way home. At the last one the power made to give her its hand from habit, but she was down before it had the hand ready. It felt for a moment the peculiar emptiness of a hand held out for somebody who has got down perfectly well. Then she passed up the whistles for it to carry. She needed both hands to shake the grit out of her apron.

The slopes are there still. From the landing they look like four pale strokes in an immense extent of yellow, and a stranger generally has to have them pointed out. There are houses under two of them now. If you ask who laid that part of the country you will get a much more satisfactory answer than you get about the rest of it, though there is some difference of opinion as to who did the heavier share. The power says it only carried the tiles. The old woman's account, which is the one the children like, is that she had hold of the country at one end and the power very obligingly tipped it up.
