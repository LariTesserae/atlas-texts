There was one of the clear animals that found the sound of its first grinder rather often. Its pebble had come up on the back of a pillar, in a pocket of the black cap, and the grinder, now as big as a bed, worked alone up there. All the others had settled on the softer stone below. It had a peculiar way of clearing its teeth. It knocked them together three times, with a long rest before the third knock. In a little patch of afternoon shadow the sound came through very well indeed. The animal would pass a dozen excellent hollows full of mites to get to that patch.

The power that had taken an interest in the plateau came to know this animal chiefly by its stomach. There was usually something red in it. From a considerable height one could follow the red speck along the shade, lose it under a projecting lip of stone, and pick it up again where there was apparently nothing for it to walk on but its own small reflection. One afternoon it went to the listening place and stayed there. The shadow was already off its tail.

To the power it seemed a simple matter to hold the shadow still. But the shadow of a pillar half a mile high is attached at its near end to a good deal of country. Along one edge of it young grinders were just getting the first light they had had since morning. At the other edge a number of brown coats had split and were still full of legs. It would have been possible to attend to all these things in turn. Meanwhile a little curl had appeared in the clear animal’s tail, like the curl on a shaving of horn.

The power put the shadow of a pebble over it.

It was the shadow of a quite small pebble held a long way up, where there was nothing for the pebble to rest on. Down on the frost it made a faint oval, not much darker than the mark of a thumb on a sheet of white paper. This was enough. The tail straightened. Presently the animal lifted its hair and walked to the edge of the oval. It put out one foot and drew it in again.

The next shadow was eight strides off. The power could have taken it there in less time than it takes for the hind foot to replace the forefoot. Instead the pebble waited. When the animal tried the edge a second time, the oval gave a little in that direction. The foot went down. In this fashion they crossed six strides toward the next pillar and two more in quite another direction, to a hollow where the wind had left a good pinch of mites. There the pebble had to wait a long time. By the time the animal had finished eating, the natural shadow had overtaken them both.

For several afternoons the power carried the pebble. It was a pleasant occupation, though it required rather more attention than one might suppose. A clear animal approaching a sleeping bird, for instance, slows down well before there is anything visible on the frost. Once the oval went on by itself for nearly a yard. The animal stood with its forefeet in the air, waiting for it to come back.

On the sixth afternoon the grinder on the cap stopped work. It had come to the bottom of the pocket and its teeth were on slate. It tried the slate in several places. The sound in the listening patch was like a spoon being drawn across the bottom of an empty dish. Then there was a considerable fall of old brown husks down the north face. The grinder had taken its mouth off the stone.

The clear animal came at the usual time. It put its hair down here and there and made a short excursion toward the base of the pillar. High up, the grinder was coming over the edge. Its plates were nearly vertical and reflected, instead of the sky, a greatly enlarged red bird which was running along beside its mouth to get the last of the meal. It took the grinder the better part of the afternoon to descend its own length. When the sun went behind the cap it settled into a vacant place in the top ring. The other animals made room to the extent of an inch or two on either side, and the shoots from a flat tree began feeling along its belly. It set its teeth to the butter-colored stone. The whole pillar took up the sound. There was no longer a particular patch of ground where one could go to hear it.

The power had the pebble ready. Below, the clear animal stood in a narrowing strip of blue, its feet close together. There came the two knocks and, after what seemed even to the power a considerable interval, the third. The hair came up. Without once trying the open ground, the animal turned along the strip and went about its evening business.

The pebble was put down at the foot of the north face, just where the slide of husks had come to rest. It is still there, though it would be hard to find now without disturbing things. A tree has grown across it. Under the overlapping leaves, where it is warm and there is a pattering for ten minutes in the morning, a blind beast has made a nest in one of the coats. It goes in brown at the neck and comes out yellow through the split along the back.
