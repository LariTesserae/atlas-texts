The girl is not waiting for the sake of the fly entirely. She wants to see how it does it. It turns head on to the wind, which is the very thing she would have tried to avoid, and gets first one wing and then the other out of its case. Once it is off the sill she loses it at once. She takes the mug along to her mother with a thumb in it, there being no point in keeping it clean on the way to a scalding.

The power which has taken an interest in the borough can follow the fly quite easily. Indeed it has a difficulty of the opposite kind. It can see the chip in the mug from the top step. It can see both sides of the curtain. It takes some trouble, standing where the girl stood, to find out at what point a fly of that size ceases to be visible. A little beyond the coil of cable, it turns out. Beyond that the girl has to take it on trust that the fly is still going.

The power spends a Wednesday here. There is a bed free on Wednesdays. It does not require a bed but is interested in what happens to a person who lies down on one, and in particular whether the train noise goes on all night. It does. With one ear on the pillow you can distinguish a knock which you cannot hear sitting up. It comes in threes, with a considerable wait before the third. Towards morning the power finds itself waiting for it with some impatience. It begins to understand what a night in the borough is like for a lodger who is not particularly fond of the sea.

There is one thing it does for the place.

On the wall of the machine hall, a little above the wooden gear cover, is the handle of a pump which can be worked by hand. This is for use in the event of a failure of the electric supply. The men who put it in had no notion of keeping all that water moving by the strength of one man’s arm. What the hand pump does is fill a little leather cup on the suction side, so that when the current comes back the motors do not spend their first few minutes drawing air. Instructions for its use are on a card under glass. The man in blue has used it twice. On the second occasion there was a job getting the handle down at first, on account of the stiffness of the leather.

Under the leather there is now a white eel, about as thick as the drawstring of a flour bag. How it got in there would be quite a question for the surveyor. It went in when it was considerably thinner. There is room for it to turn round, and both the water and its small affairs come and go through the hole by which it entered. If the handle is brought down it will have practically no thickness at all.

The power has no objection to an eel being eaten. There is a sort of heron two miles up the coast which could scarcely be asked to live on anything else. Nor does it propose to make the leather give way, since the hand pump is a good pump, and those few minutes of air, though of no particular consequence to the people upstairs, are a long time behind a button animal’s plate. What it does is to let the man in blue feel the eel.

He is in the act of taking down the handle for its yearly oiling. He has shut his hand round the iron and has not yet drawn out the pin. A little piece of the coolness inside the pump runs across his palm. Then comes the unmistakable business of something alive trying to get its tail past its head in a confined space. He lets go.

For a while he stands there opening and shutting his hand. He is not a fanciful man about machinery, though he has known a loose ball in a bearing to give a very fair impression of a mouse. Finally he fetches a basin from under the bench and undoes the four nuts which hold the cup. A quantity of black water comes out, followed by the eel, which goes round the basin twice and lies with its chin slightly up on the enamel where the water gets shallow. It is a perfectly ordinary eel. There is a little row of marks along its side from the stitching in the leather.

He takes it to the nearest well. By now the girl has come to the hall to return the mug, her mother having taken the opportunity to scald the handle of the gear cover too, and forgotten the mug on the bench. She goes out with him. He tips the basin and the eel is gone almost before the water, leaving a long wet stroke on the brass lip. The girl has a good view of the whole proceeding. She is rather sorry not to have seen it come out of the pump.

The power leaves the rest to the man. He thinks the matter over at dinner and puts a little domed strainer over the hole, a dome being preferable to a flat piece since a flat piece can be covered by a single shell. He puts the date in the book. Under *Reason for alteration* he writes *Eel*. There is space for a good deal more than that.

In the evening the former weighmistress puts the mug back. It has come up a different colour, especially inside, and for a day or two people drinking from it get a much better view of their noses. Nothing is written on the sill. No one is told to be kinder to small things. The girl, asked what she has been doing, says she helped Mr Vale put an eel down a hole, which is near enough. She sleeps that night in the last house, with her case upright under the chair so that the toothbrush will dry.

The power goes up the steps before it leaves. At the top it turns to look at the third house. There is the mug, and there is the handle, and this time, with a little care, it can just manage not to see the chip.
