At first the power came down where the families were sitting out in the light. There was water in a bowl and it put a finger under the bowl and raised it. The water ran out through the place where a root had rotted. The young went in so promptly that the last one caught the end of its beard under the lid and had to come out backward and do it again.

It was easy to keep the finger there. But toward noon one of the adults came out, went a little way over the moss, and lay down with its beard flat. Then it came back and tried the same thing on the crown of the hummock. It went to and fro six or seven times. Outside, all the hairs lay over together at intervals, as grass will show the passage of something too small to see. On the crown not a hair moved. At last the animal opened the lid and began to carry the young out.

The power let its finger give a little. The adult, which had a young one tucked up under its narrow jaw, stood for a time with four pads on the lid and two on the moss. It put the young one down. By evening it was taking pieces of root below again. When the power could feel through the bowl the little tug of a place a long way off, it took its finger away.

After that it spent some time at the other ends of things. A motion that left a bowl might come out in open water. There would be a ring the size of the lid, then a ring as wide as a leaf was long, then nothing you could distinguish from the rest of the rain. Or it would come out in a bed of pith where the grubs were, and an air bubble would travel the length of a dead stalk and stand at its broken end. Once the power followed the dip of a bowl with five young in it and came to another bowl. There were two young here, considerably older, quite able to climb out on the lid when the water came in. It was a bitter morning. The smaller of the two had only just got warm enough to go back when it had to come out again.

Now there is a place well down in the mountain which gives rather more than stone. The power has put the palm of its hand there. A few ends of root come down a seam almost to meet it. They are no different from the ochre cables in the water, except that the part of them which lies in the dark is quite white. The hand does nothing to a root that is not touching it. Even of those that touch, a good many have their business elsewhere; under the palm one can feel the far-off, rather ticklish business of a bank of reeds in a high wind. But where the weight comes on and goes on coming, beyond the give of the bog at the other end, there is a little more room now. The fingers open. Down in the mountain, in perfect darkness, the hand takes on for an hour or two the shape of the underside of a nest.

There are still families to be seen in the leaves after rain. Not all their roots go deep; not all the pieces that the adults bring home are alive. On those mornings the power sometimes puts the back of its other hand against the weather, a little way off. The leaves in its lee cease beating against one another. An adult there can lay its beard among the stalks and feel a good deal farther. To see where the hand has been, look for a place no wider than a few strides where the dew is still on the rims of the grazed windows, although everywhere else it has shaken through.

The young ones sometimes come out on such a morning before there is any call for them. They sit in the shelter and put their short beards down. One will find a loose root and pull with all six pads until it comes away quite suddenly and the animal is sitting on its rump in a cup of water. There is generally an adult within reach. It does not take the piece away, even when the piece is plainly no good. It draws it once through its own long beard, very slowly, and gives it back. The power, which can feel a storm settling its weight on a slope a hundred miles away, keeps the wind off the two of them a little longer.
