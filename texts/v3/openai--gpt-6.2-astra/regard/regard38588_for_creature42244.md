The power saw the wing open at the frog’s foot. It was a small effort of attention, for a power that could follow the rain all the way down the slope; but to see what the frog had seen it had to come lower. From the height of the pale nick there was no second opening in the fig. There was a little overhang of purple skin, with a grain of silt adhering to it. The flies appeared to come out of the ground some distance away, already in flight.

While the power was down there a fly came out backward. For a moment its two wings lay along the ground like the blades of a little pair of shears. The frog took it. In wiping away the pulp the frog turned a little, and when it put its hand down again it was looking under the overhang. The power went on sitting beside it. By dark the frog had eaten well.

It was farther back, behind the dead wood, that there was something to be done. The wood had rested for a long time on a stub of its own branch. Termites had come up through the stub and eaten out the middle, leaving the hard wood on the outside in a ring. A crack had gone halfway round the ring. When it went the rest of the way, the wood would turn over against the bark. There was room in the black place for the frog to turn round, to draw its legs up out of the warmth and to let them down again. There would still be a black place when the wood turned over. It would be about the thickness of a wing.

The power found a pebble in the silt, red on one face where it had lain in red earth before the rain brought it here. It rolled the pebble into the angle at the far end of the wood. A springtail that had been standing on it went off at the first stir and came down on the same face after it had turned completely over. The pebble fitted loosely. For most of a morning it did nothing at all.

Toward noon there was a dry tick. A little meal came out from under the dead wood, and the wood lay on the pebble. At the other end, where the frog went in, the sill was lower by the breadth of the pale nick. The frog put its chin up on it that evening in the ordinary way. Something about the new height suited it; it brought one hand up too and sat with the fingers hanging out.

For a while the power could feel the whole weight of the wood on the little red face. Then the feeling wore off. There were roots thickening under the pebble, and beetles working farther up the piece of wood, and a fern coming on whose shade would presently reach the slit. It left the weight to them.

The next rain took some of the earth out from under the fig. It tipped, very slowly, while the frog was sitting by it, until the second opening stood up in plain sight and the old split was against the floor. Out of this considerable disturbance came one fly. The frog missed it. A little later it took another.
