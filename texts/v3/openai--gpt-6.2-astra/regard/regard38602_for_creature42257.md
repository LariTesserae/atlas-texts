The first things the power left alone were the fish. It had seen the one that went broadside against the pier. In the last instant the fish had got its head round and was swimming strongly, in quite the right direction, inside the eel's mouth. The power could have put it back a foot in the water. There would then have been another fish a foot from the mouth, and the eel was hungry.

What held its attention for rather longer was the heel of concrete under the middle pier. There was a piece of brick in there no bigger than a cake of soap, set on end. One face was quite clean and the other had little white tubes on it. The conger brushed the clean face with its flank every time it changed sides. At the top of the brick, where it bore against the concrete, there was a fresh red line. This was the last of a course of bricks that had once gone all the way across the pier.

The pier was not about to fall over. There was a great deal of it let down into the floor. But a considerable flake was coming away from its foot, and in a few months, or a few days, the brick would split and the flake would come down flat. If the conger was out when that happened it would have to find another lodging. If it was in, there was nowhere in its lodging where it would not be under the flake.

For three nights the eel stayed in. On the fourth, an hour or so before the change of pump, it went as far as the first pier, following a smell. A dogfish outside the grille had been at something too big to swallow and a good deal of the news of this was coming in through the bars. The eel went up almost to the weed. In the empty place under the pier the power took the weight off the brick, laid it down on its clean face and let the concrete settle. There was no more noise than when a mussel drops off a wall. A little milky water came out at both openings. The anemones took themselves in.

The conger came back along its own track. Its nose met the concrete where there had always been water and it went up, following the face of the pier for nearly the length of its body before bending away. At the other opening it did much the same thing. It went round again, very slowly, with the white rim of its eye close to the brick. There is no reason to suppose it found anything satisfactory in what had been done.

There were places outside. The power had seen them; it did not undertake to put the eel in one. For the remainder of that night it lay with its head in one of the rung slots and nearly all the rest of it in plain view. The prawns kept to the far end of the ladder. When the pumps changed it coiled rather awkwardly against the pull, then let go and went down the room with the fish. At the grille the fish divided to pass through a dozen spaces. The eel chose one near the bottom and took what looked like an inordinate time to go through it. The gull, in possession of a sprat, missed the whole thing.

Beyond the lip of the intake the sea floor slopes quite sharply. There are boulders there with skirts of pink weed, and under one of them, about level with the floor of the room, a run of coarse shell sand goes in at the front and comes out at the side. A fortnight afterwards there was a clean sweep in the sand at the side opening and a blunt head resting in it. The power, passing that way, knew the little nick in the upper jaw. It had not been necessary to know how things turned out, but it was a pleasure.

In the intake the flatfish is still behind the sill. The butterfish has given up the bottleneck. At the foot of the middle pier the two anemones stand a little closer to the floor than before, in a good wash of water, and the rusty nut is nowhere to be seen. When the pumps change, fish still come sideways against that pier. For the present they have time to put themselves straight.
