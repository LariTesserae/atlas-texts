The power that had taken notice of the basin could have set all the fallen stones upright in the course of a night. For that matter it could have brought back one of the hills. It had a fairly good notion of where the top of the hill had been. It was less certain what would become of the red threads under a stone that it set upright. It went about for a while at the height of the dog, which was a good height for seeing things other than the things one had come to see.

What it did in the end was to take a corner of the white hide on an afternoon when the woman holding it fell.

She had been standing on a slab tipped a little outwards from the wall. The slab went out from under her like a cake from a greased dish. She came down sitting, with one leg in the slot it had left. There were three children at the baskets. She got the hide above her head once, but from where she sat there was nothing behind it but white sky. For the signal to mean anything at all it had to be lifted against the wall.

The power took the corner that was furthest from her hand. She felt the pull and hung on. Between them they got the hide up where it belonged and let it drop, and got it up again. The smallest child saw it the second time. She gave the boy beside her a shove in the direction of the ridge. He passed the shove on rather harder than he had received it, and the three came in.

By then somebody had got the woman free. There was mud on her stocking and a place on her shin as white as the hide, which in a little while would begin to hurt. She wanted to know who had held the other corner. Nobody had. For a day or two she thought it might have been a gull, and looked with considerable tenderness on those birds, until one took the skin off her knuckle over a fish head.

The power had felt, through the hide, the instant at which the woman understood that what held the other end would go up when she went up and down when she went down. It had liked that exceedingly. It was tempted to stay at the wall and lift the hide every afternoon. But there were days when the children ought to be called in early and days when they could perfectly well have another basket. From the wall the two sorts of day looked much alike. The woman could tell them apart even while talking to somebody about something else.

They put the slab back flat. The old man cut a shallow nick across it, just where the woman liked to set her heel. When he had finished, the girl took off his spectacles to blow the grey dust out of the tuft of wool. He stood squinting into the distance while she did it. Down by the widest pool one child had stopped on the way home and put her face to the water.

“What’s she lost?” he said.

His granddaughter gave him back his spectacles. “She hasn’t lost anything.”

The power went down to see. For a little while it lay beside the child on the wet lip of the pool. There was just room under the sill for a brown fish and the shadow of a brown fish, and then the two came together again.
