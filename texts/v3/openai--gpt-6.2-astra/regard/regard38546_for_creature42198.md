The power that noticed the plain noticed this foot. For a little while it went round and round on the pad of your finger. Then it found the edge of your nail and the animal drew itself into the corner there, which was too dry. You put your hand back under the lip. It took a surprisingly long time to persuade something to let go of you in the very place where it had been so willing to take hold.

The power kept its hand out of the water. This required some care. Its manner of attending to a thing ordinarily let a little light fall on the thing attended to, and already three or four fans of fish had come apart on the floor.

It attended instead to the back of the floor, where a little light did no harm. Here the maker's writing occurred again. It was very small this time, a pale yellow transfer on the shoulder of a pipe, with an arrow indicating the direction in which the contents ought to go. The stuff in the pipe was going the other way. There was no particular trouble about this. Two pans away a valve had failed in the shut position perhaps a million years ago; the stuff had found its way round by a longer pipe, and now came up on the wrong side of the arrow. A population of little white purses lived on the downstream side of a burr inside the pipe. Each held its mouth open with two stiff hairs. Had the power mended the valve they would have folded up like a row of empty pockets.

There were many such arrangements. Under one of the great wheels an oil cup fed nothing at all. The shaft had shifted a little in its bearing. The oil hung to the bottom of the cup in a gold pear until a long gray tongue came out of the dark and took it. Presently the tongue came out again, with a quite different movement, and ran a little oil along the shaft. There was a bright line there exactly as wide as the tongue. In the warm rooms below, whole banks of inspection lamps had gone out. What shone green through the water was in some places the lamp, and in some places a soft hood grown over the lamp, with the green going about in it like the light in an opal. The power looked a long way down without coming to the place where it would have been wise to begin putting things right.

It came up again in time to see the freight-car animal change beds. For all the hurry of its eyes it did not go far. One pace, a considerable pause, and another pace brought it to the hollow just vacated beside it. As its belly went down the eyes ceased traveling to and fro. One of them settled on the rib. The other had a six-legged thing to look at. The little thing was making almost no headway across a grain in the pink glaze, a grain you could not have felt with your thumb. First three feet and then four got across. The blue bead followed each foot. When the last foot was over, the eye went back along the grain to the place where the crossing had begun.

The power had been thinking of making a window in the rib. There was so much to see from even the height of your knee. Instead it stayed for a week and looked at the rib. Once the little carrier came back the other way. This time it went round the end of the grain.

You in the meantime had walked west to the rain. On the return walk you went rather less quickly. There were places to drink but no very good place to sleep; the bridges were occupied even on the undersides of their handrails, and when you lay down along a lip one whole flank of you got cold while the other stayed warm. Toward the end of the second day you came to a gap in the walkway. There had never been a bridge here. Across the gap, no farther off than a man could toss a shoe, was the lip you remembered by the little flaw in the spillway. The long way round was visible for nearly its whole length. You sat down.

It was you, after all, who had no particular fitting in this place. Even your shadow had to be dismantled every time you went on. The power could feel the weight with which your wet heel pressed the corner of the ceramic. It put out something level with the lip, quite close to your heel but not under it. It had the dull warm color of an unglazed tile. There was room on it for both feet.

You tried it with one foot and the thing remained quite still. When you had both feet on it and had sat down again, there was room behind you to lie back. You did so. Nothing went anywhere. For a long while you lay with one arm over your eyes and the other trailing in the water up to the wrist. Something kept coming to the end of your middle finger and cleaning it with an industrious little rasp. You missed it whenever it went away.

Only in the morning, when you sat up and looked across at the spillway, did another tile come level with the first. You went across singly, the tiles appearing within easy reach, with a little pause after each one. Behind you they stayed where they were. Halfway over you turned to see what was following. Under every tile there was by now a fan of fish.

You would have liked to pick the tiles up. That was plain from the way you stooped over the last one and got your fingers under it. But you could see the fish too. You let it be and went over to the bright track to see how the two thin young ones were getting on.

The power stayed to take up the crossing. It waited until the vaults began to dim. Then it took the tiles away from the top down, a little thickness at a time, leaving at the last a film no thicker than the skin on warm milk. Through this the dark heads of the fasteners began to show. One fish turned a little, and another put its nose into the place the first had left. Well before the films were gone there was nothing under them that needed them to stay.

It kept the first tile, the one on which you had slept. The plain had no store of such things, and your heel had left on it a shallow wet crescent. The next night, when you were tired, you found it beside you again. This time you drew it a little nearer before you lay down. It moved quite easily.
