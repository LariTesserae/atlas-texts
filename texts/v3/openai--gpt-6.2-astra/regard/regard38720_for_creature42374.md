The power came to the shelf by the wheel road, and arrived just too late to see what the little girl was showing. It waited with her for the belt to come round again. This pleased her; most people said they had seen when they had not. The next time the frayed place went over she held out her hand, and the five fine shadows ran up the inside of her wrist. The power saw them very well.

It stayed on the shelf for some time. It could attend to a great many things at once, though there were things about the town which it learned only by making mistakes. It took the ironed collars in the little room off the barber’s for an offering of some kind, until a man with wet hair came in and put one on. It thought the postmistress waited on the steps so as not to frighten the brown animal. One evening, when the shell had proved a particularly easy one and the animal had gone almost at once, it saw the disappointment on her face and understood the arrangement rather better.

There was one thing it could see that nobody on the shelf could. In the stone above the fourth wheel room the underside of a scale had come to rest on a knob no bigger than a bun. For longer than the works had been there the knob had held. Now a thread of water, working round it on the uphill side, had nearly made the circuit. When the ends of the thread met, the knob would go to meal. The scale would settle by about the thickness of a closed book. This was the usual way of things on the shelf. Unfortunately the top of the path to the fourth wheel room went under one edge of the scale and the bottom went under the other. A man there at the wrong time would have very little room for his head.

The power might have held the scale up. To do this properly it would have had to hold it up for good; and even then the water would have gone elsewhere, perhaps to a place less convenient. There were four people in the room on the morning the two ends of the thread came within a hand’s breadth of one another. The power went down to see about getting them out.

At the door was a slate on which the wheel hands put down anything that wanted doing. There were entries in three hands about a lamp wick. Here the power wrote, in the rather labored hand of anyone using a slate pencil for the first time, *The roof of the path is coming down. Will you go outside?*

The first man who saw it took his coat off its peg, went up the path and looked at the roof. Then he came down again and wrote, *Which bit?* He had spent a good deal of his working life outside rooms in which somebody had smelled something, or thought he had, and it was going to take rather more than a notice to get him out into the midday sun.

The power liked him for the question. It drew a section of the knob, the scale, and the thread of water. It put in the coat pegs to show which way round the drawing went. By now all four were looking, including the postmistress’s brother, who had missed the conversation but could follow the drawing quite well. He picked up his stick and went halfway up the path. At the place marked on the slate he put the ferrule against the wall. A little water ran down the stick into his sleeve.

He came back for the others. They went out by the lower door into the third room, taking the slate with them because it was the only indication they had of how much of the path would go. About an hour later there was a noise as of somebody upstairs moving a trunk a few inches to get at something behind it. On the surface a line of bubbles crossed a road where no bubbles had crossed it before. A child got a straw and tried to blow them back.

For some years afterward newcomers to the fourth room were shown the drawing. One of the wheel hands had copied it onto a blue tile before anyone could want the slate for something else. There was much discussion as to who had drawn it. The brother was out of the question, having been at the lower end of the room in full view, and besides he always drew a coat peg with a coat on it. The question of who had written *Which bit?* never arose. Anybody in town would have known that hand.

They cut another path, this time open to the sky for most of its length. It let a bar of fierce white light into a part of the room that had always been dark. For the first few days the wheel hands found the light troublesome. Then one of them moved his bench three feet to the left and found he could see to thread the little brass oilers without having to take them up to the door. In the afternoon, for a short time, the colorless flies crossing the bar of light cast enormous shadows on the curve of the wheel guard. The rush cutter’s granddaughter was brought down expressly to see them. She said they were very nice but not the same thing at all.

Nothing else on the shelf required the power to put its hand to it just then. It went down the long stair to see the birds dive. It had seen them come up out of the cloud, and was curious about the other end of the business. At the foot of the stair it took a seat beside an old man who was stripping the leaves off a bundle of cut rushes. For some time the two of them watched a brown ledge with no birds on it. Then the old man, without looking up from his rushes, put out a finger and turned the power’s chin a little to the left. What it had taken for the ledge was almost entirely birds.
