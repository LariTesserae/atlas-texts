The power that took an interest in the field did not put a finger through the hatch. It had seen what happened to the electrician's pencil mark. For the electrician it was a mark of no appreciable thickness. For the short clear animal it was a bank, down the side of which it had gone a considerable distance before finding a place to cross.

Nor could the power make out, from the sound of the heels, whether anything was wanted. It listened for a year. There were bad strokes certainly. One could hear a foot come down soft three times, miss twice, and come down hard on the new heel. But the little trot at the far end went on very much the same in the dark as it did when a man was there to be pleased by it.

What brought the matter to a head was the sale of the works. The presses were to go to Leeds and the wheel for scrap. There was some talk of keeping a piece of the field in the technical college. The electrician pointed out that one would have to take the pile too, and as nobody knew where the lower end of it was, the proposal got no further. On the last Friday the drive belt was cut. This time the man who knew the step took off his shoes and sat with his stocking feet against the hatch until it was over.

The power waited. There had been something living on the spar before there was an axle to be made, though not necessarily anything that would know its way about on the spar as it was now. On the ninth night, with the heels quite still, it felt the pile move. The movement came from below. A goods train was standing at the signals half a mile away, and through the wet ground under the railway there came the beat of the pump that kept the brakes off. At the bottom of the pile the beat was quite distinct. It was lost about a foot short of the leaves.

This was where the power did its work. It made a collar at that point in the iron. There was no new metal in it; it was rather as if a man had taken up a little slack in a trouser leg and turned it back on itself. A pulse coming up the pile went round the fold several times before it died away. It took some adjustment. At first the pulse came out sharp enough to knock a scale of rust into one of the pockets. The power took the scale out and slackened the fold until the nap just stirred. It did not arrange for a train to be standing at the signals.

For a while there was very little to show for it. On a wet night one strip would work and on a dry night another. The clear creatures did a good deal of travelling. There was one afternoon when the field was perfectly quiet except for a brown animal under the cotton and a white one immediately beneath it. The spar there is thin. When the white one struck, the brown one received enough of a charge to give a stroke in return. They kept it up between them for eleven minutes after the train had gone.

The canteen is a store for rolls of carpet now. It is too soft a place to hear much through the chairs. The hatch was left accessible on account of the annual reading of the plumb line, which is still required under the terms of the lease. The man who takes the reading has noticed that there are seven leaves at the pile instead of six. The new pocket is empty. It is a narrow one, and occasionally, when a train is held a long time, one of the mites backs out of its own pocket and tries the fit of the new one. So far it has always gone back.

The power has left the collar as it is. It might have done something about the width of the pocket, but the leaf is still growing.
