The power, when it came, sat on the fifth step. It had meant to go lower. But with its weight on the rail the high scraping note let up a little, and in the northern shade something finer than a hair paid out an inch or so and hung there. It took its hand away. The thread drew tight again. After that it watched from the fifth.

It had seen the black pocket and thought of rain. There was a loose stone in the field above which it need only have turned over to bring a runnel of good water down the stairs. Before turning it, it looked to see where the water would go. There was a brown line across the works, hardly higher than the ash door. Below that line the mortar was pitted. In the pits were the linings of old purses, so thin and so nearly the colour of lime that they had escaped notice. Some had dried with their tongues still out. There had been water enough here once. It left the stone as it was.

A power has some advantages as a watcher. It could stay through the ten minutes of good light without wanting them to last longer. It could put its attention, without putting any weight, on the dark side of a pebble. On the other hand, it had some difficulty seeing what happened to a single black grain; it was inclined to see what happened to all the grains at once. It spent the better part of one summer learning to follow one.

By the end of the summer the new purse had a neighbour. The two did not always take in their tongues together. A nick in the grass cord, where an outer fibre had parted, gave the second one a slightly different purchase. Sometimes it got a grain the first one had let fall. Sometimes neither got it. When a swelling came through the thread it went to both.

What the power eventually did concerned the handrail. The lower end had been let into a socket in the wall of the works, with a little lead poured round it. The lead was still sound. At the upper end, just under the lip of the field, the iron went through a square eye. Rain had been getting into the eye for a great many years. One night a flake of rust as big as a laurel leaf came off in the power's hand. Beneath it the iron was bright at one edge. Each time anybody leaned on the rail the edge bent a little and came bright again.

It would have been easy to make the iron whole. The power did not know quite what sound the whole iron had made, or whether, when it was last whole, there had been any grey hem under the terrace. Instead it put a second eye a hand's breadth below the first. It made this one out of the iron of a gate staple that lay loose in the field, the gate and both its posts being gone. Between the new eye and the rail there was clearance enough to pass a folded shift chart. Nothing touched. The rail went on wearing in the old eye.

Years later a woman coming down with a folding rule and a bag of early figs put her foot on the warm step and felt the rail give. It fell the width of the clearance and held. One of the figs went over. She sat down rather hard and, after a little, began to laugh. She had come to measure the chimney, which someone in the village remembered as twice its actual height. She went on down sideways, keeping a hand against the rock.

The jar sent a quantity of clean sand out of the hem. It also shook loose three caps a little before their time. Two came to nothing. The third lodged, mouth upward, in the furrow behind a pale pebble. It stayed there until the fallen fig, shrinking in the heat, tipped off its stalk and rolled against it. For some days there was more yellow bloom within reach of that tongue than it could take in. Then the furrow moved on.

The power did not keep an account of these things to set against the two lost caps. It had put the eye there for the sake of whoever might be on the step when the rail gave. It was glad it had held. When the woman went up again she had a smear of fig on the seat of her skirt, and the right height of the chimney pencilled on the inside of her wrist.

Under the northern leaf there are now purses on both sides of the cord. They are not especially numerous. In a dry year the furthest six hang flat for months. The power still comes sometimes to the fifth step, where there is no longer any need to be careful of the rail on their account: the new eye takes its weight into the field instead of the wall. It sits with its hands in its lap all the same. From there, by looking a little to one side of the place, it can follow a swelling all the way out to the last dry purse. There is a short delay. Then a crease that looked to have set for good comes out of the grey skin.
