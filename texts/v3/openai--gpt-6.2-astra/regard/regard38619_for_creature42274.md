At the foot of the wire, where it enters the mountain, there is a stretch of slack to allow for the movement of the rock. It is a considerable length, though on the drawings it looks like a turn put in by a man who has changed his mind about where to make a hole. Here a person coming down from the summit can stop. There is no station. One simply fails for a time to arrive below.

The power that had taken an interest in the summit waited there. It had first thought of putting a question to the woman at the bath, but she had her ears under water, and it seemed a pity to make her take them out. It could wait the rest of a winter as easily as it could wait for her to finish a length.

At changeover she was the sixth to come down. The seventh had stayed to show the new drain keeper the brush. She felt the question as a slight inclination to stop, very like the feeling one has passing an open door behind which an acquaintance is at work. She could have gone on. Instead she stopped, and there was the power, wanting to know if there was anything she would like done on the summit.

She thought about this. It was a disadvantage not to have the eyebrows. There were several things she might like done, but it made quite a difference who was proposing to do them.

‘Can you mend a stair?’ she asked.

It could.

‘So can we,’ she said. ‘I only wanted to know what size of thing you were thinking of.’

It had not settled on a size.

The seventh person went by. In the wire there is no need to make room, but out of long habit they both did. She had an impression of the man with the brush being rather pleased with how things had gone at the exchange.

‘There is something,’ she said. ‘When the rock ticks, we wait for the reading. Sometimes it’s a long wait. Down here you must get it before we do.’

The power did. The instrument in question was a pair of fine rods, one sunk from the summit and the other from the valley, with their ends close together in a little chamber in the rock. A tick put the rods out of line. The reading went down to the valley office, where somebody had to decide whether or not to send it back up. Small readings were held over until there were enough to fill a line. There were excellent reasons for this arrangement, all to do with the expense of sending a line in the early days of the wire. It cost nothing now.

‘We know it can’t be much,’ she said. ‘If it was much they’d send it. But all the same one stands there with the tea half poured.’

It was an easy matter to give the summit its own reading. The power offered to set it up in the calibration hall.

‘You’d better ask them where,’ she said. ‘I shan’t be there.’

At that she felt a little sorry for herself. She had enjoyed this winter, and below, for all she knew, somebody was going about with a cup held quite ordinarily by its handle, whom she might pass every day for years without knowing him. But this was an old difficulty and she did not bring it up on the strength of having thought of it in company. She thanked the power and went the rest of the way down.

The new shift wanted the reading at the bathhouse. Everybody went past it, the windows could be read from either side, and there was a concession of a narrow strip of glass along the bottom of the middle pane where nothing much was to be seen in any case owing to an ill-judged application of putty. The power put the reading there, in figures the colour of the putty so that they did not obtrude. Under the figures it put a little square which went dark as soon as the rods moved and cleared as soon as they were still. This proved more useful than the figures. From the exchange landing you could see the square.

It did one other thing, at the request of the valley office. It left the old line in working order. The person who had been making up the lines for thirty-one years found that he liked to have a whole winter of them on a card. He could run his finger down it and put his finger on the day the thaw had begun, which had never yet been the day fixed for it on the calendar.

Two winters later the woman came up again. She drew four, a body a little long in the leg for her taste, and took a house at the back. She knew about the square on the bathhouse window but had not seen it work. The first tick came when she was alone in the freight court with both arms round a bale of clean hospital linen. It was quite a tick. She put her chin on the bale and looked over it. Across the street the little square was dark; then the patch of putty was all one colour again. From an open door came the sound of tea going into a cup.

She stood there a moment longer. With the extra length of leg she could see over the bale very well. There was no occasion to put it down.
