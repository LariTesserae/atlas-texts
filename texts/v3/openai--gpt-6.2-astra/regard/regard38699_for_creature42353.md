The power that took an interest in the basin could have put the worn steps back at the southern door. It had its hand under the bottom tread, at Fourteen, before it felt the small feet moving across the backs of its fingers. There were several thousand in the gap. A cane root as thick as a wrist had gone through the hole for the old handrail and forked on the other side. To put the steps back would have taken very little time. The power slipped its hand out the way it had put it in.

For a season it followed the young ones. It learned, among other things, that the warmth behind the brick did not come from the bulb. When the filament at last parted, the little yellow backs went travelling across the floor in the dark by much the same routes as before. The warmth came up an iron stay from the buried foot of the engine. One could follow it with a finger in the plaster. At the top, where the stay turned over, there was a patch no bigger than a saucer where a wet print dried almost as soon as it was made. On the other side of this patch, in the wall, the second shields were growing.

The power left the bulb out. There were things to be seen now that had not been visible under it. In the last of the violet light the fresh shields showed almost white through the bodies of the animals carrying them. For several evenings after leaving the brick a young one going down through the brown water had a light spot in it, like the seed in a grape.

What eventually needed doing was a small job at the southern works. One of the copper staples in the belt had worn through on the underside. Its two ends still rode in their holes, pink and level with all the others. Once an hour, where the belt turned down into the basin, the holes opened a little. Water got in between the plies. There was a dark blister there, at first the size of a fern leaf and then as long as the loose brick. The power watched the seam go round with this blister on it for nine days. On the tenth day the belt came apart.

One end went into the water. The other ran in through the slot in the yellow wall, knocking the bricks out in pairs, and lay down in great wet folds about the engine. For the first time in many years there was no travelling shower among the cane. It took some time for the machinery to run down. The last thing heard was not the flywheel but a little brass cup on the engine bearing, which had always been loose and now rattled three or four times on its thread in a perfectly quiet room.

There was still heat enough in the iron for the brood already in the wall. The next evening the white spots went down through the water as before. The evening after that a number of single-shielded animals came out backwards from behind the brick. They searched the plaster for a while, going up as high as the roof. By morning they were back in the water.

It would have been possible to mend the belt. There were even spare staples in the book cupboard, done up in a twist of paper. The power took one out. On the paper where the copper had lain there was a green mark, and in the mark, quite legible backwards, part of a man's pay for a week. It put the staple back in its twist. Instead it laid one finger on the iron stay. It kept its finger there till the patch behind the brick was as warm as it had been with the engine running, and no warmer.

The next lot that came in found it. So did the next. This was all the power undertook to do, and for a long time it was enough. Outside, the bright red line among the ferns faded. The half mile of sunken belt acquired a cream-coloured skin over its staples and then a fine silver writing. At the station the clay steps still came to the door. With the flywheel stopped and the bulb out it was possible, on putting an ear to the sill, to hear the tread form in the clay: a short inward sigh, repeated twice, and then the ticking of the water draining off.

One evening something came against the other side of the stay. The power felt it first as a coolness in its fingertip. A very old stair louse had taken hold of the iron deep down, where the foot of the engine bedded in the black silt. A little of the warmth went away along its flank. By the time the steps sank there was a warm place out under the cane, scarcely bigger than the one behind the brick. Three of the small ones stopped there. They put their forefeet together, swept outward with the others, and went in.

After that the warm places turned up a little farther from the works each evening. Sometimes there were two; sometimes for a week there were none except the old patch in the wall. The power could feel every one of them through the stay. It did not push the heat along when it seemed to be going slowly. There was a particular thickness of clay through which an old one would not take it at all, although there were young ones on the far side. Those came round by water.

Well out toward the bluffs there are beds of silt warmer than the engine ever was. It was into the edge of one of these that a length of the old animal presently put itself. That evening the iron pressed warm against the finger instead of drawing warmth away. The power lifted its finger a little. The temperature held. It took its hand away altogether.

The stay has rusted through near the bottom since then. There is a space of nearly an inch between the two rough ends. At dusk, when the water goes down at the southern door, the patch in the plaster still dries in the shape of a saucer. Far off under the cane, in places that have never been near a wall, there are others. Turn up the cool lip of clay at one of them and the little backs are packed edge to edge, yellow beside yellow, each with its damp white grain behind it. Put the clay down gently and the feet take hold of it again, beginning at one end and going on a little after the hand is gone.
