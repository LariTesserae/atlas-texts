The first the power knew of the people was the weight of a jar. It had attended for a long time to the two beds of stone, which weighed very considerably more. In a place where the upper bed had sunk a little, and was likely to go on sinking, it found a weight going back and forth beneath the stone. It was greater going than coming. There were a number of fine oscillations in it on the outward journey which did not occur on the way back. By attending to these the power learned the shape of water carried in a jar; by attending to the much slighter oscillations on the way back it learned something of the pleasure of a person going home with both hands free.

The sinking was going on above a mat. In the ordinary course of things there would be a sharp report, and a flake of stone, nearly as broad as the mat and about as thick as a finger at its thickest, would come down edge first. A person was lying there doing up a split in the skin with a bone needle. The power held the flake where it was. This required no appreciable effort. Getting the person out from under it was another matter. After a little consideration the power let a grain go. It struck the back of the hand that was holding the needle. The person put the grain to her lip, as one does to distinguish roof grit from the little curls that come off a worn needle, gathered up the mat with everything on it, and went to finish her work on her sister's floor.

The flake made very little noise coming down flat. She had been expecting it to come down edge first and was rather pleased. There was a long unbroken piece in the middle of it out of which she subsequently made three cistern covers.

For some time after this the power was occupied with a difficulty. Having come to distinguish the weight of one person, it could distinguish a great many. There was scarcely a settlement in which somebody would not have been the better for a grain on the hand. There were also people who got up at the least sound from overhead, took a sleeping child off a mat and sat with it on a food slab until their arms ached. It would have been a poor kindness to teach them to do this oftener. As for holding up all the loose stone in the country, there were wet joints which would never open unless something gave, and an entire sort of little white animal, hitherto overlooked even by the power, which lived in the flour of freshly broken spar.

It went for advice to the woman who had made the covers. Speech troubled it at first. To speak in stone is to be heard a long way off, and the woman had a right to her own conversation. In the end it spoke in one of the covers while she was fitting it. She took her hands off. It stopped in the middle of a word. She put one finger back.

“Was that you with the grit?” she asked.

It said that it was.

“I thought you had let it down uncommonly well.”

It explained its difficulty. She listened with her finger, moving it occasionally to get a clearer sound. Her first idea was that the power might tell people which wet joints were likely to fail. On learning how many things bore upon the course of a drop through a bed of rock, and how far off some of them were, she took this suggestion back. She had once had to divide a cistern between nine households on the strength of an incorrect report as to the depth of it. She did not care to undertake anything of the sort again.

“What I should like,” she said presently, “is to be heard when I am knocking.”

This had reference to an inconvenience of the water roads. At certain places a walker must leave the groove, set a jar through a low opening, and go through after it on hands and knees. A person taken ill in one of these places can beat on stone that runs without interruption to the very elbow cup of a seat, and be quite inaudible to somebody sitting there. The woman had spent the better part of a walk under such an opening once, with her shin caught between two loose pieces. By the time a party came along she had worked herself free. What she remembered with particular dislike was hearing them a good while before any of them could hear her.

The power tried bringing a knock to the cover under her hand. It brought the sound very faithfully, including the small dry sound of skin moving on stone after the blow.

“That will do,” she said. “Only you must give me the way of knocking back. Otherwise I shall have to try every opening.”

It took the two of them some time to get this right. A person going to help likes to put a number of questions, of which the first is generally whether to bring water; there is little to be gained by making the questions carry unless the answers carry too. Everybody concerned preferred to speak. Stone spoken into at one end and listened to at the other gives a quite inaccurate impression of the speaker's age. Apart from this, the arrangement worked well.

There are now places along the roads where the groove deepens to take the whole hand. These are made on application, as it were, by people who use a road, and not always at the points one would expect. A great stretch of blade spar has none. People go over that stretch singly, with somebody waiting at either end, and would consider any further precaution rather a reproach. There is one, on the other hand, within an easy call of a settlement, at a place where water jars are commonly changed from one shoulder to the other. It is used almost entirely for finding out whether there is anything else wanted before one starts back. The power takes as much care with these messages as with the others. It has learned the difference between two words for a small jar which it had supposed for a considerable time to be the same word.

No connection was made at the house of the two echoes. Nobody who used it asked for one. But at the near end of the smooth floor, off to the side where it will not confuse a hand put out in search of another hand, there is a stone in which one can speak to the last roadside seat. A woman who had always gone to the house with her father went there once after his death and sat for a time beside this stone. It had not occurred to her until then that, of the two of them, it had invariably been her father who knew the way. She was a fairly old woman herself. She did not much like having to go home on this account.

She put her hand to the stone and asked whether anybody at the seat had been to the house. There was a wait. The power could have given her the way to the breadth of a feeding fringe. It kept the connection open. Presently a young voice said that he had been there, that he was just finishing a cake, and that if she did not mind waiting he would come.

He walked a little too fast for her on the smooth going. On the way back she discovered that this was because he was uneasy without a groove and had all his life hurried over this particular piece of floor. She showed him how to put the ball of the foot down a little in advance of the weight. They came back very comfortably. At the seat he put his hand to the stone to say that they were both back, an unnecessary courtesy which has become quite usual on that road. The power felt their two weights go off together. After a little while it let the stone go still.
