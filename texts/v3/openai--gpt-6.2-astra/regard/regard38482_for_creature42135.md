The power came to know the place by putting a hand into the hole. There was no snow on the opposite bank that morning. It could see nothing whatever. Under the ball of its middle finger the soft wood went down, and something under the wood, which had been moving, stopped. The power took its hand out. It sat on the bank with that finger turned up in its lap until it had some notion of how little pressure it had used.

Below, the woman who had kept the tray was trying to get a catkin cup out from under the long barrow. She was old enough now to be buying them. The tilting of the market had sent hers rolling. It was a point of pride with the long-barrow man that he could get a wheel over a thing that size without crushing it, but what he had done in fact was to come to rest with it neatly cupped in the hollow end of his axle. They had to get a thorn to fish it out. By that time there were six people giving directions and she had bought another cup.

The power did not know any of this. It knew about the stopping, however. At the next low water it came back, and this time it put one finger on either side of the hole, on the sound wood, and waited. It could feel a considerable traffic. There was a dragging that went on for a while and gave place to a quite surprising lightness and speed; this was the barrow going round the knot. There were runs of water. There was also a small irregular trembling which it could not account for until it took its fingers off the wood and found that it had the trembling still. After that it was rather better at distinguishing what came from the root.

It would have liked to close the hole. A piece of sound willow fitted into the corners could have been made to take in a season. Then the market would have been safe from fingers, at any rate. But on a morning of snow it saw the light go in. The power had an eye no better adapted to that size of opening than its hand was, and by bringing the eye close enough to see, it cut off the light it wanted to see by. It could, however, sit a little to one side. Something pink showed on the underside of the torn wood. From time to time a shadow went across it carrying a number of little points of light, as if the stars had been put on a tray. It gave up the idea of a plug.

What it did in the end was chiefly work on the opposite bank. There was an old cut there where people came down for water. A foot set on the last stone of the cut would rock it, and in trying to steady oneself it was natural to put a hand across the narrow channel, exactly where the willow grew. The power went down into the water and got its shoulder under the stone. With the other hand it worked gravel well back into the hollow underneath. It tried the step afterward, first at one end and then at the other. An elderly man who came down with a pail took it for a person having trouble getting up and gave it his hand. The power took the hand and came up. The stone held beautifully under the two of them.

There was one other thing. In a thaw a length of dead alder came down crosswise and caught in the willow fork. The river was rising behind it. Each time the butt lifted, a short broken branch at the other end went into the hole a little way and came out with pink on it. This the power did not stand on the bank to consider. It waded in, lifted the butt clear and went downstream with the alder, for it was heavier than it looked and had got well under one arm before it could be turned. At the bend both went out of sight. The alder fetched up on a spit of gravel. The power came back by the high path with one sleeve torn out.

In the market the damage amounted to a trough across two streets and the loss of a post that had stood for years in everybody's way. The woman was among those who went to feel the stump. There was a leaf on it, folded so tight that at first she thought it was a splinter and tried to pick it off. They put a little fence around it. This took up considerably more room than the post.

By then she did not often go as far as the arch. On a good day she could get there on somebody's arm, but it was a long way back. The man who did the grooves came to see her instead. He brought news of the join, which was wide enough now in one place for the swimmers to pass one another. They did not always wish to. Sometimes one backed nearly the length of the street with the other following it nose to nose. He showed her on the back of her hand how it felt. She corrected him on a small point: the second stroke on coming about was the stronger one. He tried it again. Yes, she said, that was the thing.

The power still comes sometimes to the cut for water. It has learnt to fill a pail without dipping it, by setting the lip just under the surface and letting the water run in over one side. There is much less disturbance that way. On snowy mornings it goes a little farther down to do this. From the last stone of the cut to the hole under the willow there is nothing in the way of the light.
