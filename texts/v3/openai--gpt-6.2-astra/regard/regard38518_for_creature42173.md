The power that took an interest in the wash court first noticed the boy by his elbows. Even with the cloth down over his eyes he held them very high. It took some strength to do this. At the other end his mother could make a tent of the blue stuff just by standing up straight.

The power had seen the toad come out. In fact it had seen rather too well. It could see the toad before it came out, sitting with its chin on a chip of tile, and could see through the thin skin below its chin. It had to attend very closely to the boy to understand the interest of the moment when there was no toad and then there was.

One evening it came down the steps with them to see if there was anything it might do. It had thought of letting a little light in under the cloth. But the trouble was not with the light. The boy could not get his head down to see and keep his arms up to hold the selvedge, and he would sooner have missed six toads than let the cheese woman's property trail in the wash.

His mother gave the pole rope a shake. Down came the blue cloth in its usual unmanageable abundance, and down also came a little white feather, which the pigeon had lost in the course of the afternoon. This was the one thing the power put a hand to. It tipped the feather over the selvedge. Instead of settling on the blue cloth to be folded up with it, the feather sailed under and stuck to the wet nose of the toad.

The boy saw none of this. The little girl, whose eyes were at about the height of his knuckles, saw a white thing going along by itself some way above the tiles. She took the boy by the back of the knee. He very nearly sat down on her.

“Under,” she said.

He looked at his two occupied hands. Then he put them together and gave her the bunch of cloth with the oil spot in it. “Both hands,” he said, though she had already used both, and got down on his stomach. He had expected to have to look into the dark under the footrest. Instead he was looking straight into a pair of eyes the colour of the flat brass weights at the cheese stall. A white feather went up and down between them. The toad put up a foot, very deliberately, like the woman at the trough putting her hair back behind her ear. The feather came off. There was a wide place in the toad's mouth which turned out not to be its mouth at all but the beginning of a fold in its side. It went on past his ear with a dry skirting noise. He had imagined it would be wet.

At the other end of the cloth his mother waited. The hump in the middle which was her son subsided, and he came out with a crosshatching on one cheek from the little floor tiles. The girl had kept the spot up. Quite a lot of the rest of the short side was lying on her head, but none of it was on the ground.

“Was that it?” his mother asked.

He nodded. He was a little put out to find that she knew what he had been waiting for.

“Did you ever see it?”

“Once in my dinner basket. I thought somebody had given me a pear.”

She took a step forward for the fold. The girl opened her hands very wide, believing her part to be over, and the boy caught the cloth. Then he found the corner for her again. There was a trick to the next bit, when you had to let go with one hand but not the other.

The power went up to the street. It was pleased about the feather, though it knew better than to suppose that all the rest had been its doing. On the ridge of the roof a man was shifting a basket from his sore hip to his sound one. It sat beside him for the length of his rest, with the warm wind up its sleeves.
