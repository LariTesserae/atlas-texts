What troubled the power was the weight of a hand on the turf. There was no hand there now. But the shelf was known to people. Someone had carried the horn up from below, and someone, for the sake of a cup of water, had spent a good part of a day on their knees with a pecking tool. A person coming up tired would sit on the square without giving it a thought. There was nowhere else that looked as comfortable.

Near the entrance to the shelf, where the overhang was too shallow for nesting, there was a block with a level top. It was still attached to the wall by a narrow ankle of stone. In the ordinary course of things the ankle would have lasted another winter, possibly two. The power took the weight of the block, parted it from the wall and set it down at the upper end of the old shelter. It was just high enough for a person sitting there to get the load off their shoulders without having to undo the chest strap of a pack. There was room in front for a pair of long legs. The sun had been on the top of it all day.

There was a little white spider on the back of the block. For some time after the move it stood with two legs feeling for a wall that was no longer there. The power stayed to see what it would do. Presently it paid out a thread and went down the back of the block to the floor.

Four days later a man came off the ridge with a coil of blue pipe on his pack. He meant to stop only long enough to get a drink. But there was the seat, and when he had got his shoulders eased he stayed the better part of an hour. One of the young stoats came as far as the horn peg. What it saw was the black end of a bootlace, flicking a little as the man worked his toes inside his boot. It took two rapid steps toward the lace and then, on grounds quite sufficient to itself, two rather longer ones back.

The man neither saw it nor knew what was under the turf. He drank the water in the hollow, wiped a little grit off the seat of his trousers and went on over the ridge. That evening it was a good deal colder. The mother brought in a vole by the better door, having first to get past a heap of her children, who had lately taken to sleeping in the entrance. In hauling the vole over them she bent the long swift feather almost double. This time, when it sprang straight, the quill went into the willow lining and held. For the rest of the time they lived there it lay along the roof, quite out of the way.
