I would be sitting in the doorway of one of the knuckle houses, a long way inside what had once been the doorway. The strip overhead would have grown out for twenty paces beyond its pegs. People coming up from the flats would see my feet first. I would do repairs of a sort, chiefly on things that had had a long time to grow into other things.

There is the matter of a mitten, for instance. A woman hangs it up to dry, palm outward as usual, and goes after water with the other one tucked in her belt. When next anybody comes to the house the peg has put a little branch out through the thumb. It is quite a good mitten. To cut the branch is to have a wet end dripping on the bedding, possibly for the rest of the summer. To cut the mitten is a shame. What you do is turn up the hem at the wrist and look for the end of the thread. It is pleasant work in a doorway. As the seam comes out you find bits of the original color, particularly down in the fork of the thumb. At last you have a flat piece like a little hide on your knee, and the branch, when you let it go, stands up a good inch higher in the air. I would have a reputation for patience on the strength of this. In fact I would be extremely curious to see the color.

I think I would have come from farther down. There would be some evidence of this which forty years of lifting the ice properly had failed to remove. Down there, perhaps, the houses have doors after all. I would be forever getting up to let somebody in who had been sitting quite comfortably beside my feet for some time.

One summer a boy would bring me a difficulty. He would come empty-handed, sit down, go away, and come back with his little sister. The difficulty would turn out to be in a house some distance off, and not to be a difficulty of theirs in particular. In the wall of the house there was a comb which the wall had nearly finished eating. They had been picking at the tissue with a seed husk. I was to come before they broke it.

It would be a considerable walk. We would take our bedding. The girl, who had made the journey twice already that day, would spend most of it well ahead of us, walking backward to see why it took so long. Toward evening the boy and I would come upon her sitting with her back to us and know from that alone that there was something ahead worth seeing. A tongue of new floor was coming out. It was still rolled tight enough at the lip that you could see straight through the roll to a little circle of white at the other end. While we sat there a drop collected inside it and ran the length of the tube. We heard it come out. After a bit we got up and went round.

The comb would be just where they said. Six teeth and the back showing, and a very old cut in the wall above it. I would work a loop of thread around the foremost tooth and draw it gently to and fro to clear the stuff away. The children would kneel one on either side of me. Presently I would give the thread to the boy and ask him to pass it behind the next tooth. There would be room for it to go in, but no finding the end on the other side.

“It grows that way,” the girl would say.

I would try the first loop again. There is a feeling in the fingers when a thing is coming free, a little willingness on its part which is sometimes only the beginning of a break. I would put my cheek against the wall to see along it. The teeth would all be leaning very slightly up toward the old cut. Then I would see what the girl had seen at once, being shorter. Somebody had cut a little fringe down from the wall itself. The six narrow strips had dried and curled away, and the wall in growing had brought their tips nearly home again. It was a comb, certainly. It had been made there to comb something. But there had never been another side to it.

We would sleep in that house. In the morning I would wake with the girl standing over me in her underthings. She would be drawing the woolly inside of her coat up over the teeth. A quantity of pale fluff would come away and stay there. She would pick it off in one piece, turn her coat right side out, and get back into it. Then she would do her brother’s. I don’t know if that was what the comb was for. It would work beautifully.

For years afterward people would be bringing their coats to my doorway. There is a way an account of a thing goes about the flats, arriving on a shelf a little before the thing itself or a great while after. In this case what would arrive was the news that I had something for taking the hard rolls out of wool. I would explain about the house. Sometimes it would be in sight. More often I would sit with the coat across my knees and tease the rolls out with my fingers while the owner told me where it rubbed.

I would like that part of it, particularly when the owner stayed on in the doorway to put the coat back on and go through the movement that had hurt. A reach across the body, say, to get a scoop out of a belt. First the cautious reach, with the shoulder held a little high in expectation; then the ordinary one, the scoop coming up level in the hand as if it were full.
