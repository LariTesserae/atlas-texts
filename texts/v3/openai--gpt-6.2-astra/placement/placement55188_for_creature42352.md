From under the square the stair is quite easy to find. One goes along a level ceiling until one's forehead comes up against an edge. An inch lower there is room to go on. After this has happened eight times there is a good deep pocket where a body can lie on its side. I have been there long enough to wear the last edge round.

At low water I fit rather badly. My back contracts with the stone, but there is a fold near my neck which always closes late, and which for a while contains a pinch of my cheek. I have to draw it out with a hand. This is one of the few occasions when I have felt my face. It is cooler than the rest of me. There is a shallow groove from the corner of my mouth almost to where an ear might be useful. A little salt collects there, quite pleasant when the water first comes in.

I used to live under a landing in the bath quarter. There was company enough there. If I put a hand out at the underside of the landing, it came to rest on a back. The back shifted a little to make room. I had no idea how much of my weight that neighbor had been taking until the landing went off during a rise and I was left in a stretch of soft ground with all my soles hanging down. For several tides I moved by reaching ahead of myself for that back. The first hard thing I found was the edge of the stair.

The knocks come here with very little sound. Each one presses my belly against the ceiling and lets it down again. In the long interval after the last, I can feel the separate weights of things on the paving. A roll of skin going over it weighs almost nothing at its front edge and quite a bit at the back, where it has gathered some grit. The little one on the step weighs less than the grit. I had felt it through several clearings of the water before I understood that part of its weight lay on the step above. There was the body in one place and, a tread higher, the light pressure of five knuckles.

There is nothing very difficult about getting a hand through there. The difficulty is knowing which way it will come out. The pores in the square run slant for a considerable distance. My first two fingers came up in open water. I felt the shed skin brush across them, the slight drag of the dirtier edge, and then it was too late for that tide. On the next I came out under the little one's wrist and startled it so badly that for the whole of the ebb it sat with both hands tucked away. I was sorry for that. It is a long time to sit on a stair.

Lately I have found a pore that opens a little to one side. I get a hand through early, before the first knock, and lay it palm down on the tread. The stone is warm, the water unexpectedly cool in the deep places between my fingers. When the water clears the small hand comes out. It does not take hold of mine. It goes feeling over the nail of my third finger, a broad uneven nail with a ridge across it from my time beneath the landing. Along this ridge it passes one fingertip, then another. Sometimes it goes as far as the knuckle. When it has finished it lays its hand palm up in the old position. I leave mine where it is.

Something has begun to happen to the step there. On drawing my hand in I feel a slight resistance at the sides, though there was none to begin with. The tread has taken the breadth of my finger and a little of its depth. It will be a long narrow hollow, quite unlike the places in the houses. Already at the lowest ebb, when there is hardly a damp patch left in the square, it holds water. From below, with my sore cheek against the stone, I can feel a small finger stirring it.
