I think I would live over the tram’s lost-property room. To get to my kitchen I would have to go through the room even on Sundays, turning sideways between the ends of umbrellas and the baskets hung by their handles from the stair rail. I would know which step to miss with a full kettle. The things downstairs would be in my charge; the staircase would be mine by gradual encroachment.

A passenger who has left something on the tram generally comes in prepared to give a description. I have a slate on the counter for this purpose. But it is very hard to describe a pair of black gloves when the person on the other side of the counter has just laid out nine black gloves, among which there may not even be a pair. People put their hands into them. There is a little turn of the wrist by which a woman gets her thumb past a darn; she makes it before her thumb has got that far. I take the gloves off the books.

For articles found in the water between the rails I have a drawer lined with pieces of old blanket. Things lifted out of warm water go on giving it up for a surprisingly long time. A comb may be put away quite dry at closing time and in the morning there will be a row of dark dots on the blanket at one end, where the teeth are set close for getting nits. The girl who keeps the limpets likes this drawer. She comes in on her way home and lifts out the things one by one to see what they have printed underneath. She is scrupulous about putting them back in their own wet.

There is a difficulty with lunch tins. The works issued several thousand of them in the year the lower gallery opened, and a considerable number are still in use. The owners have distinguished them by punching their initials into the lids. This has proved a great convenience to me as regards the lids. It does nothing for the bottom parts, which in the washing trough at the works are liable to go home with almost anybody. For years the girl’s mother has carried a tin whose lid shuts well enough at the hinge end but has to be held down at the other with a loop of green elastic. The tin makes a very good counterweight on the rail. She has learnt to give it exactly the swing it requires, whether it contains her dinner or not.

One afternoon the girl comes in with the tin under her arm. Her mother has left it open on the sill and the elastic has perished in the sun. She wants to try the lid on my collection of bottoms. We sit on the stairs to do this. It is a warm day and the smell that comes off the old metal under our hands is quite distinctly of boiled leeks.

The fourth one fits. The catch goes over so nicely that we open it and shut it several times. Yet she is reluctant to make the exchange. She puts a hand into the old bottom and feels along the inside, where the metal has worn smooth at the corners. Then she takes both bottoms home, one inside the other, with my leave.

Next morning I find the fourth one back on the counter. In it is a folded twist of paper containing a short length of unused green elastic. I puzzle over this for some time. Toward evening her mother comes off the tram carrying the old tin, neatly secured, and I understand that somebody in the household has bought a length of elastic and cut it in two. They suppose I may have occasion for the other piece. Downstairs I have four hundred unclaimed articles and nowhere in particular to put a thing that has been given to me. I take it up to the kitchen.

In winter the grandfather sometimes sits with me there. My kitchen window is one of his. If a pane has been bedded properly, he tells me, you ought to be able to rest your forehead on it without the teeth in your lower jaw meeting the teeth in your upper. We try this, standing side by side at the window, each in our own half. Below us the last tram is coming along the shelf. At every joint in the rail its light dips and recovers, illuminating first the sleepers and then a good long way ahead. We stay where we are after it has passed. The glass is cool, and it is an agreeable position from which to watch the street.

Sooner or later someone looking up will see our two foreheads and take it for a sign that the room below is still open. Then I will go downstairs. I do not mind doing this. There is a moment I am fond of, particularly late in the day, when a person sees on a shelf a thing he had already begun to think how to do without. Before he points, before he fishes in his pocket for the slate pencil, his hand assumes, down by his side, the shape it takes in carrying the thing away.
