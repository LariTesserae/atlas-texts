For several years I would have been the person you came to if you had lost the end of your shelf.

When a household expected to move east, even if it was not to be for another two summers, someone would take a length of tape and mark on it the things that fitted the old house and would have to fit the new. The breadth of the meal chest, certainly. The distance from the floor to the under edge of a shelf, which might be determined by the height of a particular blue jug with a lid. People were quite capable of keeping these tapes themselves. What they were not capable of was remembering, on unrolling one in a field the following summer, whether a certain red stitch meant the end of the shelf or the place where the chimney should begin. It was on account of this difficulty that I came to have a trade.

I had the room under the carrier’s stair, the bit with a window, beyond the turn. When the brother from the lime yard ducked his head on the way up I saw, from where I sat, his chin disappear into his collar and emerge again against the light. For years I thought he was looking in at me. I used to nod.

I made the tapes of linen, boiled and dried before marking, and wrote on them in a small square hand. You could wash out the writing if you were determined to, but not by putting the tape in your mouth, which a man working alone with both hands on a board was very likely to do. I charged for a morning in the old rooms and an hour at the new cut. In between, the tape stayed with me, wound on a flat slip of wood. My window looked as if it belonged to a shop selling very little of anything in a great many shades of brown.

The young wife with the six dishes came to me on a wet afternoon. She had measured the dishes herself by standing one on edge against a wall and making a pencil stroke across the top. There were six strokes within the breadth of my little finger.

“Which was the biggest?” I asked.

“I washed them after,” she said, rather vexed. “They’ve got mixed up.”

It was no matter; we took the highest mark. But when I asked whether she wanted them standing or lying down, it appeared she had not thought of either. At her mother’s they were wrapped in the skirt of an old dress in the chest. She had been thinking of them all these years as one thick, rather soft object with a hook and eye at one end.

We got them out. They were cream with a running green leaf, and on one dish the man who had painted the leaf had gone back to the well for more color halfway round, so that the stem, after dwindling nearly to nothing, took off again in splendid health. That was the dish she set up first. Her mother was asleep in the good chair with the child asleep on her, and we could not try things on the dresser without waking them. We laid the measuring tape on the floor for a shelf and stood the dishes against the meal chest, three and three, then five with one in front, then all six along the wall. It made a considerable difference to the room. A man would think twice about coming in with mud on his boots if he saw six dishes like that.

“I don’t believe I want to see them all the time,” she said.

We put five back in the skirt and gave the sixth a mark to itself. Then, since the tape was out, she took off her shoes and stood on it with one arm crooked as if she held the baby, and reached as far as she comfortably could with the other. I put in a stitch there. It meant that whatever shelf we arrived at, she was not to have to put the child down to take a dish off it.

When we came to stretch her tape at the eastern end, the kitchen had been dug and the roof was on, but the floor still wanted its last dressing. Her husband had made a niche beside the door on his own account. It was an excellent piece of work, square back to the corners, and in exactly the wrong place for everything we had marked. He had even put a little slope on the bottom so it could be wiped out toward you. We stood and looked at it. You could see where he had rested his thumb in the damp beside it to judge the smoothness.

“What did you make that for?” she asked him.

He said he had been at work one morning when the sun came through the door and lit up just that piece of wall. It had seemed a pity to go on wheeling it away.

We had the green dish with us, in a sack of the parcel woman’s mending. It went into the niche with an inch to spare. The young wife tried taking it out with her left hand, then her right. The husband, who had not understood what she was doing with her other arm, put the baby into it. She tried again.

That was in the first years, when I was still sorry to see a good length of marking go unused. I rolled up the tape with its measures for the shelf, which they never built, and took it back to the stair. Years later the child came for it. He had been sent for something to tie back a door that kept swinging to while they carried his grandmother in on her chair. He was a large solemn fellow by then and waited while I went through the slips, though any piece of string would have served. I found the right one by the six little strokes at its end. He held it against himself to see what length it was and the stitch for the reach of his mother’s arm came just under his chin.

For a long time afterward they had that tape for a door tie. Coming in from the bright end of town, before your eyes were good for anything in the room, you could put out your hand and know whose house it was by the writing, gone stiff and a little raised on the linen, and the small hard place where I had broken my thread and had to knot it.
