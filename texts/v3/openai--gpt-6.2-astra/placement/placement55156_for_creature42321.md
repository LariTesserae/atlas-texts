I think I would find myself at the stoker’s hatch, with one cheek against the iron lip. There is a little round patch there that the fig bark does not cover. I would have discovered, probably while trying to see how far the root went back, that through this patch one can hear the high note quite separately from the low one. I would go on listening until the iron had printed a red half-moon under my eye. For a time I would take myself for the next keeper of the chart.

I would have difficulties with the dates. The wavering is audible in the iron a good while before it is audible in the air. And at the far side of the gravel something happens even earlier. A purse will let down a fraction of its tongue and take it up again. You can spend an afternoon there with your cheek going cold and the sound as steady as ever, and come away fairly certain that somebody ought already to have put a mark on the wall.

I should like to be able to put my finger on the spot on the chart where this happens. But as soon as I imagine the finger, what I feel is the pressure of it. A blunt end comes down on me through a gap in the mortar. Water goes out from under it in both directions. Twelve yards off, in the shade, a row of little mouths wets itself without eating anything.

That is nearer. I would be in the course of brick just below the shift chart, in the length of thread that has grown too thick to lie wholly in the joint. A bricklayer running his thumb along the wall would take me for a bad piece of pointing. I have no grains there, no tongues, none of the pretty business that can be seen by laying a slate on the ground. The brick on one side is warm. The one on the other side has a crack that admits a needle of cold on wet mornings. My whole considerable knowledge of the works consists of this and the *orr*.

Even that is not quite how it would be. I would know the underside of a leaf a long way off by the weight that comes into me at dawn. There is a nick somewhere in the thread beyond the dipper which lets a little of the weight away. On dry days I can feel exactly how much thread there is between myself and that nick. Beyond it things are less distinct. Sometimes there is no beyond it until the following morning.

My time to be awake, if there is such a time, is in the week when the stone goes out. At first it feels rather as a black grain feels going round the inside of a purse, except that the stone does not come round again. There is a pull, a little water goes to meet it, and all along the terrace the tongues lie out flatter on the rock. Then comes another pull. In the wall I grow appreciably narrower. The cold from the cracked brick gets a little further in. I would have no sight of the grey cap travelling, nor any notion of a further bank. I would have this long, increasingly expensive acquaintance with one pebble.

When the fine thread parts I expect there is very little to feel. Water that would have gone out stays where it is. A purse near the dipper gets some of it and spends the afternoon over a grain that otherwise it might have dropped. By evening I fit the mortar joint again.

Still, I should be reluctant to say that I had stayed behind. Under the northern shade I would be having a very different afternoon. There the failure of the thread is not a small saving of water. It is the end of water, as far as I know water. I am close about a stone, with the damp that was shut in with it. Something rough is laid against my side. After a long time, by putting a little of myself out, I find that the roughness continues. I can keep one surface covered while the other goes forward along it. That would be my discovery of the grass.

I would not be the whole grey hem, then, with news coming in from its outposts. There is no news from this one. In the brickwork I shall go on receiving the mornings in their old order, the swelling, the pause at the nick, the slow emptying toward noon. On the grass cord it will take me some days to get as far as the leaf. The first drop there will be so much more than I have room for that most of it will go straight past me and darken the stone below.

That is where I would like to be left: at the wet end of the cord, putting down a mouth with what the water lets me keep. Late in the day somebody at the foot of the stairs might see its shadow and stay to watch the two grains go in. They would have to stand quite still. I would feel the settling of their heel in the gravel, and take the second grain a little sooner than I meant to.
