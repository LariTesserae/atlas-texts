From the white chair I would look like a fault in the gutter. There would be a little V in the water, its point uphill, and no leaf or bit of straw to account for it. On a day when the water failed to reach the chair the V would still be there for a while, travelling very slowly uphill in the last of the wet.

I would be a snail about the size of the brass screw heads in the kitchen. My shell would be flat enough to let me go under the door, though I would seldom go so far. There is a stretch of gutter a few yards from the house where the person who cut it had to change hands with the chisel. The marks on the bottom, which have all been slanting one way, begin to slant the other. Between the two sets is a little uncut island, too low to show above the water. A dark skin grows on its downstream face. I could spend the best part of a night eating one end of this skin while the other end went on growing.

For a long time I would know the woman chiefly by the foot she puts down first when she gets out of the chair. The heel comes down on one side of the gutter and the ball of the foot on the other. For a moment there is a low pink roof, with a fold across it deep enough to be dark inside. Then the weight comes on. Water squeezes past on either side and lies in the hollows left by the little toes. A fish will get into one of these and be obliged to wait until she comes back, which is sometimes a considerable time. I can go from one to another over the high places. It is one of the few advantages I have over a fish.

In the dry part of the year the woman sets her bent wire in the gutter and draws it towards her. She does this by feel where the glare is bad. There is a small click when she comes to the uncut island. There would be two clicks if I were on it. The first time this happened she would stop and go back with a finger. My shell would come off the stone quite easily at the front but at the back there would be a considerable reluctance. She would wait. When at last she had me on the pad of her finger she would feel something going over the whorls of the print as if a very small soft thumb were trying them for sharpness.

She would put me down above the place she had cleared. By the following morning I would be back. It is not the chisel marks I follow but the taste, which grows stronger for several inches before the stone itself begins to have any colour. After that she would lift the wire a little at the click. A beard of green would grow there, long enough in a wet summer for the fish to get underneath. From the chair she could see it lying all one way, except for a snail’s width of it near the root that lay the other.

Once, perhaps, I would go into the house. It would be after a long run of the column, with water coming out under the whole width of the door, and no edge anywhere to tell a snail where the gutter stopped. I would go up the side of a table leg as far as the first dry inch and shut myself in. In the morning the woman, reaching down for a dropped spoon, would take me for one of the brass screws and try to turn me with her nail.

There would be a round mark on the wood afterwards. It would be lighter than the wood, with a dot of the old colour in the middle where my mouth had not quite reached. She might notice it from time to time when she washed the floor. I would be outside again by then, at the change in the chisel marks. The water there is hardly deep enough to cover my shell. When it falls a little, the very top of the shell dries in the sun and turns white. At that height a fish passing between me and the house is enough to wet it again.
