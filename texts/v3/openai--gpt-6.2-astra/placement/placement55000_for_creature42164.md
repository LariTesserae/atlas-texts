I would be under the right-hand end of the sleeper. For a long time I would have known the older animal chiefly by what came off it. There was a good place to put my chin, just where the underside of the seat began to turn up at the front. Powder caught there. When a pleat opened overhead, a little roll of black living stuff would come down through the powder and stop against my mouth. Lately I have had to lie rather far back to get my chin in that place. My hind feet stick out into the cold.

The first hollow I get to use is the one nearest the wall. The older animal has moved over a little in the night. I have always supposed, from feeling the shape of its foot against mine, that the bottom of a hollow must be soft. It is harder than the street. My fringe slips on it until it comes up against a rough place at the side. There I can feel quite well. There is something going on a long way off, a steady pull with a catch in it, as of a grain too large to pass going round and round in a constriction. I stay awake with that foot after the others have gone slack.

When the older one goes, I go a little way with it. We set out for a wall which has fed both of us before. My track on it is at the height of the dark line it leaves behind; I can eat along the line without once having to bring my chin down to bare stone. This time, though, the wall comes up with a slight tilt on it. The older one gets its forefeet on while the bottom edge is still coming in. By the time there is room for my feet it is feeding well above me. I work along the bottom. There is plenty. After a while a thin blade of cold passes between my chin and the wall, and I am on my own side of the street with a little black paste still working in the corners of my mouth.

That night the six hollows are full of flour. I clear the one by the wall quite easily. It is much more troublesome to clear the others without putting the loose stuff back in the first. By the time I have done it I am standing a little crooked, with two feet well down and the rest braced on the rough sides. From this position I can feel the pull with the catch in it through nearly the whole length of my body. It goes on after the streets have parted. Whatever is happening is happening in the seat.

It takes several warm afternoons to find the place. At the back, where the seat goes into the blind wall, is a row of pits such as a coarse grain leaves when it is rolled between two stones. The last of them has no bottom that my fringe can reach. As the sun goes down, flour comes out of it. Very little comes, and most of it goes in again, but it has the taste of a room I used to feed in when I was small enough to go through a seam sideways. I put my chin there. For a time there is only the familiar rubbing. Then something inside puts its chin to mine. We work for a while on the same bit of flour. Its stroke is very short. I have to keep still for two or three strokes to feel it at all.

I come to use the seat the other way round from the animal that went away. It stood with its head to the street, where any useful movement of the paving reached its forefeet first. I get the movement a little late. Occasionally I miss a wall that would have fed me. But the fit this way is rather better, and the dust I bring home, instead of shaking down over the front edge, works back into the row of pits. There are several short strokes to be felt there now. On a cold morning, when nothing much is moving anywhere on the ridge, they come through very distinctly.

In time there will be a small difference in the hollows, not easy to see unless the light comes along the seat. The pair by the wall will have a shallow fan worn in the polish at the bottom of each, from the sideways play of my fringes. I am making those marks now. One foot is busy with a grain that will not go down into the room. It is yellow, a long way from its court, and a little too broad whichever way I turn it. Something takes hold of it from below. I let the weight off my foot. After a while it goes through.
