I would live in a door that had never been hung. Its wagon would have been shunted into the bank near the foot of the fallen chimney to let a wider load go by. The bank, having made a place for the wagon, had gone on making a place for it. There was a fair-sized room there now, with the wagon in the middle and a little daylight laid on, though no provision for getting the wagon out.

For a long time I would have thought I was the door. I had a good sense of my four edges. In the cold from the west they drew in a little and I could feel my frame all round me, the sill first, then the two jambs, then the lintel settling down on me like the flat of a cool hand. When a parcel hand sang in the room I answered out of my whole breadth. There was a knot low down on the left that gave a slightly woolly sound, quite easy to distinguish from the rest if the hand held its note.

It was a mouse-sized injury to the backing felt that would tell me otherwise. A fold had got pinched in unloading, and every year a little more of the fold came away. One afternoon a piece dropped onto the wagon floor while I was sounding. I felt it go down. For the space of a finger between the felt and the floor I had no edges at all, and then I had a fine sense of the length of the wagon, of one petal rather heavier than its fellow, of the six places underneath where it had grown fast to the bed. Behind me the door was still giving out the end of its note. I could hear the knot.

After that I tried a number of things. A coupling is a poor place for me, all one pitch, with nowhere for the rest of a sound to go. In a leaf there is room enough but one is forever being turned out into the sky. The best quarters I found were in the little space between a thing and the case made to carry it. Even a very close fit leaves something over. I could lie along the back of a switchboard in its wrapping and feel, through the hard buds, rain striking the leaves three galleries up. I liked knowing it was raining while still being warm from the hands, or whatever they were, that had packed the board.

I got out of the room in the last bead of the old parcel hand. It had come up over the sill and stood singing there, tipping a little further forward each time it took breath. Its ribbon lay slack on the wagon floor. All the solid weights gave the note straight back, but the hollow one took a little while about it. I went where the sound was slow to come out.

It is a curious lodging. The view through the amber is excellent in one direction only and that direction keeps changing. I have seen the underside of a moss flower large enough to shelter a platform, followed at once by a mile of railway small enough to close up in a bud. When the moth is in there with me I see very little. I hear the separate ridges of its wings go over the amber as it turns to get comfortable. It has a way of putting one foot out to stop the bead rolling while it finishes its turn.

The old hand still goes to the well. Down there I discovered something I should never have known in the door. When the fields draw near and the ribbons lift towards one another, the hand below has a hollow bead too. There is a knock from it. The two knocks come quite distinctly, like a person arriving at a house and another person, much further inside the house than one had supposed it went, putting down a cup. I can take up the first knock and keep it until the second comes. For a little while I tried giving them back together. The old hand stopped going round the rim when I did this. It stood with its two free ends feeling at different heights, until the fields were miles apart again. I have not done it since.

I think I would be a sort of hearing that had grown up among the packing materials. There might be a great many of us. When a load of doors goes by, what sounds from the bank like one note has, from inside the bead, its separate places of origin and its small disagreements, and once, well after the load had passed, something in the empty siding had a try at the knot low down on the left. I nearly went after it. But the hand had got one end up over a sleeper and was working at getting the rest of itself over, and I wanted to feel the little run forward the bead would make when it succeeded.

That is where I would be most evenings. The moth comes in dusty from the silver flowers. The bead tips under its weight and a little of the dust spills out at each turn. From the bank it would look as if someone with a very fine point were marking off the sixty yards beside the chimney. Down where we are there is a comfortable walk between one mark and the next.
