I think I would live at the crossing, on the bank that has no shed. I would be the one people sent for when the cord had to be washed. It is a two-person job, since you must have the new cord laid before you can take the dirty one home. There is a surprising weight of green in a cord by the end of a summer. The wash water, tipped into a white pan, is the colour of a bottle held up to the light.

I would have another use on misty mornings. Someone who has not crossed before will often stop as soon as the bank goes out of sight behind him. He can feel the line continuing into the mist, but for all he knows he has taken hold of a loose end. I sit at the last post with a turn of the cord laid across my thigh. I feel him stop. If I lift the turn an inch or two, he feels the cord come up in his hand, much as if a cow on the far side of a hedge had raised her head. Generally that is enough to bring him in. Sometimes, when he comes out of the mist, he is still holding his hand quite high.

For a long time I believe I know everybody by their hold. The woman who tends the posts scarcely touches the line except to put it back in a fork. Her daughter used to drag on it so hard that I could tell which side of her mouth she had her tongue in, or thought I could. Then one morning there is a person on the cord I can't account for. Two short draws come all the way to my leg. There is a considerable wait. Then two more. Whoever it is has covered scarcely any ground at all. I put my breakfast in the grass and go out along the line to meet them.

It is the daughter, quite grown by now. She has a basket hanging from her arm and a baby asleep against her front. The cord passes through her hand without a check. It is the hand below hers that has hold of it, with four fingers so short that the tips go white at the effort. As the girl walks, the cord slips out of them; in its sleep the child reaches on a little and takes hold again.

We stand there a while. Mist runs in drops down the hairs on the child's wrist. The girl has something she wants to say, and of course it is the wrong hour. At last she takes my hand off the cord and lays it under the basket. It is very heavy. I carry it the rest of the way.

What I like best about my place is the few yards just past the post. There the cord stops and people go on. The baby wakes; the girl stoops for the lid of my breakfast tin, which has rolled down the bank; we eat what is in it, with our backs against the same post. By noon the mist is gone and I can see, a mile off, a man coming with a pipe wrapped in a length of old shirt. There is no need yet to fetch a knife or clear a place on the sill. At this distance, on this ground, he will be a long time coming.
