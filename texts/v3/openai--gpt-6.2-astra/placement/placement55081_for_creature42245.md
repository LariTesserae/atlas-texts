For a while I would be on my back in the bottom of the water, with nothing to do but make a little cold place against a lip. There is such a place in a good many of the jars the boy makes that winter. One of the two curls is deeper than the other. A mouthful of water stays in it after the rest has gone, and if you wait with the jar tipped up you get the mouthful, quite cool, against your front teeth.

It would suit me to be that curl, except that I should like to see the jar from the other side as well. This can be managed. There is a woman who goes round among the kilns collecting the pots that sing badly. She puts a finger through a handle, hangs the pot clear of her skirts and taps it with the bone end of her knife. A crack too fine for water to get through makes a noise you can hear across a room. She buys these pots very cheaply. I think I would be her. I would come down the ladder with an empty net and go up it looking like a cluster of windfalls, the jars knocking at my knees in their separate bits of net, with grass between to keep them from finishing one another off.

Most of them are wanted above for keeping things dry. A man who has lost half his barley to the little red beetle doesn't much mind whether his barley jar will hold water. But a few I keep. There is a shelf of them on a knob too small for a kiln, with room below it for me to sleep on my side or, if I draw my knees up, on my back. I have a leak overhead. It comes through a patch of blue about the width of my finger and it is very fresh. There must be some hollow up on the plain which keeps the rain a long time. I have never found it.

At first I set my best jar under the leak. I came home from a round of the kilns and found a lizard drowned in it. A thumb stair is a good thing if the water is low enough for the creature to get a purchase on the bottom. This lizard had swum past the stair a hundred times, for all I know, with its front feet going in the clear water an inch from the wall. I took it out and gave it to a crab who carried it as far as I could see.

Now I lean a broken platter under the drip. The water runs down the platter into the jar by a twist of wool. Anything can get ashore there. Also the drip makes a pleasant noise on the platter, particularly just before it falls, when the last drop is still travelling down the tilt and the whole platter is just so much the heavier on one side. It tips against the jar. There is a tick, and then a splat, and after a while another tick. I find I can tell in my sleep when it stops.

One night there is a tick without a splat. Then there are three or four ticks close together. I put my hand out of bed and lay it on the platter. It goes quietly on tipping under my hand. At the lower edge, where the wool lies in the water, two thin corners are busy coming up the wool, each going round it the opposite way. I get a light. It is a sole no bigger than the bowl of a spoon. Behind it, in the jar, there are little oars going round the wick's reflection.

It takes me most of the rest of the night to understand that they have come from above. Below is where I have always seen them. Above is the place I go with my net full of pots. There are cart tracks there which have kept the print of the same donkey shoe since I was a girl. You can sit in one at noon and cook an egg in the hollow of the heel. Yet here, out of that, comes a wet little thing with a right and a left to it, taking great pains over a piece of wool.

In the morning I carry it, platter and all, to the boy. He turns the platter this way and that to catch the light. There is a chip in the edge and the animal has fitted itself into the chip so neatly that what we seem to be looking at is an entire platter with a small transparent piece.

“Did you see it fall?” he asks.

I didn't. We go back to my knob and look. The blue overhead is dry. On the underside of the shelf where I keep my jars there is a damp trail with a bend in it, just such a bend as would be made by a drop running sideways along a crack before it fell. We climb up. In the foot of the boy's own leaking jar, high on the back of the shelf, two curls of clay are holding a little water from the last run. One curl is full of oars.

So I have not found water on the plain. I am rather sorry. The boy isn't. He fetches a ladder and spends the morning going round the backs of my jars, and presently I can hear him calling to his mother that she must come, there is a whole spring's worth of them in a pot no one would give a shrimp cake for.

That is where I would live, on the narrow knob, within reach of all the places a thing can have come from. I would still go up sometimes and look for the hollow. Down here I would want the cracked pots, and the wool, and the boy handy to ask whether I actually saw what I am already halfway through telling him happened. And I would keep one sound jar for going up the ladder. There is a long walk from the top to the first shade. I like that last mouthful as well as anybody.
