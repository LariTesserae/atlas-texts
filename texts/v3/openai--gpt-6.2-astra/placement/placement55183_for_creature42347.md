I would be on the underside of the lip, farther up than the shrimp has ever been. From the pool I would look like a flaw in the pour of the slag, a round place with a dull center. To see me properly you would need to be there on one of the afternoons when the water has drawn back almost under the shelf. The round place would still be wet. There would be a clean little track leading to it across the dusty glass, no longer than the white part of a fingernail.

I think I would be a snail. Not one with a shell you could put a pin through the windings of. Mine would be a low cap, with its peak a little off center, and the animal under it nearly all sole. I could go over the turn of the lip without any part of me losing its hold. In fact I would have gone over it many times before I discovered the upper face of the step. I would come to the dry, turn while there was still water enough to turn in, and go in underneath again. My track seen whole would look like the mark left by somebody trying to strike a match.

There is food up there already. The green below is only the part a person can see. On the underside, where the water never quite lets go, there is a skin on the stone so slight that the only proof of it is the difference between where I have been and where I haven't. I take it off in little overlapping bites. Behind my head, under the cap, something goes to the trouble of making those teeth; by the time they come forward into use there are more coming on behind. All afternoon a portion of the main under the road will have been shaken at one heavy beat and two light ones, and a portion of the stone will have been cleaned about equal to the place a comma takes up in small print.

Toward evening I would get farther round. There is a difficulty at the very edge, nothing to the shrimp, which would have no business here, but quite a difficulty for me: a bubble opened by the break in the bank, with a sharp rim all round it. My head goes down into it easily enough. The cap catches, and the back end of my foot is still on the other side of the rim. For some time there is a snail going down into the bubble and a snail staying out of it, with no appreciable result. Then a little more of the foot comes up under the shell. The shell tilts. I am in, and have the whole brown lining of the bubble ahead of me, unvisited, with the light coming through the water that has bridged its mouth.

I wouldn't know the shrimp by sight. I might know one place where it had been. On a cool evening the two tracks could come together, mine coming down the face, its own going up through the green in short, rather untidy advances. It would have pulled down a tuft and let it spring back with a little pink mud caught in the fork of it. I would get hold of the tuft at the base. For a long while the mud would stand clear of the stone on its green stalks. Then, without anything that looked like enough work having been done to account for it, the tuft would lie over and the mud would slip away. The shrimp by then would be back in its square.

What I should like about being there is a thing I would have no means of noticing. Up on the road the hollow is new. The two ends of iron still look as if a man with a jack might bring them level again. Down where I am, the underside of the lip is an old feeding place, the turn round the edge is fairly recent, and that bubble dates from tonight. By morning there will be a dry gray crescent inside it where my shell has kept the silt off. A person looking down the bank may take it for one of the marks the slag acquired in cooling. I will be a little way in from it, out of the sun, doing the other half.
