I would have a bench on the landing, at the turn just past the melon. People would bring me things that were quite sound outdoors.

The first would be a pram. Its owner would bring it up folded, with the baby under her other arm, and show me how nicely all four wheels spun. Nevertheless, on the way in from the street, the baby always woke. It took a second journey with a shallow plate of milk on the mattress to see what happened. At the place where the landing began to take up its proper length, one wheel was already indoors while the other three were still outside. The jolt was no more than you get running over a pencil. It was enough for the baby, who had very little to do just then except notice things of that size.

I put a leather washer on the axle. After that it was understood I did repairs. In fact I had been given the bench because somebody had to sit there when the glass was new and write down the time at which the sun reached the turn. There was a ruled book for this. On the first page there are six entries to the minute. On the next somebody has drawn round a hinge.

I would get to know Lena by her stool. She wore the front feet down carrying it. She held it before her by the hole in the seat, and as her arm tired it went lower by degrees until the feet began to bump along the ridges. I offered to shorten the back two to match. She said she had better ask the tide first. This was her word for the narrow run of water that stood along the landward foot of the wall. If the stool tipped forward she could see straight down into it, between her shoes, without taking her hands off the coping. So we left the stool alone.

What I should like about the bench is that from it you could come to know one piece of the city very well and still be wrong about where it was. For years I would hear, towards the end of the afternoon, a brushing followed by a small, quite distinct sneeze. There was a ventilator above my head. I supposed the sound came down it from one of the flats. I pictured an old person powdering after a bath. Then one day I went out to mend a split paddle for Bette, and as we sat together on the clay she took off her left shoe, brushed out the shell grit with two fingers and sneezed. My bench was on the other side of her elbow. We had come out by different doors and walked a good way to meet there.

After that, if it was nice out, she used to knock and I would come and eat my bread with her. It was never convenient. I had to put away the small tools on account of the children and walk as far as the school entrance; Bette would have finished her bread by the time I got there. She kept the paper open on her knee for my stalks and stones.

One wet summer the melon sling gave way in the night. The melon did not fall far. It lodged in the turn of the rail and grew there another fortnight, taking on a groove that divided it into two very unequal portions. When the children cut it they brought me the little end on a saucer, with four black seeds loose in the groove. I rinsed the saucer and put the seeds in the old sunlight book to dry. Next morning Lena found them flattened there beside the drawing of the hinge.

She wanted to know what else was in the book. We went through it from the beginning. There were the heights of three children who no longer lived in the block; a recipe for getting green off brass, with *doesn't* written under it; the address of a woman in the upper town who could put an eye back in a doll so that it shut at the same time as the other one. Near the end we came upon a page of short upright strokes in blue pencil. Seventeen, a space, then three more.

“Yours,” I said.

She counted them with the side of her finger. By then she knew about the crabs. She had known for some time, it appeared. You could tell without turning one over if you waited for it to climb the sloping end of the soap dish. There was a flash just before it slid back.

I gave her the pencil. I thought she might want to put a line through the tally, or write down what it really counted. Instead she drew a little shelf beneath the strokes and legs at either end. It was the sea wall, she said, with everybody looking over. The gap was where she would go when she got there with her stool.

I think by then that would be my place: not precisely at the turn, which had shifted somewhat as the glass settled, but at the bench with the book that could be drawn in. I would have a drawer full of leather washers and another that stuck in wet weather and had nothing in it worth the trouble. On a Thursday Bette would leave the pudding bowl with me while she played. It fitted over the small tools very well. At the end of the evening, well before I saw either of them coming along the landing, I would hear the front feet of the stool begin to drag. I would turn the bowl up the right way and slide it to the near end of the bench, where a hand going by could take it without anybody having to stop.
