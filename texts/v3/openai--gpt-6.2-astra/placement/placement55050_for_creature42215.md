The first place I should try is under the flake, where the blue wings are. From there the arrival of the mouse would be a considerable event. The wings would begin to go up and down before there was any mouse to be seen. Then there would be toes, with dust in the creases of them, and the little noise of something brittle being turned about and about until there was a right place to bite. One could be quite near her there without ever seeing the nick in her ear.

But I think I should want a hole of my own, and there is one a little above the flake. It is the socket left by a grain that has weathered out of the roof. There is room in it for the body of a small spider, though to get all eight legs in with me I have to put four on top of four. Ordinarily I leave two out. One rests on the stone and one on a thread that goes down to the high end of the flake.

A thread in that position is of very little use for catching flies. The loose feathers come along the back wall wheeling slowly on their quills, and a single breast feather will take up all the stickiness a spider can afford in a night. My catching place is higher up, across a pocket in the rock where the draught turns. A fly that has come in with the cloud and got past all those beaks will sometimes spend the last of its strength trying to go farther into the mountain. It walks up the wall quite briskly until it comes to the overhanging part. There it opens its wings. I get a few.

The long thread is the way I came up from the floor. I have been down and up it often enough for it to be several strands thick at the top and quite slack towards the bottom. When a bird steps on the flake I get one sharp pull that brings my foot out of the socket. The mouse eating is a much smaller business, a succession of little pulls with clean stops between. Once, after the mouse had gone, the eating went on. I came down half the length of the thread to find out about this. A fly's head, with a good deal of fly still attached, was caught on the lowest strand, and a flat beetle was trying to walk off with it. I went back up. By morning the head was gone and I had a loose end to attend to.

On the afternoon the little mouse comes out, the flake is still. Its mother is eating on the floor beyond it. I am well down the thread, putting in a new fastening, when the tail feathers open. For that instant my shadow falls on the stone. It is wonderfully large and has a crook in each leg, even in the one I have bent up under me with a dab of silk on the end. Then the light goes. I finish the fastening in the dark.

The little mouse has seen the thread. At any rate, when its mother has gone into the split, it stays outside with its nose turned up, working from side to side in the small space where the thread was bright. This takes it farther from the split than it has yet been. A whisker catches. Up in the socket my foot comes out so far that the next foot comes out to support it, and down below the little mouse backs the whole way home with a length of perfectly good silk across its face.

I do not go down again that afternoon. There is a fly in the upper pocket; there are things to be done with a fly even after it is quite dead. Towards morning I let myself down to the flake on a fresh line. The old one, where it broke, has drawn up into a minute white curl. I gather it in with my front feet and eat it as I go. At the bottom I make my fastening a little farther round the end of the stone, out of the direct way of the split. It is still a good place. Before I have got all my legs back in the hole, the blue wings stir, and I feel the mouse sit down to breakfast.
