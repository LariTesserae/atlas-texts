I think I would be the one on the wire. There is room for one more lid underneath it, where the copper comes up out of the grit. Mine is so new that when it shuts you can still see the dark fan folded inside, the way you can see a fly through the skin of a yellow plum.

I spent a little time loose in the basin. At that age I had one long hair with a kink in it. A stroke of the hair would send me quite a good distance in a direction I could do very little about. I went through an arch twice, once in the company of a grain of pollen and once alone. For the better part of an afternoon I was in the print of a crab's foot. The water there was warming up and getting salt. When the crab came back the other way it put a foot down beside the print, and the two little waters joined and ran out through a nick in the sand. That was how I came to the copper. I tried it with the soft spot on my belly. By morning it would have taken a knife to get me off. The hair went on beating for another day or so, scrubbing a bright place about the length of a dash.

What I have instead of the hair is a fan of sixteen threads. I can draw any one of them through my mouth without disturbing the others. On the washer there is no leisure for this sort of picking and choosing. Here I can spend half the morning on a single thread that something delicious has gone to grease on. A crab going over makes quite a difference to breakfast. Its weight bows the wire and sends my whole fan down into the meal. I shut. The crab goes on. When I open again there are sixteen red furrows with a little clean copper showing at the bottom of each.

For a long while no swimmer comes. Then one day after the blow there is a pull on the edge of my lid. Something takes what is sticking out and gives my hinge an experimental nip. It stays in the pool until nearly the next blow. I feel it turning over and over in the tight space, trying to go on through the hole that ought to be there.

It comes back. By the time its back is too broad for the water it has worn a comfortable hollow under me. Even then it will come over the bank lying flat on its side, with the upper eye out in the air. It gets a mouthful or two. To get home it has to lie still until the tread strikes and the lip of the bank goes soft. There follows a day when it has sense enough to stay in the well. In its place come two so small that they can lie end to end in the hollow and neither touch the wire.

I get heavier. This does not amount to much in the way of growth as measured from the beach. From inside, it is considerable. There is a part of my lid that was once the edge and that now lies well in over the soft of me; I can remember being pinched there. My hinge goes white. In a dry week the water retreats until the top of the lid sticks out. A midge rests a forefoot on it and changes its mind.

What I had not reckoned on, in choosing the wire, was that a thing need not turn to come round to another side. In the autumn a load of grit shifts behind the bolt. The wire eases up out of its bed at one end and settles at the other. My hollow is a good way downhill from me now. I spend the interval between blows shut up in the air. Inside I have a drop carried over from the last wetting; when I tip my fan it goes from one end of me to the other. Outside there is a scratching which persists through several strokes. A small crab has found the bright dash left by my hair and is working at the green on either side of it.

At the blow I open. It is quite a business, at first, to do that very thing at the sound at which every creature of my kind is shutting. But the water is there then, running the length of the wire in a clear sleeve. I put out all sixteen threads. For just as long as the stair rings I am in the main wash of the tower, tasting things that have never lain on the bottom of a pool. Today there is soot and the yellow dust of a flower long since done flowering down in the town. The water thins. I get my fan in with a little to spare.

If you have your thumb on the latch you may feel a faint tick after the knocking has quite finished. That is how far the sound goes now that I am big. It goes along the wire, through a packing of damp grit and three pieces of shell, into the middle bolt and out into the brass of the stair. Down in the hollow the two swimmers turn at it. Up here the crab lifts its foot.
