I would come up with the cans, I think, with a needle stuck through my lapel and a roll of felt under my arm. Somebody below has complained of a ringing on the cable. The station woman has sent word twice that she cannot mend it with the cans in motion and that when they are not in motion she is at the wrong end. So here I am. I have brought a coat.

There is nothing wrong with the felt between the cans. The woman lets me discover this for myself. It takes most of the morning, because an empty can swings from the yoke quite differently from a full one, and until the cart comes back there is no way to hang them as they hang on the downward trip. We eat with the yoke across our knees. She has a pear with a brown place that amounts to nearly half the pear. She eats the brown place first, with a spoon, and is left with something like a little drinking vessel, which she can eat at leisure while she works.

It is the handles. Where the wire turns in the tin ears at either side of a can, there is play enough to put a fingernail in. As the full can goes over the lip it takes up a new angle and one end of the wire slips home before the other. At the station this makes no more noise than the woman’s spoon makes in her pocket. Down the taut cable it goes a long way. A small leather washer does for it. I have leather in the heels of my boots, coming loose already from the heat, and by evening we have sent down a quiet pair. I shall have to stay until the morning to know whether it was the right noise.

I like having come all this way with the wrong material. Below, where I do most of my work, people bring me things already taken apart. A child comes in with a hinge wrapped in paper. Which way up was the door? He puts his hand out at the height of the latch, which is a help, but the door itself is three streets away and his father has already planed the sticking edge off it. Up here the can is still on the wire, the wire still goes over the rim, and tonight someone a mountain’s length away will sleep or not sleep according to what we have done. There is a bunk behind the dock office. I put the felt on it and use the coat for a blanket.

Toward morning I hear a scratching level with my ear. The little animal has found the cut end of the roll. It goes along lifting the edge with its nose, trying for a way in, though if it went two inches farther it could walk into the middle without stooping. I lie there wanting quite badly to turn the roll for it. After a while its nose comes up against my thumb, which is holding down the very edge it wants to lift. I take my hand away.

In the morning there is a slip of paper in one of the cans. *That was it*, it says, and underneath, in a different hand, *send back the other boot too*. They know me below. The woman finds me a pair of cart shoes with rope soles, and I go out with her to fetch the milk. In the first gully I walk bent over, watching the wheel in front of me and missing every place where I ought to put my foot. On the way home I do better. There are little dull patches on the shiny stone, no broader than the ball of a foot, where the rope will hold.

I think I would do that journey a number of times. I would still live below; my door sticks in wet weather and there is a child who knows where to find it. But I would be the one they sent up when there was a noise, or a drag on the return, or a can arriving with a bright place rubbed into its side. I would grow to know quite a lot about the mountain without knowing the names of the humps or ever getting to see inside a pump house.

The woman would keep the roll of felt. Next time I came up I would find it on the shelf above the bunk, tied loosely with string to keep it from unrolling. On that shelf there would be all the usual things a person puts away against a future need: a spare wheel pin, a new cake of soap, a tin of washers of precisely the right size. The felt alone would stand a little proud of the shelf edge. You could put a finger into the hollow end of it, though by then I would know not to.
