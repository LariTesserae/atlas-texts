I should have to put a finger into the upper socket. There is something in the account of that last foot, the one that tries the hole and goes on. I should want to know what it felt.

Cold iron for the length of the first joint, then a ledge of silt, much too soft to bear any weight. On withdrawing my finger I should find a little square cap of it on the nail, entire except for two cuts close together at one corner. The foot has been there before me. I could spend a good many tides at the gate doing things of this sort. But I think that is only where I should come to look for myself. I should be farther down, in one of the slots that you can't see the end of from the track.

About a yard short of the chain there is a saddle with a piece of bottle glass caught in it. The glass is brown in the thick part and the colour of weak tea at the lip. Underneath, it has a channel worn in it like the mark of a gouge. I should be one of the animals there, with two feet in the channel and the other four on stone. On a rising tide I should get my first water a little earlier than my neighbours. It would come stealing along the glass and wet the hairs on one side only. For quite a while it would be worth working half the paddles.

I like to think how little of the hollow I should have to carry about with me. The pump house, for instance. On land it is a building you might get into if somebody had kept the key. Down here it would be a red dimness which fell across my back late in the day, and which I could leave by walking about the length of myself. The chain would be a much larger affair. I should know the near curve of a single link very well indeed, the agreeable roughness of the iron, the difficult place under the curve where a foot could get in farther than it could readily come out. Of the fact that this was repeated at intervals all the way down the passage I might have no notion at all.

There would be things I knew which you couldn't find out with a finger. Towards the end of the flood the glass gives a little in its lodging. It doesn't shift visibly. There is a moment when the two feet on it cease to be pulled away from the four feet on the shale. Then they begin to be pushed very gently towards them. Time to come out from under the lip. I should back out into the open and there would be some water left running through the hairs, a last mouthful to wipe away before letting go.

I wonder about that letting go. From the track it looks as though a single impulse went through the company. From my saddle I should see the underside of a neighbour go past, all six split nails quite clean, the pink skin gathered into pleats where the legs had folded. For an instant I should still have hold of the glass. Then the red dimness would be below me as well as above and there would be no drag on any foot. If another rim met mine, I think I should keep the paddles on that side folded and let the other animal do a little of the work. We should travel in a very slight curve. Neither of us could make that particular curve alone.

It needn't be the glass I came down on. A few inches would be enough to miss it by. I might spend the ebb at the blind end of the next slot, with a wall of shale as high as a house between me and the channel I knew. There would be food enough. What would keep me walking, I think, would be the feel of the feet all at one level. I should keep putting the front pair up on things, a flake of shell, a twist of fibre, trying for that small difference of purchase to which my back had grown accustomed.

By dark I might have found something quite good: the lip of a black stone standing a little proud of its bed. I should get under it backwards. It would be cool along the groove in my back and there would be just room to move a paddle now and then, to change the water. A grain might want taking out in the morning. I don't think I should yet be the colour of the underside of that stone. That must take a while.
