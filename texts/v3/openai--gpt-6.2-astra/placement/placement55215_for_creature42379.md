My desk would be in the survey office. I would be the one who answered the girl about the depth of the hollow. She would have asked a rather better question than the ones we generally got. She wanted to know the distance from the *top* of the paving slab. Our figure was from the underside. I had to go over to the yard and find out which batch of slabs they had used. There was a difference of four inches, quite a lot if you are buying cord with your own money.

I think I would have come up from below. For a long time I would have gone about the office with the quite mistaken idea that it was our business to keep the plans right. There were days when I went home discouraged. We would spend a morning settling the position of a standpipe, only to have the woman who sold buns beside it put her head round the door and say that if it was the one with the cracked handle we meant, the handle had been changed. It took me some time to understand that she had come in because we were the people who would like to know. Afterwards, when she came, I put the kettle on. She preferred the marked-up sheets to the fair copies and could generally find somebody she knew from the alterations to a drain.

The letter about the hollow would have been my first reply to come back to me in the form of an object. Some months later the girl brought the net in to show me. She had made the mesh small at the bottom and very large at the sides, so that it looked less like anything for catching a cup than a string bag that had carried something much too heavy. She had also made a little cuff for the wrist at the free end. I tried it on. She stood across the room with the net, looking at the places where the cord touched the desks.

“Will it turn a corner?” she asked.

It had not occurred to either of us until then that the people at the edge of the seam would leave very little room for the person with the cord. We conducted a trial round the end of the long plan chest. The net turned the corner nicely, though it brought two pencils with it. She took it home well pleased. For a few weeks afterwards there was a small handsome plaited cuff to be seen around her father’s wrist on the early tram. He had discovered that if he took it down before he was quite awake he arrived at work with most of the contents of his kitchen.

What I should like best, I believe, would be going out to settle things that could not be settled at the desk. There was a request from an old woman for the name of the lane she used to see from her kitchen. Her sister below was making a little painting from memory, with the name to go on the back. It was important to get it right because the painting was a present for the woman herself and she had undertaken to be surprised. There were three possible lanes. I brought the relevant sheets and we sat at the kitchen table holding them up one after another to the light. In the end it was neither the plans nor the view that settled it. She remembered a boy sent across for hot rolls coming back with the twist of paper between his teeth because it took both hands to climb the rail. Only one of the lanes had ever had a rail.

She gave me a hot roll to take away, which suggests that at some point in these proceedings she had sent somebody out for them without my noticing. On the way downstairs I found myself holding it in my teeth, although the stairs were perfectly good.

I would go to the square when they tried the cup. In the office there would be a space on the sheet for the width of the seam, and I would take my rule along in my inside pocket. But I would not want to be the person to put the cup in, or to stand up afterwards and announce a figure. I should like to be somewhere in the second row, behind somebody a little shorter, with one arm held away from my coat and the girl paying cord out over it in long loose turns. It is a job that takes no particular knowledge. What it takes is being asked beforehand, so that you know to come, and which side to stand on, and to leave that arm free.
