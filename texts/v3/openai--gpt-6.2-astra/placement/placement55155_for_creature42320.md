There is a mark on the underside of the mouse’s whisker, a little way down the bow from the red mite. From the hinge it looks like a place where somebody has tried the whisker with a penknife. There is a slant of white there, quite unlike the long white shine along the top of the hair. Through the bead it is too far off to be seen at all. Its likeness is to be had, however, in one of the four water gloves. The innermost glove on the swimmer’s left foot holds a good upside-down view of it. The swimmer’s foot shows pinkly through the middle of the view, like a finger laid across a photograph.

What looks like the cut of the knife is in fact a pair of jaws. That is where I should be, with my jaws on the whisker. I should have a transparent body, rather flat, and a back thrown into three pleats, each with a little floury bloom along its fold. In the glove you can count the pleats. My six feet are gathered up close under me. I have the look of a person being carried across a puddle and taking considerable care of his shoes.

I should have supposed I was biting the mouse. My mouth is certainly hard at work. There are fine teeth in it, set at an angle like the teeth in a rack for raising a window, and two brown muscles behind them, as big between them as all the rest of my head. But the whisker passes quite free between the teeth. What they have hold of is a husk. It is my own, and has split down the back to let me out. The old feet are still in their first position, all six affectionately embracing the hair. Five of my new feet are clear. The sixth is in its stocking up to the heel.

One could make a very fair little beast out of the parts of me that are empty. Better, in one respect, than the one that is full: the old feelers stand out handsomely on either side, whereas the new ones are wet and lie together along my cheek. A grain of chalk lodged in the old eye gives it a knowing expression. I can see this eye very well with my own. It is about as far off as the eye of another passenger at the little window of a carriage when both are trying to see out.

It is not the great gulp of the cup that you hear at my place. That belongs an appreciable distance down, where the water fits the chalk. Along the whisker there is a dry singing sound. It runs through the old feet, and you might expect it to run all through the husk; but the empty abdomen, with its two edges laid a little over one another, has a homely sound of its own, like a paper bag of screws. I have both sounds in my teeth. In the one foot that is still shod I have only the singing. I think I should know that foot from the other five even in the dark.

There is a view from between my wet feelers which is not to be had from the hinge or from the bead. The two feelers part a little at the tips. In the fork is the underside of the red mite, all joints and buckles, and past it, seemingly held up by one of its crooked knees, the slab of spar. A brown spot in the slab is a seed that was in the way when the stone was made. The seed shows to advantage from here. It has a nick in one end and is tucked up in a little white bolster of its own. From the field above there must be acres of sky to be seen, but I doubt whether there is anywhere in the field from which you can see that seed.

I like the fit of the wet new skin at my elbows and the surprising room in the pleats of my back. I like the old foot too. There is a sort of seam at the heel, nicely rounded to take the weight, which is entirely my own doing. No other foot could have made it just so. Through the water glove the two heels, the occupied and the unoccupied part of the one heel, make a bright crescent. The swimmer has this on its finger. I have the swimmer, much reduced, in a bead of damp between my jaws. Its eye is brown even at that distance. The square of daylight in it is the last thing I can make out distinctly; then comes the edge of my old lip, with a fine white nap on it that I had no notion was there.
