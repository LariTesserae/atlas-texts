I would live in the flooded shed, halfway up the east window. The sill is already occupied. When the hem there puts out its fans they come within a finger’s width of me, and the lowest part of my house is kept rather cleaner than the rest.

My house is a tube of grit on the inside of the glass. I have laid it along the curve of the iron rim, as an animal living on a stone will lay its tube along the sheltered side of a shell. There is no particular merit in the curve. If the window had been square I should have come to a difficulty at the corner and done something else. At the growing end two soft feelers come out and go over the ground ahead. A grain of about the right size is picked up, turned once or twice, and bedded in the wet lip of the tube. An unsuitable grain is let fall. Quite a number of the same unsuitable grains come back on the return stroke and have to be gone over again.

The glass makes it possible to see a thing that would otherwise have been a matter of some dispute. I do not put a floor in the tube. From inside the room there is very little to be seen but a grey cord with a freshly broken appearance at one end. From outside, looking in, you can see my whole yellow sole travelling to and fro under the arch. For traction I have rows of minute knobs which go white where I press them to the pane. When I back down the tube the whitening travels the other way. A man once cleaned the outside of the window with the ball of his thumb to get a better look. For some minutes afterwards there was an agreeable warmth underfoot.

The shed is a tolerable place to live because the door has come off and lies a little way inside, propped on the pump. Water entering the doorway has to go round it. On the inward stroke a little of the water goes up the face of the window, bringing flour with it; on the outward stroke a good deal comes down. I feed going up and build coming down. The hem on the sill keeps different hours. Between us there is a place no bigger than a sixpence where the two streams turn past one another. Things dropped there sometimes stay for a surprisingly long time. For the better part of one morning there was a single pink stomach, with its owner quite invisible, going slowly end over end between my feelers and the fans.

I should have been found on account of the hem. Someone wanting a picture of its nursery from underneath took one through the glass, and in the top left corner of the plate there were several lines of white dots. On the next plate they were farther up. For a year or two after that a boat stopped at the shed in summer. The divers measured the grey cord with a pair of dividers and put a little grease-pencil mark on the outside of the pane opposite the growing end. Their marks record a very fair rate of advance for so small an animal. What they do not record is what became of the other end.

It came loose. Iron swells as it rusts, and one winter a scale lifted from the rim and took the first few inches of the tube with it. I was up at the mouth and suffered no injury. The broken piece went down behind the oil can. There is another piece there now, and one lying across the toe of the door. From time to time I have to go back and stop up the breach with a little wall. I do this from the inside, setting the last grains by touch against the end of my body. The result, viewed through the pane, looks very like a heel.

By now I have gone nearly round the light. There is still a clear space in the middle through which a ray can be seen, first grey at the far window and then, if it rounds the shed, close and white at mine. Nothing of this is visible to me. What I get of the ray is a brief stoppage of the upward current, followed by an excellent delivery of grit when its wing clears the door.

One day I shall come on a little wall of my own making. It will be old by then, with the cement washed out between the grains. My feelers will take it down quite readily. There is no reason to suppose I shall know it for anything but an unusually convenient supply of pieces all of about the right size. From the boat, on a fine day, it will look as though the east window has been fitted with a narrow grey gasket. It will be necessary to go down and put a cheek almost against the cold pane to see, under the gasket, the small white marks of someone still at work.
