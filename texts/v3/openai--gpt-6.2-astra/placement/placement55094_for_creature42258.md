I would be in the groove that goes to the missing tree. Not at the head of it, where the bead stands, but a little way down, where a lip of stone divides that groove from another. On a dry afternoon the lip casts a shadow about the width of an eyelash. With my foot in the bottom of the cut I would know it as a wall. I could lay the whole soft side of myself against it and still have room overhead.

The water would reach me along that wall. First the part of my foot pressed against it would come unstuck, very pleasantly, like a fold opening in sleep. I would go up without lifting my shell. There would be a little roughness in the stone that kept passing from the front of my foot to the back. I think that is how I would have distance: not the sight of the green getting nearer, but a succession of things I had finished touching.

At the top I would go on putting out my foot long after there was anywhere to go. It would take the shape of the bead. The last of it to leave the stone would be a spot directly under the peak of my shell; with that spot loose I would swing down, and the shelf and all its pools would be above me, holding me by the sole.

I would spend a night like that. There would be no particular sense of a tree being gone. To have that, I would have to have come in some other year and got my foot into the plush of a root. There would just be the failure of the small gathering motion to gather anything. After a while I would stop making it. My shell would hang quite still. One advantage of that empty place is that nothing arriving there could pass over me. If another came we would hang a little way apart, each in a dent of its own making, until the skin between the dents drew flat.

I would come down hungry. I would also come down rather easily. In the morning the difficult bit would be getting off at the pool. The thread would deliver my front edge into deep water while my back edge was still fast to the groove, and for a while I would be bent in a way no one looking at my little round back could possibly guess. Then the back would let go. I would settle among the sand with one fan already out.

There would be other evenings, and on most of them I would go to a living root. I would like the meal best just where a root turned under the moss. There it would be damp enough to lift in a strip, and I could draw my foot back with a yellow hem on it all at once. In time the growth on either side of the missed night would nearly meet. Nearly, but not quite. The blunt place would come to be on my left side, well back from the rim, as high above the working parts of me as that stone wall had once been.

I would have no use for it until I was broad enough to live under the lip. Then one day a smaller animal, making its way down over the backs, would come to rest with the edge of its shell in mine. Usually the small ones would slip a little at each filling of the fans. This one would stay. For several afternoons I would feel its weight in exactly the same place. Water falling from its fan would run across the blunt place into my open one; when it drew in to eat, mine would straighten a little for want of the water. We could feed like that without either of us having to move.

That is where I find myself most distinctly: under the ledge, too far around the curve to see from above, with something no heavier than a wet seed seated comfortably in the night I got no supper.
