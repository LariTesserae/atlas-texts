I would be at the other end of the straightedge, with my thumb on it to stop it rocking. I would be the one who holds the lamp. This is not quite the same thing as being the one who sees. If the man on his knees says he has lost the light, it is generally because I have begun to look at something myself and my hand has gone with my eyes.

The lamp is a good one except for the switch. It has a rubber cover with a split in it and you have to find the little hard place underneath. I do this on the stairs, while there is still light from the high window to do it by. My coat goes on the second hook. I have tried putting it farther along, to keep the sleeve of his out of my pocket, but coming up hot from the room I reach for it where it ought to be, and several times I have got as far as the upper door before remembering I came to work in a coat.

On basket days there is very little for me to do with the lamp. I hang it by its loop on the rail. The basket has two handles which lie flat against the sides and must be picked out with a fingernail. We lift together, and for a moment, before the water starts to come out of the bottom, we have the weight of the whole square of water between us. Then his end gets light a little before mine. He has a bad wrist and it suits him to stand uphill.

I was not the one who saw the toad. I saw a place the color of the back of my hand, with a shine in it. By the time he had said what it was we were committed to setting the basket down on the ridge. Coming back we had to agree on what he meant by left. We have it right now. There are four rust marks in the glaze where the feet used to stand. From my end I can see two of them while we lower away. It is easier to miss those two marks than to remember a toad.

For a while I thought the gray stuff that flew out from under the edge was an indication that the basket needed doing oftener. It troubled me that there should be dry dust in a place we had just lifted so much water from. One day a grain came to rest in the crease at the base of my thumb. I had my hand open on the rail. It walked along the crease until it came to a place where the crease petered out; there it stood feeling the skin ahead of it with two things finer than the hairs on my wrist. I could feel the extractors through the heel of my hand but not the feet of the thing walking on it. When I turned my hand toward the lamp the grain was gone.

The ruled book lives upstairs in a drawer with the spare rubber washers. There is a column for the date, one for the clearance, and quite a wide one headed *Remarks*. That is where the first man wrote *inches*. He put it there once, at the top, and drew two little strokes under it every year after. When we changed to millimeters I began a fresh line, not a fresh page. For the first three years our figures gave the impression that something quite serious had happened to the floor. I put *mm* beside each one after that, in case anyone read across with his finger and stopped short of the remarks.

This year, with the lamp down and the book open on my knee, I find a flake of blue glaze between the pages. It has been pressed into the paper just below last year's entry and has left a little hollow of its own shape. I tip the book to let it fall. Its shadow goes a surprising distance across the tile. Out beyond it, at the end of the chute, something makes the small double movement of a person trying to get a tight collar comfortable. First the chin goes up and then the eyes go down into the head.

My mate has his pencil ready to point to the line he wants; his hands are not clean enough to put a finger on the page. I keep the book tipped a moment longer. There is the brown chin again, and below it a pale throat which goes on moving after the rest has stopped. When I pick the lamp up the chute throws its ordinary black shadow and I cannot find the place.

Upstairs he asks me if I have put down the figure. I have. He takes his coat, and the sleeve comes out of my pocket bringing with it one of the little flat washers I keep for the lamp. We hear it run down several stairs before it lies flat. He goes down for it, still with one arm in his coat, and I hold the light over the rail.
