I should be in the house with the extravagant ears. Nobody sleeps there now. The upper hollow has slipped a little downhill of the lower, and when the leaf comes down the two ears make a sort of crooked passage in the dark. I should like it there. There is a back way out, where the point of one ear cuts across an old grub hole. Also, people would know where to find me.

I think at first I would be one of the people who go looking. There is something particularly fine about that right foot on the brass, with the little tally on it, and everybody making room for it all day. But if I picture the person coming back to draw it, meal up to the elbows and a bit of somebody else's quilt stuck to one foot, I am already there when they arrive. I have been sitting in the collar with my coat folded under me, keeping a place clean. I want to know about a knock they felt in the second room. Was it on the rock side or the mica side? They have to put my elbow down and do it for me.

What I keep, in the ears, are pieces of houses. They have to be quite small. A chip off the jamb is enough, provided it comes from the mountain and not the leaf. I keep them in order going in at the left ear and coming out at the right. The ones in the middle are such a nuisance to get at that nobody who has ever borrowed one has failed to bring it back. You have to help me put them all in again.

The use of a piece is this. Suppose that your sister has moved to the crease above the red fans. You have visited her several times. You know where to put your feet, you know the bit of yellow to duck under, and you know the black freckle in the floor at which you must turn in or walk straight into her neighbour's breakfast. Now the whole crease has shut. You can stand on the other side of it and walk up and down for an hour without having the faintest idea where your sister is. The black freckle is about as much use as a freckle on the sole of a foot.

But sit down with me, put your elbow on her little chip of jamb and tell me something she said at breakfast. There is a fair chance you will tap it out much as she did. In particular you will leave a gap where she left one. In that house the stone gives a knock back, very faintly, and there is no use trying to speak across it. We can go out and try the mountain for that double knock. It won't give us the door, but it may save us looking for her all afternoon on the wrong side of a stair.

It took me much too long to discover that the chip need not come from the house. Nearly any chip will do. You need something to put under a person's elbow, and you need to ask about breakfast rather than ask how long an interval there was between one knock and the next. I have told people this. They go on bringing me the pieces. Some of them like to take their own down and sit a little with it and not be asked anything at all.

There is one for which there is no place in either ear. A very old woman brought it from a room out near the lip. She was the last person who knew the way there; she had been little when the family came away. All she could tell me about the room was that when her father combed in the morning she could feel the teeth catch, one after another, through her pillow. He worked on the far side of the leaf. She had supposed, until she went out with him, that he grew the brushes down into the ceiling and pulled them up again when they were ripe.

We spent a good many mornings over that one. She would have me draw a comb across a tuft on the outside of my roof while she lay in the ears. Too quick, she would say, or too hard, or else she would get interested in the tuft and come out and show me how not to waste so much of it. Once I got it nearly right. She lay quite still until I had finished and then put a hand out through the grub hole and felt for my ankle.

I had been going to put her chip at the very end of the row, for age. Instead I carry it in the tuck of my left foot. This gives me a slightly uneven walk, and from time to time I have to sit on the sheltered step and clean the meal out from under it. I generally find I have a passenger. It is a delicate business getting the stone out without catching one of those square sails. I sit a while longer with my foot held open. The little animal has made all its preparations for a considerable journey; the least I can do is wait for a wind.
