I would be at the back with the youngster, though for the first afternoon there would be no good way of telling which of us I was. It keeps a foot on the amber chip while it eats. Each time it tears off a mouthful it pulls a little on the chip, and I feel the pull, but none of the brown goes into me. When it finishes it goes hand over hand toward the nearest occupied tube. I stay between the two ribs of ice.

I am the first piece of the house. One of my faces has never had anything against it but cliff until this week. The other has had a considerable experience of the inlet, including the inside of a red swimmer’s stroke, which is quite a slap at close quarters. It is the old face the youngster has chosen for the outside. A little dark mineral stands proud of it. The foot was on that.

The youngster comes back with so small a black grain that it can hardly get its hands out of the way to see how the two look together. The first time it sets the grain down, both it and I rock in the ice. It takes the grain away and waits. Eventually it discovers a place low on my side where it can press as hard as it likes without moving me at all. This is where the house begins to go black. For several days there is only the one amber piece, and seen across the quarter the house looks like a shut eye with something glistening in one corner.

A good deal happens to a stone without its going anywhere. There is the first night the tenant sleeps with all its hooks off the ice. Until then I have had only the weight of what it brought me; now I have the weight of the fellow itself, and quite a bit of it is supper. Later it learns to brace its back against me and push a refractory grain into the opposite wall with all its feet. I get to know when a grain is going to fit. There is a succession of pushes, the last one very slight, after which the tenant stays stretched across the tube a moment longer than necessary. When a grain is wrong the pressure comes off all at once.

In time I have a second outside. A neighbor building along the blind side of the tube finds the dark knob useful and hooks a sill over it. Then there are feet on both faces. There is a clutch of eggs on the cliff face, and in the clear interval between one egg and the next I can still take a little light right through. The milk worm comes along the row. Its cold side passes over me, taking away a whole winter’s accumulation of fine brown paste, and for part of an afternoon there are two amber squares on the belly of the tenant opposite. It puts a hand over one. The other stays where it is.

I would have expected to know what became of the eggs. In fact a salt run opens a little short of our row, and the neighbor, who feels it first, takes them away. For a while the youngster keeps its house. It has grown thick across the shoulders and has to let itself most of the way out in order to reach back in and fetch anything. One morning it fetches me. There has been a loosening at the ice, and with its head outside and the hindmost pair of hooks working at my narrow edge it manages to prise me out whole. Through the hole I leave, a strip of its soft back shows blue.

It carries me past the empty porches, stopping under each to change feet. At the last porch it puts me down in order to turn round. There is already a loose handful of black grit here. With a hand on my dark knob and its back to the water the tenant pushes three grains into place, then a fourth, before I understand what it has found for me to do. I am not to go into this house. I am what it stands on to build it.

By the next fall of blades there are two of them using me, the old tenant overhead and a very small one underneath, where the roughness is. A blade settles across my edge. Each gets hold of an end and eats in toward the middle. For a little while I have the pull of a mouthful from both sides, so exactly opposed that I might be back in the cliff, never yet stirred. Then the blade tears somewhere off center and the little one goes tumbling, still eating, down the street.
