I would have to get down to the height of the tilted bench. From the walk, looking down, the slats are so close together that they make a serviceable table; the attendant can put his oilcan there and be reasonably sure of finding it where he left it. From the height I have in mind, the next slat has a shore of its own. There is even a view between the two, of the black handle of a stopcock rather a long way below.

My bench would be higher on the left. Anything worth having which could not hold on went to the right. A drop went that way, gathering the smaller drops; a piece of bark went a short distance and stuck; very occasionally a little white animal came down on the bark and got off when it stopped. I would be one of the brown frogs, of a size to take the animal but not yet of a size to take the gap.

There is a particular difficulty about going the long way round. Where the wood comes to an end on the frame there is a bolt with a square head. The wood has rotted away from two sides of it, and the water that comes down the slat stands in the recess. Approaching it for the first time I would go straight on until my chin was in water. Thereafter I think I would turn aside a little before the bolt, on quite dry wood, even on a day when the recess was empty. A small part of the yard would have taken its shape in me. It would look, to anybody who could see it happen, like a frog going crookedly along a perfectly straight edge.

For a resting place I would have the underside of a palm rib a few inches above the bench. The good part is not the part nearest the stem. It is farther out, where a second frond coming up from below bears against the first, just enough to take some of the swing out of it. A drop striking either frond is felt in both. A foot on the walk is felt first in the back toes, which have hold of the lower one. There would be a good deal of news of this kind and very little occasion to do anything about it. Sometimes the foot goes on down the walk. Sometimes it stays, and a warm hand parts the leaves overhead, and for a minute the light shows through my toes.

In time I would make the crossing from slat to slat. I don't think it would happen on an expedition. Something small would stir on the opposite edge and I would be there, my belly a little bruised by the landing, looking for the thing which had stepped off the edge in the meantime. Coming back would be harder. There would be nothing on my own slat to go for. Quite possibly I would come back by the bolt.

On the afternoon of the count I should like to be on the lower leaf. For an appreciable time the leaf above has been coming down. Its point is almost touching my back; the light on either side of me is narrowing, and the rib under my feet is beginning to take a little of the load. Then the water falls away. Both leaves lift, though by different amounts, and there is the attendant at the stopcock, looking directly at the place where I have been all along. His lips move. The other three stay where they are. I put one foot out on the blade of the leaf, which is still rocking a little, and draw it back onto the rib.
