I would be behind the nut on the hanger. From the front it looks as if a single strip goes up one side of the shank and down the other. At the back there is a third thickness, with its nap turned the wrong way for either of the other two. That would be me. I have been there since a change of shift at which the strip I belonged to went down to the floor and took all but half an inch of me with it.

It was a good place to be left. The fitter who put the nut on started it crooked, backed it off and tried again. There is a little curl of iron in the spoiled thread. Even before I had hold enough to resist a pull I could put a fold under the curl. There was food, too, though very little of it came by water. The two lengths that passed me had their tracks full. Where they bent to go round the shank, the outside track shed a little of what it carried. For a long time I lived on what fell out at that bend.

Of the noise I could distinguish chiefly the bearing. It made one tooth of the thread very disagreeable to lie on. A new edge put down there would draw up thick and blunt while its neighbour went on growing. In this way I came to have a notch which fitted the tooth quite neatly without touching it. One morning the bearing was greased. For the rest of the week I grew into the notch. The grease lasted rather less time than it had taken me to do this.

What I had of the rest of the room came to me by way of the strips. At ten past two the one on my left would go narrow enough to let warm water in against my side. Then the one on my right would pull across the place where my track ended. For a while I had a track as long as the lower pipe. A speck which I had been working down since morning would go the whole length of my body in an instant and be carried on. There was a particular ease in that, after the rather costly business of collecting the speck. When the two lengths drew apart again I went on working at the empty place.

In time I got broad enough to meet them both at once. This altered the business of the crossing a little. The pull from the floor used to run up one side of the shank and spend itself in tightening the turn at the top. Now some of it came straight across my back. I would lift in the middle, keeping both edges down, so that for a second or two there was a passage under me. The shrimp found it. On several afternoons it was waiting with its head against my edge well before the pumps changed. It went through backwards as readily as forwards. What was on the other side was the same iron, with much the same picking on it; perhaps it was simply a piece of the hanger it had not been able to get at before.

I would have said that the underside of the nut was mine, and the long white bag going over in the hot water was the creature. But one afternoon the little curl of iron came away in my fold. There was no tearing. The whole of that familiar bit of thread went off from under me as easily as the shrimp went through. For the length of the crossing I was the corner of the bag that did not quite fill, on account of the weight of iron in its hem. I felt the basalt first through that weight: a small hard knock, quite distinct in all the uproar. Then the nap on either side of it took hold.

The iron is still there. On the floor I have grown thin enough for its shape to show through, a black comma with a little orange water at its tip. At the change of shift the long strips come down over me. One edge fits the notch left by the bad tooth; it lies there best when the pull is hardest. For half a minute there is no finding the place where I leave off. Afterwards I am a patch on the basalt about the size of a small coin, with two tracks to keep clear instead of one.

Behind the nut there is a little of me yet. It has the poorer living of the two, but lately its edge has come into reach of the outside track. At the bend, where a speck used to drop clear, there is now a white lip to receive it.
