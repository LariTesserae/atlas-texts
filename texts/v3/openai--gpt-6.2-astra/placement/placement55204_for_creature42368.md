I would be on the snag, about a hand’s breadth above the highest mark the water leaves. There is a hole there with a pointed roof. On cold nights the grey bird fits into it tail first and sleeps with its beak laid along the grain. I would be well up in the point, above the bird: a spider so flat that when I lift one foot off the wood the rest of me looks like the shadow of that foot.

For much of the year my web would be a modest affair across the mouth of the hole. The bird takes the middle out of it coming home. In the morning I eat the two sides. They are generally worth eating; little flies go on blundering into them in the dark, attracted perhaps by the warmth of the sleeper. On a very cold morning there would be a cast of the bird’s back in my lower threads, three or four overlaps of feather picked out in rime. These I would have to leave until the sun was on them.

In spring I would do rather better. When the snag begins its passage it goes close by one of the root lips. For a moment the lip bears against the wood hard enough to rock it, and a little pulp comes squeezing up between the two. I can walk out on that. I go a short way along the lip, put down silk, and walk home paying out more, and when the wood and the root part company I have a line across the gap. At first it hangs in a deep loop. I run out to the bottom, gather up an armful, bite it free and join the ends. There is still time to do this twice more before the line begins to draw of itself. Then I get off it.

I should like to know that part of the morning through my feet. A sunlit meadow is passing, with its beds of flowers and its one upright tree; what reaches the point of the hole is a steadily finer trembling, interrupted now and then by the quite heavy kick of a seed caught on the silk and shaken off. Just before the silk parts it lifts clear of the edge of the hole. For a moment there is nothing between my forefeet and a root halfway across the meadow but the work I have pulled out of my own belly. Then I have a loose end to haul in. It is often furred with the little flies. I eat as I haul, like somebody stripping currants off a stalk.

A moth would be too much for that line. On the morning of the blue band I would lose it at once, and start another, and lose that. There would be moths in the hole itself, going round the walls in the wrong direction, trying every splinter with their feet. One might stay up in the point all day with me. At such close quarters its folded wing would disclose a narrow edge of quite another colour, brown and polished, like the lip of the root it was meant to walk under. I would draw my feet in. There is room in the point, though it does not look so from outside.

That evening the bird would come home full, wipe its beak once on either side of the entrance, and back in. A little air goes up the hole when it does this. The moth, feeling the draught and then the end of the draught, would step out over the bird’s head onto the bark. By then the snag is almost back where it belongs. A purse left out on its thread brushes the wood, draws away, brushes it again. It would take the moth about six steps. In the morning there would be blue dust on the sill of my hole, and two clean strokes through it where the bird had put its tail.
