I would come to rest in the boot-sized pool, on the underside of the flat stone nearest the place where the water goes out. There is room under it for a thing no bigger than an oat grain to turn round. Looking down, you would see a few short lengths of dark rush lying there. One of them would have a pale bit set in it crosswise, and would be making fair progress against the current. That would be me.

The rush is mine. I bit the pieces off when they were soft and fitted them to a tube of silk. The pale bit is the split end of a grass stalk. It was longer when I put it on; it used to catch between two particular grains of gravel, so that several times in a morning I would come to the end of my legs and find the rest of me detained some way behind. It has worn down to a quite serviceable length. I can take my house anywhere in the pool now, except into a crack so narrow that there is no getting a house into it at all. Such a crack often has the best eating in it.

I should know a good deal about the dipper without knowing about its white front. First the light goes, which can mean any of a number of things in that meadow. Then the water bears quite hard in one direction and immediately afterward in the other. A stone which for the whole of my life has had a top and a bottom turns over, and there is a great deal of white daylight in a place where daylight has no business to be. My way of meeting this is to go indoors. It is a fairly good arrangement against most of the things that take an interest in me. Against a bird that knows what the rush is for it is very little use indeed.

There is luck in being close to the outlet. Once, with the stone already lifting, I would let go and be washed a little way under the green patch. The roots hang down there like the hair of a brush. Two would go by close enough to touch; on the third I would get a hold. All that afternoon I would hang in the dark, with very cold water coming in at one end of my sleeve and going out at the other. Overhead the bird would be walking about on a roof that gave a little under each foot. It might be the first afternoon in its life that the dipper had stood directly over a dinner for so long without either of them having any particular occasion to move.

Later I would fasten the rush case to a stone. For a while that would seem to settle where in the meadow I belonged. Then one evening there would be a small, rather disreputable object hauling itself by its forelegs up a stem at the margin of the pool. It would split down the back. I would come out with long feelers and four soft wings, and stand a long time doing nothing with these considerable acquisitions. The rush with the pale bit in it would still be under the water. If the bird took it then and beat it on the stone, it would get very little for its pains.

I think my first flight would be a poor one. The meadow has not much use for wings that have never been tried. I would start off toward the pink flowers and find myself, without having made any turn that I could recollect, clinging to the upright slab of the shelter. There is a dry strip there no wider than a wing is long. Here I would fold mine over my back, one slope meeting the other, and be a small dun caddisfly on a grey stone. A seated person might put a hand within an inch of me and never know that anything had happened in the pool.

From there I could see the dipper coming up from the notch. It would fly straight at the slab, check, and drop to the gutter for one last turn at the wet stems. For the first time I would be above it. There is a little patch of brown on the crown of its head which from that height is much more conspicuous than the white. I would keep very still until it went home. Then, when the cloud had come in and every stem held a drop at precisely the height from which a thirsty creature on six legs could reach it, I would try the wings again.
