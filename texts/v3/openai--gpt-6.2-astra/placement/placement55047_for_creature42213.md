I would be one of the young in the flute. At first I should not know which one. A hind foot would be pushing at my throat, and by working my mouth along the edge of it I should come to a place where I could get my nose through the grass. There would be a considerable space there, enough to take a whole breath in without getting anybody’s fur up my nose. I would keep coming back to it. From outside, the way to tell me from the others would be to look for the two grass stems that had gone dark at the crossing.

Something of the birds would come through the bed of the nest even when my ears were shut. The few times the stone was quiet, everybody in the nest would stir. Then the sound would build up again, beginning well down the flute, and we would settle into all the little hollows we had made in one another. I think for a long time I would take this sound to be something our mother did, though it went on in her absence. There was a warmth she left behind her too.

On the stone I would be the one next to last. I would have the double business of keeping a good mouthful of the fur ahead and going slowly enough to keep that slight pull on the skin of my back. Where the stone stepped down, the pull would come almost straight up. For a moment I could go no farther, although the one in front of me was going on and its long guard hairs were slipping out between my teeth. Then two feet would land quite hard on my tail and we could all go on together.

When the last one lost hold I should feel wonderfully quick. I could get over a lip of stone in a jump. It would take the turning of the whole file, and the sight of that small open mouth on the bare place we had just crossed, for me to know what the lightness was. Our mother would put her nose to its nose. I would have to turn about in very little room to offer it the proper end of me again.

Later I would live a little way along the wall, where there was scarcely water enough to swim in. A tongue of stone would lie across the seep there. At its upper end it would have a white dry surface, good for sitting on; its lower end would be dark on both sides and just loose enough for a shrew to get under. I would learn to go in flat, with one cheek to the grit. There would be food all along the underside, little things that came loose at a touch and others that had to be taken by one end and pulled until all their feet gave way. Outside, a drop falling off the moss would send a pulse of cold water the length of my side.

That is where I would look for myself once the procession was over. Not at the deep pool, where even from the height of a standing person one might follow the bright spot down. Look instead at the dry end of that tongue of stone. There will be half a dozen white husks on it, all split along the same side, and a wet place no bigger than the end of a finger where I have been sitting to eat. If you wait, you may see me come out backwards. My fur will stand the wrong way on one cheek. I will give it two or three strokes with a wet hand before I attend to anything else.
