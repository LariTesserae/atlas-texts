I think you would find me a little below the three noses, on the front of the step where the young mouse has landed. I feel both his hind feet come down. While he sits washing I put out one of my own feet and try the air. From where he sits there is nothing to see but the tip of a bent straw appearing over the edge. He leans forward. Above him the three noses come out a little farther, though what they can see now except the back of his head I don’t know.

I am one of the spiders that hunt without a web. Standing still on the old stair I am very like the stone, except for my eyes. In the daytime, if you move a finger along in front of me, I will turn on the spot to follow it, in little hitches, like a button being worked loose. At this hour I have very little use for eyes. I wait until the footfalls go up the step again. Four mice going to bed make a good deal more commotion in the stone than one mouse coming out of it. For some time afterward bits of grit come down.

There is a flake lifting from the front of the step. Behind it I have a white silk pocket, open at either end. I go in at the lower end tonight, on account of the grit. In the morning I shall go out at the upper. Already a little light comes in there while the rest of the hollow is dark. A gnat has made the mistake of spending the night on the other side of the flake. I can see its legs through the silk.

Most mornings I go a good deal farther for my breakfast. The best way off the stair is by a fig twig that has grown against it. At first there was a jump to manage there; now the twig rubs on the step and has a flat place in its bark. Beyond it is a fork, and beyond the fork I have a choice of half a dozen ways, one of which yesterday ended in a yellow wall. I went round it. On the underside was the hole the mouse had made. A fly was walking in and out of it, stopping each time on the sill to rub its hind legs together. I missed the fly and went in through the hole.

I had not known what it was like inside a fig. The light came through the skin everywhere. There were little red shreds hanging from the ceiling, and where my feet took hold the floor gave a little without breaking. I stayed until the yellow light had gone out of the walls and the hole by which I had come in was the lightest thing there. By then the juice had dried on my feet. I had to sit in the fork for a long time drawing one leg after another through my jaws.

Today I might go back. The trouble with a place of that sort is that it can be on the tree when you set out for it and on the ground when you arrive. From the fork I look along the twig. There is the little pale button where the stalk came away. Far below, going along very slowly on the back of the tortoise, is a scrap of yellow skin.
