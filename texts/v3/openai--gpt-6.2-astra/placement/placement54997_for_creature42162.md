There is a place on the way up from the lower beds where I would have to set my basket down. The street takes a step up there, rather a high one, and I could manage the step with a load, or the shift of the load from my hip to my head, but not both together. I would have a particular block on which to set it. One day I would find a young fellow asleep there in the sun and discover that I had come to think of the block as mine.

I think I would make the slings that people use for carrying awkward things. A basket does well enough for anything that will go in a basket. But there are the long grating slabs to get from one kitchen to another, and there is the old woman who wants to take her basin with her when she moves, although there is a perfectly good basin at the other house. She has had this one so long that she can tip the water out of it one-handed, letting it turn in the hollow of her elbow. The new one would have to be lifted against her belly and tipped away from her with both hands. She demonstrates this to me twice. I have already agreed to take the basin. What she wants the second time, I come to understand, is for me to try it.

My work would take me into houses at a time when they looked very little like houses. Things which had lived quite peaceably together for thirty years would all be out on the pavement, where it would seem absurd that anyone should own so many of them. Then the mats would go off on somebody's back and a pair of sisters would take the kitchen shelf between them, and presently there would be nothing left but a small heap of things nobody had thought it necessary to arrange to carry. I would have learned to bring an empty basket for these. On the whole I would be a better person to have at the house one was leaving than at the one one was going to. I like knowing where a thing has been. I am too ready with an opinion about where it ought to go.

For the basin I would make a cradle with two loops, short enough to keep the rim clear of our knees. The woman would take the front loop herself. From behind, the back of her dress would be visible to me down to the place where the great gray lip of the basin cut her off. Every so often one of her feet would appear beneath it, smaller than I had supposed, in a sandal the color of the road. We would go a long way like this. There would be a good deal I could see on either side of her and nothing whatever I could see of the patch of street I was about to put my foot on.

At the high step she would stop. I would be getting my breath to tell her about my block when I would feel her end come up a little and travel round to the left. We would put the basin down on a ledge I had passed a thousand times, too low on my side to be of any service but just the height of a resting arm to somebody standing on the step above. She used to come this way with her mother, she would say. Then we would change ends.

I would find this quite a different journey. I could see my footing beautifully now, and had to keep myself from going too fast. Beyond the last turn I could see people waiting to receive us, one of them an officious girl of about nine clearing a space for the basin in exactly the wrong place. Behind me the old woman would begin to laugh. I would feel it first in the plaited loop, a succession of little tugs which for a moment I would mistake for a wish on her part to put the thing down.
