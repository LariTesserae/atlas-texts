I think I would still have hold of the coat. The others have gone out from under it at various points on the stair. Where there were feet to either side of me there is a considerable expanse of smooth, rather greasy ceiling. I can see through one of the windows, provided I stand on the very tips of my fingers. It shows me a strip of sky with a notch in it. When I walk, the notch stays still and the sky goes by.

There is some advantage in being the last. When the stair folds I have only to let myself down flat. The edge of the coat catches in a groove, the tread comes down on the crown, and there is still a little space round me, cool even in the middle of the day. The trouble comes when the stair opens. I have to go right to one end and pull, then to the other end and pull, and even so I come free with a jerk that sends me several times my own length down the incline. By the third tread there is daylight at a place in the ceiling where there was none before. One of my siblings would have done very well there. I put a foot through the hole. It does nothing for it.

It would take an afternoon to go down that stair in sunshine. In rain I get down before I have let go at the top. There is a knock; the coat cants over; a current of deliciously cold water goes in at one eye and out at another. I have several feet on a surface as hairy as the inside of a seed pod. I take hold. For a little while the coat floats beside me, and through the four windows in turn I see the brown top of the stair, the brown top of the stair, something green, something green. Then it sticks to the wet hair and I am able to walk out from under it without having to lift it at all.

I would be in the bare place under the ridge of that eastern house. The hairs are on the stubs from which the yellow leaves have fallen. The ends of the stubs are pearly with sap. There is meal enough on a single one to put a line through me, quite a handsome line, wider in the middle where I have been a little immoderate. I spend the remainder of the shower cleaning my feet. All the business of transferring a creature from one end of the basin to the other has been accomplished without my having put out a foot to find anybody. In fact the first foot put out to me takes me so much by surprise that I bite it.

It belongs to a very large pale animal. There is a tiny clearing in the gray dust on its pad where my mouth has been. It sets the pad down, lifts another, and goes on over me. Several more follow. They are going up the ridge, where it seems to me there can be nothing to get under. I go too, in the shade of the last one. At the very pinch of the ridge there is a way in. The two halves of the roof are pressed together so closely that the light coming through them is a deeper green than the light anywhere else. The first animal inserts its fingers; the next inserts its fingers a little farther along. For a moment there is a fine comb of light on the wall below. I get in through one of the teeth.

I should like to say that I find my place at once among those feet. In fact there are days when I am hardly more use than a bit of grit. A movement comes down the row to me and stops. There is a wait; a long foot reaches across my back and takes hold on the other side; the movement goes on. Nobody puts me out. The hollow of a pad is quite a good seat for something my size, and there is the advantage, for the animal whose pad it is, that I am always willing to clean it. In time I get so that I can tell the weight of a roof coming on from the weight of somebody wanting to get up. The first takes the slack out of all the feet at once. The second begins with a curious lightness in one finger, like the lift at one end of the old coat when there were still two of us to carry it.

What I would come to know best, I think, is the feel of somebody deciding to let go. It is appreciable well before the fingers open. There is a little testing pull, almost in the wrong direction; for an instant I must hold on rather harder, not less hard. Then I have the whole soft weight of a foot in mine and can put it down or pass it along as the case may be. Much later, at a wet sill, I feel that pull from a foot I have never touched before. The drop is going; the dun wall opposite is already as thin to look at as a flake of skin. I hold a little harder. We come down together among the yellow stubs, neither of us getting the least bit wet except on the one foot.

By then there is very little left of my old coat. A corner of it has stuck fast under the ridge. Once, coming out in the morning with my own back still white and tender, I fit a foot into the hole I made on the stair. It admits only the tips of two fingers. The rest of the household comes up behind me and for a while we lie along the bare patch in the sun. From the lip of the basin you might just be able to make us out: a brown line across the green, with one small interruption through which, until my coat takes color, the green shows nearly clear.
