I think I would live under the ironmonger's, which is not where people would hear me.

The ironmonger has a very good floor. It was laid when the shop dealt in machine tools, and a man could set a lathe going at one end of it without disturbing a much more particular piece of work at the other. From underneath there is consequently very little to distinguish it from the natural roof. I should spend a good part of the evening there, getting a roll of skin round a rather inconvenient corner. The place where people would hear me is the public weighing machine, some distance up the street. Its base has four iron lugs sunk into four separate stones. You can hardly cross under one of them without making the needle move.

Children have discovered that if one stands on the machine at supper time one is liable to gain two ounces and lose them again. There is some competition to see who can gain the most. The trick, which rather spoils it as a competition, is to lift one heel and put it down smartly. I should be known, accordingly, as Weights and Measures. There are less dignified names for quite important animals.

From my side, I don't think there would be a street. There would be places where a foot could go on ahead and places where it had better wait for another foot. There would be the long easy reach which happens to lie under the ironmonger's, and a region a little beyond it where it is possible to be warm on one side and cool on the other. In the afternoon the warmth slowly changes sides. I should have been doing something with this circumstance for years before anyone thought to build the shop.

What I should like best, I think, would be getting the last turn into a very full roll. By then the skin has lost nearly all its tack. A foot can slip on it, particularly towards the ends, where there is less dust and the roll winds tighter. It takes a number of feet to do the thing well. One has to travel along behind the roll keeping an edge turned in; two others, well apart, feel the roof ahead. There comes a point when all that breadth of me, which a little while ago had room for every grain to lie separately, fits quite comfortably in a hollow no bigger than a rolled-up shop awning. The feet that have been at either end come into contact. For a little while I should know exactly how large I was.

One evening, years after the last machine tool has been sold, the ironmonger will have a daughter to mind the shop for him. She will take out a bank of shallow drawers in order to paint behind it and find, close down against the skirting, a hole meant for a shaft. The shaft is gone. The hole goes down into the warm reach, and if she puts her hand over it she can feel a little air going in between her fingers.

It won't be her fingers I come for. In taking out the drawers she will have dislodged a square iron washer. It will lie half across a joint and rock when any considerable weight goes past the shop. She will be trying to get it out with a bent bit of wire when a nail comes up through the joint and holds the washer quite still.

There will be a small difficulty here. She can't get the washer out while I'm holding it, and I won't be easy about going away while the roof has a loose piece in it. After a bit she will put down the wire. She will be an ironmonger's daughter brought up literally over the business; it will occur to her to press on the other corner of the washer. When she feels my weight come off it she will slide it sideways onto the sound part of the floor. We shall neither of us have had to be particularly clever. It is a thing people do all day in a shop, taking the weight of something while somebody gets a better purchase.

I should like to be the part of her day that she finds rather hard to report upstairs at supper. She will bring the washer with her, which won't help much. Her father will be interested to learn that the old shaft hole is still open. He will advise a cover with holes rather than a plug, on account of the draft. It will only be when he's had the washer in his hand a while, turning it over with his thumb, that he'll ask her which corner it was holding.

Under the floor, meanwhile, I shall have put a foot back once to make sure. By the time they are doing the dishes most of me will be well along towards the weighing machine. A boy who has been out much later than he ought to be will step on it on his way home, both heels down, no tricks, and for a second or two he will be four ounces lighter. Nobody has thought to keep a record of that.
