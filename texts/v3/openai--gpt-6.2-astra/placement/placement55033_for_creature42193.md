I should be on the bank, I think. There is a dry place just above the glazed brick where one could sit with one's heels in the two hollows that used to take the ends of the ladder. From there one could put a finger on a passing leaf without appreciably altering the speed of the orchard. I should like to have an occupation of that order. For a while I think I am the person who comes down to take the time.

There is a difficulty about taking the time. The brick is fixed, but nothing that goes by it is quite what went by it last. The pearl strip is longer at one end and shorter at the other. A knot that was level with the top of the brick last time is now well up in the clay, writing a separate, rather expensive-looking scroll. One would need to choose a mark further in. The shed door, for instance. But the door can be left open. One could get a very good week's error out of that.

Besides, on putting my finger to the leaf, I find that what I feel is the finger. It has been resting on damp cloth, probably the knee of a trouser leg. It has a small pulse in it which is not at all well conveyed by the leaf. There are two beats, then a great many at once, then nothing. Further down, where I am spread rather better, it is possible to distinguish the separate beats again. I am evidently on the moving side.

It is tempting to say I am the whole business under the trees. But of the shed, for example, I know only a long narrow oblong of agreeable warmth and a most disagreeable tick at one corner. I had supposed the tick to be part of the warmth until the four days when the two ceased, about an hour apart. Of the five rows I can make out three. There is a fourth on wet evenings. The gate I know very well indeed on one side and not at all on the other. An orchard ought to have a better idea of itself than that.

My best part is a little below the glazed brick. When the branch has passed, if one scrapes away the satin face of the bank with the edge of a penny, there is a slot as fine as the slit in a money box. Its two sides are lined with the same pearl as the branch. It goes back a surprising distance. At the far end, where there has never been any light, is a little pad the colour of the crumb of a new loaf. That would be the place to put the point of a pencil if it were necessary to put me on the man's drawing. Unfortunately it would be just off the drawer.

I have been left there by degrees. At first there was only the usual pink rub on the branch and a pink rub to match it on the brick. The one on the brick lasted until the first shower. There came a dry summer when it lasted until the branch came round again. After that there was a bit that pulled out like the thread between a spoon and a treacle tin. The thread broke. Both ends drew in very smartly. Mine took a grain of glaze in with it, which is still there; I turn it from time to time. It has one perfectly flat face.

I don't hear the domestic squeak. It must be much too large a sound to hear from where I am. What happens here is that quite suddenly I have the use of three rows of trees. There is a boy standing in the first row with one foot up. There is water going the wrong way in a red cord. At the far corner something has taken hold of the iron and is having a great deal of trouble letting go. All this comes in while the lip of the branch is sliding over mine. Then there is the bank again, which has in it one slow worm, a stone that gets colder before rain, and in winter a very fine instruction in the several weights of snow. A cat walking on snow is heavier on the way down the bank than on the way up. I have had ample opportunity to be sure of that.

What goes the other way I couldn't at first tell. It seemed unlikely that three rows of trees had much use for the cat. But once, when the brick had worked loose after a frost, the branch missed me. I felt it go by overhead, polishing the clay to no purpose. For the next circuit it hung lower. Not all at once: the red cord nearest the bank had let itself down by about the breadth of a bootlace, and the next by rather less, so that the limb came to me on a gentle incline. The loose brick had fallen face down in the grass by then. We met without making any noise at all.

That evening, inside the slot, I had the taste of a pear. Only the bit close to the pips, where there are little hard grains in the flesh. I worked one of them against my piece of glaze for most of the night. By morning it was gone. Out on the branch, a foot above the bank, the hair went round and round and then, for once, carried its tail straight down. It had got the length of the slot very nearly right.

So I should not, after all, have to go down the lane with a watch. I should be the small part that stays behind, with clay at its back, getting a little wider each year. There would be long intervals with very little to do. I think I should like the approach best. A day or two before the branch arrives, the water in the slot begins to taste faintly of mint. Then the slow worm goes elsewhere. Then, although nothing has touched me yet, I find I can lift the leaf under the boy's raised foot. Just the one leaf, and only the thickness of a penny. Enough to meet him coming down.
