For a long time I should be mistaken for a fault in the floor of an empty nursery. There is a faint milky crescent there which does not come clear when the rasper goes over it. Indeed the rasper is rather careful about going over it. At one end of the crescent it lifts its forepart, puts it down on the other side, and draws the rest of itself across with a little hitch. In poor light there is nothing to be seen even then. But let a herd lie down somewhere above the blister and the crescent will shorten at both ends. There will be a thread of clear stone where the milkiness was. That is the space between my chin and my knees.

I have been sitting with my chin on my knees since before the bird went away. I was considerably smaller then. Several of us came in at a feeding, stuck to a clot of fruit against which the young bird wiped the side of its bill. For a while we lived very well in the scratches made by its feet. One could go the length of a scratch and find a little of everything in it: fruit sugar, yellow meal from the hole, a transparent stocking that a mite had got too big for. I still have a liking for that last thing. There is a great deal of chewing in one of those stockings and scarcely any taste.

The others went out on the bird. In nearly every blister there is one that stays. It is not necessarily the smallest or the slowest. When the bird takes hold of the belly hair and pulls, its last movement inside the blister is to brace its tail very hard against the floor. Whatever is under the tail stays under it until the bird has gone. 

I have six legs, of which four are fit for walking. The hind pair spread out into pale soft fans. In the scratches these were a nuisance. Here on the clean floor they fit so closely to the glass that I can let the rest of myself hang from them, head down, and look out. A bird returning to put its bill into the hole, as old birds sometimes do once or twice after the young one has left, sees in the roof of the blister a very small face with an extraordinarily black mouth. I have no use for the bird by then. I keep my mouth open for the damp.

The bright country first appears no bigger than the end of a quill. Usually it starts under one of my fans. There will be a feeling of grit there. I raise the fan to smooth it with my mouth and there, quite distinct in the glass, are the pale seed heads of grass. When I put my foot down again I can feel them stroking it one way, then the other. It is windy on those slopes. This is something one would hardly know from the pools, where the fish only lets one see the place for as long as it takes to swallow a gnat.

For several days the view widens. I move ahead of it, lifting my feet in turn. Where the grass is in shadow the glass tastes much as it always does. In the sunny parts it has a strong taste of salt. I go about licking these until my belly is quite round and the two halves of my back have come a little apart, showing a neat white stitch between them. The rasper in its corner does not stir. Its great yellow rump, plugged into the syrup, is by now the only thing between us and a drop of some hundreds of feet. I could get past it if I cared to. My brothers and sisters are out there among the birds, growing long narrow hind legs that will hold them quite securely in a feather. Mine have gone on getting wider. When I draw them up under me I look like a creature carrying its own shallow dish.

One evening the grass lies down flat. It stays down. The change is so considerable that for a while I cannot tell what I am looking at. What seemed a whole hillside of separate stems is a smooth green surface, with the hairs all running one way. Then a fly walks out onto it. It is a large fly, dull brown, with a drop of water attached to each of its forefeet. It stops to rub them together. I put my mouth to the glass under the drops and for the first time in my life drink cold water.

After that it is quite easy to get through. There is no hole. I set a fan down on cold grass instead of warm stone, and bring the other after it. My head comes last, rather painfully on account of the jaws. For a little while I stand in ordinary daylight casting six little shadows down the several sides of a grass stalk. Up close, there is nothing very easy about this country. The earth has dried in great hard lumps. A seed head which looked soft enough from the blister has an awn driven clean through a brown fly's wing. The fly walks round and round the stem, trying the wing at intervals. It might be the one whose feet I drank from. There are a great many such flies here.

I go downhill. The broad hind feet are good on the dry earth, where a narrower foot would go through the crust at every step. Under a stone, about a morning's walk from where I came out, I find the others. Some have come from blisters, some from pools. The pool ones are dark and have a little notch in the forehead where a fish has tried them. We lie with our feet overlapping. As the stone warms, the salt we have brought begins to come off us in flakes. There are roots against the underside of the stone, fine white things with a pink tip to each; by evening the tips have found us. It tickles abominably. The ones that have been here longest are quite furred over with root hairs and have given up trying to scratch.

I should spend the dry part of the year there. I should get to know the weight of a hoof on earth, a quite different thing from the weight of a foot on glass. Here the ground gives a little and keeps what it is given. After a herd has passed there are hollows over our heads that fill at the first rain. The roots let go of us then. We come up in the hollows, washed clean, with the white stitch along each back loosened into a row of little mouths. They are the young. For a day or two they ride on us and eat the skin between their feet. Then a silver fish comes nosing through water scarcely deep enough to cover its eyes, and there is a rush to get aboard.

Not all on the same fish. Not all in the direction of the glass. Some of the hollows run together and spill into a brown stream, and there are fish in it I have never seen the underside of in any blister. As for me, I crawl out when the water is still quite shallow. There is a good warm place on the rim of the print, with a fern coming up, where I can sit and work the stiffness out of my fans. From below, if a browser has stopped to drink and a fish turns just then, I suppose I might be visible. I should be the small thing on the bank in the bright weather, holding one foot in both hands.
