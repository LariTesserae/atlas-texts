I would be on the shelf, but I would have missed the face. From the place where I usually stand to work, the moon clears the head of the taller animal by a good inch. I have had an excellent view of the setting moon for most of my life and have consequently given up looking at it.

I go round the tableland mending things made of hide. There is a prod of bone for making the holes and a second prod, quite blunt, for opening a hole that has closed up. These live side by side in a fold of my coat. I can tell which is which by putting my finger in the fold, an arrangement the old person on the shelf thinks very funny. The old person taught me a good deal of what I know about hide and has long since given up trying to teach me anything about pockets.

On this occasion the cup of horn has lost its handle. The cup itself is sound. There is a light place the shape of an ear on its side, where a loop of hide has kept the sun off it, and the two holes for the ends of the loop are still good. It is a job that takes a strip no longer than the distance from the tip of my nose to my lip. Yet I have taken up most of the approach to the shelf doing it. Here is my head at the lip, upside down, asking if it is the cup for hot things or the cup for cold. Here I am sitting up again to look in my bag. There is a surprising amount of me looking in my bag.

To get to the old person with my bit of hide I have to come down sitting, just as the child did. It is no small thing to turn round on this shelf wearing a bag that somebody has mended with the stiff part of a grazer’s rump. I take it off and pass it down first. There are two small brown hands ready for it. This is the very last I shall see of my bag for quite a distance.

From the country below, if you had eyes good enough, you would see three heads ranged along the lip of the waterfall. One is white, one dun, one nearly bald, with a little white husk adhering to it. For years I thought the husks fell out of the sky onto us. It was only when my hair began to go that I found out how many people are inclined to put a hand on a bald head when climbing past it.

The child does not want me to look at the moon just yet. There is something the matter with the face. The old person has seen it perfectly, but has said it looks hungry, and that was not the expression intended. There is a good deal of moving of my head from side to side by the ears. Some distance along the shelf my hand is still at work on the cup. I like this about my hands. There have been occasions when I have gone a considerable way in embarrassment or grief and come upon a perfectly serviceable piece of stitching.

The loop has to be knotted inside the horn. If you leave the ends too long they dab your nose when you drink the last of anything; if you cut them too short the wet knot will pull through. I am considering the ends when the child puts a finger under my chin and there it is. The eyes are not quite of a size. One is a little higher than the other. The face looks like somebody waiting to be told whether something they have made is any good.

I know this look. There is a place not far from here, above the shelf, where I am holding out two pieces of hide joined by my first seam. I had selected the edge of the old river for the purpose because the light was good. This was a mistake. Against that much blue air you can see daylight through every hole. The old person’s hair is black there. One of the pieces of hide is gripped between two knees, while a pair of strong brown hands pulls on the other. The seam, which is very ugly, holds. There is a little place in the air where you can hear the hide creak. For reasons I could never have explained to anyone I have gone back to listen to it more than once.

I say that the moon looks as if it has just mended a cup. This satisfies the child, who has not yet had a good look at the repair.

I should like to say that I leave behind me a pair of excellent little shoes or a sound roof, something on the scale of the work done by the people at the channel. In fact what you will find of me most readily is a stretch of very undignified crawling about with one arm under the mat. My blunt prod has gone. The child has emptied the bag to some purpose: along the edge of the shelf there is a procession of my possessions arranged in order of size, beginning with the smallest hone and ending with a lump of yellow fat in its wrapper. But the prod is not among them. At length a hand with blue veins in it goes, without any preliminary feeling about, into the fold of my coat and takes it out.

By then there is cold water in the cup. The old person drinks first, and then the child, who has to use both hands and regards the handle as something to look through. I have the last of it. One of the ends touches my nose.

A little farther round the lip, where there is room for a person to climb out, you can see the soles of my feet, a good deal patched. The child is staying. I have the stick to take back to the house. On one side of it, in a tender place where the bark has come away, a thumbnail has scored two uneven circles over a crescent. I carry it by the other end.
