I think the boy would find me before his grandmother did. I would be on the underside of the lid of a pan, which is a place grown people see rather seldom. When you lift a heavy lid you look at the water. But if you are small, and it is somebody else doing the lifting, the underside passes before your eyes like a second sky. In this sky there would be a thumbprint of wet clay and, a little way out from it, a snail no bigger than the little toe of a new baby. That would be me.

I would have come in on the bark fringe, when the bark was a strip the woman was combing over her knee. My shell would still be the flat color of its inner face. I could spend most of the day going the length of one thread. It would be a different journey going back. A thread drawn tight by the weight of a drop is a good wide road; the same thread, lightened, can curl until the place you are trying to reach is against your own back. There would be a certain amount of arriving without having done the walking.

The boy would put out a finger. His grandmother would say, “Wait till I put it down,” meaning the lid, but he would take her to mean me and leave me alone. On the inside of the pan there is a high-water mark of quite tender green stuff. I would eat along its upper edge. By the next cleaning there would be a pale line in the green as if somebody had tried to draw a circle and been interrupted by the need to go to bed.

For a while he would suppose that every pan had one of me. Indeed several would, though in one of the pans what he took for my shell would be a little curled shaving left by the woman who trimmed the bark. It would stay in the same place for days. He would tell his mother that the one at the far bed was a poor eater.

What I would know of the boy would have little to do with his size or his face. I would know the season of his wrist. Every few days, when the lid came off, there would be laid against the lip of the pan a warm damp loop of root, already a little green itself from having been thrust into so many pans. Once I would go onto it. The boy would see me there and face a difficulty. Somebody a long way off would be making the hole in the sky, and this was the arm he had free. After a little thought he would hold it up very slowly.

It would be a good while before he could bring it down. The person on the rise was looking for somebody else, rather taller. Meanwhile I would get across the root and onto the skin, where there would be salt and a fine tremor from the effort of keeping still. Nothing in my life on the crown would have prepared me for so much salt. I would go back onto the root. By the time the arm came down I would be on its shady side, tucked into the place where the ends were spliced, and the boy, turning his wrist this way and that, would think I had fallen.

That night he would sleep for the first time at the third of his beds. He would have meant to go home. It is a long way between the moment when the stone falls quiet and the moment when one can bring oneself to stop listening, and then almost no time at all before dark. His grandmother would be able to see him from her roof, standing by the far pan with his arm up. She would make the answer. He would wait a little, in case she meant to come. Then he would lay the loop beside the slit in the lid, because a root left out all night on bare stone is stiff in the morning and hurts going over the hand.

I would come out there. There would be the damp bark, and below it the curved wall, and against the wall a green line with never a break in it. In the morning he would lift the lid to drink. For once he would have to do the lifting himself, bracing one edge on the rim while he bent to get his mouth down to the water. He would drink as much as he wanted, ease the lid back into its bed of grit, and go home rather pleased with himself, never having looked up.
