I would be at the crossing where she puts them down. There is a hollow in the moss there, about the size of a young vole folded up. When she lifts the last one, the hollow stays a moment. Two clear drops come out between the small green heads and run together at the bottom. I think I would be one of the things that stirs then, too small to have been felt through the skin of its belly.

A springtail. In the light off the snow I would look grayish blue, but there is seldom light enough at the crossing to make out a color. What can be seen is a speck on the water which, when you look again, is on the side of a root. There is a little fork held bent under my belly. I can send myself a distance with it, though I have much less say in where I come down. For the most part I walk. The moss that looks like a strip of black felt from above has quite separate stems. I can go round one without coming in sight of the next.

I would live a little way up inside the arch, on the root that has grown a flat side from lying against another root. On the flat there is a skin of fungus, finest at the edges, which is good to eat. My antennae go on ahead of my mouth, touching now to one side and now to the other. In a day's grazing I might clear a patch that could be covered by the willow scale on the young one's back. I wouldn't often have occasion to go down as far as the street. A loose outer shred of the root reaches from my feeding place nearly to the moss. The water takes hold of the end and lets it go, and the movement is quite distinct underfoot, even where the shred is still fast.

On the evening of the move it would go still. The water has hold of it for good this time and is creeping up its underside. I go up the arch. At the crown, where the smaller vole stopped to wash, a few blue leaves have been pulled partway through to the dark side. The wool on one of them is full of beads of water so small that I can pass between them. There is a good deal of room here that doesn't show from the boulder.

It might take me till morning to get to the chamber in the broken face. There is a willow leaf caught by its stalk in the yellow grass of the old nest. All summer it has hung outside, getting knocked against the earth whenever there is wind. The blue skin has worn away in one place and the wet inner stuff has begun to soften. I would feed there with the drop below me, as readily as on the floor of the crossing. When the wind lifts the leaf I would go up with it and come down a little after it, my six feet taking hold again among the hairs.

On the other side of that thin wall of earth the young ones are beginning to dry. The scale between the little one's shoulders, flat while it was wet, has curled away at one end. By the time it falls out there will be a space under it. Not a chamber for anything so large as the vole. Enough for me to turn in without touching the roof.
