I would come down on Thursdays with the mechanic’s dinner. I would be about eleven, old enough to be sent across the tram square with a tin of soup but not yet allowed to carry an open tin down a stair. He would have to prise the lid off himself. It fits so well that in cold weather it goes in a little at the middle. There is a spoon in my pocket making the dark patch on my good coat which will be remarked on later.

At first what I like best is the plinth of the disconnected pump. There is room on it for two people provided one of them is small. The enamel has chipped off the corner where the mechanic sits; my corner is still blue. He eats with the tin between his knees. My job is to keep the lid somewhere clean, and this is how we come to have a looking glass down there. The inside of the lid is bright. If I hold it out beyond my knee and tip it back toward the pump I can see a square foot or so of the water without leaning over. I have been forbidden to lean over. Nobody has thought to forbid this.

Things in the lid are on the whole easier to see. For one thing there are fewer of them. For another I can get a bead against the white of the mechanic’s shirt, where it looks like the full stop at the end of a sentence in my reader. He has to keep quite still while I do this. Once he goes on holding a spoonful of soup halfway up for so long that a little skin forms on it which goes into his mouth all in one piece. I see him consider it before he swallows.

There is a particular tenant I know by a gap in the binding of its left side. In the lid, of course, I get to know it as a gap on the right. Just there the yellow gives out for the width of a hair and begins again a shade paler. It is usually to be found at the place where the lane leaves the pump step for open water. The drop from the step is considerable for a tenant. Instead of going down the riser this one puts up its hands and takes the water, and in passing from one to the other performs a turn which I spend a good many Thursdays trying to see the whole of.

I take to putting my free hand through the motions on the blue enamel. Two fingers for the hands, the heel of my palm for the hind end, and nothing much to do about the six feet. There comes a Thursday when, by attending to my hand instead of to the lid, I get it. The step has been giving me trouble. I have supposed that there must be an instant when the animal lets go of the step and trusts its weight to the water. In fact the water has the weight already. The last foot on the step is doing scarcely anything; it could stay there or come away. I lift the heel of my hand. The enamel beneath it is quite warm.

After that I think I would be the one with the gap. Not all the time. In particular I would not care to give up the walk home with the empty tin, striking the lid with the spoon at each of the two turns to hear the difference. But for the length of that last foot coming away I can manage it. I have the hard grain under one nail and the long pull on both my hands. There is a large pale thing out beyond the edge of the step. It has been there every seventh day for some time and has never come any nearer. Today there is a dark round opening in the middle of it and in the opening something bright moves.

The mechanic tips the last of the soup into his mouth. When I look for my tenant again there are three at the edge of the step, all with their good sides toward me. He wipes the rim of the tin with his bread and holds it out for the lid. For a moment we sit like that. He is looking into it too. From where he sits he cannot possibly see the step; what he can see, I find out by moving my head over beside his, is the underside of my chin and the spoon handle sticking out of my pocket. He pushes the handle in with one finger, not quite in time to save the coat. Then we press the lid down between us, working our thumbs round opposite ways until they meet.
