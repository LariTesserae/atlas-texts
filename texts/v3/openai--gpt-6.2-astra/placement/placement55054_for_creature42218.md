I think I should be on the lowest terrace, which is a poor place to look from and a good place to listen. From down there the middle terrace is a wall. Of the five thickets you can see only the upper work of two, and of the stair nothing at all. But the little splinter at the foot of the wall is a considerable object. It has landed on two points and an edge, leaving underneath it a space shaped rather like the space under the lid of a piano. That is where I should have my back against the salt.

I would be a grub, though not, I think, one of those particularly handsome cream ones. There must be an age between the green comma and the animal that can span a mouth. I should be at that age, when there is quite a lot of belly and the feet still look as if they belonged to a much smaller establishment. On the bare salt the difficulty is not so much getting a hold as letting go of it. One little pad comes free with a tick. In the thicket that tick goes into a hundred folds of skin and comes out a vowel. Here under the splinter it is very distinctly the sound of my foot.

What took me down there? On the face of the riser is the cut end of a yellow thread. There is another just opposite it in the splinter. Before the salt split they were one thread, and the plant above fed on it, perhaps; now, between the two ends, there is a hair's breadth of clear air. I have got my mouth to the lower end. The grease is good. It has none of the slight resistance the frill puts up before the teeth go through. I have the look, to anyone who could get a light under there, of a creature earnestly trying to eat its way into a stone.

The odd thing is that I can feel the grub with the parcel. Not the parcel, of course, nor the turn of its head. What I have of it is six small pressures quite close together in the knock of the stair. The flake I lie under takes the many sounds of the chamber according to its own shape. The deep note scarcely troubles it. The fine sound of the rims runs along its edge and is gone. But to the knock it answers with its whole flat underside. There is a place against my back where, if I had another foot, I would put it, as naturally as one puts a foot out to steady a thing somebody else is carrying.

Perhaps that is what you would notice about me: not the mouth at the thread, which is hidden, but one foot lifted rather high and turned pad outward. There is no wall on that side. You might reasonably suppose that I had slipped, or that grubs at my age do a great deal of kicking about to no purpose. You would have to put your nail on the splinter to understand the fit of that foot to what is there.

I should not know about the afternoon. I should know the exact breadth of the warm place on my back, the taste at the broken end of the thread, and the weight that wants steadying. My foot is out for it. In the dimple, where the salt shines pink through the thin cream skin, there is room for the knock entire.
