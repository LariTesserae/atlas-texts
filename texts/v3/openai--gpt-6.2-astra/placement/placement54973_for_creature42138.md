I think I should live rather far back, where the turnip nets leave off and there is a strip of bare paving on which anyone who drops a needle has a fair chance of finding it. I should like the daylight at the front, but I should have a reason for going there. It would be better to have a reason. Otherwise I know how often I should put it off until tomorrow.

I should be the person who unpicks things.

It is generally possible to get one more piece of sound stuff out of a garment than the person who has worn it supposes. The best is usually under the arm, if the wearer has had a sitting occupation; with gardeners it is just the other way about. There is often a good piece under a patch. People forget what a small hole they started with. I should have a shallow bowl for the thread, and a shell with a lip on it over which I could draw the thread to take the little crooks out. There would be two rates. If you wanted the thread back you paid me in milk or something for the pot. If you did not want it back I kept it. Children would come to me for lengths with the crooks still in; drawn along the paving in front of a very young lizard, these would give both parties something to do.

I think it would be a long time before anyone brought me a hat. A coat comes to pieces willingly enough in the mind of its owner, who very likely has a use already for the pockets. But a hat that has been seen from a particular doorway every morning is another matter. There would be a shelf in most houses on which a little flat greyish thing was left undisturbed when the other things were taken down to dust.

The first would be my own. I should want a lining for my bowl; the ends slip about in it, and are very hard to pick up with a cold finger. Under the turned-in edge of the crown, where the light had never got to it, there would be four narrow lines of a quite lively pink. I should take it out to the end of the street to see. The man with the shutter would move his cup.

I suppose I should have forgotten that my hat had once been let out. There would be the holes of the first stitching well inside those of the second, and at one corner a very large knot indeed. My father was a poor hand at finishing. He could make a stitch as small as anybody's while there was still a stitch to follow it, but when he came out into the open at the end of a seam he lost confidence and put the needle through and through. I should have to sit with that knot on the table for some time. The man would ask if I wanted his flint. I should say no, not yet.

After that people would bring hats, though by no means always to have them unpicked. They would like to see the colour. One could undo a corner, turn it back about the width of a grass seed, and put it as it was. I should do this at the shutter on fine afternoons. Sometimes the grown child and the person who had made the hat would come together. They seldom agreed as to which of them had chosen the shape. There would be a woman of forty or so who, at the first sight of the pink, would put up a hand behind her ear to feel for a string which had annoyed her all the way through her sixth summer. Her mother would say, with some heat even now, that without the string she would have had to go down on the flats three times a week to get the hat.

In wet weather the man would leave the shutter at home. I should miss him on those afternoons. It would come to me, rather late, that he had cut and smoothed this handsome piece of shell for quite another purpose than to furnish me with a place of business. One evening, helping him carry it in, I should ask to see the rest.

I should like to be there when he put his house together. There would scarcely be room for the two of us in his kitchen with the walls up. We should have to stand inside the house, and his daughter, coming in with the milk, would be obliged to pass the jug to us through the round window. From inside, the shell would be pink all over. It would be the first time I had seen the shutter raised. It would fit so well that when he drew it to, the last line of kitchen light would go out all at once, and we should stand there in the lovely dark with the milk between us.

I don't know whether he would ever build it out beyond the branch. I think the next fine day I should take my bowl out as usual, but also a piece of doubled rush matting on which to set it. There is room for two or three people to work on a mat if they sit with their knees a little sideways. He might come without the shutter for once and put his feet over the edge. I should have my father's knot still in the bowl, saved whole with a short end at either side. It would make a very good fastening for the lining. All that going through and through, which used to vex me so when there was something to be let out in a hurry, would have made just the sort of button one can find with a finger without taking one's eyes off the sky.
