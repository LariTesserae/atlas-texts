I would be looking up through the water in the fork of the middle tree. The underside of a fern is a good deal paler than you would suppose from seeing it from above. There is one leaflet with the end eaten off which I know in all its conditions: lying flat on the water, clear of it, and just touching, when a little silver pocket forms under the tip. I used to be able to hang by my mouth from the underside of that pocket.

Now I have feet. For a day or two I go on swimming as before, with my new hind legs trailing like things that have caught on me. Then one evening I push against the bottom with them and am out. I sit on the very leaflet I know. From this side it is an ordinary dark green, and rather gritty. The pool is so small that from where I sit I could almost put a foot on the far shore. There is somebody down there still, going round and round, making a great business of the distance.

I would like to be one of the ones that takes two nights to get down. On the first night, a yard below the fork, I come to a cane lying against the trunk. There is a good wet crease between the two, tapering to nothing at the bottom. I go into it headfirst and stop where my sides touch. All day I stay like that. Little alterations take place in my hindquarters. When I come out backwards at dusk there is room to turn round in a place in which, yesterday, I should have had to take account of my tail.

It is on the second night that I find out about the stone. I have been hearing it all my life. Up in the water in the fork there would come a stirring that put all the sunk leaves on their edges for an instant. Here I can put a foot down and take it up and put it down again and get the stirring in just that foot. I stand with my forefeet on the shelf and my hind feet still on a flake of bark. For quite a while I stay half on the tree and half on that extraordinary moving thing. Then something minute and white runs past me and I am wholly on the shelf, with a taste in my mouth.

By the time I am brown I have a place in the bank. It is farther back than the dangling feet would lead you to think a frog could go. There is a flat pebble in there with a clean underside and just enough clearance for my back. Some mornings another frog has got in ahead of me. We do not sit companionably side by side. I work my nose under an elbow until there is a shifting of the whole damp arrangement, and in the course of this one of us puts a foot out into the light. It would be difficult even for me to say whose.

And on an evening much later I would be the female out in the grass. From within, there is very little of the solemnity you see in us. There is the vexation of a blade that has got across my eye and that travels with me for three steps before it lets go. There is a fly almost close enough. I have the near hind leg drawn up to go for it when the knocking comes, and turn with the leg still drawn up, so that I sit for a moment rather badly askew.

The water starts again. I wait. A loose husk alongside me taps against a stalk, stops, taps again. Under my belly the stone is still warm. When the next knock comes I feel it in the bare patch of skin just behind my jaw, on the left a little before the right. This time I go well out of the grass. There is a stretch of open floor ahead of me, and at its far end, level with the floor as though it too could be walked upon, a bank of cloud with the last of the light on it.

You would lose sight of me here, I think, if you were standing by the trees. There are so many small brown interruptions in the shine of the rock. But I have come close enough now to see a throat go pale and draw in again. Between the two widely planted feet in front of me a thread of water works its way over the sill. It touches one foot, then the other. I put mine down in it too.
