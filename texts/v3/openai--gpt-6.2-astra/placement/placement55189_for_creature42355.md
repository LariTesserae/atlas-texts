I think I would come down to count the birds and end up taking soundings. There is a stool in the hut for somebody who can keep a flock in his glass while calling out the number. I should be of little use on the stool. I lose the flock when it turns. But from beside the person sitting there I can see the iron cap whose white band has all but gone, and beyond it, apparently on the same level, the back on which the rail's chick stood. I should like to know the difference in height between those two places. My place would be in the bow of the little boat, with a rod that comes to pieces and a rag to clean the ferrules with.

There is a difficulty about what to put on the doorjamb. The rod has a brass shoe about the size of a shilling. In the soft places it continues to go down under its own weight while one is leaning over to read it. I would have a second shoe made out of a tobacco tin, to slip over the brass one. This gives two quite good bottoms, as much as a foot apart. The upper one will carry a sandpiper and the lower one, with luck, will carry me. In the book I would enter both. On the jamb, where there is not much room between the hinges, I would keep to the old shoe. There are thirty years of brass on the jamb.

My first summer I would put the bit of stick in the book as a station. It is convenient to have something one can pick up in the glasses from the hut. From there, with a small correction for the position of the stool, I could follow the lengthening of the back without putting the boat across it. It would be a pleasure on a wet afternoon to have a useful occupation that could be carried on with the door shut.

Then one morning the stick would be down by the apron, going round very slowly in a patch of froth. I would know it by the fork, one prong of which has been peeled clean and the other left black. Through the glasses the crossing would look extraordinarily wide and empty. I should probably spend some time in the bow trying to set the stick back where it was. It is surprising how many places out there answer quite well to a particular place seen from the hut.

I would take it home instead. That evening, from a little to one side of the stool, I would see the rail come out and make the turn. There would be the brief broadside view, the bars showing quite plainly, at the very spot where I had expected the bird to be at a loss. In the book under *stick* I would write *loose*, with the date. I wouldn't change the earlier entries. It was there then.

After a time the paint crew would get to know me. They would save the bow seat for me without being asked, and when somebody new came down from the road I would be described as the fellow who knows the way about. I should have to be careful of that. I know the way about with fourteen inches of water and another man sitting aft. When he gets out to paint a cap the stern lifts and we can go to places neither of us could reach together.

That is where I see myself most distinctly: waiting while he paints, in a boat drawing rather less than usual, with one hand round the cold iron below his brush. Across a few yards of water a rush inclines. From down here I can see what the counters miss. The stem goes over before the rail appears because one of its great toes is already on it. For a moment I have an excellent view of the toe, pale at the joints, tightening as the boat moves and sends a little wash into the bed. Then the man steps back aboard. I feel for the channel with the small end of the rod.
