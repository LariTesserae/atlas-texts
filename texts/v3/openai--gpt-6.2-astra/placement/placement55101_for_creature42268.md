I would be a little way up the blade, on the side where the ridge is coming in. At first I would belong to the handle. The rind that has closed over it is sound on top, but underneath there is a length that has gone pale from lying against the wood. When the blade tilts, some of this lifts with it. In the space of a knock its edge dries and curls over. It makes a very small eave. The powder lost by the first chip has gone in under there. That would be my first meal.

On the street side of the dividing house there are a number of eaves like this. A keeper coming up with a loaded foot used to rub off what clung to its underside before going in. There was no sense in taking street meal into a house where the meal had already been divided. What was rubbed off went under the eave and kept it supple. The street side of the house is still quite tidy, though if an edge were lifted there now it would come away in a strip. Mine lifts without breaking. For quite a while this is all there is to distinguish me.

The next piece to come down is long. It comes end over end and gets onto the blade crosswise, one end caught under my curl and the other lying on the middle prong. The prong sinks. The piece has a thin place about halfway along it, which begins to whiten.

There is a way of letting go that the wood of the scraper does not have. The wood, once it has taken a set, has to wait for the pleat under it to change thickness. I am still damp on the inside. As I take in the dust along the underside of the curl, the tucked edge opens a little, just as the pleat of a keeper used to open after it had carried something a long way. The end slips out. Down in the bed the thin place closes up dark again. If you ate the piece from that end you would come on a little crispness in the middle and then it would be soft the rest of the way through.

I should like to say I felt the other keeper receive it. What I feel is the grit coming up against me when the weight goes off the blade. The bed is quite far away. But at the third delivery the landing keeper thins its pleat before the load has quite cleared my edge. The blade settles lower; the bed, giving under the load, comes level with the blade; and for a while the smooth outer face of the middle prong lies along mine. No meal passes between us. The two surfaces fit poorly, one having taken its shape from the round of a handle and the other from years of small irregular loads. There are three places where we touch.

Between that blow and the next there is time to grow considerably at those places. By the time the prong draws back it leaves three little low spots in an otherwise fairly high rim. One is wide enough to pass a chip. The other two will pass flour. This makes a difference later on, when a piece comes down badly and the bed is needed for what is left of it. The flour goes through the two low spots and falls clear, onto the floor. At each blow a little of it slips under the skin on the far side of the landing keeper. The fold that holds up the blade begins to have something to live on besides itself.

I would be reckoned part of the scraper, I think, for as long as anyone reckoned at all. A scraper can have a back, a lip, a bed of grit, and now this edge that gives if a piece of meal pulls hard enough on it. Yet there are long intervals when the landing is quiet and the fork has finished its work, and the pleat is free to ease the blade down nearly flat. Nothing requires the middle prong to rise quite so far then. It does rise. Of the three places it has worn in me, the narrowest is by now a very good fit. In the interval we put those two parts together. It does not shake the house.
