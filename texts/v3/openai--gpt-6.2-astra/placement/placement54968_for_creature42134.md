On the old dividing dam there is a place where a sweep mark runs straight under the concrete. The floor was finished first. Whoever drew the broom there had room to take another two steps backwards, and did; the men who set up the forms for the dam stood them across the mark. I would be down there, near the square end, with my little rakes in the part of the mark that is still open.

I think I would be a hermit crab. There is a small inconvenience about living on that dam which would suit me. All the food comes to one face of it and all the good lodging to the other. On the clean face, when the works are running north, one can stand with one's feet well apart and comb things out of the water: a bit of egg skin, a thread with something soft still attached. On the shell bank the dead snails come to rest mouth up, mouth down, sometimes whole handfuls of them nested one inside another. To go from dinner to look at a house is a journey round the square end. It would take most of the slack water. I should know the way in the dark by the change underfoot, from the ridges left by the broom to the little round pits left by bubbles against the boards.

For a long time I would go about in a grey shell with the point gone. A thumb sole could lie across it quite comfortably. There would be a white patch on the high turn where the shell rubbed on the underside of a ledge, just at the place where I tucked myself away when a skate went over. To get farther in I would have to turn a little to the left. I should go on making that turn long after there was any occasion for it.

One night the woman in the launch would lose something there. She would hear the little brass cap from the vent of her petrol tin run the length of the thwart, and put her hand down flat just behind it. It would strike the dam halfway down and come to rest among the snail shells. For a few days it would be much the most conspicuous thing on the bank. I would turn it over with some difficulty. There would be a good clean lip to take hold of and inside it a promising succession of ridges. But the ridges would lead nowhere. After carrying the cap backwards for an inch or two, trying to get the purchase in it that was to be had in even a very poor snail, I would leave it mouth down. In time a little red worm would build a tube on the brass, and that worm would have more use out of the cap than anybody on the late shift had ever had.

What I was looking for would turn up quite close to home. The reversal, working at the bank, would uncover the shoulder of a whelk shell so much like the rest of the grit in colour that I would walk across it and only turn back when a foot went well down inside. It would take a deal of digging. Once free, the thing would be almost too big to manage. I would sit between the two shells with a claw on the lip of the new one, turning it by small degrees, while overhead the launch went by twice, once with the bottles and once without.

The actual removal would be very quick. For an instant there would be a pale crooked thing in the water, soft right to the end, with two small hooks on it; then nothing showing but the same old foreparts in a rather splendid doorway. I would go straight to the ledge and turn a little to the left and stick fast, with most of the whelk still outside. There would be other ledges. Down by the sweep mark the concrete had lifted a fraction from the floor, enough to get under sideways, and the front of the new house would stand square to the water there. By the first full run of the works I would have both combs out.

The grey shell would lie where I left it. In another season it would be up near the crest, light enough empty to travel in weather that scarcely disturbed the bigger pieces. A young sole coming down on it would raise a little cloud, settle, and lie with one eye just clear of the worn white patch. From the landing six rungs up there would be nothing to see but the gentle side going on getting gentler. Close to the floor there would still be that inch of clean white, made in a dark place by the same small turn, night after night, of an animal now living a few yards off.
