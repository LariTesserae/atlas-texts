I would have looked under the green slab. There is a little light in there even when the water is dirty. It comes in level with the floor and picks out the lower curve of each egg; the upper half is lost against the stone. From the doorway the male can see a whole roof of little yellow crescents. From the small opening at the back one would see, instead, the underside of his chin, very pale for such a dark fish, and the two fins working in a space in which there hardly seems room to open one.

But I think I would be some way out from the slab, down in the gravel. A pair of threads comes up there, so fine that the water lays them against the same pebble and makes one dark hair of them. At the turn they part. One is a little longer than the other. It feels well down the valley side of the pebble while the short one stays near home. When something touches the long one a wave of shortening goes down it, and for a moment there is no hair on the stone at all.

They would be my palps. The rest of me, a rather ordinary pink worm, would occupy a tube going down obliquely through the grit. There would be no great skill in the choice of the place. As a speck in the water I might have come down anywhere. But there was a crumb of shell here with a hollow under its lip, and I got into the hollow before the next wash came. The shell is somewhere well down the side of the tube now. I have built up past it, a grain or two at a time. The mouth is soft and rather beautifully made. If a shrimp treads on it, it folds flat; when the foot is gone I can push it round again from inside.

What I should know best is a length of stone rather less than the length of the fish’s chin. There is a notch in it that keeps something to eat long after the rest is swept clean. There is a rough white crystal over which it costs some trouble to draw a palp back with its load. The palp has a groove. A brown fleck coming down the groove can be seen to travel steadily towards the tube even while the palp itself is going out over the stone. On a good tide both threads are extended and there is traffic coming in on both. Most of it is poor stuff. A very small quantity of it suffices.

In the summer there would be a change in the notch. Just when the water ought to lie easy there, a faint spitting of grit begins. Three or four grains come over the rim. A bit of skin comes after them and catches; then nothing for a while; then more grit. It is the male at work under his slab. The dirty thread that leaves by the back opening comes this far before it spends itself. Without ever touching the fish, or even putting out enough of myself to cast a proper shadow in his doorway, I would have the use of what he sweeps from his house.

Once, towards the end of the sitting, one of the yellow beads comes my way. It is empty. It rolls badly, being split down one side, and lodges on the white crystal. I get a thread into it. For a little while the pull is quite astonishing; the empty skin opens in the stream like a fin, and I go down fast into the tube, taking a few loose grains in with me. By the time I come up it is gone. The young fish is gone too, a long way up the valley by now, in water that has never touched the floor.

Below, the male shifts his weight. A little sand slips off the green roof. I put the shorter thread out first. The long one, when it follows, goes straight to the notch.
