There is a little flat in the broad rail of the stair, at the turn where a person carrying a score of pads has to change shoulders. A bundle will stay there without being held. I believe I should come as far as that flat and put down whatever I had been given to carry, on the understanding that I was only going to look for a minute.

You can see the fourth door running in and out. You cannot see what Nell is doing to it. From up there it looks as though the boy were trying to get into his house and Nell, in a perfectly good temper, were preventing him. I should want to go down far enough to put this right in my mind. Then, having gone down, I should want to see whether the wheel she took off was solid brass all through or only brass on the running face. There would be the bundle to go back for. A fair part of my life in the court would be spent going back for the bundle.

I think I should keep the drawer.

It is not much of an office. The drawer is in the long bench beside the pump house, and contains the things which are too good to be thrown away and not quite good enough to have a place in the stores. There is a distinction, well understood by Nell, between a washer and a thing with a hole in it. When she asks for the latter she generally has something quite definite in view. It is advantageous at these times to have a person at the drawer who has spent an apparently useless morning putting a finger through everything in it.

The bench has its back to the pump house and its feet on the pavement. Until quite recently these were supposed to amount to the same thing. Then one morning I pulled the drawer out by its two half-moon handles and had the whole considerable weight of it in my arms. The back of the bench had gone up with the wall; the front legs, after some resistance, had gone on living where they were. I stood there with the drawer against my stomach and the blood going out of my fingers. Vale passed with a score of pads.

“Taking it somewhere?” he said.

I said I was, having found that this was so, and we took it between us to the flat on the stair. It fitted very well. For three days afterwards people coming up with pads found me sitting in their place, with a quantity of old iron in my lap. We got to know one another rather better than we had at the bench. A person obliged to stand still under a load is economical in his account of what he wants you to do.

It was on the stair that I came into possession of the small square of looking glass. It had been put in the drawer face down to protect it and taken for a piece of tin for upwards of a year. I was holding it to the light to see what sort of tin when I got a remarkably close view of the underside of my nose. After that I was always finding something that required to be looked at from the other side. There is a red line along the back of Nell's left ear where her pencil goes. The grey fliers, seen from above when they are asleep, look very little like washcloths and a great deal like purses with no money in them. I managed, by laying the glass on the top step at a particular angle, to put the figure eight from the weighbridge dial into Vale's dinner tin. These were private successes. The first useful thing I did with the glass was at the little tree.

One of the six branches had quite suddenly ceased to drip. Nell noticed it because she stood under that branch. She moved twice thinking she had got the place wrong. Then she fetched me, since by that time if a thing had an underside it was considered to be in my department. I lay down with my boots in a wheel track and held up the glass.

On the underside of the branch, exactly the colour of the wood when it is wet, a child had tied a length of brown knitting wool. It went from the branch to the trunk and from there, very neatly, down into a little white cup set among the roots. The water was travelling along it. There was already half a cupful, with a transparent fly spread out on the surface, visible by the six small hollows its feet made. Nell squatted beside me. We watched a drop go the whole length of the wool, swelling as it went, and turn into nothing at all at the lip of the cup.

“That's mine,” said a voice behind us.

It was the girl who had made the additions to the printed account. She had been trying to find out how much rain fell off one branch after the valve knocked. There were difficulties. The wool took some of it and kept it. The cup, which had come out of a set belonging to a doll, held rather less than she had hoped. Also there were people who could not see a cup of water without picking it up and tipping it out.

We put the little fly ashore with the end of the pencil from Nell's ear. Nell wanted to know how many more days the wool would be wanted there. The girl said she didn't know; so far she hadn't had the same answer twice.

By way of rent, they agreed that before the cup was emptied Nell might dip one finger in it and put the drop precisely where she wanted it. This proved unsatisfactory to Nell. It is apparently quite impossible to surprise the top of one's own head with a wet finger. It has to be a drop coming down of its own accord, and there has to be a certain amount of waiting about not knowing when it will come. The wool was moved to the next branch. The girl had to begin all over again, but did not much mind, as she had begun to have doubts about the first set of figures.

By then the bench had been shimmed up and I could go back to my place by the pump house. I should have preferred to keep the flat on the stair, but one cannot have people going about all day with a deep dent in one shoulder merely for the sake of the view. Besides, there are things to be seen at a bench which you will never see looking down the length of a court. In the brass wheel from the fourth door, for example, there is a small flaw shaped like a comma. The door has run so many times over its own length that a corresponding succession of little commas has been printed in the runner. You can find them with a fingernail. The last three grow progressively fainter. That was how I came to understand why Nell had changed the wheel that morning rather than the morning after. She had felt the wheel begin to run light. The boy, holding the door up with his hip, had been doing a little less work than he thought.

My drawer goes very sweetly now. I have had to put in a stop at the back to prevent it going too sweetly. There is a place in it for the looking glass, lined with a pad rejected on account of an interesting irregularity in its middle, and a place for the old brass wheel. Once in a while somebody asks for a wheel just that size. I hand it over with a reluctance I hope is not apparent, and generally it comes back because what was really wanted was a thing with a hole in it.

Towards the end of the early turn, if there has been rain, a small hand comes round the end of the bench and puts the doll's cup in its place behind the half-moon handle. I keep it upright there. The figures are on a strip of paper underneath, in pencil, and have to be unfolded with care. So far the smallest quantity has been seven straight pins' weight of water and the largest has been nineteen. The pins are in the drawer too. They are pretty good pins. Nobody is going to be allowed to have them.
