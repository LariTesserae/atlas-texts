I would come up on the cuff of a fitter who had been down on his elbows at the edge of the settling pond that morning. There would still be a little mud in the seam. In reaching through the grating to feel whether the nut was running hot, he would scrape some of it off. Most would go straight down the shaft. I would stop at the upturned fold, in the narrow space where the steel does not quite nip the felt.

For a while I would be rather a doubtful addition to the place. Even under the glass I would look like two grains of silt with a hair sticking to them. It would take a good soaking before the grains slid a little apart and the hair, which is a leg, bent at the knee. There would be five more legs to get clear. I would spend the first few minutes attending to them, drawing each hind foot in turn between the other feet. In the cramped space under the fold this would look like a considerable struggle to get out. In fact I would be quite well situated and would stay.

I would be one of the little round water springtails. The felt would be useful to me for much the same reason that it is useful to the rotifer, with the difference that I would have the use of the top of the water. When the bead by the washer empties, water comes nosing out between the moss leaves from below. A yellow grain lifts clear of a leaf and begins to rock; a grey scum which had dried in wrinkles draws smooth again. I can stand on that. Where my feet come down there are six small hollows, visible in a good light from almost as far away as I am. I bend my head to the scum and go forward eating, slowly enough that the hind feet sometimes come down in the hollows the forefeet have just left.

There would be one place I would frequent, off the end of a rather low leaf in the middle patch. Though sheltered from the trickle, the water there would often be moving. A flake I have got my mouth against slips sideways. I follow it half my length and it stands up on edge, too deep now for me to reach. For a little while others come along, describing much the same curve. Then the loose end of a felt fibre sags into the water and everything stops. I put one forefoot on the fibre. It holds. I walk ashore on it with no particular idea of the inconvenience I have just been spared, or caused.

Under my belly there is a fork which fits away so neatly that I could go about for days and nobody looking down on me would suspect it was there. One day the fitter puts the end of a clean rag in at the fold. The wet goes out of the felt very fast indeed. I let go the catch that holds the fork. There is a fair space of open water in front of me; nevertheless I go backwards and a little sideways, clear two of the bolt heads and come down in the spanner dent. The fitter, lifting the rag to see what colour the seepage is, misses the only really handsome thing I do.

For the rest of that day the dent would suit me. There is food at its edge and I can walk all the way round without once having to climb over a leaf. Towards evening the circuit is appreciably shorter. In one place the water lets go of the steel with a small jerk and carries me with it, leaving a curved bank of material I had not quite finished with. I can see it perfectly well from where I stand. I go up the incline to it, this time making no hollows. Among the grains is a short pleated thing on which my feeler rests for a moment. I pass over it. There is a green smear a little farther on.

I would like the dent less when it was dry. There would be a long walk back, following the white fork the water no longer takes. At the first crease I would spend some time trying to climb a face of steel that could be walked round in either direction. By the time I reached the fold the fitter would be gone and a little more light would come through the grating. One could see the three patches very distinctly then. On the middle one a drop would have collected since the rag was taken away. There would appear to be a nick in one side of it, moving round towards the back. That would be where to look for me.
