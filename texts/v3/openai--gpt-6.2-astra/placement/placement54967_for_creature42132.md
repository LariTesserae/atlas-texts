I would be under the bearer when it began to move. For six days there would have been a ceiling just clear of my tooth, pink at first, then almost colorless as the water went out of the hollow. Each time I rose I would touch the same place in it. By the sixth day I would have made a dimple. It would travel off toward the sill on the underside of a foot, one of the very few marks I had succeeded in making on anything in years.

Then the cold trickle, and the extraordinary weight of the fry. It would take two strokes to get it under. At the first I would merely turn it until it lay in the long direction of the hole. A little silver would still be showing when the water settled. At the second stroke the silver would go out as neatly as if someone had shut an eye.

I think I would be the hook. But there is the difficulty of where, in a hook of that age, to put the rest of oneself. The groove that used to hold the young is gone. The tooth has worn back past it. Down in the rock the tongue has got so thin that there is daylight through parts of it, a weak red daylight at dusk when the seam lights. There is hardly a distinction between the salt inside the animal and the salt outside. A change of weather travels through both. The one place I would know quite certainly for my own would be the bend in the tooth where the fry was lying, still giving, at long intervals, a small muscular objection to the shape of the world.

For some while I would have the use of its eye. Not to see with. An eye is a very good plug. It would lodge in the narrow end of the hollow and stop the leakage that ordinarily leaves me high and dry between strokes. All afternoon I would fish in water of my own, lifting a full measure and lowering it again. A grain of grit would go round and round in it. By dusk it would have scoured a pale circle on the wall. After so many years of fitting the hole exactly I would have a little room to turn.

Once, in a deeper place, I would have turned at every blow. The gray mouths hanging from me in winter had to be brought first to the catch and then to the young, and there was a particular half twist that kept the smallest mouth, the one nearest the tip, from slipping off. There would be no memory of the mouths now. There would be the twist. Given that much room I would perform it, and the grit would drop into a crack it had been passing over all afternoon.

Below the crack is one of the clear spaces between beds of stone. Nobody on the rim could pick it out. It has a roof as long as a winter crossing and at its deepest there is barely room for a little fish to lie on its side. There are fry there quite unlike the silver fry above. They are transparent except for two dark flaps which close over their gills at the blow. In that instant the whole school becomes visible, like a handful of tea leaves stirred through the brine. They open again and there is only stone over stone, with a grain of grit coming down between.

I would like to say I went down with it. In fact I would stay in my spoonful. The eye would soften. The old leak would start up again and the water get too low to turn in. It would be a good while before there was another catch.

What would come up the crack, though, would be the close smell of those thousands of fish. At the next knock the bearer, not yet a foot away, would stop. A row of its hind feet would lift and feel back over the gravel. One would find the hollow and the others would come round, walking the heavy plate in a half circle until it was over me again. For an hour or two the feet would sort industriously along the crack, looking for a supper that was on the other side of several feet of rock. Then the whine would begin. All along the basin the stones would sit down.

This time it would be a different pair of feet above me. The skin under both would be quite smooth. I would rise in the dark with the last shreds of the fry on my tooth and begin on the left one.
