I would be up under the sixth slab, I think, where a flake has come away from the concrete and left a ledge about the thickness of a shilling. There is nothing on it that hangs down. A fish passing underneath sees a little more roof.

At first I should know the conger chiefly by what the prawns do. They come up from the ladder and strike the ceiling quite hard, then turn and go along it on foot, upside down. One will come as far as the lip of my ledge. Its feet make six or eight experiments on what it takes for concrete. I let it get a good way on. To reach after it would be to show a prawn, which has so far met with no difficulty, a very good reason for leaving.

I should be a small octopus. There would be rather more of me on the ledge than could comfortably be put there by anybody else. I put myself away a little at a time, the soft bag going in first, the eyes last. One arm generally stays out. It goes along the underside of the slab in a leisurely fashion, attending to a limpet, the stub of a bolt, a second limpet. There is a pleasure peculiar to being built this way in coming upon the tip of one's own arm from the other direction. No time need be spent finding out what it is.

I would not have the conger's understanding of the pipes. For quite a while I should suppose that there were two congers. I have seen a head by the rusty nut; I have seen a head a good distance round the pier. Both places are to be avoided. It is only when I am out late, carrying a cockle back across the floor, that the necessity of avoiding a third place arises. There is the head under the ladder. Behind it, for once, I can see what the head belongs to. I put the cockle down. My skin goes the colour of the brick, including, in patches, the pale stuff between the bricks. The eel goes by close enough for the loose edge of my skin to lift in its wake.

After that I would do less travelling over the floor. There is a fairly good living to be had on the roof, though it looks so clean from below. The living is chiefly in the joins. Along one of these I can go from near the grille almost to the black mouths without ever having to take all my suckers off at once. I come to know the change of pump there. First the old pull lets go of the skirt between my arms. Then for a moment I can spread myself very thin indeed with no expense of holding on. In that moment things that ordinarily keep their distance from the roof come within reach of it.

One day it is a scale. It settles against a sucker, smooth on one face, finely ridged on the other. There has been a clap down by the middle pier. Under me the fish are all going one way now, except for a little group at the bars going the other. I take the scale along to the ledge. There is nothing to eat on it. For a while it lies outside among the cockle shells, catching what light there is, and might give away the whole establishment to anything that looked up.

In the next run of water it goes. My arm stays out a little longer, feeling the place where it was.
