I would be the one who takes the door in at night. I live in the last sound house beside the crossing. There is a place in my wall for a peg, left over from when the house had a front, and the door stands up against it quite well. If it is left on the river overnight it may still be there in the morning, but once it had gone a considerable way down the dry channel, and six of us had to carry it home. There had been no rain on our side of the valley.

On the evening in question I have trouble getting it up. One end comes away easily. The other makes a noise like a person eating a pear. I put my fingers underneath and find that the bank, which I have always thought of as chalk, is chalk only as far in as the first joint of a finger. After that it is mud. A piece the shape of the end of the door comes off into the water, and the fish go about among the falling crumbs with their mouths open.

In the morning I set the door a little across its old place. It rocks. I sit on the high corner until the women with the rolled hides have gone over, and then I go up to the stair to look for something to put under it.

The children are there with the bowl. The potter’s boy has put down some fresh blue and the girl has chosen an animal and told the boy not to touch it. It has been some time since either child saw it, and there is a difference of opinion as to whether it is in there at all. I suggest listening. The three of us lie down with our ears to the paving. There is a quite distinct noise which turns out to be a woman in the next enclosure grinding something for her dinner. I have heard the stone often enough standing up. From down here I can hear the little return stroke on which she takes the weight off.

The girl lifts the bowl. The animal is partway through the notch and comes up with it. For a moment it hangs there, showing a pleated yellow sole the like of which none of us has seen, for when one turns these animals over they draw in their feet. The girl sets it down with more care than she took in picking it up. The boy puts a dab of blue on the bowl at the height to which the shell has come.

What I take back to the crossing is a broken grinding seat. I have to roll it. The children come and help, not so much with the rolling as with getting everybody’s toes out of the way. We sink it in the mud, hollows down, and put the door back. It takes the weight of the three of us without stirring. The girl gets off and goes underneath to see what the fish have made of it.

I had supposed I would be the person who knew about the crossing, from having it in my keeping for so long. But it is the girl who tells me that the end we have mended was the good end for fishing, because water ran under it even when there was very little river. She comes out with chalk in her hair and one wet shoulder. There is still water going under, she says, only it goes in two places now, through the hollows for the heels.

That evening, lifting the door, I find a fish in each hollow. They are so clear that at first all I see is the green each has lately eaten, lying on the stone like two snippings of thread. I tip them back. After that I always tip the seat a little when I take the door in. It adds hardly anything to the work. Sometimes there is a fish and sometimes there is not. From my house, a little later, I can hear the girl coming down to feel in the hollows with her hand.
