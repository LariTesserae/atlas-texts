I would be the person the daughter puts a bowl down beside. She has been round the house with her father and has met all the hands except the one who is making up the beds for the stop. That is where she finds me, on my knees in the room over the wheel, shaking the pith out of a bolster. She has brought me some supper. A little broth comes over the rim onto my fingers when she puts it down. She had allowed for the table to come up and meet her.

We eat sitting on the rolled beds. You can talk up there if you sit hip to hip and put your lips to the other person's ear, but it is a poor arrangement for eating. She takes my hand instead and lays it on a place on the floor where a peg used to be. I have been catching the edge of my foot in the hole for eleven years. I had supposed it held down a board. She feels about in the pith till she finds a hard twist of it, pushes it into the hole and lets go. It stands straight up and turns slowly about, unwinding, with a ridiculous little wag at the top. It was a thing she used to do in bed when she was meant to be asleep. We make three or four of these before we finish the broth.

There is a good deal to making a bed for the stop. In ordinary running a person can sleep on quite a thin pad over the wheel. The boards are warm and the motion keeps the stuffing loose. But in the hour after the belt comes off, the running seam rises up the wheel pit and the cold comes through the axle stones. Those on the floor will draw still closer together in their sleep and the ones against the wall will wake up sore. So I take them off the floor altogether. There are hooks in the roof for the ends of the beds and holes at intervals in the walls to receive the spreaders. A dozen people can sleep in that room without any two having to agree on when to turn over. I like it best just after they have all gone to bed. Somebody has always taken a boot up with them and is trying to get it down without letting on.

I came to the house originally to sleep for a few hours. I had been fetching a woman home to the divide from a pasture that had turned half round. We could hear her dogs on the other side of a channel all the way there. It wasn't wide enough to keep a dog from swimming across; they were dogs she had made stay, and it troubled her to hear them getting farther away, still doing as she told them. I offered to fetch them. I was younger then and it seemed to me that if you knew where a thing was by its noise you were most of the way to having it in your hand. It took me three days. At the house they put us all to bed in the room over the wheel. I woke with one dog behind my knees and one at my back and the woman gone to fetch her goats. I have never slept quite so well since.

I stayed on to wash bedding and in time was given charge of the room. I know a fair number of people who have no recollection of me at all. I have had a lift hand arrive after two turns on the cable, get as far as taking hold of my sleeve to ask where she might lie down, and go to sleep with most of the question still to ask. She came back a season later and introduced herself. I knew the weight of her head.

When the machinist's daughter has finished helping me sling the beds, she tries the one in the corner. There's nothing wrong with it but she lies there a long time moving one shoulder up and down. Gradually I understand that what she wants is the floor. I take two beds down and make a double thickness of them over the boards. We have scarcely got ourselves settled when her father takes the belt off below.

The twist of pith falls over. From behind the wall comes a sound like a man running a hand through a crock of dry beans. It goes on for a bit, stops, goes on again. She sits up.

“Is he still there?”

I have been hearing it at every stop for eleven years. I should like very much to be able to say yes. Instead I take her hand and put it to the place. Presently something quite small comes up the other side of the board and scratches vigorously where the warmth of her hand is. She scratches back. We wait. In the quiet we can hear her father coming up the steps, knocking the side of one boot against each riser as he comes; he has done up the tongue of it wrong again. The thing in the wall moves off, and a good way farther along, beyond the reach of either of us, something else begins to scratch.

“There used to be just the one,” she says, and moves over to make room for him.
