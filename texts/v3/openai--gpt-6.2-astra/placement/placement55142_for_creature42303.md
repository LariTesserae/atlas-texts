I should have liked to be the person who made the door. I would have gone a long way up the mountain on the strength of that idea, and carried home six leaves from six different places, each rolled separately in a good shirt. But I know what would happen when I spread them on the kitchen table. Somebody would have to tell me which was which. I am not much good at keeping a note in my head once the sound has stopped.

I should have done better at the laundry. There was a long board there, sloping away from the window, on which the sisters turned out the pockets before they put the clothes to soak. Whatever wouldn't run down the board into a bowl had to be pushed with the side of the hand. I think that would have been my end of the business. There is a nice question, with a shirt that has come down three days by mule, whether the little twist of paper in the pocket is something the owner very particularly intended to keep or something he could find no convenient place to throw away. Generally it is best to dry it and put it back.

The sisters knew from the salt on their father's clothes where he had been. If it lay in a crescent across the seat of his trousers he had sat on the doorstep; if there was a whiteish line from armpit to cuff he had been lying out at the lip again, with one arm under his head. They were less impatient with him than the people up at the hut supposed. What vexed them was doing up the shirts. He had acquired a way of wriggling out of a shirt without undoing either wrist, and expected to get into it again by reversing the operation, which was not at all the same thing. The younger sister had twice had to go up to the notch with a needle.

One winter he sent down a shirt with something in the pocket that would neither run down the board nor come away against my hand. It was the shape of a little finger, rather less than the weight of one, and the colour, where it lay on the wood, of the wood after it had been wiped with a wet cloth. The board showed through it. I got a spoon under it at last. On the spoon it was pink and as clear as ever.

The elder sister said it was a piece of the table that had yet to come up. The younger said it was a boiled sweet that their father had carried about beyond all decency. Put it in your mouth then, said the elder. Neither would. We sent it back in the pocket with a note. In the spring it came down again, this time in a little bag with a drawstring, and with it a request that I should come up when I could be spared and show the old man how I had got it onto the spoon.

That was how I got up there after all. I set out in my ordinary shoes and borrowed a pair at the notch; they belonged to a much larger person, with a longer stride. The thin places in the soles came under my toes. It was like walking all day with the intention of sneezing.

The old man was at the lip. He had brought four spoons up, all different, and laid them on a square of plaiting. I sat down beside him and took the thing out of the bag. On the black rock it could scarcely be seen at all. I pushed at it with the largest spoon. Nothing happened. It was a long way to have brought somebody to fail to pick a thing up, and I felt very badly about it. The old man took off his coat, folded it twice and put it under my knees.

After a while I thought of the board. I told him to fetch me something that sloped.

There wasn't much choice out there. In the end he stretched out his hand with the fingers together and I tipped the bag over it; he lowered the hand from the wrist until the clear thing began to travel. It went from the heel of his hand down the old deep line in the palm and stopped at the first joint of his middle finger. I had the spoon waiting there. It came onto the spoon as easily as a poached pear.

He was delighted. He had found the thing just under the lip, he said, in a place he had been obliged to reach down to without being able to see it. There were dozens more down there. He had been trying all winter to get a good look at one. We held it up. Against the nearly black sky it was a little flat piece of darkness, with one end turned over very slightly. There was a hole in the turned end. I put the point of the drawstring through, and there it hung. Neither of us knew what it was for. He gave it to me for my trouble.

I stayed till evening to see the steps. The little figure was there, three down, in a coat that spread out behind it on the tread. I could see why the daughters had got out of patience. From up there practically anybody sitting on a step looks like somebody for whom one has been cutting up meat quite recently. I asked him if he had ever tried calling out. He said certainly not; look where she was sitting.

We kept quiet, and after a time he went to sleep. The light climbed a little higher on the underside of the lip. Where he had reached down there were indeed dozens of the clear things, hanging by their turned ends from a white fibre. Some were broad as my hand, some no bigger than the husks that come off rice. They overlapped. I lay on my stomach and looked along them, and saw the slight dip where his hand had come away with one. It reminded me of the line at the laundry when somebody has gone out in a hurry and taken in just the one pair of stockings. I slipped mine off the drawstring and hung it back. The fibre sank under the weight. Farther down, out of reach, the next one lifted a little.

I cannot say that this was a necessary piece of work. Nothing much has come of it. The sisters' father still goes up, though less often, and has taken to wearing a shirt with open sleeves which the girls made for him out of the sound parts of three others. But sometimes there is something in a pocket which I know ought to go back to the place it came from, and if nobody is making the journey I go myself. I have my own shoes now. I do not get the ache in the kitchen, or see anything remarkable in either hut. I have a good eye for the place on a crowded line where something has been taken away.

Down at the laundry they keep my spoon on a nail above the board. It is a poor spoon for eating with; one edge is worn as thin as the edge of a leaf, and lets the soup down the front of one's shirt. For getting under things without hurting them I have never found a better.
