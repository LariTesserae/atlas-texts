I would come down with the bag, I think. There is a woman on the bar who always puts her hand back into it after she has given it to me. She is feeling for her thimble. Once it went down to the basin in the thumb of a mitten and she had to turn up a hem that evening with the end of her finger in a winkle shell. It did very well until she wanted her finger out.

At first I would be in a hurry to get back. There is a time at which the people on the bar sit down to eat and it is quite an undertaking to get one of them up again. But the mender has a way of slipping her hand into a thing as soon as she takes it from the bag. She likes to have me see where her finger comes through, especially if it is somewhere other than the place I have been told about. Sometimes I have to put the thing on. I have stood in the blue lane wearing a little girl’s coat with the sleeves up round my elbows, while the woman pulled gently at a gathering across the shoulders to see how much of the child’s growing there was room to let out. There is a particular care one takes in getting out of such a coat, having seen this.

I should get to know the way in wet weather and in dry. It is not the same way. There is a narrow place quite near her seat where in dry weather you can go through sideways, drawing the bag after you by its neck. In rain the mud swells and holds the bag. For a long time I should go round the end of the drift in rain. Then one day it would occur to me to put the bag down on top of the wall and collect it from the other side. The woman, watching, would say that that was how the last fellow did it. I should be a little put out to discover there had been a last fellow. I like to think she would have the tact to wait until another day to tell me he had been twelve.

The place I would come to think of as mine is just past that narrow bit, on the side away from her seat. A valve has come loose high in the wall and turned in its bed. There is a shelf there just large enough for the bag. Under the shelf you can stand with one shoulder dry and watch the rain darken the other. It is not much of an advantage until you come to pick up a parcel and want a dry piece of yourself to hold it against.

I would be there the evening the boy stays. I would know he was staying by the weight of the bag. His mother has sent down a shirt to sleep in and a folded cloth to go under his head and more food than a person of his size could reasonably put away between one tide and the next. At the bottom there is a pear with a soft place. I should have been obliged to carry the bag in both arms most of the way, having been told about the pear after it was packed.

They have gone out already, one at either end of the bend. From my shelf I can see the boy. He is crouched down, looking so intently at the place where the bird is to come out that he does not see it come up behind him. It steps onto the lid next to his and stands there doing something quite ordinary to the feathers under its wing. There is no use calling. The lower beds are still sounding and in any case what would I call? By the time he had looked at me and I had pointed, it would be two lanes off.

I would put the bag down. I would have the odd experience of standing quite still for fear of alarming a bird much too far off to take the least account of me. Presently the boy would shift his weight, putting one hand out behind him on the lid, and turn his head at whatever he felt there. For a moment I could see his face and the bird’s feet and not the bird, which would be hidden by his shoulder. Then he would turn very carefully back to the bend.

It is this last thing I would want to ask him about. Perhaps the woman has told him to watch the bend whatever happens. Perhaps with a bird as close as that it is better not to have to do anything about seeing it. But when they come back he is full of something else. He has knelt in the mud and the knees of his trousers have stayed out in front of him when he stood up. He walks up and down to show us. There is a little pool in the top of each knee.

We would eat the pear first, on account of the soft place. Then I would go back to the bar with the empty bag tucked up under my coat. Well after dark somebody would feel bound to ask me how the boy took to being left down there for the night.

It would take me a moment to get the question right. Of the three of us who ate the pear, I was the one who had to go.
