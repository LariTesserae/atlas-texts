I would have to come down from the lip. From there I would put myself in quite the wrong place, in the dark line that goes round the hollow, as if it were something a creature could get into and follow. Down on one knee I lose the line. I can see three bright leaves with a brown leaf under them, a bent stem with a nick in it, the black space left when the vole takes its face away. It is the nick I keep coming back to. There is a piece of grass caught there that the wind ought to shake loose and doesn't.

I would be a small brown spider. My abdomen would show through the old grass on a bright day as a seed shows through the paper of a seed packet. I would live a little way out from the knob where the vole sits, in the angle between a dead stem and a live one. The dead stem has the nick in it. From that firm notch to the live stem I have put a floor of silk, much too slight to carry the piece of grass that has fallen on it. The floor sags until the grass rests on the moss. I can walk down the slope to it. For the time being I do.

What takes me some time to get right is the height. Low down, the mist does not get at the threads, and there is hardly any wind. But almost nothing arrives there except the little white things that jump out of the moss, hit the silk from below and fall straight back into the moss again. An inch higher there are flies. An inch higher is also where a leaf goes on a windy day, first its pale face and then its green face coming at me, wiping everything away. Between the two heights there is a space about as deep as my folded legs. Most days it is enough.

The vole's head goes by below the low edge. I can feel it coming some way off, but the place where it actually passes is always a little different. Sometimes a cheek rubs the dead stem without moving it. Sometimes a shoulder takes the live stem along quite a distance before letting go. Then I have a slack thread to gather in. Once a long hair from the vole stayed caught by one end and hung there, stirring, for the better part of a morning. I went down to it several times. There was a quite convincing tug from it whenever the air moved under the leaf.

There would be things that happened to me that had nothing to do with the vole. One afternoon a fly no bigger than a grass seed would come down through the upright threads onto the sloping floor. I would be underneath. For a little while we would travel together, the fly running up the slope on its six feet and I keeping pace below it on eight. At the top, where the silk is pulled tight against the notch, I would get hold of it. I would stay there feeding through the long roll from the yard and through the quiet after it. By the time I let it go it would have no more weight than the hair had.

Later, with the light still quite good, the mother would come back by a way she seldom takes, pushing the loose length of grass ahead of her breast. It would lift clear of the moss. I would lose the whole low edge at once and drop out of sight, paying out a thread as I went. Above me there would be the brief comb sound, then a much smaller rustle farther up the bank.

I would climb back up the thread. With the weight off it, what is left of the floor has drawn itself into a narrow white twist. There is still a sound fastening at the notch. I would start there again. This time I would take the first line across to the underside of the brown leaf, a shorter reach. From the lip you could watch that bit of heath all evening and see no change except that, well after the leaves around it had begun to shake in the wind off the water, one brown leaf kept still.
