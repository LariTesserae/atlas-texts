I would have a drawer in the old shelter. It would still say TRANSFERS on the front. In it I would keep a folding rule, a school looking glass with blue tape over one corner, a tin for the pencil ends, and a great many pieces of paper spoiled on the back by the damp. I think I would be the person sent down to find out how much street the city was getting back.

It would begin as quite a good job. There would be a bench up at the interchange where I could eat. Nobody would want a reading during a shower; it was the reading afterwards that mattered. A brass stud at the foot of the stair was supposed to be twenty metres from a similar stud in the sill of the tobacconist's. From the third step I could see both. I had a little instrument with two images in it, one of which could be moved by a screw until the studs lay on top of one another. Somebody upstairs had taken the trouble to find me a screw big enough to work with cold hands.

Most days the street came back to within the thickness of the pencil line. On one Thursday in June it came back eleven inches short.

I was there till nearly nine that night. I washed the glass. I went up and got the spare instrument out of its green baize case. There was a box on the form for an obstruction, so I took off my trousers and waded out to the tobacconist's to see if anything had got into the sill. The stud came away between my finger and thumb. There was a clean round place behind it and a little orange meal on my fingers. For some time I stood there in my shirt looking at what I held. Then I went back for the folding rule.

The eleven inches were quite real. The screw that had held the stud had been eleven inches long; the head had worked loose and sagged towards the stair. The street was all there. I was so pleased that I whistled, which sounded dreadful in the square. By then the light had gone out of the stair and there was no finding my trousers without going down on my hands and knees under the tree.

That was how I found the ones that did not come home big.

There were five of them on the dry side of the kerb notch. I could feel their little backs with my knee. Each had got a nail down in a pore of the concrete and there was no lifting it without hurting it. I left them alone. In the morning four had gone. The fifth was there for six days, turned away from the water, its lips so close together that a film of dust lay unbroken across the seam. I drew a ring round it in pencil so that I should not kneel on it again. People going down to look at the wall saw the ring and bent down and looked at it too.

The next shower washed the ring away. The animal stayed. When the water reached the arrow I had my eye on it instead of on the stud, and saw it put out its foot. It let go of the concrete and reached, not for the tile that was beginning to open out under the water, but for the underside of the notch. There were dozens up there. With the looking glass I could see their feet quite plainly, making and unmaking their hold, while the whole white breadth of the crossing went by them like the side of a goods train.

I could not see what they were getting until I put the glass down flat. On its face there collected, one by one, the things washed off the feeding ground: a thread of green, a roll of brown skin, something with a gold joint in it, a bit of wrapper with the blue leg of an R. All the small leavings of that immense stretch of tile came past one handspan of concrete. Under the lip the fans were going furiously. I had made a ring round the only one whose dining room I could see from the stair, and spent six days feeling sorry for it.

I would still take the readings. There is a certain pleasure in looking through a good instrument and bringing two things gently together, especially when one of them is the sill of the tobacconist's, where I used to sit with my feet not touching the pavement. But I would get into the habit of going down a little before I was due. There is room in the notch for the glass if you hold it by the taped corner. From the stair you would see a city employee lying full length with one cheek on the rough paving, apparently trying to get a look at the sole of his own shoe.

The woman with the shelter key would know. She would put my tea on the dry step and go back up without calling me. When the light was right I could tip the glass a very little and send a patch of it up under the concrete. For a moment every shell there would be pink to the hinge, and beyond the last one I could see farther into the old kerb than anyone who walked that street had ever had occasion to see. Then my wrist would tire, or a drop fall on the glass. I would put it back in the transfers drawer with a thumbprint on its face and go and see whether we had all our distance.
