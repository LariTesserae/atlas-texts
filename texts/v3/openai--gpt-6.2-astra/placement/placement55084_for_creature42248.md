I think I would keep the photographs. There would be a little pencil work to do on the backs before they went up. The date, the height of the water at the intake, whether the dark on the far slope was cloud shadow or wet ground. On a fine afternoon, with the hut door hooked open, I would turn one over to write on it and have to put my fingers on the corners to keep it flat. From that position I could see the man coming back. Long after I had cleared a place for his mug he would still be coming back.

One day he would empty a pocket on the table. A nut, the short pencil he uses on the grease chart, and some greyish stuff with a clean streak through it where he has opened it with his thumb. He would put the nut and the pencil back. We could speak quite comfortably if I shut the door.

I would want to know which sleeper. He would have to think. He knows the place by the way he sets his feet to cross it, and there are two stakes quite close together there, one a replacement for the other, with the number gone off both. In the end we would go out.

The old grass under the timber is full of water. If I press it down with the toe of my boot there is a little rush of clear water at the other end, followed some time later by a brown one. There is no finding the shape of a passage in it. A pace or two uphill, though, in the lee of a willow stem, I can put my finger into a round hole. I take it out again. There is a small warmth there which may only be the absence of the wind.

What I would like to put on the back of the photograph is the distance from that hole to the old place. It would make quite a good measurement. The difficulty is that neither of us saw her go in. The man, down on one knee again, lays his lace out to show where she set the young one, and from there he can give me a direction for about eighteen inches. After that the willow leaves closed. We leave it at eighteen inches. He does up his boot with two hard outward pulls and goes off across the pass, getting smaller very slowly.

Back in the hut I get a square of paper and fold it up over the grass. A little of the black comes off on the fold. For a while I consider pinning the packet beside the print, down in the corner where the track runs out of the picture. But it came from the screen. There is no telling how far it went in the water or even whether it is hers. I put it loose in the drawer with the spare pins.

I would be the person who, years afterwards, could open that drawer for a new hand looking for a pin and say, wait a bit, let me move this. By then the paper would be yellow at the folds. We would open it on the table and the grass, released from the weight of the paper, would stand up a little along the groove his thumb had made. On the wall above it there would be a whole row of summers in which you could see nothing at all going on at that sleeper.
