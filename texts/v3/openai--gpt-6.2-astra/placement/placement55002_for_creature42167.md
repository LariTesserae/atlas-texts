I would be the one bringing the lid. I live in the next kitchen, and happen to be coming up with my hands empty when the woman hears the baby wake. She puts it into my hands. The little girl has already gone round the turn. For a moment I can see the milk going down the stairs by itself, a white oval moving along below the rail. Then I get round the turn too and there is the top of her head.

The crock is heavier than she expected. She has brought it up against her stomach after all. At the step her grandfather named she looks for a dry place to put her knee, and finding none, stands a little longer with the crock in her arms.

“Sit on the one above,” I say.

She does, rather suddenly. Some of the milk comes over onto her skirt. But now the bottom of the crock rests on the step between her feet and she has only to get her hands round to the back of it to push it in. The water divides at the foot of the crock and goes on down either side. I give her the lid. We turn it a little until it stops rocking.

My own work is down here. I take the measures for the man who makes the wooden things that have to fit against stone. He works at the upper end, where there is a yard in which a length of timber can be turned without putting one end through somebody’s window. If it is a square thing he wants, he can generally trust the householder to bring him the size of it. What he sends me for are the shapes people have difficulty carrying in their heads. The back of a shelf, for instance, which is to go against a wall with a belly in it. I take little slips of wood and lay them with their ends against the wall, and tack another slip across them. It looks like a poor sort of comb. He lays it on his board in the yard and makes a dot at the end of every tooth. That way a shelf can go into a kitchen a morning’s walk from the yard and not have a gap at the back big enough to lose a spoon down.

The old man taught me that. He used to do it for steps, though the slips for a stone step had to be stout enough to take to the quarry and back. When he first let me go for a measure on my own I brought it back beautifully true, with nothing to show which face went up. He made the step in the end by walking over and looking at the house. He has never mentioned it to anybody, so far as I know. To this day I put a thumbnail cut in the upper face, even of a thing I am only taking into the next room.

The little girl wants to stay and see whether the milk will come over the edge again now that she has let go of it. I tell her it won’t. We stay a bit nevertheless. There is a curved white run on the brown crock. Where it reaches the water it draws out into a thread that goes a surprising distance down the stair.

From below us comes the voice of the older girl. She has found something by the pipe hole and wants her sister to come. We go down together, the little one stepping in the places where I have stepped until we reach the broad stone where there is room abreast. What the older one has found is a spot on the ceiling of the bread room. The light from the pipe hole strikes the risen water and goes up under the floor on which we are sitting. If you put your finger in the hole and stir, the spot breaks to pieces. You have to put your cheek right down to see it. They make room for me.

I know this particular stone from both sides. I was the one who took the shape of it for a wooden cover when the pipe first came out. The cover was made, a neat little plug with a shoulder so that it couldn’t fall through. For some months it did very well. Then somebody wanted a foot for the chest with the drawer that sticks, and it proved to be just the thickness. I can see it from my place at supper if the drawer is out and the lamp is standing low. There is my little cut in the wood, the right way up.

The water is cold against the tip of my finger. I give it a stir for the girls and then they give it a stir for me. What I had taken for a spot of bare plaster is a whole patch of small hollows left by the ends of the laths when the ceiling was mended. They come out one by one as the light runs over them. Somewhere above, the woman has got the baby quiet. We can hear the old man drawing the wool along his board again.

By the time we go up, the white run has washed off the crock as far as the water can reach. The little girl stoops to feel that the lid is still on properly. It is; she had it right the first time. On the turn she puts her damp hand into mine, quite casually, as people do on these stairs when there is no rail on the side they happen to be taking. My kitchen is the first one we come to, but I go the few steps farther with her.
