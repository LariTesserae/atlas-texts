I should have been on the bench a good while before anybody thought of a use for me. From there it is easy to mistake the opening of a door for the end of the old woman's performance. In fact it is generally the beginning. She goes in with perhaps nine whistles in the pocket of her apron and comes out with none. Each person who has heard a whistle from the bank wants to know what it sounds like from the other end, with one's own teeth in the way. It is quite a different thing. A note which made scarcely any impression in the air will go down through the jaw and set a particularly tender spot in the elbow tingling.

I would be one of the larger people, with a rather ordinary ear but an excellent elbow. At the end of my first afternoon the old woman would give me a flat whistle with a brown thread running through it and tell me to come back when it was dry.

It took three days. I had it on the bench beside me, on the dry part, under a saucer turned upside down. When I lifted the saucer the whistle gave a little jump. It had been trying to unwind all that time. One end now stood up like the handle of a cup. I took it across to her in the hollow of my hand, being careful not to press it down again.

She was at her light door rubbing yellow stuff on a boy's shoulder. He had been carrying a beetle there and the beetle had taken hold in order not to fall off. She finished the shoulder, wiped her fingers on the boy's hair, with his leave, and put the whistle to her mouth. There was a sound such as a melon makes if you slap it in the right place. The boy came back. She tried again. This time there was only the familiar damp puff; the raised end had settled against her lip.

“Do you know the way across in the dark?” she asked me.

I said I thought I did.

“Then you might take them home in the evening. They get sat on here.”

That would be my occupation. I should be the person with room for things to dry. In the long rooms there is wonderfully little dust but a great deal of passing, and anything laid down in advance of the household has eventually to be combed over or eaten off or slept on. At the landing there is the whole underside of the roof. The basket man made me a set of very shallow hanging trays. On still days their shadows on the bench were fine enough that people tried to brush them away before sitting down.

For a while I took pains to remember which whistle had made which sound. I notched the edges of the trays and had a scheme of my own involving knots in a bit of cord. The old woman took a courteous interest in the knots. One evening she brought me a whistle she said she would like to hear again, and I promised to put it where I could find it. It was a good one, short and thick, with a sort of stammer before the note came clear. In the morning I took it straight back to her. She blew on it. It made a very fair imitation of a fish basket being dragged over a sill.

I was more put out than she was. I said I was sure it was the same whistle.

“Well, I should hope so,” she said. “I haven't time to make them all twice.”

What she liked about my roof was that it did something to the whistles which neither she nor the roof had quite the means to do alone. The tying of knots gradually fell into disuse, though the cord was kept. A little girl had discovered that if you drew it slowly through your closed hand you could feel every knot quite distinctly and yet have no idea how many there had been. She used to take it to bed with her and in the morning bring it home the long way, trailing behind her in the runnel, with the fish coming up to nose each knot as it went by.

In time I got so that I could walk across in real darkness, which is different from finding one's way while there is still light on the tiles. There is a place halfway over where the runnel doubles so closely on itself that the strip between the two waters is only just the breadth of my foot. I used to go sideways there. One night I found the old woman sitting on the strip with her feet in one bend and her apron floating in the other. I sat down too, on the broad side. She had left the whistles at home. From somewhere quite close came the stammer and then the clear note of the short thick whistle as I remembered it.

I was about to say something when it came again, rather further off. There were two boys under the rim, going home from a visit. One had spent a good part of the day learning to do the noise against the back of his teeth. He was teaching the other. We heard them stop at the difficulty, go back, get past it by accident, and start again. The old woman had got her apron out of the water and was wringing the end of it without making a splash.

I think that is where I would most often be found, though my trays and my roof and what little reputation I possessed would all be at the landing. There is space on the broad side of that turn for a large person to sit without putting either foot through anybody's ceiling. The little people passing below cannot see who is there. Sometimes somebody practices something the whole length of the bend in the belief that he is alone. Sometimes a head comes out at my knee and asks me to do the loud part.
