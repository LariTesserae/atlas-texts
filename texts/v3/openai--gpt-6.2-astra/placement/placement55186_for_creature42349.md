I would be the one they sent for when a door began to stick. There would be a bench for me in the freight passage, just short of the place where the trolley has to turn. I would have to keep the last foot of it clear for parcels. In December I would have very little bench.

The first thing to do with a sticking door is ask the person who uses it to open it. If you go straight at it yourself you can miss the small business with the knee that has kept it working for eleven years. Often enough the remedy is a longer screw in the top hinge. Once it was a coat that had hung on the back of a door for so long, with such a quantity of small change in the pocket, that taking the coat to the cleaner had put the latch out of line.

I should like the work for the number of rooms it would give me a reason to go into. From one kitchen in particular there would be a view of the bay I could get nowhere else. The woman there would know perfectly well why I was taking so long putting the tools away.

The lowest door would come to me in the ordinary way, on a slip left under my plane. *Catches coming in with weight.* Emil’s daughter would have written it. I would take the empty trolley outside and come back in quite easily, and then have to wait for her to finish her round to find out what she meant. Coming in with weight, she put her bottom against the door and walked backwards. The front wheels went light over the sill. It was when the back wheels followed that something gave a little squeal underneath.

We would try it with me on the trolley. There are people who would remember seeing me come through that door sitting on a sack of onions long after they had forgotten what I did for a living.

It would be the sill rather than the door. Taking it up would disclose a little run in the brown earth, polished along the bottom, with parsley roots hanging into it. One end would go seaward and the other straight under the mailboxes. There would be no animal in view. Emil’s daughter would fetch her father a chair on the grounds that if she didn’t he would stand there anyway.

What I would want, about then, would be somebody to tell me how wide a mole was with its elbows out. Emil would say he had only ever seen one going away from him and another one practically head on, neither under circumstances in which it had seemed a good idea to take a measurement. We would have to do the best we could. I would make a sill that rested on the sound paving to either side, with a hollow cut across its underside and a strip of brass on top to take the wheels. While I worked, Emil would sit in the entrance with one trouser leg tucked into his sock. People going out would promise to look for whatever he had lost.

I don’t think I would see the mole. I think I would start to tuck a note under the sill, giving the measurements and the reason for the hollow, and then consider the poor chances of paper in that situation. In the end I would take a pencil and write on the inside of the lowest mailbox door: *Before lifting sill see me, freight passage.* I would sign my name. For as long as I worked there this would be very useful. After that it would be one of the things in the ward that a person might open a little green door and find.

There would be one last trial with the trolley loaded. Emil would stay in his chair and his daughter would go out and come backwards in. I would be down on my hands and knees with a finger resting where brass met stone, feeling for the least bit of play. Just as the wheels came over, a hand would settle on the back of my collar and pull me clear. I would have forgotten, at the last minute, the turn the trolley had to make.
