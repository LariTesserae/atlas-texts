I would have taken it for a chip out of the shell. When the notch comes round again there is a little pulse of brown just behind it, like the movement in the keeper when the mill shuts. It is the end of my foot that has been going round. My head is tucked so far down against it that for some time the two ends have to make a turn together. The other notch belongs to the egg. I get a purchase there with my toes.

There is room at last to straighten. The keeper on the low side of the drop takes in both wheels when I come out, though there is a good deal of water between us. A piece of the egg rolls down past it. I stay where I am, with one toe in the shell and one on the grass. There is a difference. The shell gives a little wherever I push it, whereas the grass has a ridge too high for me to feel over without letting go with one foot. My first meal comes along that ridge. It is a row of yellow specks, all going at the same pace until I put my head out. Then the middle of the row comes toward me and the two ends go on down the grass.

By late Sunday there is a skin on the drop. I can push it up from below. A springtail sets a foot on it from above, and a round hollow comes down into my part of the water, with a dark claw at the bottom. I take my wheels in. When I put them out again the claw is three grass ridges away and the hollow is still travelling after it. At the low end the keeper is milling steadily. The few specks that get past it come up the far side, so worn-looking after the journey that I let them pass the first time. One comes round twice and proves quite good.

I should have stayed there had it been a matter of choosing a place with food in it. But the grass is lifting. It lifts by such small degrees that nothing moves until, at one lift no bigger than the others, nearly all the water goes to the root. I go with it. The old keeper stays behind in a bright button over the nick. For a little way I can see the red grain. Then the blade turns edge on.

At the root the water has hold of a thread of the packing string. Most of the twist lies up in the bank in the dry, but this one thread has come loose and makes a long wet loop with both ends going into the same knot. It is a good place for something with two toes. I can take hold on opposite sides of a fibre and let my head out as far as it will go. Little things knocked off the string by the springtails come down the loop. Some are too big. For these I have only to draw in one wheel; they brush along my side and stick in the fork below my foot. By dark there is quite a tuft there, and the water, which used to go both ways round the fork, goes mostly my way.

On Monday the first cough of the works shakes the bank before there is any sun in the hole. The string pulls tight. One side of my loop is laid against a sliver of green glass, and at the next cough that side stays still while the other rubs past it. A single clear bead is wrung out of the knot. For a while I have water over the whole of my back again. It happens twice more that morning. The third bead is so small that the tuft at my foot takes it all.

That is where you would find me on Thursday, some way up from the place where you would think to look for a keeper. The string is grey except for one short length that is nearly black. One end of me has dried to the glass; the other is in among the fibres with the wheels put away. A springtail grazing down the twist comes to the black length, puts its mouth to it and goes on. There is a mark left by its mouth, quite round and shallow. It lasts until Sunday. Then the water comes up the thread from the grass root and fills that mark first, and I have a little cupful against my head before the rest of me is wet.
