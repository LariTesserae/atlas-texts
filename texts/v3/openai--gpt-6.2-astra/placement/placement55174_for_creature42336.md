I think I would be in the near wall, the one with the collecting tray. Quite a lot of me would be in the back of the tray, where there is a long dent from having once been folded away with a chip of gravel still in it. The chip came out years ago. When the tray is empty and the rest of the wall has taken the temperature of the water, there is still a little cold place at either end of the dent. Between those places I should know I was at home.

I would not have seen the fourth light. There is a lip to the shelf which cuts off the view from the wall at about the height of the glass. What I should have seen, the first evening, was the underside of my own tray lighting up twice. A very poor blue, on account of the deposit there. I put the tray away to see where the light fell next.

Nowhere, as it happened. I waited. By and by I put the tray out and there it was again.

For several evenings that was as much as we knew of one another. During the day I had the white dust to collect. It must not be let down into the six lower streets, where there is no great wash of water and a surprisingly small quantity will keep a shutter from going back into its slot. I took it round the square a trayful at a time. A person looking very closely would have seen a succession of sills acquire a white edge and come clean again. On coming home I generally found a little dust left in the dent. I got it out with a short, sharp tilt to one side. I did not do this after the lights came on. There was nobody else who would have noticed the distinction.

Then one evening I came back rather later than usual and found the underside of the tray quite dark. Down toward the hinge there was a narrow blue line. I tried folding the tray. The line stayed where it was.

A new joint had opened in my wall. In the afternoon, while I was round the square, the hollow under the fourth and fifth beads had got a little longer. It ran up behind the hinge and ended against an old red paving block which had been used, for economy, on its side. Now the light was coming through the joint from behind. I put a little weight on the block and felt, a considerable way down, two pieces of glass tip apart. I took the weight off. They came together again. It was the first time I had done anything down there. I had supposed all along that the young one was making some progress toward my wall; it had not occurred to me that my wall was making as much progress toward the young one.

I went down a little farther the following evening. It was very pleasant in the hollow. No dust could lodge in it yet. The stone was so new that one could let an edge down anywhere and take it up again without that slight reluctance there is in old work to part on a line other than the usual one. I could have gone on to the fifth hole. Instead I stayed at the fourth and did my short, sharp tilt. Above us, at the edge of the square, my tray emptied the last pinch of its load. Down here a single grain came free of the ceiling. We waited an extraordinary time for it to go past the blue glass. It was pink on one side.

I had almost forgotten the gate. I got back to the wall just in time to take my share. It was a heavy opening. The red block went over flat, the tray folded so far in that the two ends of the dent touched, and for a while there was no hollow under the hinge at all.

When the weight came off, the street on the shelf had its first turning. It went round the red block. From the tray one could now see the fifth bead quite plainly and the fourth not at all. That evening the fifth shone steadily. At the fourth hole there was a good deal going on that I could not see, and presently a grain of white stone came up through the hinge and settled in the dent.

I left it there. In the morning, taking the dust round, I felt my usual tilt made for me, rather too vigorously, three sills ahead. Some of the load went down into a lower street. We spent the better part of the day getting it out. There is a shutter down there which still comes open with a little white eyebrow.

That is where I would look for myself now, if the tray were empty: three sills apart on the less well swept side of the square, doing a small piece of work in two rather different ways. At home there would be the grain in the dent. It rolls to one end when I come in from the square and to the other when somebody comes up from below. Sometimes, in perfectly still water, it goes once each way.
