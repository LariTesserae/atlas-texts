I would be on the first cream tile, a little way in from the sill. In the evening it would look as if the longer thread had got a neighbor. There would be the green line, and there, on the side away from the bath, a line of much the same fineness, but of no green at all. If the keeper put a fingertip down to feel for the second one, I would go up his finger as far as the first joint. He would feel only the tile.

My length would be wrong nearly all day. A wash of light off a moving heel could stretch me halfway across the tile without the thread having grown the least bit. At noon a whole new length of green could hang out beyond the lip and leave no account of itself inside. Evening would be the good time to see me. The water would have to be quiet and the loop lying almost down. Then even the rather blunt shape of the patch where the thread held on would be legible in me. There would be no interval at that end. The hair’s breadth of bright tile between us would begin a little farther along, where the green lifted clear.

Once, when the loop had come off its catch, a grain of lime would settle under the lifted part. I would lie across it. It would be a considerable hill to me, white on both sides with a little dark saddle over the crown. Next afternoon the grain would go out with a bucketful. I would come down on the flat tile that evening as though there had never been a hill there.

It would take a number of evenings to tell that I too was getting longer. One would have to wait for the plug to go in, for the last uneasy drawing of water along the sill to cease, and then for the sun to reach just the old height. There is a brown pinprick in the glaze one could go by. At first there would be cream between the pinprick and my end. Then one evening the little brown point would be in me, showing through quite plainly. Out in the bath, where the green was so fine and the water so big, the new growth would have been hard to see.

I would have no business farther in. The red chip has a shade of its own, close and ruddy underneath it; the whorls at the back have theirs for a few minutes at the close of day. Mine would be the place where the sill begins to be a floor. After the light went I would leave nothing lying there to be washed away in the morning. Only the two green threads would remain, one of them long enough now to account for that brown point.
