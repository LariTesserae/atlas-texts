I would be at the rough inch where two grooves meet. For the first part of my life I would take it for granted that everybody had such an inch beside their bed. Our mat would be a little below the road, so that a hand going past in the groove would be just above my head. I could lie awake and hear people fail to be strangers. There would be the slight drawing in of breath in anticipation of the bad place, followed by the beautifully careless passage of the fingers over it.

My mother would make the route tablets. There is less call for a new tablet than one might suppose. A groove is a great expense of labour, and a settlement, before undertaking one, will put up for generations with a very inconvenient way of going round. Most of the work consists in replacing tablets that have been dropped. A broken tablet is brought in on its cord, the pieces threaded through the holes if there are holes enough, the small bits otherwise done up in a twist of skin. My mother can put one back together on a slab of wet clay and read it almost as well as if it were whole. There is generally a piece missing. It tends to be from the easy part, which is thin.

My job at first is to take the new one down to the recess and sit with it. A maker in her own house will turn a tablet this way and that, holding the clay clear of the slab on the tips of her fingers. It is quite another matter to read it with an elbow in a cup and the weight of a water jar still pulling on one shoulder. I have small hands for one of our people. If I can reach a turn with the cord at full stretch, almost anyone can. If I have to put down the jar, it goes back to my mother.

In time I would come to be good at something my mother finds difficult. She has walked almost every road for which she has made a tablet. Given a very slight indication of a bad bit of floor, her foot already knows what to do there, and her reading finger goes on without asking very much of the clay. I have been almost nowhere. I can get quite lost on a tablet of hers while sitting within reach of the food slab. At first this causes some irritation on both sides. Then one day, halfway through telling me what ought to be perfectly plain, she takes the tablet away and pinches up a little more clay on the inside of a turn. I get through on the next attempt. After that my mother has the sense not to tell me about a road until I have had a go at it cold.

People from other settlements would bring me work. There would be a time in my life when I knew how to get almost everywhere, provided I stayed on the mat. I would have a particular affection for a certain crossing of blades, which calls for a most satisfying turn of the thumb at the end of it. On eventually coming to the place itself I would find that I had to sit down and go over on my bottom, with my legs out in front of me and somebody on the far side taking charge of my cakes. I would ask, rather crossly, why there was nothing about this on the tablet. The person with my cakes would say that most people just stepped across.

It is my mother who would take me to the house. She has been once, as a girl, and has never had any particular wish to go again. What she wants to see is what I shall make of a way that no one has put into clay. We leave the groove and for a long while there are quite sufficient things to go by. There is a place where our soles pick up grit and a place a little farther on where they shed it with a sound like somebody salting a dish. I am putting these things away in order. Then we come to the smooth floor. My mother puts out her hand and I take it.

In all the years of living on her mat I have scarcely had hold of her working hand. There is a thick pad on the side of the last joint of her first finger, with a little straight dent in it. I know that dent. It leaves a fine low bar across every deep turn she makes in clay. I have been lifting my finger over it since I was small, without once supposing it to be a feature of the country.

I don't think I would be particularly good at hearing the note. My mother would get up from the bench, well pleased, and I would sit for much too long afterwards, trying the heel and then the outside edge of the heel, much as one tries different parts of a tablet for a way on. In the end she would come and sit beside me. The bench has room for two if neither insists on the middle. She would tap once for me and there it would be, a sound with room inside it, quite gone by the time I had made out what was agreeable about it.

On the way back she would give me the lead as soon as we came to the grit. I would be all right from there. We would come home with a good deal to say about the journey and very little about the house.

I would go on making tablets after her. For a while people would bring them back to me with a complaint about the turns: perfectly legible, they would say, but there used to be a little something there to let the finger rest against before going on. It would take me one or two spoiled pieces to find how best to put it in. My own finger makes no such mark. I would use the blunt end of the pin we use for clearing the cord holes, laid almost flat while the clay is still soft. It is a very small addition. A person sitting down tired, with a full jar, can stop there, change shoulders, and find the place again.
