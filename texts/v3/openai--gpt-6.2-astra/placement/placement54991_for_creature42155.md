I would be the one who thaws the ropes. I would live at the uphill end of the bath, where the flue takes a turn round a lump of green stone that nobody could get out. There is room in the turn for a bed, provided the sleeper is prepared to get into it feet first. One of my feet would be a good deal warmer than the other. I think after a few years I would be unable to sleep any other way.

In my cupboard there would be six lengths of spare rope and a shallow pan of the wet sand they rake out of the hot end of the bath. A rope frozen into its eye can be got free by pulling, but it leaves a little beard of fibres in the ice. By spring it will pass for a sound rope in a poor light and come in two over the knee of a baby. I would carry the sand in a bag of doubled hide and fold it over the eye and sit there. There would be a great deal of sitting there. I would know who sings. Not at a feast, where practically everybody does, but faced with a small necessary job and much more time than is needed for it. The woman with the forked pole has one tune for the first six steps and another for the seventh, which has to be lifted rather than pushed.

I would have been brought to the notch full grown. There would still be a visitor’s spoon in my cupboard, rubbed thin at the lip. At first, not knowing how far a voice went through a roof, I would have made the mistake of answering questions. A man quite distinctly asked me once if I meant to lie like that all day. He had his hand in a nest box and was addressing a hen. I had got as far as one boot.

The morning the boy is to have his ride, I am outside his father’s house with the sand. It is a disagreeable place to work. One sits astride a ridge with one leg in the smell of fish broth and the other in a wind that comes all the way from the pass. The girl is already there. The boy has woken early and dressed himself, and there has been some delay while his father gets him undressed again and puts the long things on first. A hand comes out to feel the day. She takes it. Then the boy comes out by the door a little way down the roof, and for a moment she has both the boy at the door and the boy she is holding by the hand. She drops his father’s hand. He goes back to whatever he was doing without remark.

They have allowed a good deal too much for the boy lengthwise and not enough for his boots. My work being finished, I offer to mind the boots. He gives me a look. Quite right too. He has got himself out into the world at last and is hardly going to begin by parting with his boots. In the end they sit back to back with their legs hanging off the sides. They go broadside down the slope at about the speed of a platter being passed very carefully through a wall.

It is a way of travelling that has something to recommend it. At the first gap the girl does not have to turn round to take the empty basket, and at the second the boy, without having been told anything, leans the other way to take its weight. By the third gap they are going along quite briskly. I pick up the bag of sand. There is a tiny bare footprint in it where something, finding it warm, has stood on it with just one foot. I stand a little longer in the fish broth and the wind, waiting to see if whatever it was will come back for the other foot.

In the afternoon the girl finds me in the turn of the flue, taking the stiff parts out of an old line. She has a difficulty. The boy considers the ride a fair exchange for his tooth, but he has now done half a morning’s carrying. There is an account open again, and on the wrong side. She has brought my share of the catch up with her. While she is explaining the difficulty her hand goes into the basket and comes out with a crisp tail. I put my hand in and take one too. By the time we have got to the bottom neither of us has thought of an answer, but it appears she owes me half a supper. This cheers her considerably. She knows several ways round a difficulty once there are three people in it.

Long after that I would be known as the person to whom the girl owes half a fish supper. Strangers wanting a rope seen to would ask for me by that name. One of them, putting his head in at the turn of the flue, would find an old woman lying on her back with her boots on a shelf above her head, darning the warm heel of a stocking with yarn unravelled from the cold one. That would be me. There is a spoon on the wall beside the bed, within reach without sitting up. If you tap with it very lightly on the green stone, the boy, who by then tends the fire, will turn a little more heat into the flue. Sometimes I do it in my sleep. Sometimes, out of long acquaintance with my feet, he does it first.
