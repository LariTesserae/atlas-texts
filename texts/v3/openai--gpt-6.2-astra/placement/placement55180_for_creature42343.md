I would be in the laundry loft, though not at the window. There is a place behind the woman who hems the sheets where a child can sit with a board across its knees. I would be that child. My job would be to put my hand on the pile of finished work when the engines start. Until you have seen a stack of folded linen go over in a draft this seems a job invented to keep a child out of the way.

From the floor there is very little view. I can see the mains and a piece of vine that hangs below them, and I know the opposite loft has a green sill because sometimes a little green light comes through the sheet. What I chiefly see is the underside of the work. The needle comes down with a little tent of cloth on its point. This collapses; the point goes away; a loop narrows so fast that I have never been able to put a finger through it, though there is one scar on my knuckle from trying. Up here the first news of the river is the woman putting the needle in her mouth and flattening the work with both palms. She feels the belt through the treadle of the idle sewing machine. My hand goes on the pile a moment after hers goes on the hem.

On a slack afternoon she lets me take the sweepings down. There are pins in them. I sort them on the bench, the good ones on my right, the ones that will need rolling under a flatiron on my left. The crooked ones always roll in a curve and come back to me. Once one goes over the edge and in looking for it I put my ear to the stone. It is a great surprise. The noise that fills the square is much less loud in there. What I hear instead is the loose foot, which gives two knocks very close together, like somebody being polite at a door.

For several days I am occupied with the idea of mending this. I bring down scraps of selvage. Folded four times, a piece will just go under the foot if I get somebody heavier than myself to sit at the other end. For an afternoon people sit in peace. Then the river comes up and the cloth works out, very dark and flat, and lies across the mouth of the drain like the strap of a lost shoe. I fish it out. There is a smell in it quite unlike the smell of wet washing. I spread it on the brass shelf to dry.

This would be my nearest approach to the shrimp. For part of an afternoon there would have been, between its ceiling and one foot of a considerable weight of stone, something soft that I had put there. Whether the difference reached it I have no way of knowing. I would be much more interested in the mark left on the cloth: a black oblong with a little bite out of one corner. I had supposed the bench had four sound feet and the fault was in the paving.

I take the cloth upstairs. The woman holds it to the light and turns it over; on the clean side the mark is four pale oblongs, one inside another, each a little smaller where the damp has failed to come quite through. She shows me how to fold it again so that the thickest part will be under the broken corner. Before I can go down to try it we hear the slap. She puts her hands on the hem and I put mine on the pile. This time, being curious, I leave the top sheet free. It lifts at the corners first. Then a wide shallow hollow travels under my palm, from the heel of my hand all the way to the roots of my fingers, where I let it out.
