I would be under the last tooth, on the side with the number. In particular I would be on the ceiling of the little space between the tooth and the shoulder from which it stands out. A bare foot will fit into that space as far as the toes. I would know a foot had been there by the warmth left in the iron. There is enough traffic at this tooth to make small distinctions worth attending to: a foot stays warm a long time; a walker leaves eight places out of which the warmth goes almost at once.

I would come out after the walkers had fallen off. I picture something at first rather like a loose black eyelash on the number. It puts one end down in the cut that makes the upright of the numeral, and draws itself through. In going through it becomes considerably wider. What looked like a thread was the edge of a very thin body held straight out from the iron. Laid down flat it covers most of the number. You can see the number through it, a little enlarged, except where some old grains of yellow have collected in its stomach.

Those grains would be what I was after. The tooth sheds its substance very slowly, and the walkers, in getting a purchase on the wall, lift scales too small to fall of their own weight. I could get a tongue under a lifted scale. I would work it to and fro until the scale came off, leaving a bright place with the shape and sometimes the whorls of the foot that lifted it. Seen by the light from the floor, the underside of the tooth would be covered in little gilt feet. No one looking down on a walker would know what a handsome sole it had.

I think I would live long enough to eat the bottom of one stroke of the number. There would come a morning when my tongue went out along its usual groove and onto the plain iron without having to lift. For some time I would go on lifting it there. Possibly this is as near as I would come to remembering a thing.

But the tooth is a long way from the next piece of iron that still stands out in the air. How would I have got there? Not on the wind. A body with so much wet surface to so little inside cannot comfortably let go of anything. Even the passage across a grain of powder is accomplished by flowing down it on one side before letting go on the other. I think the black eyelash is only the part of me that happens to live above ground.

Below the tooth the metal is continuous for miles. The great wheel is still there, though most of the spaces between its teeth are filled and shuttered over. Lower still, where the axle crosses an upright floor, there is a sheltered corner into which the morning of the cool country shines every evening. Here a considerable quantity of me would be laid out flat. At that depth there is no need to stand on edge against the drying of the air. I would be pale yellow and large enough for a company of walkers to travel over me without getting their back feet off before their front feet were well on. What they took for a small failure of the film to come away under the lip would be my back.

There would be no call to know about the number down there, or about the other morning up here. What could pass from one place to the other would be a pull. A scale comes loose under my tongue; the narrow part of me running down the root of the tooth gives a little, and a fold a long way down can open. In the slant light the opened fold is nearly clear. The first walker sets its forefeet on the new strip. After a while there is the weight of the hind feet too.

On the underside of the tooth, in the already dark basin, I put my tongue out again. This time there is nothing loose to eat. I leave it there a while. Something is going over me in daylight, and until it has passed I cannot quite draw it in.
