I would come out of the yard badly. One heel would be ready for walking a good while before the other. I would sit on the step eating the little cups and putting the troublesome foot back into the water as soon as the person sitting with me had occasion to look away. This would give me a long acquaintance with the step. There is a hollow in the edge of it, made by generations of heels. A child far enough along to have an instep can hook it there and sleep sitting up.

I think my first work would be bringing things to that step. A good many things are needed there that are almost of no use anywhere else. A piece of rush with the pith pushed out will let a child smell its supper before it has quite got the trick of breathing in to smell. There is a particular age at which this is very funny. A child puts its mouth to the rush instead of its nose and blows the supper off the step. I would have done it myself and would be inclined to let it happen rather oftener than necessary.

In time I would go about with the man who renews the pavement. There has to be somebody to try the marks who hasn't made them. The hand remembers where it has been, and to the man with the comb a street is perfectly legible for some time after the turning has gone smooth. He would do a piece in the morning and leave me to walk it in the afternoon, carrying something. It is surprisingly little use testing a street with one's hands free. With a tray one sets the feet wide; with a sleeping child one comes down on the outside of the foot and rolls it in, to spare the head that little drop against the collarbone. There was a turning by the long-barrow knot that nobody ever missed except with a child asleep. We had to carry the marks almost a foot farther out.

I would particularly like the errand of trying a street that had been taken out of use. The comb man would work the old grooves shut with the ball of his thumb, and it would be my business to find out whether I could still get there. Usually I could. There are a great many ways to a place besides its grooves. The house with the inconvenient post has a draught under its door that goes over one's feet like a loose thread. The gutter above the settling yard smells faintly of the insides of the cups. And there is a spot, quite a distance from the market, where the pavement gives a little sideways. Nobody putting down a foot there for the second time can help putting it down in expectation of that give. It is as good as a name.

Once, trying a disused way, I would come upon the woman who had lost the shoe. She would be sitting on one of the fine curled staircases behind the houses, with the tray beside her and one cup left on it. She would ask whether I knew where I was.

“Behind your shoe,” I would say, for that was how the place had always been described to me.

“Am I?” she would say. She had imagined the shoe much farther down.

We would go to see. On the market side there would be people quite certain of the place, but when asked to stand on it they would stand far enough apart to set down a barrow between them. The pavement had taken again long ago. We would get down and lay our hands on it. What could be felt there was a stirring that stopped at once under a warm palm and began again a little to either side. By moving our hands we could move the still place around. After a while she would give me the last cup and we would leave off bothering the shrimp.

It is there, I think, that I would find my own occupation. The new children know the settling yard very well and the market not at all. On their first errands they are told to turn at a shoe that no one can show them. I would take them the long way the first time, by the door with the draught and the sideways place and the gutter that smells of supper. Coming home I would let them take me. A child lately out of water is apt to stop at the most unpromising piece of wall and put the side of its face against it. There are still things it can hear that way. It takes some practice on the part of the person going along not to give the hand a tug.

One would take me under the seventh arch and well past the place where the man does the grooves with his nail. I would think we had missed the turning. The child would have a warm cup in each hand, one for me and one quite spoiled from being held so carefully. It would set them both down and put my hand flat on the wall, then take it off again and turn it over. With the backs of the fingers touching, the passage of something very slight could be felt going away from us. We would wait for the two strokes of the turn. They would not come. Farther along, where the wood thickened, a third person coming home empty would stop and put down her tray.

After that, if you asked for me at the step, they might send you some distance. I would be the one going along with somebody who had only just begun to walk, carrying the supper now, since it had turned out that both hands were wanted.
