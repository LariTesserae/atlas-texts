I should come into the street on a Wednesday with the wrong shoes on. A pair with a little iron let into each heel is quite a good pair for walking inland. Here the irons give a succession of small distinct taps after one has stopped walking. Outside the weighhouse I should put one foot on top of the other to find out which of them was doing it, and be standing in that foolish position when the woman from the third house came out to see what I wanted.

I should want the room. The lodger who goes away on Wednesdays goes away for a fortnight every summer, leaving his big things behind. There is the use of his basin but not of the blue soap in the little separate dish. From the bed one can see the corner where the two walls bulge towards one another. A wagon going round it appears first to be coming into the room and then, quite unaccountably, to have got by.

I think I should be a mender of small metal articles. There is not a living in it in the borough, but there is a fortnight. I should have a narrow box with two handles, one on the lid for carrying it full and one at the end for pulling it out from under a bed. By the second morning there would be a toast rack on the parcel tray and the brass part of a pepper mill, each with a twist of paper through it bearing the name of a different house. The trouble with the toast rack would turn out to be in the table on which it was used. This is the sort of thing one has to go to the house to explain.

For fine work I should sit in the machine hall. The man in blue would clear me a place at his bench by moving his tin a few inches, lid and all. In the felt quiet a loose bit of solder falling on the bench makes a positively domestic sound. One looks round for the person who has dropped a key. He would show me the photograph of the three men and the lobster. The man holding the lobster by the feelers is his father; the hand coming in at the side with a fourth plate belongs to his father too, the photographer having had some difficulty with the exposure. On closer inspection this is quite impossible. He has been saying it for years.

The mug would not come to me as work. The woman is satisfied with it. But on the evening the girl takes it away I should happen to be at the other end of the street, fetching the pepper mill down to see whether the bottom turns more freely with pepper in it. In the kitchen the mug is upside down on a cloth, still too hot to hold. The girl has noticed something about it. If one puts a wetted fingertip over the little hole at the top of the hollow handle and takes it away again, a bead appears at the hole below. She wants to know if it is the same water that was in it before the scalding.

There is no telling. I should show her how to blow it clear with a straw. Quite a respectable little quantity comes out. She would do it twice more, after all the water was gone, for the sake of the note. In the morning the handle would be turned out as usual. There would be nothing on my bill about it.

What I should like best, though, is to be required once at the lifting of the grating. Not to mend it. It is much too big for me, and the waists in the bars are allowed for in some calculation kept at the town offices up the steps. There are two trestles to be got level on the paving. It takes three people: one to press on the trestle, one to slip a thickness of wood beneath the short foot, and one a little way off to say whether the foot at the other end is lifting. I should be the one a little way off. When the iron came down there would be a short interval before anybody went near it. Water would be running out of the shells held in the folded claws.

I might not hear the crab, even with my ear on the bar. One can get rather deaf on the side towards which one does one's hammering. But while I was down there I should see, under the parcel tray across the way, the leg and the fine curved bowl of the grandson's R. The light reflected off the wet grating would reach it for perhaps half a minute. I should know enough about making things to see where he had finished the curve with a much narrower tool than the one he had begun with.

At the end of the fortnight the lodger would come back. I should leave the soap quite square in its dish and take the irons off my heels before setting out. The pepper mill would go on sticking a little in damp weather. If the woman had occasion afterwards to say who I was, she might have to put down what she was holding and think. Then she would lower her head sideways, almost to the table, and say to her grandson, ‘The one that saw your letter.’
