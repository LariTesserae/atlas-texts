I would come up the western side with a woman who had bought a file in the town on the other side of the pass. She had been trying it on me most of the afternoon. A new nail had come up through the heel of her boot; she had the boot off and the file between her knees and every so often she put the boot on again and came a little way. By the time she got to the pass the nail was well below the leather. There was a pleat in her stocking where she had eased it round the sore place and this now hurt nearly as much as the nail. She took the boot off once more, in the bay with the dish, and crossed one foot over the other to pull the stocking straight.

I would be the way she walked when she got up. There was nothing the matter now. Nevertheless the left heel came down rather tenderly and the right foot went forward no farther than it had to. Out in the sun she took a few long paces for the pleasure of being able to, and lost me. In the room I kept on going.

There is a limit to how far a walk like that can get without the weight that made it. For a while I went along the actual way out, as far as the turn where she had put the file away and used both hands on a pier. My right foot went a little farther than my left. After that I could get no purchase except close to the dish. Under its nearest bead there was enough weight to do a creditable heel and something of the small swerve she had made to keep the heel from bearing long. There was no room for the rest of the step. I did the swerve over and over in a space you could have covered with the end of the file.

I should not have known what to call the thing that kept me there. It had none of the woman’s impatience with a limp. Where the boot had come down hard it gave a little, and where it had scarcely touched it reached up, and went on doing both long after there was any prospect of getting them right. In time a queer thing happened to my left foot. It began to take the weight quite willingly. I had a good long stride under the bead by then, though the distance the bead actually went could be judged from the fact that one grain of grit, caught under its entering edge in autumn, came out from under it the next time the mules went up without their winter coats.

I did not stay altogether in the dish. When a person came through the gap with one foot held back, there was the old unequal bearing again, on a scale that allowed of doing something about it. I went out along the bedding to meet it. I was of very little use to anybody coming in on the right foot. But once a boy got halfway through with a basket against his chest and stood there unable to see where he was putting his boot. His heel touched first. For the length of time it took him to bring his toe down I had a whole foot again, a sound one, much bigger than hers.

That is where I would look for myself now: not quite at the threshold, but one paver in, where there is room to turn the other shoulder and so discover that one need no longer go sideways. Most people take a mean little step there and then a better one. The better one is partly mine.
