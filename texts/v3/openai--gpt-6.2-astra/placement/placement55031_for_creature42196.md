I would be on the underside of the porcelain, quite close to the corner where the living thread goes taut. From below, the corner would look badly made. There is a place where the glaze drew back in the firing and left a little bay of rough white biscuit. One of the old combs lies in that bay. It has nine teeth. The first eight are clear of the porcelain; the ninth has its tip in a pore. I think I would be that comb, though for a long time I would have said I was the thing that made it.

When the house was on the ridge there were two quite different motions at the corner. The mountain moved the block and all that was fast to it. The winding moved the iron tongue. On a day when the winding was much used there was no need for a tooth to go feeling along the back of a leaf to find a second pitch. One could put out a foot almost anywhere. The feet on the biscuit held particularly well. A whole row of mouths faced out from under the block, as if there were something worth seeing there.

What survives in my teeth from that time is not the signal. A long pull of the iron and a short pull repeated often enough made much the same addition to a tooth. There is no reading off the weather, or the hour at which somebody up there had occasion to send. What survives is how well I fitted the others. Laid on their sides, my teeth would pass between the teeth of the comb next to mine without either set having to give. There must have been a time, toward the end of a stroke, when all that gray work stood up together in the dark. For something that never had an eye it is a curious pleasure to picture it.

When the pulls ceased, the feet let go of the biscuit one pair at a time. The soft part went back along the thread. It did not all go on the same day. For a while there was enough of me in the bay to feel a foot elsewhere take hold of something smooth, lose it, and take hold a little farther up. That was how the purple blade was found. Then there was the long business of drawing the hinge free. A minute scale came off the ninth tooth. The tooth stayed in the pore.

I would have no sense now of the years since that happened. There would be the eight free tips and the one held tip, and, across a small clearance, the thread stretched over the corner. Most of the time that clearance might as well be the mile of air outside. But if the iron were raised and let fall, as you suggest, the thread would come down on the eight tips. It would take their impression, very lightly, like the pad of a finger pressed on the cut end of a screw.

For the duration of that contact I would be in the garden again. Not everywhere in it. I would have the underside of a single round leaf, the one whose neighbor was going the other way just then. I would have the flat place on the fan stem, and the considerable weight, transmitted through the crook of the tooth, that it cost to ease one fan past another. Last of all, when the tug had almost spent itself, I would feel the roughness of the bay. It would come to me from the other side: eight little checks in a thread that ought to have slid cleanly over white china. I would know what had got in the way.

There would be no new tooth. The thread would lift. If afterward you slipped a bit of looking glass under the block, all you would see in it would be the old combs, pale gray in a light too weak to give them shadows. You might wonder why one had stayed so neatly in place when the others had slewed a little downhill. You would have to turn the glass almost flat to see the ninth tip. That is where I would be held.
