For the first three turns round the stem I would be some extra length on the fish. My feet would be shut in its tail and the rest of me laid out straight behind, including the two long feelers which I generally keep curled against my belly. The fish would be going much faster than I have ever gone. On the fourth turn we would pass the hole it came out of. One feeler would go into the hole, and I would stay there, and the fish would go on. For some time afterwards my feet would go on shutting.

I belong to the hollow sides of things. There are plenty of these even where there has never been a hatching: the coats of the split seeds, for instance, and the little boots of skin the mites pull their legs out of. In a mite boot I have to sleep with one arm up, but there is room enough. I am broad across the back and nearly transparent, with a yellow crumb moving freely inside me. If I lie with my head downhill the crumb comes down behind my eyes and the light is beautiful. I will lie so for no other reason. One has to be a little careful about the moths.

The egg is better than a boot. There is a sort of curd on the inside where the fish's back has lain, and the tail, in coming out first, has made a narrow slit that stays shut when I am not using it. I eat the curd in lanes, leaving enough between them to keep my footing. For a day or so I come across the impressions of spots. They are not brown on this side. They are places where the curd is particularly sweet.

The fish comes by quite often. I know it by a kink no longer than a grain of pollen near the end of its tail. Where my feet shut on it there are two more minute kinks, but I need my feelers to make those out. It has the habit of eating right up to the slit and a little way past it, so that twice in the course of a circuit its white comb comes through my door. The first time this happens I am so thoroughly combed that the yellow crumb shows for a moment between two teeth. After that I move up the wall when the stem begins to tremble.

There is a great difference between the sound of the fish going round and the sound of the water going up. The fish makes a rub followed by a silence in which one can hear the rub coming again from the other side. The water makes no sound until it leaves. Then every fine hair on the stem lets go with a separate tick. Lying against the skin one hears a little field being reaped. By the time it is quiet the collar is a good way overhead and I have a house which is rapidly turning into a husk. The slit draws wide. A draught comes in smelling of pear, then of nothing much. My fish goes by above me with its white side out, making for a patch of moss it has not yet tried.

I ought to leave then. An egg well eaten out weighs less than a mite boot and has no laces to hold it on. But the bottom lane of curd is still intact, and I am going along it with my mouth full when my feet tell me there is nothing on the other side of the floor. We fall, the husk and I. The air gets in through the slit and turns the husk over. I see the lit fork, a dark interval, the lit fork again with a moth on it. Then something takes us very softly on a multitude of upright points.

It is the hare's bed. I could get out and walk across the tops, if I would leave off eating. Instead I finish the lane. In the time it takes to do this the husk has worked down among the moss leaves, and one leaf, clear except for three cloudy grains in its tip, has come in through my door.

I find myself for the first time with a house that has something growing in it. The leaf goes on lengthening. I have to turn on my side, then flatten myself against the roof. Presently the tip finds the far wall and pushes the whole husk ahead of it, and we go down in considerable comfort, with the three grains swaying together by my head. Through the skin I can see the shins of a beetle. A little lower, I can see under its lifted foot. There is a whole tuft of pasture springing back into place there, and a pale thing much like myself going on with its meal.

Where the two fish lie together the leaf stops. Its tip has met the cheek of the younger one. The fish raises its head just enough to let me and my husk slip under, then puts it down again. Here there is water enough to keep the walls soft. There is a slow working overhead, a sort of affectionate grinding, as the two fish breathe at slightly different rates. I put my feelers out to find what it is I have come to rest on. It is smooth, round at the edges, sweeter in the middle. I have the twelfth spot all to myself.
