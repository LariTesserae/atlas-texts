I would keep the shelves at the lip of the plain. You would find me by running a hand along the top of the dry bank until you came to a bread board with a hole for a thumb. The board goes across my knees. If you put something on it I can tell, even when I am asleep, which end you have put it on. This is useful on mussel days when the people coming in are apt to have both hands full and no great opinion of the necessity of waking me.

There was no call for a keeper at first. People put things down on their way out and picked them up on their way in. The trouble began with a pair of overshoes. A woman left them to go barefoot to the eastern house, found them where she had left them, put them on, and got as far as the dry road before she came upon herself in the act of taking them off. There was a good deal of hopping about on one foot. She came back and sat with me for a while. I had a stall there in those days for opening mussels for people whose hands were getting stiff. We put the shoes on my bottom shelf. After an hour she was able to get into them without difficulty. The left one had a warm heel.

Now it is understood that anything of which there is an inconvenient number can be left with me. It is mostly cups. I have room for forty on the middle shelf, provided none has a saucer, and strips of old blanket between them so that a hem can get from one end to the other without setting the whole row off. A cup with a hem in it has a different note when knocked. A cup that has had a hem in it quite recently has almost the same note. I lost a little money in the early days undertaking to distinguish the two.

People sometimes ask me whether anything very valuable has come in. The most valuable thing on the shelves, to my knowledge, is half a wooden last. It is the forepart, from a shoemaker in the dry country who used to make a particular allowance for the second toe. There are a number of people in the district with that toe, and a young man comes down once or twice a winter to take the shape off the last with a piece of lead. He has made very comfortable shoes for people who had supposed that a certain amount of pain on going downhill was due to the slope of the road.

I have an allowance for the blanket and nothing for my time. For a living I still open mussels. The bread board was my father's. There is a hollow on the underside, worn there by his left knee rather than mine, and on a slack day I have only to turn it over to have somewhere to put the smaller articles that would otherwise roll off. It was in this hollow that I first felt the child breathing.

I ought to say there was no child on the board. There was a lid from a round tin, with the edge bent up in one place, and beneath the lid there was a hem. I could feel it all right: it was sitting crosswise to the hollow, giving itself a thorough scratch with a hind foot. The breathing was much slower and came against my own left knee. I put my hand under the board. Somebody small had settled there with a cheek against my stocking and had got very nearly off to sleep. When I put my hand down farther there was only my other knee, and my two feet on the shell grit as usual.

I minded this considerably more than the woman had minded the overshoes. I shut up early. I took the board home and stood it up on end in the pantry, a foolish thing to do, as I spent most of the night thinking what it would be like to have to sleep standing up in there.

My father was no longer alive to ask. I went instead to the elder attendant's mother, on the grounds that she was of an age to remember his stall. She listened to the whole account with my hand in hers. It was the afternoon she set aside for cutting her nails, and as she listened she felt along the ends of mine, stopping now and then at one she evidently thought could do with attention.

‘You used to go under there,’ she said.

I said that I knew that.

‘Well,’ she said, and let my hand go.

What I had hoped to learn I can't now say. I remembered being under the board, but I remembered no particular happiness there. There had been the smell of opened mussels, and the difficulty of pulling up a stocking in a confined space. My father's knee used to start against my cheek when the knife went through the hinge. One got used to that. The thing I had forgotten, until I put the board across my lap again, was how long he could sit, of an afternoon when trade was bad, without getting up and tipping me out.

I do not think the child is there all the time. I am perfectly able to get up to piss, for instance, or to reach down a box for somebody who wants to try a lid. But sometimes toward closing there comes a weight against my leg, less than the weight of a wet cap and quite unmistakable. I have learned to open mussels a little to the right of the hollow. It is slower work, but then there are fewer customers at that hour.

If you came in late from the eastern house you might find the stall apparently deserted, the cups all quiet and the keeper unwilling to stir. Put your shells on the right-hand end. There is a clean spoon in the thumb hole. I can generally manage the rest without waking either of us.
