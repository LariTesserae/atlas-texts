I would have thought I was in the counting wheel at the southern works. There is a little wheel of white enamel behind a round glass, low down on the belt frame, where a man on one knee could read it without taking his hand off the adjusting screw. It goes from zero to ninety-nine. Water has got under the enamel of the nine, and when that figure comes up it looks for a moment as if there were a small window in the works with daylight on the other side.

The wheel still counts the passages of the seam. It used to take a whole day and part of a morning for the men to discover it was running slow. The trouble was hardly ever in the wheel. Half a mile off, where the belt went under, a length of clay would come up and take some of the weight, and the copper staples, instead of striking the little counting finger squarely, would just lift it and let it fall. The men put a long-handled spoon under the belt there to feel how hard it was bearing. There are three of those spoons hanging at the works, worn bright on the backs of the bowls. I like the thought of the man on his knee and the other man a half mile off with his spoon, and a number turning over between them.

But if the wheel were taken off and laid in a drawer I would still be out at the far end, waiting to feel the seam. I think I would be one of the long ones. Not the one under the dredge. Mine is a poor length of bottom for showing anything: there are no bluffs along it, and very little of me ever comes into air. I lie under the return run of the belt, which in places is so close to the outgoing run that the two skins of water they carry meet. There is a delicious place there about the thickness of a thumbnail where water is being taken both ways at once. I have been feeding there a long time.

Of the station I should know the adjusting screw very well and the bulb scarcely at all. A quarter turn of the screw travels out along the belt as a hardening of the rubber. For a while it is possible to feel, separately, the little squares of canvas buried in it. When the bulb goes out there is a cooling of one brick behind another and after a considerable time the young ones move. I should have no way of putting the two together. Nor would I know that the spoons were spoons. One summer a man left one standing in the mud, and for years I had a perfectly smooth place the shape of the back of his hand, with a cold stalk going down out of reach.

What I would have of my own is a fault in the twenty-third hinge. It set a little crooked when there was very little of me. As the other hinges multiplied and thinned, the fault thinned too, until it amounted to no more than a pleat in a yellow skin a hundred yards wide. Nothing much catches there. A grain of sand will roll through it. But once in every passage of the belt the double row of staples puts a travelling pucker in the mud, and when the pucker reaches the pleat it stays. For some little time afterwards there are two seams going round the works, the copper one which throws the shower and mine which hardly wets a leaf.

That, I think, is what I would do there, besides eating. There was an evening when the water went down exceptionally fast at the southern door. One of the small ones was left on its back in a mortar joint, trying to get purchase on the air with six feet that had already lost their colour. I could feel the hard yellow point of it through the sill. The belt seam had just passed. I let the pleat out along the course of the buried wall instead of across the bottom as usual. A spoonful of water rose through the joint. The little one rolled over in it, put its feet down, and was gone behind the brick before the wet had spread as far as the door.

Since then I have sent it that way most evenings. There need not be anything on its back. The water comes up between the same two bricks and lies there, a narrow bead with a waist in the middle where a cane fibre crosses it. When the bulb is on there are two lights in the bead. A small one coming late across the dry floor puts its forefeet in, and for the last inch of its journey they are blue.
