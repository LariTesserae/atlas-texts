The second time I turn my head, my left ear goes on round a little farther than my right. There is no pain. I have much the sensation one has on taking off spectacles and finding that the bridge of the nose is still wearing them. I go a little way into the street. On my right the water makes the noise of water washing against a shin. On my left a reed is rubbing another reed, high up where their skins are dry. Each time they part there is a little tacky sound, quite delicious to hear. I stand listening to it until the girl whistles again, when my left ear comes back so smartly that I put a hand up to catch it.

I am of considerable use to her for a while. We find that the best place for me is not in the street but on a root which comes out of the pink lip, takes a dip in the marsh, and enters the lip again about a yard farther on. It is the only dry place for my heels. She gives me back both ears between attempts. Once, out of curiosity, I turn before she has made the note. The reeds remain perfectly still. I have the distinct impression that someone is waiting for me to finish being a fool.

It is on the root that I first notice my other occupation. A plum comes bumping along the inside of the lip. It is a poor specimen, with a patch of corky brown skin on the cheek, and when it reaches the dip in the root it sticks. I put my foot down to ease it through. Before my foot is halfway there, the plum rolls a little to one side and goes through of its own accord. What stays with me is the expectation in the sole of my foot of the slight, quite specific give of a plum. I have it all afternoon. In a court on the way home I come upon a woman trying to work one hand free of a glove. There is nothing to be seen of this except five narrow leaves drawn tight together in a crack. The expectation leaves my foot. The leaves come apart. For a moment I have a very fine feeling in the web between my thumb and forefinger, where the air gets to the skin.

After that there are a number of such errands. In the older wards, where everyone knows the song, they have very little need of me. My district is the strip of half-grown houses below the marsh. A sill there is still soft enough to take the print of an elbow. People are continually reaching for things which have moved a little since yesterday. There is the lift of a shoulder intended for a branch which now clears the head; there is a whole elaborate gathering-up of skirts at a place where two pale roots have met and made a dry crossing. These things don't go to waste. The lift of the shoulder is wanted three doors on by somebody having her hair combed. I get quite deft at carrying them without doing them on the way. The skirts are difficult. For several days I walk about with my hands full.

I would have said by then that I was a sort of porter. It is an agreeable trade in a place with so many landings. But one afternoon the girl gets her whistle exactly where she wants it. I know because there is no impulse whatever to turn my head. There is a note among the reeds, close, a little rough at the beginning, and a startled intake of breath in precisely the place where the note leaves off. Then she does it again. I am so pleased that I lift my heels and knock them together against the root.

The note stops. Presently there is a knock in answer. It comes from the underside of the root, through the wood and straight into the bones of my feet.

Until then everything I have carried has been on its way to somebody else. This is for me. I sit down rather abruptly in the warm shallow water. She waits. I knock once with my knuckle, and she knocks once. I knock twice, and after a pause she knocks three times. There is a good deal of afternoon left. We put it to very little use.

I can still be found on that root, though it would be a mistake to look for a seated figure. My ears are rather often on loan, and the woman in the crack has acquired my way of spreading the fingers after a tight glove comes off. What I keep is in the short length of wood between the place where the girl knocks and the place where I knock back. There is room in it to consider how many times to answer. Sometimes I make her wait disgracefully long. Sometimes, with all that weight of mountain sounding under us, I give the wood one tap so light that nobody who wasn't waiting for it could possibly tell it had been made.
