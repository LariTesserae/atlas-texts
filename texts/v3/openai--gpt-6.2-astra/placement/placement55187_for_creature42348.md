At first I would go along with the trader. There is a small pleasure to be had in putting down two heavy stones, and by the time he reaches the patch of rock I would feel I had earned a share in it. He sets them down a house-width apart. On second thought he moves one of them nearer the other. A man laying out a house is much influenced by the length of the piece of bark he happens to own.

The boy has stayed behind to watch the gap. He has taken his carved runner off his finger and is dangling it by the tail. Presently the black snout comes up again. It touches the runner all along its belly, from the place where the first two legs join to the curl of the tail. It has a very good touch. It can distinguish a thing made to the likeness of food from food, even when the likeness has six proper feet and has been kept overnight in a pocket with something to eat. It goes down again. The boy, who had been prepared to lose the runner and a little sorry already, puts it away.

I think I would stay down there with the snout. From above one sees scarcely anything of the animal but the part that does its marketing. Underneath, it is a considerable person. It has the shape and suppleness of a hand stripped out of a glove, and can put most of itself through a hole that would refuse its nose. Its house is a loose pocket slung between the two fans, where there was no occasion for a pocket until yesterday. The outside is made of the long wiry stuff from the knees of grazers. The inside can be made of almost anything, and the animal has strong preferences. Sandal hair is near the top of the list. It has been trodden until all the spring has gone out of it. One can put it down and find it in the same place on coming back, a property by no means common on the underside of the world.

There is a litter in the pocket. Here I think I would find myself, the last of three, with my nose still the color of a peeled bead. My mother puts the sandal hair in with us in a lump. We lie on it till it separates. At this age we will lie on anything she gives us, including, once, her face. She has to back out of the house wearing all three of us and wipe us off on the lower fan.

What we know of the people at first is the difference in their weights. The trader goes back and forth over us for several days. Outward he has an easy rolling tread; inward he is, variously, a man and an armful of husk, a man and a doorpost, a man with one end of a roof while another man comes after him with the rest. We like the roof best. For a few steps the two sets of feet have to go exactly together, and our pocket tosses us up and catches us. Long after the roof is on we wait for that to happen again.

Then there are visitors. A visitor stops with both feet at the edge of the rock and gives a little forward lurch as he goes down on his knees. The post the trader has bought is the one with the six streaks in it. Its foot sits in a shallow cup pecked out of the rock, with a wedge driven in at the back to hold it steady. The wedge sticks out over the fan. When somebody speaks at the door, we feel it where we live.

It would be pleasant to say that I grow up understanding the speech of people. In fact I get one word. The word is used several times each morning by the trader before anybody is at the door at all. It travels through the wedge, down a stiff strand of grazer hair, and into the soft place behind my jaw. Soon after it comes a dusting of flour, with quite often a whole tooth from one of the orange ridges, still closed at the base and full of the good meal. My sisters are slower than I am at making the connection. For nearly a week I get the tooth.

One morning the word comes and nothing falls. I go up the grazer hair and along the underside of the wedge. It is my first journey alone, and at the end of it I can see, through the triangular opening between the post and the rock, a surprising part of the trader: one eyelid, the side of his nose, and his thumb with a small legless animal of wood tucked into the crook of it. He is kneeling inside his house. Beyond him, on the floor, lies a scoop made out of a split husk. He puts the runner in his pocket and reaches round behind him for the scoop. It is a hand’s breadth too far away. He says my word again.

Years afterward, when my own nose is black almost to the eyes, I shall still come up for that word. Sometimes the scoop has gone under the bedding. Sometimes a customer has gone off with it in a sack. Once it is hanging by its loop from the wrist of the very hand with which he is feeling for it in the flour. It means something different to the trader on each of these occasions, and something quite dependable to me. I put my nose through the opening. He sees it and laughs, and sweeps a little of whatever he has been measuring into the crack.

There is a hollow there now, too small for a knee, polished across the grain of the wedge. If you were helping him take the house down you might put your thumb in it and wonder what sort of tool had been used to drive the wood home. I would have made that. I would be just below, holding very still through the unaccustomed daylight, with a piece of singularly fine old sandal between my teeth, waiting to see where you meant to put the post.
