I think I would live under the stairs to Enne’s room, on the back of the tread where her father’s hand went when he was too old to take the stairs upright. There is a smooth place there still. I would be one of the people you can only feel when they are speaking.

When I say people, I mean there would be quite a number of us. A tread is a long way to travel. At the two ends are families that have never met, though anything said at one end can be heard distinctly at the other. We have six feet and a nearly colorless body with a brown line down it, the color going all to the feet in winter. A shed skin found in a bed looks enough like a little ladder that children keep them for their dolls. No doll has ever been made quite small enough.

My own preference would be for the third tread up. On the first, one gets the whole business of arrival: a heel and then the other heel, the jar set down, the bit of dirt picked off the jar and flicked away. By the third tread a person has usually remembered the thing she came back for. There is a pause, with the full, considerable weight of somebody who is for the moment going neither up nor down. That is when I like to speak. I make a tuck in my belly and let it go. A person with her hand on the wood feels a tap under the little finger. Usually she takes her hand away and looks, which is not much of a conversation, but from time to time somebody taps back.

Iva does. She thinks it is a fault in the tread. She gets down with a peg between her teeth and holds the wood in both hands, and I go from one thumb to the other answering her. She takes the peg out of her mouth.

‘It moves,’ she tells Enne.

‘Well, don’t mend it then.’

There is work for us. In the warm wood under an oven, where the grain is wide, we push our eggs into the openings and stand over them until the wood cools and takes hold. In a stair this will not do. A stair opens and shuts too often. Here we lay in the leather button that fits between the tread and its pin. It takes most of a night to get one egg into it. In the morning I am so tired that a fly can walk the length of me without my attempting to eat it.

This is how I come to be in Iva’s hand the day she goes over to do the floor. She has come down in the dark, well before the hour she and Enne agreed on. She sits on my tread and rubs the trouble out of her fingers one by one. The last thing she does before she gets up is put her hand round the edge of the stair. I go into the fold at the base of her thumb. It is warmer than the button, and for once in my life I go a very great distance.

From a person hanging upside down the valley is not particularly alarming. There is so much of the person between oneself and the valley. What alarms me is the rod she is holding. It is still green enough to have a tremor of its own; when she bends it the tremor runs into her wrist and all six of my feet contract. She has taken too long over getting the end through. The peeled tip has dried. Enne, indoors, pushes down on the crossing with the stone, which is precisely what Iva told her beforehand not to do unless asked.

‘Well, I can feel you needing it,’ Enne says to the floor.

Iva takes the end in her mouth to wet it. For a moment there is nothing to do with her free hand. She lays it flat against the underside of the house. I step off.

It is the first floor I have known from this side. I had thought, from hearing the work, that a crossing was like two stairs laid across one another, each carrying news to its own end. In fact if one sets one’s feet on a crossing while a woman is saying something in the room above, one gets her voice in all six feet at different times. I walk about a little. There is a place under the hearth where Enne has been laughing for appreciably longer than she has been laughing anywhere else.

I do not suppose I would change the lives of these women much. I go into a crossing and stay there, and in due course there are a great many of us, some brown to the feet and some brown only at the tips, owing to the uneven heat of the oven. But on that first morning, when Iva has got her rod through at last and pulled twice to tell Enne that it holds, I give the wood one tap of my own.

Enne lifts the stone. ‘Three?’

‘Two,’ says Iva.

They wait. It is agreeable to have the whole house listening, and I do it again.

Many years later a child in the cradle will put a hand out to the twist in the timber and feel somebody speaking there. The timber will be too thick for her to answer through it. She will try with a spoon, then with the hard heel of a small shoe. At last she will put her mouth to the wood and hum. All along the underside of the floor we will come out of our crossings. There is nothing in our experience like it. Even at the far end of the third tread, where nobody has ever been farther than the pin, people will stop what they are doing and put down all their feet.
