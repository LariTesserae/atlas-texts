I think I would be on the lower slab. Not quite on the slab: there is a fringe of grass on its upstream edge which the water keeps folded under. A foot put through that fringe from above finds another thickness of leaf where it might reasonably have expected stone. I would be in the fold.

For most of the year it is a good place. When sand comes over the sill it goes a considerable way out before it falls. One can put one's lips to a shoot there without getting grit between them. I would be rather small for a grown beast. I would know the underside of a number of larger ones quite well, particularly one with a place in the middle of its right row where two feet set down together and the next does not set down at all. In a narrow way I would have to flatten myself for this one, as for the others. Just as the weight began to be rather much, there would be that little relief on the right.

The wintering rod on the lower slab is a piece of fence that has come bodily out of another place. One can tell because the rods lie across the set of the wall. There is room on the longest for perhaps thirty beasts, though at the end it is a poor lodging. The bark there has parted from the wood and works up and down. I would winter three or four places in from that end. Under snow, with a heel over my lips, I would have very little by way of a distinction between myself and the other beasts. If the bark lifted, we tightened. It settled again under all our feet.

It is the lower grass that gets the light first at this particular gate. By the time there is much company on the sill above, I would have been out for days. I don't think I would have any notion of a second bed. The water that comes down from it has small differences in it: sometimes sand, sometimes a blade that slides the length of my back with no more weight at one end than at the other. It is quite another matter when the tip of a blade comes in among the folded leaves and stays.

At first it saws a little. I would move from under it. Then, some distance away, with several thicknesses of grass between my feet and the new blade, I would feel something set foot on it. The pull is much too long. On the bed it passes from leaf to leaf and one can tell fairly well where to get out of the way. Here the whole upstream edge of the slab seems to have a beast on it. I would get underneath, and after a considerable wait a small one, smaller even than myself, would come down through the fringe without passing over me at all.

I might go up that night, when the run of water slackened. The difficult part would not be the middle. Once my outer toes had closed on the blade on either side, I could go quite well, even where it twisted and obliged me to travel for a little with my pale side uppermost. The difficult part would be at the butt, where the leaf is pinched into the stone joint. There the last few feet must keep their hold while the first ones try the bare sill. I would be a long time stretched out there, with good grass almost touching my lips.

By morning I would be one of the pale places a man could see from the punt, though he would put me on the wrong journey if he took the marks on the slab for arrivals and those on the sill for beasts that had not yet gone across. A little way inland the bank bends. I would go that way because the grass goes that way. Before long something heavy would be coming towards me along the only good course, and I would put myself underneath. All the feet on the right would set down.
