I think I would be found in the socket of the loose staple, though not in the bottom of it. About a grain's depth below the surface there is room, between the metal and the stone, for a small gray crescent with a black dot at either end. Ordinarily only one dot shows. A person trying the staple with a finger might take it for a pit in the metal. If the finger is kept there, warming the metal, the dot goes slowly round the bend and the other one comes up.

The two dots are the ends of a single gut. I feed in either direction. In dry weather there is very little to choose between the ends, but after rain one generally has a greenish stain about it and swells a little, and that is the head for the next few days. I have no part in keeping the weather late. In particular, nothing dreadful would happen if I were picked out of the socket with a grass stem. I would curl about the stem, which would tickle the person holding it, and the ferns would go on making their small uncertain movements.

What I live on is the skin of the staple. It grows best on the side toward the cupped leaves, a film too thin to have a color of its own until it is rolled up. My way of eating is to put one lip down very firmly and slide the other up to it. A little bolster of green collects in the crease. There is a clean track behind me of precisely my own width. On the three tight staples the skin grows undisturbed, and in August it comes off in flakes like the silver on the backs of old mirrors. I could eat for a year on any one of them. There is no getting one's lips about a staple that fits.

A shower is a considerable inconvenience. For the first part of it the water works down the outside of the socket and the inside remains dry. I go round onto the inside. Then there is a short interval in which I can have my lip on a dry place and feel, through the thickness of my belly, water running over my back. It brings down all sorts of things. Once it brought the little head plug from a case in a leaf, with the marks on it where the head had pulled free. I wore it for most of an afternoon. When the inside rain begins I have to go round again. It is not a matter of nine breaths to me. It is a matter of whether the bolster will come away now or whether I must leave it where it is and begin another.

Only once would you be likely to see all of me. This is when the socket silts up. The first sign is a pair of very short green threads waving beside the iron. They are my two ends feeling for a way back down, and between them, slowly heaving into the light, comes the rather handsome pleated length that has never touched anything but metal or stone. In air the pleats fall open. I am nearly long enough to go round an almond. I do not know this until I have gone round one and found my own lip in the way.

I would like to say that at this point I make for the fern. There is shade there, and the sheared place on the lowest frond smells quite distinctly sweet even from the staple. But I go on cleaning the almond. There is food on it; there is a curve to follow; on its far side there is another curve exactly like the first. By the time I have made three circuits there is a pale belt about it and the sun is coming out.

What saves me, if anything does, is a mite on its way home. Its feet adhere very nicely to a damp pleat. For perhaps half an inch I go with it, drawn out like a bit of gum, and when I let go I am in the gutter, which is still wet underneath the glossy leaves. There are stones there with skins that have never been grazed. There is a particularly good one wedged across two others, with clearance on the underside for both lips at once.

So that is another place to look. Do not lift the stone at first. Put a fingertip on it. There will be the great rasping coming up from the floor, of course, and nothing I do is strong enough to be felt against that. But leave the finger there until the stone warms. Look then, along the lower edge, for the dot going in and the dot coming out.
