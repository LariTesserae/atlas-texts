I would be on the underside of the ridge, about half an inch from the procession. There is a place there where the stone has chipped and the chip has not quite come away. At low water it has a dark blue border on three sides. The border is water, drawn up into the break. It joins the pale ring at one point only, by a scratch so fine that a young woodlouse can straddle it without getting its belly wet.

I think I would come out of that scratch. For some time there would be no particular reason to suppose that anything had come out. One end of a shred of mussel flesh would flatten, as if somebody had pressed it with a wet finger. The flattening would travel along the shred. Afterwards, where the stone was cleanest, there would be a faint fork of brown moving off with the two prongs foremost.

I would be a flat little worm, nearly all the color in me belonging to something else an hour ago. The brown fork would be my dinner, distributed into the two long side passages of my gut. I would have eyes, but nothing here would be visible to me in the detail in which it is visible from above. The passing of a woodlouse would be a considerable dusk, preceded by a rocking in the water film. I would know the one that had just shed its plates from the others by quite a different circumstance. It would be possible to get across its back. On an old one the bloom of lime takes up the wet in separate little beads. Halfway over I would have to turn round.

At the tick I would be late. The tick travels very well in iron and in air and scarcely at all across my half-loose chip. What I would feel would be the hind feet of the woodlice taking the strain. By then the fine sand would already be coming, knocking grains out of the scratch in single file. I would put as much of myself into the break as would go and leave the rest streaming out. There would be days when I went in head first and came out tail first, and days when a grain lodged beside me and I had to spend the high water doubled over it, with both ends out in the current.

My good time would come considerably later. When the sea falls away from the ridge, the little animals go on brushing for some minutes in water that is no longer running anywhere. Each brush leaves a minute wake. I would go along those wakes taking the soft particles the brushes had stirred up, sometimes arriving at the brush itself and being passed briskly through a mouth and out again. There is very little of me to get hold of. Once, on the back of the mother, I would find the hollow where a youngster had been walking. It would contain three grains of gray paste and a piece of green glass too small to look green except through water. I would cover all four and presently move on with the glass showing in the middle of me. By morning it would be lying in the scratch. It would make a tolerable doorstop.

If you wanted to find me, though, I would wait for the rain. The first drop through the rivet sends the young ones in under the ridge. For a moment the ring is bare and the dimple fuller than it can hold. The surplus goes down the scratch, meets the salt water in the break, and brings me out rather faster than I ordinarily go. Look just below the ring. There will be a tiny clear fold in the water, turning a grain of grit over and over in its efforts to get past it. The grit will stay where it is. The fold will divide at the front, go round it on both sides, and draw together again, and by that time the first pair of brushes will be out feeling the place where I was.
