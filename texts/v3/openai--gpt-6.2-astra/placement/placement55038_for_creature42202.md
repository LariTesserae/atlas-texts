I think I would have a key to the blue gate, but no authority over anything on the other side of it. I would be the woman who comes down on Thursdays to read the pump-hour meter. The meter has five black figures and one red one. The first four black figures almost never change. In wet weeks I have to wipe the glass with my cuff to see whether the fifth has gone round.

The fitter would know me by the sound of my key. His turns easily and mine has to be drawn back a little before it will turn at all. We would have met at the gate a few times without having much to say. Once he would ask whether I had a rubber on the end of my pencil and I would hold the torch for him while he rubbed out a sum on the cabinet door. It is his line on the middle pier. I used to think it was a water mark and wondered how a man who worked on pumps could have got a water mark quite so crooked.

The first I would know of the shrew would be the peculiar state of my trousers. There would be a little wet mark just above the turn-up, as if I had caught the edge of a freshly painted bench. Then another, on the inside of the same leg. I would stand looking at these marks on the landing and something behind me would give a perfectly audible sneeze. That would be all, that Thursday.

After that I would do the reading first and sit a while on the landing afterwards. There is a satisfactory place to sit there, with a flat on the wall where one shoulder blade goes and room on the dry step below for both heels. The first thing you lose by sitting still is the impression that the crickets are making one noise. There is a bad scraper among them, a long way to the left of the others. It gets in three good strokes and the fourth is nearly always a slip. The next thing you lose is the ability to tell how long you have been there.

From that seat the two fans behind the pier look like one bank. It would take me several Thursdays to understand the little hitch in the animal's progress along it. Runs, gathers, runs again. Then one day I would be late coming out of the gate and see it from the other side: the running water, the four feet clear of it, the rather considerable spread of the toes on landing. I would go home pleased about the toes. I had not supposed it had so much foot to put down.

There is a space on the meter card headed *Remarks*. It means such things as glass cracked, seal missing, access obstructed. For a while I imagine writing in it the width of the jump. But to get the width I would have to go out on the fan with a rule, and my heel would make a new bay into which the water would go at the next rise. So the card goes back into its tin with nothing in that column. Upstairs at the kiosk I hold my finger and thumb apart while I wait for my change, trying to get the distance right. The woman gives me my coins one at a time because she thinks there is something wrong with my hand.

I would be away the week the banks join. On my return there would be a curl of plane bark lying across the place, much too big for the feeble thread under it to carry off. For two visits I would see nothing of either animal. On the third the smaller one would come down while I was sitting there, so close to my shoulder that I could hear its belly going over the lip of the socket, a sound like a thumb drawn across a peach. It would stop on the step below my shoes with a bit of yellow grass in its teeth. Then down.

I would lean well out to see what it did at the crossing. It would go straight over in three or four short steps and disappear. A little disappointing, after all that looking. I would be getting up, easing the stiff knee first, when it came back the other way at a run. This time it would jump. Not at the old place, though. Six inches farther on, where a wet twist of grass lay across its path. Its own grass, dropped on the way out. The ends would still be trembling when I went up to the street.
