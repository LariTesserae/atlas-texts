I think I would live a little way above them. There is a room over the old eave that gets enough light to sew by for about as long as it takes the kiln crew to draw a firing. I would have a good deal of sewing ready against that time. In particular I would have the aunt’s apron, turned inside out across my knees. She wears through the cloth behind the pads sooner than anybody would suppose from looking at the outside. The grit works in by the stitch holes and stays there. When I open a seam I put a cup underneath.

My needle would be copper, with a flattened place halfway along where somebody once shut it in a door. There is no particular advantage to the flattened place, but I have got used to finding it with my finger. I could tell my needle from anybody else’s in a cup of thirty.

It would be over the matter of one of the pads that I got to know the boy. His aunt has had to go out in a borrowed apron, much too wide at the knees, and it is the boy who is sent up to fetch hers. I have it all sewn up except for the bottom pocket. The pad that came out of that pocket is on the sill in two pieces. There has been a fine split in it for some time; the broken edges are polished where they have worked against one another through the cloth.

I have made a new pad, as like the old as I can manage. You cut these out of a course that has stood a little too far from the fire. The hardest tiles are no good. They eat the thread.

He puts the apron on. It comes up nearly to his armpits. After some difficulty with the ties he goes down on the floor and takes two or three steps on his knees.

“Did she tell you to do that?”

He shakes his head. He is trying to get up the little difference in level between my room and the sill. On the sound knee he goes up it easily enough. On the new one he has to lift himself by his hands. He unties the apron and asks if he can have the old bits.

We try them in the pocket. He has to stand still while I close it with three long stitches. This time both knees go over the sill with the same small hitch, scarcely a hitch at all. Coming back he stops with the mended knee halfway over and has me put my hand against the cloth. I can feel the two pieces change their angle. His aunt, who has been wearing that pad broken for the better part of a year, has a joint in her apron there. It seems a pity to take it out.

There is a difficulty, of course, which is that however well the pieces work they will presently work through. We spend what remains of the light making a little bag for each piece out of the sound part of an old wick, picked open and spread flat. I join the bags by their hems. I do the first stitches and let him do the rest. He sews very close, closer than I would do, and has to bring the work up to his nose to see where the needle has gone. By the time his aunt comes to see what has swallowed him, we are both sitting on the sill with our feet in her way.

I would like to be there when she puts it on. There would be no need for us to say which knee it is. She goes down, rocks back on her heels, goes forward again. Then she reaches round and pulls a little at the cloth behind her left leg.

“Too tight?” I ask.

“No. There was a bit used to pinch me. I was looking for it.”

She takes the apron away on her knees, so to speak. It is quicker than getting up. The boy goes after her by the ordinary stair, with my cup of grit to empty in the broken ware.

After that he comes up sometimes when there is nothing in particular to fetch. I have a shallow drawer of things too small to be much use and too well made to break up. He likes the drawer. One evening he brings me the head with the shut eyes, wrapped in a square of cloth, thinking perhaps I can make it a body that will bend. He has tried a string of clay beads, but the string doubles between the beads and makes a set of ugly corners. Besides, he says, the thing he saw could bend anywhere it liked.

I have never seen one. I get him to show me with his hand. He lays it flat on the sill and pulls it forward a little at a time, letting the knuckles rise in succession. We make several bodies, none of them quite satisfactory. The best is a strip of cloth cut on the slant and not filled with anything. It takes a very good turn indeed, but has no means of keeping the head on. Eventually he takes the head home in its wrapping. The strip is left on my sill.

Some mornings, feeling for my needle before the glow comes along the floor, I put my hand on that strip. It is finer than the cloth I generally keep by me. I know what it is at once and could put it in the drawer, but there is a little air coming up between the sill tiles, and in laying my hand on the cloth I have felt it settle out from under my palm, first at one edge and then at the other. I lift my hand and wait for enough light to try that again.
