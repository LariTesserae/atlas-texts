I would have been on your finger once. There was a good bar there, whorled instead of straight, but with room on the underside of it to tuck the soft last joint of a leg. I went round the end of the bar and found more finger. By the time you put me back I had been to the end of three bars. I had never before been on a letter with no water overhead.

What I remember of the air is the weight of my feet. Underwater I could let go with five and the sixth would hold me. In the air, letting go with two brought my belly down against you. There was quite a knock inside it. I had a little pellet of salt left over from the making of my new skin, loose in there like the last grain in a shaker. You could probably see it fall.

I grew up to be considerably broader than the letter. This happens to all of us, although from above you see only the little ones going in with their groceries. At a certain breadth we give over carrying flakes and begin to catch them. We stand on the grating, one foot on each of six wires, and a flake that would otherwise go edgewise through a square lands flat against our bellies. A good many mouths can feed off it before it gets so small it slips away. Then for a while we carry the mouths. They are quite nice to have on, except when they all turn in the same direction. It is like having a stocking twisted on you.

Farther down, where the light is green, there is a vacancy of an odd shape. The main goes round a corner and the inspection glass, being flat, cuts across the corner. Between the curve and the chord is just room for such a creature as I eventually became. I have five feet on the main and one on the glass. I cannot see through it. You have seen the underside of that foot.

Here there are seasons about six minutes long. First the main fattens with warm feed until my back comes up against the glazing putty. Then the feed goes off among the pans and I have room enough to turn over. Things collect in the putty: shreds of lash, the clear cast shoes of the carriers, once a whole fish, alive and so astonished at the absence of a floor that it kept turning its dark side toward me whichever way I rolled. When I flatten my foot out on the glass these things come up between my toes. The fish went away in the next contraction. For some time after that I took care to leave a space for it.

You might think the place rather a come-down after the great pink shelves. Yet it is here, in the slack of the six minutes, that I can do a thing I could not do in the open water. I can lift my foot a little way off the pane and put it down hard. At the settling pans this makes one bubble come out of a dark fastener. It goes up tilted, with a quick shine on one cheek; a bird puts its bill in the place where it breaks. I know about the bird because the tap comes back down the frame to my five feet. Sometimes it is a double tap. I like that better and will wait through two fattenings of the main to try it again. We have been doing this for some years. It may be a number of different birds.

Lately the putty has got into the crease of my sixth foot. I no longer take it quite off the glass. A crop of little carriers has lodged there, in the sheltered strip where the pressure of my pad gives out. Through the pane you could see them much better than I can. I feel one let go and another take hold, and now and then the small persistent ticking of a foot that has not found anything yet.

I could settle down a fraction and give it something. Usually I do. Once in a while I keep the foot curled for another minute, while the little leg goes out and back, out and back, describing in the water the breadth of something that isn't there. It was in just such a minute that I got onto your hand.
