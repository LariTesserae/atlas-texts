I would be halfway up the new wedge, where its underside comes so close to the rotten wood that a drop can put a foot on each. The first drop there fills with flour. It sits quite white in the dark until another drop takes it on into the wood. After several nights of this there is a clean place on the new stone, about the size of the eye of a bud. The water continues to go that way after the flour is gone.

I think I would begin on the wood side, in a flake lifted from one of the soft annual rings. A white thread comes out of the flake. Its end is clear and rather blunt. For a day or two it grows along the curve of the ring, in company with several others, so that one would take them all for the grain of the wood. Then it leaves the curve. It crosses the water between wood and stone, a distance much less than the thickness of a needle, and lies down on the roof.

It is a good way in for me. There is no need to get all of myself across. Farther back in the wood my threads run through a brown softness that was once hard enough to hold a branch out level in the wind. A grain of it gives under the tip of a thread; a little farther on there is room for the tip to divide. On the stone there is hardly anything to be had. I spread there all the same. Where the crossing dries in the daytime I have a fine freestanding span, with its shadow on the wood below. At night a bead forms on it and the span disappears inside the bead.

The snail comes up under the wedge with a white grain riding on its shell. There is clearance for the shell but not for the grain. It comes off, rolls a little way downhill on the roof of the shell and drops behind the snail. The foot goes over my threads where they fan out on the stone. For a time they are quite plain through the sole, like the veins in a very pale leaf. Then the head comes round and works back over the place the foot has been.

By daylight there is a lane through the fan. On one bank of it the cut ends have dried to little hooks. The other bank, nearer the wood, is still wet. A tip there has started out over the rasped place, with nothing much to distinguish it from the tip that first came across. Below it, out of reach of the snail, the brown ring is coming apart in short square pieces. I have been there all the while too.

When the plate knocks the wedge does not answer. It beds a little deeper each time the wood gives. There will come a night when no drop is needed to make the crossing. For now there is still that much room. The snail on its way out passes under the span, going rather more easily without the grain.
