Down on the wedge there is a place that the snow has never quite succeeded in filling. It lies under the lip on which the birds alight. Snow coming straight down builds a cornice over it; snow coming sideways is carried a little past it by the curl of air off the lip. In there is enough room for a cricket, provided it does not want to turn round. It goes in headfirst in the morning and comes out backward toward night. What looks from above like a rather deliberate choice on its part, to play only when the last bird has gone, is mostly the time it takes to get out.

I would be under that lip too. At first I would take myself for a little heap of things the birds had lost. There is a twist of down there, three empty midge heads with the eyes still on, and a leg, black, very long in proportion to the other articles, that trembles when the cricket goes by. A drop of water coming off the lip will move the whole collection half a grain's breadth to the left. By the following morning it is back where it was. The leg goes on trembling for some time after everything else is still.

It is mine, though not in the sense of being attached to me. I use it for a door. I am a larva of one of the little flies from the falls, lodged in a bend of my own making in a tube of wet silk. The down holds water, the heads keep the down from packing too closely, and the leg lies across the mouth of the tube at such an angle that I can put my head out without admitting very much else. I found it a long way off, nearly at the open end of the trough. Coming home with it was a business. Twice the wind had us both.

Most of the water I need comes in on the birds. A bird that has fed low over the falls has a row of drops under its breast, all of a size, like the teeth along a fine saw. It sits on the lip and gives itself a shake. For a little while there is a stream running past my house. I put out two fans, one on either side of my mouth. They open downward into the stream. When one has something on it I draw it in and lick it, keeping the other one open. This is a way of eating which makes even a quite uneventful meal look like a series of difficult decisions.

The cricket took some getting used to. On its first evening out it tried the very piece of stone on which my tube is fastened. At every stroke of its wings both my fans shut. There was still a good deal coming down from a late bird, and I missed most of it. The next evening it played a little farther off. I could keep one fan open. By the third evening it had found the place it plays now, a curved scale of rock with a space beneath it into which not even its flat head will go. It likes the sound of that. I like it too, for a reason it cannot have noticed from where it stands. There is generally one drop left hanging on the underside of the lip after the stream has stopped. The note brings that drop down. It is full of the very fine stuff that takes longest to wash out of a feather. Supper, after all.

There will be an interval when I do not put my fans out. The stream will go by several times unused; the down will work loose and the door, with nobody to set it straight, will slide away down the trough. Then one warm afternoon I will come backing out of the silk in a clear skin with a split already started down it. I will have six legs of my own by then and not know what to do with so many. It takes a little time merely to get them all on the same side of the stone.

From there the falls are very near. The air above them is crowded with beings to whom I need not explain anything. I expect I will go down to them. But first I should like to stay on the wedge until evening, until the feet have lifted one last time and the cricket has worked itself out backward into the open. I have never been on the dry side of the drop when it falls. I should like to see how big it is.
