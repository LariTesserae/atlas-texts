I think I would come into it by a back foot. There would be the good round pressure of a sister's belly against five of my pads and, against the sixth, something cold that kept coming and going. I would draw that foot in. My sister, asleep, would take up the space I had left. Presently I would have to wake up quite a lot more and do something about the water.

It would be the first leak in a bowl that had held all spring. Even with my eyes open I could see very little. There would be a smell like the inside of a split rush, and a cool tickle in the hairs on one side of my mouth. I would turn that side down and find the leak: a single root turning in its socket, letting in a bead of water at each turn. I could stop it very nicely with my chin. I would lie there growing proud and uncomfortable until one of the old ones came home and, wanting the very root I had my chin on, pulled my head halfway through the wall.

So I would be one of the young, but not, I think, the last one with its beard out in the evening. I would be the one already down again, trying the wet half of its beard against the dry half, to see if that remarkable crawling sensation would go across the join. There is a time in an animal's life when it has just enough of its equipment to get peculiar results from it. I would like to have that time here. A long beard lies down beautifully on moving ground. A short one sticks in it. You come away with a quantity of minute things fastened to your face, some of them edible, and for several nights running this seems a perfectly satisfactory use of a beard.

The old one who pulled my head through the wall would sometimes let me follow her. You have to look close to see that she is old. Her pads still take hold all at once, with that faint change of color in the leaf above each pad; she can go from the underside to the upper side round the thin edge without once showing daylight between the leaf and her belly. But at a fork in a root she sits back to comb her beard straight before she tries the two ways. The others can do it as they go.

One night I would follow her farther than usual. We would leave the runs where the rushes are opened and come into a stand so old that the joints have grown a soft brown fur. There would be grubs knocking inside them. I would stop at each knock. By the time I had got a tooth into the fur she would be well ahead, a bit of cream showing up now on the right and now, disconcertingly, on the left of what appeared to be the same stem.

In the middle of the stand there would be a little reach of open water. On the far bank, on a shelf of mud, three clean-rooted rushes would be laid out side by side. She would take the middle one in her teeth and ease it down until the roots hung in the water. And there I would feel a thing I had never felt from the other end before: the quick, uneven drumming of young feet getting comfortable under a lid. The mud by the pool would take each little kick separately. One, two, a pause; then a furious little burst as somebody tried to turn over who had no room to turn over. I would know that burst. I had slept against it all spring.

She would be busy on the shelf, working the roots farther apart, giving each a little more of the bank to pull on. I would put a pad on the place that had kicked. For a long while nothing would happen. Then, so gently that I could keep my pad there and go on feeling it, the mud would begin to bulge between my toes. At home the bowl would be settling for the day with everybody in it but us.

I think that is where you would come upon me, if you came down through the old rushes early enough: a half-grown animal standing up to its hocks in cold mud, with one cheek still rather bare where the hairs had caught in a wall. The old one would have finished. She would be up on a leaf already, rolling onto her back in the gray light. There would be a leaf for me a little lower down. I would have plenty of time to gain it. Under my foot my sister would give one last shove in her sleep, and this time, having the whole bank to myself, I would give her room.
