I think I should have to wait until the flies were out before I could tell. The first one over the rim lets down two legs on the stone and pulls the rest of itself after them. Four more go by the same way. For some time there is still a scratching, very light and at long intervals. There is a sixth fly down in the narrow part, washing its face with the last of the water. When it comes up it stands on me to get a purchase for the step down. Through the new horn at my edge its two feet show as a pair of red stars.

I would be the shell. For a long time, of course, I would have been the shellback, and even now there is no particularly good place to say where one leaves off. A little dried muscle still holds to the ceiling of the hollow. Wetted, it comes out in a pink tongue and the flies attend to it. There is a place in my rim that dips to accommodate a foot on which, for the better part of nine years, I came and went along this bed. The dip is of no use for a foot now. It is the place the flies go out by.

When I was alive there was a direction to the stone even in the dark. A trace of grease on one side of the mouth was enough; the whole weight of shell and meat would come round to that side, so slowly that a grain caught under the foot had time to sink into it and come out clean on the upper side. I went against the wind as often as with it. Now the wind has a good deal to say. It can push me down a pitch of nearly level stone which in the other days I should have crossed without noticing that there was a pitch at all. But a fly putting its weight on the low side can keep me from going over. Quite a number of things have a say.

I should like the nights in particular. The flies come in cold, their feet stiff enough to tick on the horn. Once inside they warm up a little. The folded knees of one fit in the spaces between the folded knees of the next, though they take no care about this and are forever getting up to change places. There is one bad place to sleep, under the dip, where the wind comes in. The last fly has it as a rule. By morning the little crowd has breathed a wash of damp onto my inside. It gathers best on the scar of the muscle. Long after the six have gone, a seventh fly, a small one with a hind leg that drags, comes up over the dip and drinks there. It has not had to spend the night.

In time I would fetch up among the cups on the nursery ledge. It would take a particular wind to get me there, one that blew *up* a little at the last step. Otherwise I should simply drop and split. I have seen the broken ones. On a gray face of stone there will be a bright dash no thicker than a nail paring, and close by it, if one looks, the rest of a shell in pieces matching the stone exactly. It is only the freshly broken edge that shows white.

But suppose the wind held. There is a heap under a slight overhang, dry on top, damp a shell's depth down. I would go in sideways and lodge, and the next shell to arrive would go a little way into me. That would be the end of the flies, except for the small lame one, which could still get in at the dip. The sedge seed would be there already, caught in the greasy bit at my ceiling. Its first root would come out straight and white and go as straight as it could for as long as it could, which would not be far. Then it would begin to go round.

For years I should be there in the dark with that small increasing pressure. One winter the horn would give along a growth line, without the pieces moving apart. A little grit would get in the next summer, and in the summer after that a little more. At last it would be possible to lift the tuft, root and all, and leave me behind. On the white curl there would be a slight jog once in every turn, smaller in the earlier turns, more pronounced in the thicker ones near the top. It would fit the place where the flies went out.
