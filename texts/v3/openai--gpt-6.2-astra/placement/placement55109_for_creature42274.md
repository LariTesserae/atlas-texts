I think I would be at the terminus, in the little space behind the address plan. There is room there for a person to stand on edge. If a brass slide sticks, it is much easier to reach in from behind and lift the pin than to do what people generally do, which is to lick both thumbs and bear down on the front. From where I stand I can see the two thumbs arrive, pinkish at the ends with the pressure, and know just where to reach.

I wouldn't be the station keeper. I would have come up for the winter to make a second plan. The first was very good when the town was new, but it has one considerable defect. The houses can be moved and the streets on the plan cannot. Two winters ago a man was sent out in poor visibility to put a blanket on a patient in number sixteen. He found the patient easily enough, having heard her sneeze, but by rights, if he had followed the plan, he would have had to lie down on the rock and put the blanket over himself.

My instructions are to make something which can be corrected locally. I have a sheet of tin, a box of little magnets and the use of a bench at the warm end of the freight court. The buildings are easy. I make them first, cutting each one out of the lid of a tea tin with a flange to turn under for the magnet. I give the bathhouse its steps and the exchange its two pillars, although at that scale the gap between the pillars is hardly more than the mark the shears leave in closing. The first time I set them all out together they slew round and take hold of one another in rows. It takes most of a morning to discover how far apart the magnets must be to leave the houses in peace. The town on the wall will have to be rather larger than the town was asked to be. The station keeper moves a coat hook.

It is the paths that give me trouble. On the old plan they are the white of the board showing between the black of the houses. There is a great deal more white than anybody could use. I go round asking which way people walk and get perfectly good directions which don't agree with where I have seen them go. The freight clerk, for instance, says he takes the shortest way to the bath, which in a sense is true. He takes the shortest way on which there is no possibility of meeting somebody coming the other way and having to stand talking in a wet wrap.

For a while I follow people about with a piece of soft wire, bending it as we go. This makes them rather too conscious of their feet. It also means that when the woman in nine stops halfway home and asks whether I would care for tea, I am left on her step with a length of wire which has to go somewhere. She takes it from me and hangs it over a peg. There are our two walks to her house, one sleeve inside the other.

Her upper room is surprisingly red. I have seen the garnet from the street and know about the patch on the bed, but not about the light that comes off the patch. Even the ceiling has a little colour. She gets up once during tea to pull the cover straight where my coat has made a ridge across it. We sit until the light has gone. On the way out I take my coat and forget the wire.

After that I do less following. I put the tin up at the terminus and set the buildings on it and ask people to move anything they find wrong. For three days nobody touches a thing. Then one evening the bathhouse is a little crooked. In the morning it is straight again. I am about to look at the magnet when I realise that somebody has been opening the door. The little piece I bent out to indicate the steps makes an excellent handle. With the bathhouse set awry, a person coming off the wire can tell from the far end of the room that the door is propped and it will be a cold walk from the pegs to the water.

By the end of winter there are several such arrangements. With the freight court upside down, the pan is out for the midge. The hospital turned on its side means somebody is asleep there who has been awake all night and would prefer that visitors use the other stairs. I make no key to these positions. There is a shelf under the plan on which to rest a cup, and usually somebody resting one.

I do put in one path. On my last morning the woman from nine brings back the wire I left behind. It has been on her peg all winter and has a modest downward bend in the middle from the weight of a damp coat hung over it by mistake. We lay it against the tin. One end is at the freight court, the other at her door. In between, it passes quite accurately through a place where there is nothing at all, a level piece of fracture surface just wide enough for two people to stop while one asks the other in. We secure it with two clips so that it can be taken down if the house is moved.

Coming back another season, I might have any hands. I might get a house at the very back and go to work on the upper instruments and have no call to stand behind the plan. But I expect the first thing I would do, even before finding a coat, would be to take hold of that bit of wire between finger and thumb and give it a slight lift in the middle. Halfway up the street somebody would see the end stir against her little tin door. Whether she put the kettle on would be entirely up to her.
