I would be a small moth on the underside of the bench. From my usual place I could put my head into one of the oldest auger holes and see daylight, and my hind feet into the next and feel whether anybody was sitting there. The woman nearly always sat a little farther along. Only when she leaned back to drink did the end of her coat come over my hole. It made a warm brown dusk with one bright hair across it.

I would have come up in the stocking. There are several such moths in the woman's village. They pass the day in a turned cuff or behind a shutter, and at evening come down the whitewashed walls head first to drink where the watering cans have been filled. Nobody has a name for them. On the morning she put in the augers I was well down toward the toe. It was a disagreeable journey. At the top she unrolled the stocking to choose a bit, and I went into the first dark place of a suitable width. It happened to be a split in the end grain of the bench. A little wool came away on one of my spurs.

The difficulty with living up there would be getting a drink. A moth that goes down a wall head first can keep all six feet dry and reach water a surprising way below the level of its eyes. A moth attempting the same thing at a mineral collar meets its own face coming up, very large, and gets its feelers wet. I should have been down the mountain again the first still evening if it hadn't been for the holes. A drip that goes through a seat doesn't fall clean away. It leaves a bead hanging. One can walk out upside down, drink one's fill and go back to bed without once having to take account of what the ground is doing.

I don't suppose I would hear much of the deep note. What would concern me is that the water in my bead, which has been drawing out to a comfortable point, should tuck itself up suddenly flush with the wood. For a moment there is a perfectly round little window. Through it I can see, inverted and very distinct, a piece of the roof with a head of a nail in it. Then the point comes down again, generally on my nose.

After some days I would begin to go a little way abroad. There is a wind along the underside of a bench even when the grass two hours below is standing still. It will carry a light creature to the steps and no farther. On the top step there are black specks that have worked loose from the lower one, and on these it is possible to land. From there the hollow behind the steps is within walking distance. It smells, in the evening, quite remarkably like the village: wet sack, pear peel, something put away in a cupboard before it was dry. I would go there to lay. My eggs would be a dull buff colour except for one green one, which would turn out on closer inspection to be a knot in the bit of stocking wool, at last come off my foot.

The young would have no business with wool. They would eat the soft tissue out of dead rushes, leaving the hard threads in place. A rush worked into the floor of a spider's room would in consequence occasionally let a small brown caterpillar through. There would be a moment of considerable inconvenience upstairs and downstairs both. Most of the young, though, would stay in the hollow. They would roll up to change in the dry ends of the stems, and presently there would be moths here that had never been down a wall.

It is one of those the woman would notice. Not the wings, at first, but a mistake in her record. In the afternoon, sitting with the open tin on her knees, she would look along the row of dry holes and see, three holes back from the one that is taking the drip, the glint of water. She would put out a finger to see how the roof had managed that. The glint would go out. A moment later, in the next hole, it would come on again.

She is good at keeping a finger out of things. In time she would learn to lean over very slowly, without letting her coat cut off the light, until in the bottom of the hole she could make out the two feelers laid neatly back along the wood. The moth would be looking up through the bench at her. It would have come to the hole for the warmth, and stayed because something overhead had made a dusk of just the right thickness. Behind it, along the grain of the wood, there would be room for several more.
