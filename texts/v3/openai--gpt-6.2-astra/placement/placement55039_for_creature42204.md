I think I would be in the shallow drawer under the bench in the works. It sticks in wet weather, but there is a hole in the back through which you can put a finger and push. There are three things in it too short to reach by pushing. I am one of these. The others are a chalk end and a little glass eye, blue, with nothing on the premises to fit it.

I am a gauge for taking the shape of things. I have eighty-one brass slips held side by side in a jaw. Press my edge to the moulding round a door and the slips go back by different amounts; lift me away and there is the moulding, in reverse, with even the runs in the paint. There is a thumbwheel to tighten before lifting. People who have an idea what I do but have never used me generally forget the wheel. They get as far as the bench with a look of great care on their faces and a perfectly straight line.

I was bought to fit the iron shoes to the runners under the morning cart, in the days before it had wheels. For a long time afterwards my chief use was in packing. A man brought in a lamp base or the elbow of a glass drain, and somebody took the shape of it with me and cut beds in the felt. That work has gone mostly to a firm up on the level where they have a machine which will do a hundred alike. What I get now are the things of which there is only one.

The woman in the bay had me for a fortnight over the pudding tongs. A basin has very little lip to catch hold of, and what looks like lip when you have it upside down on the table turns out, in the pot, to be mostly glaze. She took me round the rim in eight places. Her wife had to watch her do this to a basin identical, so far as either knew, to every other basin in the shop. It was not until the tongs were made that they discovered you could lift any basin on the shelf with them except the one she had measured. This had a chip on the underside where one of the little turned-in feet wanted to go. On that basin there is now a gooseberry in green nail varnish to show which way to put the tongs on. The varnish has outlasted the pattern.

I go out with the street crew too. What is wanted of a drain grate is that a heel should not catch it going one way nor a cart wheel strike it going the other. A straightedge will tell you that there is something the matter; I will give you the little packing piece to cut, or rather the space which the piece, if you cut it right, will occupy. A girl who worked a summer on the crew used to put me up to her mouth like a harmonica and blow before she took a shape. Everybody laughed the first time, and nobody laughed the second time; but she had found a good way of getting the grit out between my slips and it is done to this day, though without the flourish at the end.

Just lately I have been kept out of the drawer. There was talk of putting an iron stay under the rail at the bend. A loaded cart had gone too close and skinned it on the road side, and nobody was quite sure how much sound stuff there was left. The heavy walker would not give an opinion. He said he found things with the soles of his feet and was not about to stand on a handrail. So they took a shape of the hurt place with me and hung me by a nail in the cart shed. In a month they would try me there again and see whether the wound was filling.

It is a good place to hang. The cart comes in with the smell of the hollow about it, quite different from the smell at the works, where whatever the weather there is always a little hot oil in the air. The boy comes in too. He has found that if he runs his nail along the backs of my slips, which stand out from the jaw by just as much as is missing at the front, he can play a sort of tune. He does it very lightly. He has been told what the shape is for.

Yesterday he brought his mother in to hear it. She stood a while with her head bent, then took me off the nail. Where the rail was deeply grazed there was a snag in the tune which he had to use his other hand to get past. She put the heel of her left hand against the brass there and he ran his nail along again as far as her hand. This made a much better finish. They did it several times.

When they put me back against the rail there will be a thin place to see daylight through, or there will not. If there is, they will have to notice which side of the brass the light is on. It is easy, even for people long used to me, to mistake what has gone for what has come. After that somebody will slacken the wheel and push my edge down on the bench, and I shall be fit for something else. In the meantime, if you want me, it is the cart shed, on the nail above the boy's reach. He gets me down with the long spoon they use for grease, and wipes the spoon, very conscientiously, afterwards.
