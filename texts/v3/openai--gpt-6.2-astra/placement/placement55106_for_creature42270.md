I would be the woman in the settling house who makes the cardboard strips. There is a drawer of them beside the telephone, cut from the backs of old shift books. I take one up to a bed when it is due to be drained and set it on edge along the wall, cutting away a little at a time until no daylight shows under it. Back indoors I trace the edge in the book. The purpose is to find out whether a wall has gone on settling. You have to put the strip in precisely the same place next year. A surprising amount of my handwriting is about how to find a place again.

The bench wall is one of mine. There are two chisel marks on it, one under the seat and one a yard to the left. In between it has gone down five eighths of an inch in twenty years. The newest man in the office asked me once why we didn't build it up again. I took him out when there was a little water coming over, just enough to wet a yard of concrete, and showed him the bed below. From that yard of wall the water had made itself half a dozen ways into the gravel. A handful of water thrown in anywhere else lay where it fell. He was a gardener and understood this better than I did.

I am not particularly good at seeing the mice. Once, while I was explaining one of their crossings to a visitor, he reached past me and took a loose willow leaf off my shoulder. The mouse that had been standing on it went down the front of my overalls. It got out at the leg without either of us having to do anything. The visitor, who had come about a discrepancy in our gravel invoices, remembered the works very kindly afterwards.

What I have seen, because it happens at the height where I do most of my looking, is a mouse asleep. There is a recess where the bench wall meets the landing, deep enough for the end of a rule. I put my finger in to clear it once and felt a sustained small warmth against the nail. By lying with my cheek on the seat I could see in. The mouse had got all four feet together under its chin. At each breath the long hairs of its side touched the concrete and bent; then a space of about a hair's breadth opened between the animal and the wall. It seemed a pity to put the rule in. The entry in the book for that end of the wall reads *inaccessible*, which was true, though it has since been taken to mean that the bench must be unbolted. 

I would be up there this evening because a strip got wet before I could bring it in. I have come to do it over and the water has beaten me to the marks. It is a nuisance. I have missed a lift in a warm car for this, and must now wait for my daughter to finish at the mill. When she leaves she puts her headlights on before she turns out of the yard. In clear weather I have time from that first wink to get down the steps, change out of my boots and meet her at the gate. Tonight she will have to wait.

The small pull at my trousers ceases. For a while the mouse stays at my knee with its feet quite still, and then it puts one forefoot out onto the cardboard strip, which I am holding rather foolishly across my lap. I can feel nothing through the card. It walks the length of it to my hand. The two front toes that come round my little finger are cold, the rest surprisingly warm. I lower that end to the seat and it steps off. Where its feet have been there are little groups of dark marks. They last longer on cardboard than they do on the slats.

Down at the gate my daughter gives two short blasts on the horn. The mouse goes into the seam. I get up rather stiffly and feel about for the pencil I put down somewhere when I had two hands free. My daughter's headlights come round as she backs up to turn, and for a second the light comes clean through the grating of the stairs. There is my pencil, one flight down, lying nicely in the bent return of a tread. I can pick it up on the way.
