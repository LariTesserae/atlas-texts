I would be in the cold water under the nosing of the step. From the stair there would be no seeing me. From below, if one could get one's cheek against the riser, there would be a short white stroke in the drop, apparently hung a little way out from the stone. This is an effect of looking through the belly of the drop. I would in fact have hold of the husk with both feet.

The feet are at the same end. They fit on either side of the fold that used to go uphill, which is rather stiff and makes a good rail. I can let go with one foot and reach past it with the other, and in this way I can go the length of the husk. It is seldom necessary. Water runs to the lowest point of the rail and I do most of my business there, leaning out backwards until the skin of the drop dimples against the hairs of my head. When I draw back there is usually something on the hairs. I pass them between my lips as one might draw a stalk of grass through the teeth. The yellow dust is rather large for me. What I get is the stuff that lodges in its creases.

There are two quite different weathers in my drop. In one, the stone is cold clear through and water comes down the rail behind my feet. I have to lean a long way out for a meal. In the other the stone takes warmth from above; the skin of the drop comes steadily in, bringing everything on it, and for a time I can feed without reaching at all. Then it presses the hairs flat against my face. I walk backwards up the rail into the narrow place where the husk has opened away from the stone. Even when the drop is gone there is water there. It is too tight a place to eat in, but there is just room to stand with one foot a little in advance of the other, as I was when the water gave out.

For much of my life that is all the shape I have: two feet apart by the breadth of a grain, and a head pressed down between them. The white stroke one sees in the full drop is my gut. With nothing in it I am quite clear. Once a man took hold of the step in dry weather and broke off a piece of the rail. There were four of us in the pinch of skin beside his nail. Three went away with his hand. I stayed because my forward foot had hold of a bit of the fold that did not break.

It is when the cistern spills that I get to leave the husk whole. At first it feels like the end of a dry spell. I ease my head out and wait for the hairs to touch the skin of the drop. This time there is no skin within reach. The water bends my head down and goes on bending it until my mouth is beside my feet. I take hold with the hairs, let go with the feet, and am turned inside the running sheet as neatly as a cuff. By the time I have both feet fast again it may be on another husk altogether. Sometimes the thing they close on is soft. Then it opens under me along the rail, and I am holding by one foot to each half while the young thin animals come out between my legs.

I have been inside the cistern once in this fashion. One of the young ones folded an edge over my head. For a while I went about in its keeping, taking food off the underside of its fold, which it could not very well get at for itself. The water there was so warm that I kept drawing in my head, expecting the drop to be nearly gone. It was a considerable time before I could bear to put it out straight. We came down on mud. Something drove deep into the mud beside us and lifted us both, and after a long passage with hair on every side of me we were on hard clean stone with a taste of yellow in the water. There I let go. There was a little run still going out over the sill. I turned with it twice and caught.

My present rail is a worn one. The fold is quite smooth as far as I can walk and at the low end it has begun to split. In still weather a second drop forms on the splinter. It is no farther from mine than the length of my body, and sometimes, leaning out to feed, I press my head clear through the skin of my own water into the skin of the other. The two then run together very suddenly. For the rest of that day I have a great depth under me. There is a piece of brown wing in it at present, turning slowly, with a row of little notches like the notches in the outer stair. I have been down it as far as the third notch. My feet fit there too.
