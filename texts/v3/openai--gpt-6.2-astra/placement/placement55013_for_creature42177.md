I would be under the short toe, in the nick. There is just room there for a little more mountain. In the mornings I would have taken myself for the light that comes on when the toe curls. It takes a number of mornings, and one exceptionally long curl during which the light passes clear across me, to discover that I am the cheek of stone on one side of it.

I have a face the color of the shoulder, but where the shoulder is a smooth pink I am rather coarsely grained. There are three white grains in a row, of which the middle one stands out. This is the one the toe is going to rub. Already it has a polish the other two lack. In the afternoon a face in the sac can sometimes be seen in it, drawn out thin, with a tremendous distance between the pads of its chin. Once there are two faces, one coming down the fold and the other going up, and on passing they both stop. I would like to keep them there a little. The one going up has a speck of meal on its forehead. It is the first yellow thing to have appeared in the grain. Then the mother draws in a pleat and I have the underside of a leaf for the rest of the day.

On the day the foil comes down there is very little warning in the hollow. The animal sleeps, the mother works at a hair so thoroughly blanched that one wonders what she finds in it. But for some time past I have been sensible of a second face of my own, a rough one turned in toward the stone. One after another the hollows in it have acquired their answering knobs. When the last is fitted there is a tiny jar, much less than the tick overhead. The shoulder takes the foil on a slant. It slides the breadth of the white grain and stops. The youngster, which has slept through the whole descent, wakes at the stop and puts its heel down hard against me.

The toe is warm. For all the time its shadow lay there I had got this wrong. I had expected something cool, like the extinguishing of the little point of sun. There is a ridge across the underside of the toe, and a powder in the furrow behind it; in the powder are bits of the skin of the sac. The youngster stays a long while on its belly on the foil. It has only lately come by its full weight and appears to be attending to the way the weight is distributed, shifting a little of it here, a little there. At length it gets its chin up. From where I am I can see no more of the operation. The heel bears down, the ridge flattens; the minute excess of stone on my outer side comes away with the toe.

I go some distance on that foot. The pale floor, which looked bare from the shoulder, is crossed by innumerable little wrinkles, all running one way, too low to give the mother purchase but quite sufficient for a foot of this size. Where they give out, the youngster lies down. We spend the hour of sun halfway between the foil and the green curve, with one of the five shadows gone from overhead. It is cooler under the heel than anywhere I have yet been, now that there is a whole warm animal between me and the sky.

Toward evening the youngster reaches the first upright hair. It takes the cut end in its mouth. At the quite ordinary business of getting a better grip it scuffs its heel, and here I part company with it. I lodge among the hairs just beyond the white patch, pink side down, the three grains uppermost. From inside the undercut the mother puts her head out. For a moment her chin rests on the polished grain. There is no face in it now; the two pads fit on either side.

I had thought the nick was my size because I was to stay in it. As it happens I fit very well here too. The roof is a little short on this side. When the mother goes back in, the last fold hangs outside among the hairs, and the small one in that fold can turn its finished side either toward the air or toward my white grain. It tries both. In the morning there is another grain beside me, quite dull, and no seam that I can feel between us.
