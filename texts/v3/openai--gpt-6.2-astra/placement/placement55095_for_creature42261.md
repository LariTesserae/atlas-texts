On the sill of the last shelter, just inside the sweep of the door, there is a brown mark about as long as the last joint of a finger. I would know it by the taste. I spent a morning with my mouth against it, working myself round from the niche so that I could put a shoulder to the flag. It tastes quite different from the stone on either side. There is iron there, I think. Long after I had shoulders broad enough to shift the flag without much thought, I would sometimes put my mouth down as I went by, to see if I had come to the right place.

I don't remember much of the night in the niche. What I remember very well is that the first thing I heard in the morning was not the scratching. It was a knock from rather far down in the flag. Somebody outside had the pebble in its mouth. It was trying to give me the rattle through a thickness of stone equal to the length of its own back. Of course none of the fine sound came through. What came through was the knock of a jawbone. Then there was a little interval, and the knock again, somewhat higher up. It was looking for my cheek.

There were six of them out there. By the time I got the door open I was very cross, and went over the sill without giving any of them my cheek, though several were offered. They had to turn round one at a time in a space where even one could barely get a purchase. From behind, in the morning light, I could see the white scrapes on all their elbows. I lay down on the back of the last one. It took us until the first pink water to get home.

I would be a fairly old one now, too heavy for the high crossings except on the best afternoons. There is a crossing lower down, hardly clear of the present stone, where you can lie at your ease and let the young ones step over you. They come across very proudly with things in their mouths. Occasionally it is something they ought not to have taken, a warm chip from under a sleeper, for instance, and the sleeper comes along a little later, feeling under everybody with its lips. I like it there. The sheet makes a good running seal along my back and takes some of the ache out of the polished places. Through it I can feel the feet approaching some time before I know whose they are.

I have come to possess the pebble. It isn't a particularly good one anymore. The speck has worn a groove for itself and sometimes has to be sucked quite hard to get it out of the groove. But the sound, when it comes, is very fine indeed. Two creatures with their cheeks together will hear a bright little succession of blows, and then, after they have almost drawn apart, one more. Nobody likes to miss that last one. There is a young one in our gallery who will stay pressed to me indefinitely on the chance that there might be two.

If somebody were left in the shelter, I would go in the morning. I would be among the last to arrive. There is no good way to hurry the elbows, and part of the brown ridge has flaked off since my time and exposes a sharp white grain to which one must attend. By the time I got there the others would have scratched quite enough. I would ask them, by passing my lips along their backs, to let me get close to the flag.

It is not necessary to find the cheek exactly. The lump on which the door turns goes some way into the sill; the sill is fitted close to the wall, and the wall is all of a piece with the niche. I would put my jaw down there, as low as it would go. From inside there would come, perhaps, the sound of a heel pulling loose of its print. Then nothing for a long while. There is a considerable distance between the niche and the door when one has only just begun to grow.

I would knock once more. Not higher this time. I would let the little one come and find the place.
