I would be down on one knee at the uphill side of a house, making the same mistake as the young birds. The tick seems to come from just under the place where you put your ear. You move a little way along the roof and it is under your ear there too. One of the birds has gone so far as to lay the side of its head flat on a tile. Its upper eye looks out quite calmly at the other birds' knees.

What I would like to be is the animal making the much smaller sound after the tick. I have come up a long way. A joint in the bare rock, which a bird steps over without shortening its stride, has taken me the better part of the light to cross. I got my front edge over and hung there with my fringe working in air until the cool came up out of the joint. Then the far wall of it grew damp enough to take hold of. I have brought a little red mite up with me. It goes round and round my rim looking for the next animal.

There is a good smell under the eaves, the smell of a riverbed when you lift a stone out of it. I work my way in. For a while nothing much happens. A bird puts a foot on the shell over mine and I feel each toe arrive separately, the long middle one first, and then the little catch of the claw. By the time it takes its foot away there is a space between my back and the thing on which I felt the footstep. I can move in that space. It is a considerable surprise, having spent my whole life up to now moving the back too.

Here I find I have less inclination than I expected to go down to the water. The soft animal manages very well without me. It rests a while with its hind fringe still in the doorway of its old self, like a person sitting on a sill with wet feet. Then it draws the last threads after it. I stay where the bird put its foot.

I would be one of the low tiles, I think, within sight of daylight, with a flaw in one edge. Dew runs along the underside of me as far as the flaw and drops there. In a long run of dry weather there is a dark spot on the floor no bigger than the end of a grass stem. The mite stays by it. When there is a nest farther in, a bird on the way to sit will stop here and touch the spot with its bill. You could tell which of the two was coming without seeing either: one stops on the way in, the other on the way out.

For several seasons there is room under me for a wing and very little else. I lose my sharp lip on the outer side. On the inner I keep, in perfect condition, the shallow groove into which a neighbor's fringe once fitted. A brown chick discovers the groove with the top of its head. It is too tall now to go straight along this stretch of passage, and for two days it goes through with its head tipped to one side. On the third it learns to bend its legs. That evening it comes back from the open rock in such a hurry that it forgets. There is a quite astonishing knock. All the young birds waiting outside stoop at once.

I had thought the long part of being a shell would come after things like that, when the door had worn another way and nothing came in. But the house goes on growing uphill. In time I am well inside it. A little light still comes through the flaw, pink in the morning, gone by the time the birds change places. One morning at the end of the dry season the light goes out early. There is a soft pressure underneath me, first at one end, then all along the groove. Something has come in off the bare stone and is getting its breadth back in the cool.

This one is rather smaller than I was. There will be a space left over. All night it lies close against me; where my edge is chipped its new edge turns up a little, into the light. By the next evening it can carry that shape downhill. I stay in the house. A long way from here, in a bed I have never grazed, there will be one lid that does not fit quite flush against the others. Even when all the eyes are shut there will be a way in there for a red mite.
