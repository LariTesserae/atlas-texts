On Thursday mornings I should be in the box under the milk boy’s seat at the depot. It is a good box, made to take six pint bottles laid on their sides. The partitions have been knocked out of it, but the notches for them are still there, and a person with a job of sewing can wedge a card of buttons upright in a pair of notches and have both hands free. I should be the person with the sewing. There is room under the seat for my box and me if he sits at the other end to do his boots.

I should have charge of the aprons. Not just the depot aprons. There are the long ones worn by the women who silver the backs of mirrors, which must have no pockets because of what might get carried home in them, and the school cook’s apron, which has a pocket clear across the front. The little pale hand that comes out behind the bread oven has worn a smooth place on the left end of that pocket. The cook is right-handed. It took us a while to work that out.

There is very little in the valley that actually wears through. A thing generally wears away from some other thing, and what one has to do is bring the two back into a tolerable relation. The milk boy, for instance, has a rectangle of doubled cloth on the belly of his apron where the corner of the crate rests. He used to bring the crate up against it with a little hitch of his knee. For some months I went on thickening the patch and he went on coming in with a bruise just below it. In the end I made him bring me an empty crate and stand still. He no longer needed the hitch. His arms had got to be the length at which he could take the weight straight off the sill. I unpicked the rectangle and moved it down an inch. Underneath was the blue the whole apron had been when they gave it to him.

I am smaller than I had supposed when I first thought of the box. To unpick a seam I sit astride it and draw the thread up between my knees. The milk boy has never seen me whole. He has seen two feet sticking out of the fold of his apron and once, in the bright side of a churn, what he took to be a face until he turned round and found only the back of my head. He leaves me my pay in the form of the last mouthful in the bottle. I like it best in winter when it comes sliding out all in one piece and can be eaten off the end of a needle.

It was his daughter who first gave me anything to make rather than mend. She came down on a Monday by way of the long ward, with a little red insistence on one cheek where her grandmother had tried to remove a spot of butter with the corner of a towel. She put her hand into the box palm up. I had to climb onto it to see what she wanted. In the pad of her first finger was a narrow groove, and across the groove a split, no longer than one stitch of mine. She wanted something she could put on her finger at night.

The first thing I tried was felt. She brought it back the next morning. It was quite comfortable, she said; she simply couldn’t tell what the other girl was saying. They had spent a quarter of an hour on a word which turned out to be *still*, as in are you still there. I tried a little sleeve of the stuff they put between bottles. That was better until it twisted. At last we came to a bit of soft kid with a slit in it. The edges of the slit held the crack in her finger shut and the line lay on the skin between. I made one for the girl opposite too, off the measure of her finger taken in bread dough. She had pressed so hard that on the other side of the dough you could read, backwards, a little of the geography lesson on her slate.

After that I went up quite often. There is work enough in a school, and I liked the hour after the sun had gone from below. I liked sitting with my legs over the edge of the decking, waxing thread. When the children brushed a bright place on the copper under me there came up through the seat of my trousers a most agreeable warmth, which I have never been able to persuade anybody was not a thing I had imagined. I got to know which of the girls was talking through the line without having the least idea what either of them said. One let go of it altogether between remarks. The other kept just enough hold that a drop of water would travel along it to her thumb.

The evening the decking came down I was up there shortening a cuff. It was not the first of the month. There was no china on the floor. The blue saucer came to the front of the shelf as the girl had said it would, and hung there with quite a third of itself unsupported. I waited for the jar, with my needle in the air so as not to drive it into my hand. What happened was that somebody in the next street sneezed, much nearer the window than a sneeze had ever been before. The girl opposite looked out. Then she went back to her supper. We did not know until the milk boy’s wife went to shut the yard gate and had to lift it a little over the new pavement.

I stayed the night. In the morning the daughter was down before me, sitting on the inside of the front doorstep in her one finished cuff. I could have done the other on her wrist but she was afraid I would want her to move just when the knock came. We had a long wait. Nine minutes on the depot plan had not allowed for all the people who would want to send an empty by the first crate to go through, including several who had to finish their milk.

He knocked quite low on the door. From where I was, in the turn-up of the finished sleeve, it was a loud, hollow, very civil sound. She opened to him and he stood there. All her life she had known to take the crate from him while he got his second leg over the sill. Now there was no need. For a moment she had both arms out and nothing in them.

“Here,” he said, and took off his cap.

She took it. He came in sideways after all, on account of the crate. And that is where I should like to have been, holding on inside the cuff while she put her father’s cap, for the time being, on her own head.
