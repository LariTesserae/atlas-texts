I would be in the third cup down from a bend in the ribbon. The cup above mine has a little chip out of its lower lip. When it is nearly full, a bead of water stands in the chip, getting rounder for quite a long time before there is any change below. Then a dark mark runs down the face of the lime. It was by way of that chip that I came, though there must have been considerably more water in it then.

I came in thin end uppermost. The first thing I got hold of was a thread of green so fine that when I pulled on it I brought it up with me. For a while I hung under the surface eating my way down the thread. By the time I reached the place where it was fastened I had come right side up on the floor. There was a good deal to eat just there. I went no farther that afternoon than the length of the thread I had mistaken for a foothold.

Now I have two plates and a place on the west wall. My seam is rather easy to pass over on a first circuit. It has a wide mouth with a grain of lime caught in it. To either side of the grain there is room to put a head, but on one side the space narrows almost at once; on the other it continues level for rather more than the length of a foot. In going in I keep the grain against the soft margin of my second plate. There is a little notch in the margin now. A person examining the plate by itself might suppose that all second plates had that notch.

In dry weather there would be very little to distinguish my place from the rest of the wall. A tick starting a yard away passes through both my plates. Nothing in me is quick enough then to follow the tick. Even the first rain makes its way some distance past me into the bank before I begin to swell. The grain that fitted quite loosely beside my head comes to press there. That pressure is the first I have of a wet morning. By the time I can draw out past the grain the hollow has had a night full of things happening in it without me.

I seldom make the first bright cut along the red line. There is nearly always an edge already, and I keep my comb half on it, half in the soft growth alongside. In that way I get some purchase on the wall without having to go over ground picked clean. Where the line passes my seam it dips by about the thickness of a plate. Years ago I would lose the cut there and go some way up the wall before finding anything tender again. Now the thick end of my foot is already turned down when I reach the grain. The one behind me, coming upon a continuous cut, makes the dip quite neatly on its first circuit. What took me several wet seasons to learn lasts for the next comer just as long as the edge stays fresh.

I should like to be found there on a morning when there has been more rain inland. The water in my cup is still, with young ones hanging all round it. There is no rain falling. On the high side the chip in the next cup has gone dark; a little later there is a coolness along the wall against my foot. All the young tilt the same way. For the space of a comb stroke their thin ends draw fine traveling dents in the surface, and then the water goes smooth over the sill. I stay at the wall. Presently something touches the margin of my plate from above. It is a single green thread with a young one working its way down, so little weight to the two of them that I can go on grazing with the thread laid across my back.
