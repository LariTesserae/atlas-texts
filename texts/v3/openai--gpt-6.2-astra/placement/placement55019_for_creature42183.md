I would be a scale of limestone a little way below the knee place. For a number of summers I would stand on edge with the others. Water would come as far down as the corner that had been uppermost on the cliff. That corner would lose its edge; the one opposite would stay sharp enough to take a curl off a fingernail. If I were ever brought up into daylight it would be quite easy to tell which way up I had stood.

What I know of the open pass would come mostly through one point of contact. A long thin stone lying against me at the top goes nearly to the underside of the slab. The knock of a feeding animal comes down it very well. The two knocks of the big one come separately, with time between for the long stone to come quite still against me. A person kneeling to drink is different. There is a steady pressure and then, usually, a little extra weight just before the knee comes off. In a dry summer there might be only the one person. I would feel the getting up as distinctly as the kneeling down.

One afternoon a new scale would come in and I would go over. It would be a fall of less than the width of my own face. The sharp corner would catch in a nick and the round one go on down until I lay very nearly flat. The long stone would be standing free of me. I would lose the feeding noises just like that, in the middle of a thaw, with several hours of good water still to come.

There would be other things. My lower face, which had been up against stone since before the pass was a notch, would now have a little depth of air under it. In time a foot would find the round corner and come feeling along this face upside down. The buttons would take hold especially well there; there would be none of the loose meal that comes away from a surface long exposed. For the length of a winter I might be the last piece of a descent. An animal would hang from me by its forefeet until its folded hind feet touched the floor. Coming back with a young one on its wrist it would go the long way, up a slanting heap in the next room. It could manage the turn under my edge well enough on three feet, but there would be nowhere in the middle of the turn to put the young one.

I like to think of that next summer. Something well below would give a little and the floor would come up at one end. No more than the height of one of the pale buttons, but it would make a difference. Coming up by the old long way, the parent would put out a foot across the gap, as it had perhaps done every time, and this time the foot would stay. It would bring the youngster up over my round edge. For a while there would be the weight of both of them, then the weight of the young one alone. It would have taken hold of me in passing. The parent, half a body length on, would have to come back and offer the wrist again.
