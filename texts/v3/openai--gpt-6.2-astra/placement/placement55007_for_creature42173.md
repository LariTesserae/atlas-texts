I would be the woman who comes down with her dinner. I like the idea of going to work with a piece of onion pie in a basket big enough to carry a bedspread. In the morning the pie slides all the way across at the turn of the stair. Coming up is another matter. There is generally something to be taken to somebody who lives a little way past my door.

The bench that gave me the scar was quite a good bench. It had a cleft in one end, and while that end stood against the wall you could stick your paddle in the cleft and have both hands free to take a knot out of a wet drawstring. Then they turned it about to make room for a wider basin. I stuck my paddle in the old place without looking, it went clattering down behind the bench, and in getting over to fetch it I took quite a strip off my shin. There was a month's worth of advice about that leg. One of the sisters said to keep it covered and the other said to give it air, and since I worked half the day with each of them I daresay it had the proper amount of both.

If you came looking for me late in the day I would be up on the landing. There is a place for me there which isn't apparent when the poles are full. One of the ropes ends at the rail. When the pole below has been stripped of all but the last cloth, it is very little lighter than the weight at the other end of the rope, an old oven weight with a hole through it. A child can lower it. Once the cloth comes off, the weight wins by a good deal. Somebody on the landing has to take a turn of rope about the rail or the bare pole goes up into the rafters and there's an end to conversation with the baker for the day.

It is a nice job to finish with. The rope goes cool through the hand where it has lain against damp linen, then warm where it has been up by the tiles. I can see over the cloth from there. This is how I know about the toad. I have seen it come out so often that what I look to see now is which way it will go, whether toward the trough or along the foot of the warm wall. It sometimes sits with one foot still under the rest as if the cost of bringing out that last foot had only just occurred to it.

I did once try to give the boy a sight of it. I clicked my tongue and tipped my chin down. He looked up, naturally, and so did his mother, and between them they let the middle of the cloth sag into a bowl of rinse water. There was a little blue bag of water hanging between them. They had to go back a bench at a time to pour it out. I haven't done that again.

What I can do is give the little girl an end of rope. There is a yard of it past my hand that has no work to do. At first I gave it to her for the pleasure of seeing those fingers close on something at last. Then one evening, while I was settling my basket on my hip, she took her turn about the rail exactly as I do, laid the end across the standing part, and put her thumb on the crossing. The pole stayed where it was. I kept my hand close, but she had it.

She doesn't always want it. Yesterday there was a beetle in a hollow of the stair trying to get the skin of a pea turned over. I did the rope myself. On my way up with the basket I had to put a foot on either side of her, and from that position got such a good view of the proceedings that I set the basket down. The skin went over. There was another beetle underneath.
