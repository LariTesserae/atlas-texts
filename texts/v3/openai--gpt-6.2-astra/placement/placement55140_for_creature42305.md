I would be under the second of the three steps, where a wedge of stone has come loose without coming out. There is a slit on either side of the wedge. Through one of them you can see, in a very narrow strip, the sky. Through the other the water comes up carrying things that the pool has let go down. It is quite a good place to wait for a back.

It has to be an old one. A young back is scarcely more than a flake, and if I put a foot on the edge to turn it over I generally break it. The old ones have a rolled edge I can get hold of. I catch one as it goes by, turn it hollow side down, and walk in under it until the ridge inside fits the groove between my shoulders. Then I lift. For a moment, until the water has run out, I am carrying about twice what I weigh. After that it sits quite comfortably. My two hind feet hold on to the last of the overlapping lips. That leaves me four to walk on.

From above I must look very like one of the animals that grow these backs. There is even that little hesitating movement at the edge of the wet. In my case it is the back that hesitates. I have already put two feet out on the dry stone, but the low rim of my roof is still stuck to the water. It comes free with a tick. I can go on for a surprising distance then. I take the wet with me, a lining of it against the inside of the lime, which tastes at first of the pool and toward the end of the journey rather strongly of myself.

I do not cross from pool to pool. What I cross is a place no wider than a man's palm on the underside of the step. On one side of it is my slit and on the other is a hollow which is always sweating. Nothing runs into it and nothing runs out. The grey skin there grows thick enough to wrinkle. I can put my mouth against a wrinkle and go steadily forward with my eyes shut, rolling it up. It is the best part of the day. The back makes a faint grating on the ceiling all the time I am eating. When the grating stops I know I have come to the deep end of the hollow, and there is room to put the thing down.

I used to put it down wherever I finished. Once I spent most of a day feeling about for the edge of it. A white back on wet limestone is very little to see, and I was looking for it the right way up, whereas I had left it balanced on one lip and the beating of the stone had worked it over. I walked about inside it several times. Now I leave it against a little tooth that projects from the ceiling. I can find the tooth with my feelers even when the slit has gone quite black.

There are two or three backs there now that I have brought across and not taken home. The cool sweat makes them thin; the ridge that fits me goes first. In the oldest there is a little round window worn right through the crown. On a bright afternoon a thread of light comes through the slit, strikes the wet below, and puts a moving patch of silver inside this back. A very small grazer lives in it. It must have come over clinging to my roof. It works around the window with its forefeet, and when it passes through the patch I can see every tooth of its comb, enlarged on the white curve behind it.

I could carry it back. So far there has been plenty for it to eat. When I leave in the evening I feel for the tooth, take the sound back down over my shoulders, and set out across the palm's breadth of dry. Behind me the little one goes on grazing in a country which, until I brought it there, had never had a track in it.
