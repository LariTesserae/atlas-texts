I should be at the works office, on the far side of the drawing, with a damp square of paper stuck to the ball of my thumb. Mine would be the hand that had taken the square out. It is quite easy to make a bad job of a hole in paper as thin as that. I should have had to change the blade.

My bench would be the one under the high window. When a cow stood over it I should have leave to go to tea. There would be a girl at the next bench doing the fair lettering, who could do quite a bit by touch provided somebody told her when she came to the end of a word. Her letters would be on the envelope that came in beside the salt. The drawing inside, apart from the hole, would be the work of a draughtswoman called Ada, whose husband had got the minute hand back on the watch. He had returned it with a little screw left over, which was why the old man was still coming to the office.

I think I should be younger than anyone would suppose from the knife. There would be a block of wood on my stool to bring my elbows up level with the bench. It had belonged to my predecessor, a woman of four foot ten, who took it away when she retired and then found she couldn't bear to look at it in the house. On the underside, in her early hand, were the names of people she had thought she might marry. One of them was Linn's grandfather. I had been quite a while at the works before I knew him well enough to tell him, and by then I knew him well enough not to.

On Monday it would be my business to go down to the opposite landing with the girl and the pillbox. The box had a dent on one edge of the lid, over which Linn put her thumb. Under the thumb there was a scrabbling so light it might have been the pulse in her own hand. We waited for the shutter of the pumping station to open. The oilman had agreed to give us the length of one run in amber light.

It was then that Linn asked me about the husk.

‘They'll want the weight,’ I said, for I had seen the little brass scales set out on Ada's desk.

‘I mean after the weight.’

I had brought my knife, a rule, three pegs, the flight drawing rolled in a quill and an india-rubber bulb with which to blow the dust off the chalk mark. Nothing I had brought would hold a bat. I took the block off my stool and put it on the landing with my cap on top, crown down. It made quite a soft place, and was as near as we could get to where the husk would come down without undertaking a little flight of our own.

Across the hollow her grandfather lifted one wrist to his collar. We could see the collar stir. The station opened its eye.

Of the flight I remember chiefly the feet. In all our drawings of the little animal we had given her a sort of tidy pointed end. Her feet actually trailed behind her with the toes spread well apart, and in the moment when there were two of her there were twenty toes, every one picked out in the pump light like a currant thorn in winter. Linn felt the box dip. At that same instant something struck the peak of my cap and turned it over.

The bat got her worm. She ate it with her eyes shut, working her jaws sideways in a fashion none of us had seen, as all her previous meals had taken place under a cuff. Her grandfather came round by the wall. He had his spectacles in his hand, but before he put them on he felt for the nick in her ear.

There was very little to weigh. The cap had tipped it out on the block. It lay there with its fur all in one piece, down to the whorl on its breast where the fur grew the other way. The pin of the scales stayed against the stop until Ada shut the door on the draught, and then it came away about the breadth of the little screw the old man was forever bringing in. Ada wrote the weight on the grandfather's clean cuff. Linn was given the husk in the pillbox, the other occupant having no further use for it.

My part came some months later. The single fiber had gone green and flattened, and as it spread they pegged it out for width, much as you would a skin. I went down on my stomach on the new boards with the paper flattened under my chin. The square was there in the right place. Its rim, however, had begun to curl in on itself; the stuff was disposed to heal. I cut it back to the line, the first considerable piece of work I had been trusted to do in anything thicker than paper. From below, to my surprise, came a smell of hot linen. The old man had got up and ironed his counterpane for the occasion.

I kept the four narrow slips I cut out that day. Three went into the office press, to see what allowance one should make for shrinkage. The fourth I took to the plum-colored door. Linn had been keeping the pillbox shut for fear of a draught when what she wanted, naturally, was to look. We bent the slip into a little hoop that fitted inside the box and stretched a piece of blind moth silk across it. With this for a lid we could see both the bat in the box and the bat hanging head down from the grandfather's pocket, attending to a difficult place under her arm.

That, I think, is where I should find myself most often: not at the high window, though my wages would come to me there, but at the low table with a piece of something left over from work. Linn would have saved whatever it was that needed fitting to it. Her grandfather would turn the lamp up one notch, for which there was no charge after the second bell. And when we had finished and lifted our hands, there would generally be a small mark in the table between us, fresh and pale, the exact shape of the thing we had made.
