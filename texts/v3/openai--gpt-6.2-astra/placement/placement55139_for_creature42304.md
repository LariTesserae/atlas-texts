I would be on the underside, though not quite where the soles show through. I think I would live a little way round the oldest knee, where the fold is still thick. Someone looking down into the crease might stay there all afternoon without knowing I was in it.

There is an advantage to a thick place. A foot set down on thin skin comes through as a foot, with a harder part in front where the toe bears. Here the pressure travels a little way sideways before it reaches my ceiling. I get a low round hill, delightful to put a sole on. If I am combing when it comes I need not interrupt myself. It will be some time in rising and nearly as long in going away.

For a while I should take these hills to be a property of the knee. Quite a number of things happen to it in the course of a day. In the morning cold it contracts enough to bring two of my feet together; by noon there is a hand’s breadth of good drinking all along a line which at evening will not yield a drop. Nor does everything that happens above concern me. A grazer can reach a surprising distance without moving its feet. There might be a whole morning of furious work at the black leaf of which I should know nothing whatever.

What would undeceive me, I think, would be getting a foot caught. There are little scales on the white part of the fold which lie quite flat until one puts a sole across two of them. Then, if the ceiling stretches, a bit of amber goes up into the crack. Pulling only draws the scales tighter. I should pull. Five of the hills would go away at once. The sixth would deepen until the edges of the crack stood apart and I could get my foot out.

It would be very tempting to spend the rest of my life accounting for this. From the other side it might have been no more than the necessity of leaning hard on one leg while feeling about with the other five for a tenant who appeared to have moved. At any rate, for several days afterwards, though I drank as usual and went about on the ceiling, one of my soles would have a little white pencil mark across it. At every step there would be a fine line along which I felt nothing, and on either side of it the common in all its detail.

I fancy I should come to know the other animal best by being mistaken about it. Once, late in the day, the familiar hill would come up under my left forefoot. I would move on to the next station and wait. Nothing. Back at the first place the hill would still be rising. Presently a cold thread would break through its top and wet me all down one side. It had been water all along. Meanwhile, some distance behind me, my companion would be standing with a foot in the air. By the time I found it the foot would be down again. There would be six ordinary places to stand. I would put my feet on them.

At evening anyone walking the ridge would go on round to the thin place to see the lights. I would feel the great soft passage of their weight and afterwards the much smaller adjustment of the creature overhead, rocked a little on its short legs. The sun would get under the field. For the few minutes it took to travel the length of my ceiling I could lift one amber foot and look through it at the lower commons, which would be gold wherever I held it and green wherever I took it away. Then I would have to set it down. Something on the other side would have been trying the empty place, very lightly, for some time.
