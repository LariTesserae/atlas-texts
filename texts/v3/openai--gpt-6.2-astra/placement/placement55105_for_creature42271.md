I would be on the underside of a leaf that had been held up for breathing. When the hand let go there would be a little slap of water, followed some seconds later by a much smaller one. The second would be me getting back aboard.

I think I would be a beetle, black when seen in the hand but under there almost silver. The silver would be air caught in the short hair of my belly. I could stay down a long time on a good bellyful. To renew it I would back along the midrib until my hind end came just clear of the water. This would be the usual extent of my acquaintance with the upper side of a leaf.

There is a great deal to eat underneath. Some of the finest roots of the herb hang down from the joints in bunches of five or six, each root wearing a stocking of soft brown growth. I would draw a root between my forelegs and work my way down it backwards, leaving a length of white thread with a little brown tassel on the end. A fish coming through would put an end to this without necessarily eating me. There would be a brief pressure as of a great thumb, and I would be on my back among the roots with all six legs full of brown wool.

The climbers would be worth holding on through. At the place where one had put its hand, the stems would stay pressed apart for a while. Air would get in under leaves that ordinarily lay quite flat on the water. There one could clean oneself, which is difficult to do hanging head down and exceedingly difficult to do on the surface with the little herons about. Several of us would collect in such a place. One after another we would pass our hind legs over our backs, making no more disturbance overhead than if somebody had rubbed a finger very lightly over the nap of the green.

It would take a particular accident to bring me into the forest. A young climber, coming out of the herb, might have a bit of stem caught in the hair of its tail. It would go up the trunk with this trailing behind it, quite a handsome train of five round leaves, the last still carrying enough water for me to go on feeding for some way. Then the train would catch in the fork. By the time I got to the end of the root there would be air where the water ought to be and no silver on my belly at all.

From that height the knocking of the frogs would come up through the wood into my feet. I would stay still for several knocks. Below the fork would be a palm fan, every pleat in it dark at the bottom, and below that, though there would be no way of seeing it, shell quite hard enough to ring. I would open my wing cases. There would be two fairly large serviceable wings folded under them, a surprise after so much getting about by the roots.

I doubt I would fly as far as the lagoon. I would come down in one of the wet pleats and spend the evening working along it towards the stem. A climber coming there to drink would draw its finger down the next pleat, then down mine. For a second I would be on the ball of the finger, in a drop so round that my feet would find no edge to it. Then the finger would tilt. I would go down with the drop into the dark of the fan, where last year's pleats were still holding water, and where, on putting out a foreleg, I would find a root already stripped to a white thread with a little brown tassel on the end.
