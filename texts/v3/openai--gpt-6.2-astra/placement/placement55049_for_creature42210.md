I think I would come in with the freight. There is one person in a freight party who goes the whole way with the account, though the people carrying the loads change twice. You want the same person at both weighings of a sack of meal. The people at the pass can usually show that a sack has gained weight on the way down; the people on the shelf can usually show that some meal is missing. Both are generally right. I would be quite good at cutting the stitches along the bottom of a sack and getting a finger in far enough to tell how much of the meal had gone to paste.

My place at supper would be at the end, since I might have to get up. There is also rather more room there for a foot in a shoe. I would have learned to walk on the roofs by then, but not what to do with my shoes on coming indoors. A shelf person can shake most of the water off a bare foot in one good shake. If you do this in a freight shoe the person opposite gets a warm spoonful in the lap some time after you have sat down.

I would be the one the child asks about the view. Not, I think, because of anything I could tell her. There are people at the table who have been over the pass a hundred times. But I would have the account board on my knee, with a stump of black lead tied to it and the back clean.

“Which part is us?” she would ask, drawing one line along the board.

I would put a mark a little way in from the end.

“Can you see the table?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

It is an objection to which I would have no answer at all. You can see a bite out of the lip where a piece came down before there were houses. You can see the pale run below the freight outlet, which never quite dries out. I know the way from that outlet to the table in the dark, including the place where you turn one shoulder to let the flour bin past. None of that is much use on the back of a board. I would rub the mark out.

Here is where I find I want to stay a while. Not to take the child to the pass. She will get there; the freight people take passengers, and a girl who can keep her feet on a wet roof will make something of the walk once she gets over the indignity of having to be shown how to go uphill. I want to be there the evening after the question, when her mother comes to find me at the scales. She has a length of condemned sackcloth folded over her arm. Along one edge she has painted four yellow bars with the paint she uses on the engines. The bars are of different widths.

“Will any of those do?”

“For what?”

She would have to explain it to me. It was the letterer, after all, who had sat across from us and seen the mark rubbed out.

We would hang the cloth at the end of the table, weighting it with a cracked plate in a fold so that the wind off the valley couldn't turn it edge on. It would be behind the water most of the time. I would go up with the empty skins and look back at the gate change. There would be no yellow bars visible from the pass. No cloth, no plate, not even the interruption in the red that I distinctly remembered seeing on other journeys, when nobody was going to ask me for it afterward.

But on the way down there is a bend where the track doubles back to get round a bad piece of ground. People make for the inside of the bend, having little desire by that point in the journey to carry a load three unnecessary yards. I would have occasion to go to the outside, to catch a curd skin which had been working round on its cord and was about to come off somebody's back. From there, through the falling water, I would see a small steady patch the color of the grease on the scales.

I wouldn't tell the girl straight away. I would ask the letterer to take the cloth in. On the next journey I would go to the outside of the bend and there the patch would be again. Lichen, possibly, or a bit of daylight getting through a thin place in the fall. This would go down in the account. I keep such things on the back, where at either weighing you can run a damp rag over them and start afresh.

In the end it would be the plate that did it. Coming back with the girl one afternoon from the first weighing, I would take her to the bend, not promising anything. The cloth would be out drying. They would have washed the meal dust off it; there is no such thing as condemned sackcloth for very long in a house with five tables' worth of dishes. The gate change would catch us there. We would hear nothing. I think that would surprise her more than the sight of the stone. She would watch the water come apart along six miles of red and put a hand out to steady herself against a sound that didn't reach her.

The plate would turn once in its sling. A wink, much too white to be the cloth. Then another as the water took it. She would have her finger on the place before I could get down to her height to point it out.

“That's the end where you sit,” she would say.

After that I would have a different journey from the other freight people. Three yards longer. At supper I would still sit at the end, where they can get me out without disturbing anybody when a load comes in. But I would know, as I bent to undo my shoes, exactly where to look on the far slope for a person who could see the place where I was sitting. Not the person, of course. There would be nothing to see there but a bad piece of ground and the track going round it. For the first time in many journeys I would know how large, from here, a person was.
