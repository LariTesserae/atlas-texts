I would come down with the rain, but not as water. I would be the grit that gives the water in the machine prints, for the first hour or two, a slight milkiness. There is hardly enough of me in any one cup to cover the stipple of the clay. Once the water has cleared you can still see me by looking at the sunken end of a stalk. All along its upper side it is powdered like a little bread roll.

In most places that would be the end of me. Here the water withdraws so slowly, and so often by the same route, that I get made into something. The edge of a puddle hangs for a long while on a single grain. A little of me is left against that grain; next time there is a ledge to hang on. That is how I would find myself an actual part of the hollow, the low white sill around the smallest of the three cups. I would be much wider at one corner than the others. A machine that has left a tolerably regular three-cornered print in the earth has not necessarily stood level.

There are things that happen at that height which are not apparent from the steps. A dead frond, for instance, does not always come to rest when it touches the floor. If it comes down across my cup while there is water in it, the middle of the frond is supported and the two ends are not. It rides there for a day with its leaflets spread. Then the stalk catches on my wide corner. As the water goes down, the frond tips, very gradually, until one whole side of it is up in the warm air and the other side has gone soft against the clay.

Once there would be an animal on such a leaf. It would have been drinking at a notch when the stalk parted. For most of the day its weight would make no appreciable difference to the lie of the leaf. Late in the afternoon, though, the water would be so far down that when the animal moved the distance between two notches, one leaflet would lift clear and the next one settle. There would be this very small, quite orderly tipping going on in the dark.

I should like to be able to say that my wide corner is on the side toward the big stocking. In fact it is toward the valve house. But the slug, coming off the stalk, would keep for a while to the inside of my rim, where there is still a thread of water although the middle of the print is bare. It would be almost halfway round before it went up over the edge. From there the outermost leaflets of the ruff are not very far. Whether it got to them I would have no way of telling. I would have felt it only in passing, a sole so much broader than my crest that for a little way I was quite covered up.

What I would keep of it is not the track. The next filling would take care of that. But a speck of clay carried up on the sole would lodge on my top edge and the water would hang there, too. In a few years there would be a small white ear on the rim of the cup, of no particular use, on the side away from the house. If you reached down in the dark and went round me with the pad of a finger, you could find it.
