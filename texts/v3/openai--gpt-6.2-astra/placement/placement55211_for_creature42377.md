I would come out with the bed in the morning. For the first few feet I would have no choice of direction. A grass sheath has worked up into the fold above the tortoise's heel, and I am inside the sheath. Each time the foot takes the weight, the two edges of the sheath draw a little apart. Through the slit I can see the earth go by in short lengths, very distinctly, with an interval of straw color between one length and the next.

I am a small brown beetle, rather flat, with a coat so like the inside of the sheath that if you shook me out on your hand you might put me back with the chaff. My feet would give me away. I would be feeling for a way down between two of your fingers, and the little hooks would catch where you thought the skin was smooth.

At the end of one of its steps the tortoise keeps its foot in the air. It has put its head out sideways to reach something. This gives me time to work round to the underside of the heel and let go. I land on my back. For a while I have a very good view of the clearance under a tortoise. Then a moving bit of purslane comes within reach of my feet. The tortoise is drawing the stalk into its mouth from the other end; by holding on to one of the lower leaves I am turned over and set down again, several inches from where I fell.

Where a pair of leaves comes off the stalk there is a little cup, scarcely a cup at all until dust has collected across the lower opening. The nights are cool enough for a film of water to gather there. I go from joint to joint. Some are dry; at one I can put my whole face to the green skin and drink. By the time I have finished there is no difference that you could see between this joint and the others. There was hardly any difference before.

I spend the hot hours in the hollow of a grass stem that has broken across and lodged nearly level in its neighbors. It fits rather well. To get out I have to go backwards. Sometimes the wind rocks the unbroken stems and mine knocks gently against them, first at one end and then at the other. In the afternoon the knocking stops. Silt is coming in at the open end. What was a round spot of light behind me has a level bottom now, like a door with a sill.

Toward evening there is a much heavier knocking. Something has hold of my stem. I feel the pull and the release, and then a pull that goes on until the whole stem slips down through the grass. It lies at a slope now. At the lower end, just outside my feet, a worker is trying to take it under cover. The stem is considerably too long to turn the corner. Another worker comes up and, with a very good grip but no better idea of the difficulty, pulls too.

I could stay here a little longer. It is still warm inside, and there is room for all six feet to rest against the curved wall. But presently they will begin on the stem itself. I back out over the sill, bringing most of it with me in a soft flour on my wing cases. There is a chimney close by. I climb to the rim and open my wings a little without taking flight. Some of the flour slips down the outside of the chimney. A few grains go in. Down at the bend in the covered way the long stem has come round at last; I can feel the small jar of it through my feet.
