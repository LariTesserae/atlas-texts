On the upper face of the leaf, which the young bird was eventually to see, there is a little raft of empty midge skins. The feet of the skins stand up. One still has a wing, perfectly good to look at except that there is nothing with which to fold it. Seen against the cloud it is a milky colour. Seen with the dark of the bird’s eye behind it, it has seven colours laid close together along its edge.

I should have liked to be the bird for the time it took that colour to come out. But its breast is going hard against the fern. It has business of its own. In a moment it takes itself off, and the leaf, coming up rather smartly, delivers the skins to the wind.

My place would be farther in. Where a root has entered the ceiling of the works at a slant, water sometimes runs along it for a little way instead of going straight to the tip. There is a hollow on the upper side of one such root. Lying in the hollow you would be in the dark from the waist down, with the light of the third lamp on your chin. This is a good place for catching the small flies that go round the lamp. They have to come out of the light to turn, or seem to think they do. All evening they come round with the same side offered.

I would have a soft chin, for feeling them. For a long time that might be quite a sufficient account of me. A chin, and farther back a considerable stomach, and four feet which in repose would be indistinguishable from the dry little forks of the root. I would not be the animal that went down to drink. It would be a stirring event in the hollow when that one passed. My toes would take hold all along my length, one pair after the other, on seeing its shadow come immensely up the wall. Sometimes it would pass without the animal passing. There is an arrangement of two lights and the polished side of a wheel which does that. I would never get used to it.

What would make the hollow particularly mine is a sound. A fly settled on the root makes a little separate sound with each of its six feet. One caught and held under the chin makes rather a different one. The root is full of such news, going a long way in both directions. Some of it comes down. There is a coarse tearing, followed by three much finer tearings, which takes place just above the roof, often enough to be familiar and seldom enough that I come out to hear it. It is the shrew going in through the hanging threads, and then the young ones moving up to meet it; but in the hollow there would be no reason to divide the sound in that way. It would be one considerable visitor with a ragged beginning and a very delicate end.

There might be a season in which the last of the fine sounds ceased. For several nights I would go some way up the root. It would get colder towards the ceiling. At the place where the bark goes in there would be a thin crescent of light, not wide enough for the chin to go through, quite wide enough to put a foot in. I think this is where I should find myself: three feet taking a very good hold of the bark in the works, and the fourth quite out on the mountain, in the green sleeve, where a shrew not yet old enough to be afraid of much would take my longest toe between its teeth.

Very gently. It has been trying the ends of things all morning. I would draw the foot back and go down to the hollow carrying, in two small places on one toe, an acquaintance with the outside. The marks would close in a day or two. For much longer, in turning on the root, I would set that foot down rather flat.

And up in the sleeve the shrew would go on trying threads until it had had enough of them. One had pulled back.
