I would be at the lip, under the animal with the seeds on it. For a while there would be very little to see of me. Where its foot had passed, a line the breadth of a hair would turn green again somewhat sooner than the rest of the stone. The animal would come back along that line and eat most of it.

The shelf has two edges, one a little short of the other. A thin layer of the dark stone has come loose and still lies so exactly in its old place that only at the lip is it possible to get anything between the two. I would have a hold there. My green part would be outside, in the light such as it is, and the rest of me would lie between the layers, colorless and exceedingly thin. A portion well in from the edge might go the better part of a year without getting wet. When water did reach it, it would swell enough to lift the upper stone. Not by much. Along the lip a black line would appear, and out of it would come the ends of a number of little clear tongues, blunt from having grown so long against the roof.

I think the animal would know me best after the water had apparently gone. Its amber margin would come over the edge of the stain. Where it touched one of the tongues there would be a sudden darkening under its foot, as when one presses a finger on a damp cuff. It would stay and press again a little farther along. This would account for a piece of writing peculiar to that shelf: a row of short strokes with their ends hanging out over the drop. The animal could go a considerable way round the corner provided it kept its load of seeds on the upper side. Sometimes an empty coat would tip off. It would go down turning, showing alternately its pale inside and its rather ordinary brown back, and be visible a surprisingly long time.

Farther in, my manner of living would be different. There are hollows in the underside of the loose stone, shallow as the marks left by hail in mud. In each I would have room to put up a little thick growth, soft at first, then hard and dark red. The hollows would fit these growths very nicely because in part I would have made them. At each wetting a little mineral would come away in the water; at each drying some would be left in the red stuff. An old piece of me taken from one of these places would be quite heavy for its size. Rubbed on bare rock, it would leave a rusty mark. There would be no occasion for this to happen until the roof broke.

It would be the white hook that got it open in the end. For its first season the root would follow the very narrow space that I kept wet. The next season it would be too thick for the space, and for a while we would both do very well: the stone tipped up, the root snug underneath, a little more light coming to my green edge. One day the seed-clad animal, going its accustomed way, would find the stone move. It would draw itself together and sit without eating until the movement stopped. The next time the chambers breathed out, a flake as broad as a hand would come off and go away into the cloud. The animal would be left on the fixed side. So would the plant, though with a pale elbow of root showing that would never quite take on the color of its stem.

I would be in plain sight then. There would be a wet red stippling on the newly uncovered shelf, an inch or two of green fringe, and among the stipples a dry gray tract where the water had not got to me in time. A few of the red knobs would have gone with the flake. The rest would soften in the air. By the next draw their tops would have split, each into four low lips with a little fine rust between them.

Most of that would go down the seams with the snow grains. A little would catch in the rubbed places on the animal's back, where one seed coat lay over another. I would have no means of keeping it there. It might all wash out at the first trickle. But on the underside of the empty coat that had once fitted the white hook, close up by the pointed end, there would be a red smudge too far in for the water to get at readily. That is where I would find myself next: going along at the pace of a broad amber foot, dry, with a chip of the old mountain in me, under a roof that admitted a beautiful straw-colored light.
