I would be in the tick at the foot of the cliff, though it would take a while to find out which end of the sound I belonged to. Standing out on the plain, if there were anything there to stand, one would look up for the source of it. In fact one would do better to get down quite low. There is a particular stone that tips when a drop hits it. It strikes its neighbour, then settles back with a much smaller sound. For a season that would be a good way of finding me: come in out of the strong light, wait for the double tick, and put a finger to the wet place well above it. I would draw in a little from the warmth of the finger.

I would be the part of the yellow animal that lets the water go.

Very little of the sea would be apparent to me there. The news of a collision of waves would come through the stone long before the squeeze that it set going in the flesh. The two things would have different shapes. The first would be everywhere at once, even in the blunt ends pressed into blind holes, and the second would arrive along one branch while another was still quite limp. Down at my opening there would be time, between the shock and the arrival of water, to let the lip go soft. If it stayed tight, the drop would run down the face of the cliff. From a soft lip it would take off whole, with a small agreeable tug.

Sometimes the tug would fail. There would be the long business of filling, the increasing pull in the cold outer skin, then a lightness without any release. A wind blowing close along the face would have taken the water by degrees. Farther in, the squeeze would go on just the same. I think that would be the extent of my acquaintance with wind.

It is less easy to say where I would stop on the inward side. There is a place a little above the outlet where a flake of stone divides the channel. The flake is thin enough at its outer edge to show light through it when the sun comes round. A thread of flesh goes down either side. On one side the water moves often and the flesh is supple; on the other, a red paste has collected in the throat, and water only gets by at the height of a squeeze. Both threads join the lip. A drop that fell in sunshine might have spent the dark part of the year on one side of that flake, and all of the previous summer on the other. I would have no need to distinguish them. But on the shut side, a little way back from the paste, there is a patch of grey skin still good for cropping. I would be there too, working at it in the quiet intervals, while outside the lip went on losing water to the wind.

When the mountain moved, the flake would break across. Its thicker part would stay where it was. The thin edge, with the sunlight in it, would go down several feet and come to rest in a new position, wedged so firmly that it would ring. Of all the noise of the heave this one quite small high note would persist in the piece of flesh still fastened to it. The flesh would curl. For the first time there would be no continuation in either direction. What had been the shut side of a watercourse would be the inside of an animal, and what had been the taste of one particular stone would be the taste of the whole outside of its body, until the rain washed it clean.

I might stay there. The new split might be one of those narrow ones which are open to the weather for a few minutes after a heave and then take a second settling of the rock, not quite back to the old position. There would be a foot or two of darkness and, beyond that, a seam of warm water moving steadily enough to keep a blunt head occupied for years.

But the part I keep coming back to is the stump above the break. There would still be a supply of water there, and for a while it would continue to be pushed down a channel that no longer led to the lip. The first charge would have nowhere to go. The second would turn out a little fold of yellow flesh against the raw face. By the time the third came through there would be a bead on the fold. It would hang in full sun, several feet to one side of the old drop, over bare rock where nothing loose had ever fallen.

That is where I would find myself, I think: at the making of a second wet place. The first drop would strike hard and make very little sound. There is a considerable difference between striking a level sheet of rock and striking a stone that has had several seasons to get comfortable on its round side. For a long time it would seem, to anyone listening from the plain, that the ticking had stopped altogether. Only by coming quite close to the mountain in the heat of the day could one see what was going on. Beside the familiar strip of gravel, with its dry red undersides, there would be a dark patch no wider than a thumb. In the middle of it a single grain, still square on every edge, would shift a little when the water came down.
