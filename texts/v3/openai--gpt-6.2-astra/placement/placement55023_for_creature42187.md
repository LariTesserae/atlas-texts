I would be the woman who takes the mud out of the little basin behind the grain stamps. On market days I could be found there from the time the first mules came down, sitting on a board with my skirts tucked into the backs of my knees. Nobody would have much cause to look for me. The important thing as far as the miller was concerned would be the basin, which has to be deep enough for grit to settle out before the water goes on to the scoops. I would have a long spoon made from a gourd. A tin one rings against the stones and the miller has quite enough noise.

In the morning the mud comes up black, with a skin on it that wrinkles like the skin on cooled milk. I tip it into a shallow basket lined with old sacking. The hazel man saves his handles that have bent too far, and I have one of these on my basket, an extravagant high arch for such a little load. It lets me pass my arm through to the shoulder and go up the stair with the basket against my back and both hands free. The mud goes on a bed of leeks behind the house. My daughter complains of finding small white shells in the soup no matter how many times she slits and washes the leeks.

I have done this long enough to have my own idea of what lies above the ridge. I have never been there. What comes down ordinarily is fine and grey. After a long rain there is yellow sand with clear grains in it, and sometimes a needle from a tree we haven’t got on this side. I keep a twist of paper inside my jacket with a few of the clear grains in it. There used to be a man who came over to buy walnuts who said he could take me up to where they lay a foot deep, and once we set a day for it. On that day his mule cast a shoe. The next year he sent his son for the walnuts; the son knew nothing about the sand.

When the boy calls out about the water I take my board in. It has taken years of sitting to get it smooth and I lost the last one that way. For a little while there is nothing for me to do but stand under the eave with the hazel man. He has a customer who has got a handle halfway through a pair of lugs on a basket and will not have help with it. All three of us watch the customer rather than the flood. It is a very nice basket, new, and you can see the pale outer strip lifting on one lug where he has forced it. At last he takes the end out and puts it in his mouth to wet it. The hazel man and I let our breath out together.

Afterwards the basin is full of a soft reddish stuff quite unlike the morning mud. I lay the board down and sink my arm in almost to the elbow to feel for the inlet. Something taps the bone of my wrist. It is a pear, a hard little undersized one, cold enough to hurt my teeth when I bite it. There is a flat place on its cheek with the print of a cord across it. So there must be somebody up there, on the far side of what I can see, who ties their branches in as close as we do here. I turn it to take another bite. In the flesh under the cord mark there is a deep brown sweetness; the rest of it is hardly fit to eat.
