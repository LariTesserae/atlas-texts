I would be down in one of the wet seams of the knotwood, well inland, at a place where the black grain takes a sharp turn and a shrimp going home with a full stomach has to go round the long way. I would know that turn as well as anything can be known. I would have a narrow place halfway along my left side from getting through it, and no such place on my right. I would know nothing at all of the headland of which it is the tip.

The shrimp are blind, but they have a habit of looking. Something changes in the water and all along a seam the little blunt heads come out and turn the same way. On either side of my mouth there would be a pale bristle with a joint in it. I could hold one out in the water and lay the other flat against the wood. When a sac opened, the one against the wood would give a great backward sweep. A little later the one in the water would bend, quite gently, the other way. In the interval I could put my head out and take a shaving of soft brown stuff from the opposite wall. Done too soon, this would cost me the shaving; done too late, it would cost me a bristle. There would be many meals in a day and occasionally a week without a left bristle.

I would not be pale all through. In the hollow behind my head, under a lid as clear as the water, there would be a fine red wheel. Each of the spokes would be a comb. It would turn a little on the intake of breath and a little more on the outgo, combing out of the water the dust that would otherwise settle on my gills. Now and then I would reach back with a small crooked leg and eat what the combs had caught. Here, well below the depth to which red light comes down, there might be thousands of these wheels going round in the dark, all a very good red.

There would be one part of the year I liked better than the rest. For several days the water would taste of meal. In a seam so narrow, a taste does not come on evenly. I could have my head in the good water and the whole length of my back still in the ordinary water; I could back up and come into it again. Small shrimp would do this until a larger one, coming up for a share, pushed them well past the place. By the time the meal got down to us there would be very little of it. It would take most of a watch of the pumps to get a paste on the crooked leg, but it would be a rich paste, worth clearing out of all the little forks of the foot.

One year the taste would go on longer in my seam than in any of the others. The shrimp from round the turn would come in backwards, feeling rather officiously about among my legs. When they had gone I would go looking. There would be a split in the roof, too slight to let even a newly shed shrimp through, and caught in the split a bit of clear skin with some meal still in the hem of it. At every stroke of the water it would come down a little way and at every return go up out of reach. I would wait with one bristle on the wood. In due course I would have hold of the skin.

I think I would keep it for some time after it was clean. There is a business the shrimp have when they are about to shed. They try one seam and then another, backing in and coming out, looking for a bed that will not chafe the new joints. I would draw the skin into the sharp turn and lie on it. With my old case split along the back and my two bristles still laboriously drawing out of their old bristles, I would lie for a while on the inner face of a moth's wing. It would fit the narrow place in my side very nicely. Through skin that had not yet had time to harden I would feel the knock coming a long way off through the grain. The loose ends of the wing would lift in the water and settle over me again.
