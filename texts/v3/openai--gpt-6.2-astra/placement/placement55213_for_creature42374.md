I think I would live on the landing behind the smoothing shed, in the room where the returned tiles are kept. Very few come back. The freight is dear in the uphill direction. If a man in the lower country finds that a tile will not do, he lays a piece of paper over the place it was to have gone and rubs it with the side of a pencil. He folds the paper up and sends it, and I am the person who unfolds it. There is a long table for that purpose. My bed lets down from the wall across one end of the table, so that a particularly large piece of work must be finished before I can go to sleep.

What we need to know is the curve. The blue tiles go on the sills of windows, on the lips of public wash troughs, along the backs of seats where it is pleasant to put an elbow. On the shelf they go chiefly where water is wanted to fall clear of a wall. A curve wrong by the thickness of a shirt button can bring a great deal of water indoors. I have a rack of fired clay edges, numbered on their undersides, which I try against the rubbings. Those that fit I send out to the press room by number. For the others I must make a new edge. It is agreeable work for a person who likes to be sure of a small thing and is prepared to spend the morning at it.

At first I would know the lower country almost entirely by its windowsills. There is a house with a sill that takes an ordinary Fourteen up to the last six inches and then requires a left-handed piece of a kind nobody else has ever ordered. There is a wash trough so nearly round that the paper for it came cut into gores, like the peel of a fruit, and had to be pinned together over the upturned brass tray of the letter scale. The postmistress sat on my doorstep eating an apple until I had finished with her tray. From the underside of a seat once came the impression of a small hand with six fingers. One of the fingers, examined in a good light, was a nail lying in a crack. I was rather sorry to find it so.

In time I would want to see one of these places. I think it would be the house with the left-handed sill. Every few years they send for that end piece. We have strengthened it twice, and once the works sent two, the second being entered in the book as a kindness, though kindness is not one of the headings and it had to go under Breakage in Transit. When I asked the man who brings up the empty crates what sort of house it was, he said it was yellow, then considered and said it was principally the next house that was yellow. I made the journey down with him on a day when there was a small piece ready to go.

Below the cloud it was much colder than I expected. For years I had handled those soft papers, with their smudges of thumbs and occasional transparent spots of cooking fat, and had somehow furnished the whole lower country with the temperature of a kitchen. We went between trees for a long way. When there was wind, the light went over the ground so fast that once or twice I stopped walking to let it pass. The man with the crates stopped too, without asking why. He had brought people down before.

The house was the color of the paper they had sent. There was no glass in the bottom half of the window, and the sill was about level with my breastbone. A woman came and stood at it from inside. When I showed her the piece she reached out and took it and set it in its bed without looking. It fitted beautifully. I saw then that the wall had once gone straight on, and that the bend had been made in the sill to clear the trunk of a tree. The tree was gone. There was a round stone in the ground where it had stood, set a little low, with rain in it.

She called somebody from the back of the house. A girl came through, put both hands on the sill, swung her legs over and dropped into the street. She was off down the hill before the woman could tell her what she wanted. There was the little loose tile on the ground between my feet.

“We generally wait till after breakfast to cement it,” the woman said.

I stayed to breakfast. There was a grandmother, too stout now for the window, who had used it all her life; and a door, perfectly good, which gave on three steps down and seven steps up and a passage where the people next door kept hens. On the inner wall beneath the sill the toes of shoes had worn two long hollows. One was considerably lower than the other. A child could grow up the difference between them. I had been making the part that came loose when people left this house in a hurry, and had not known even which way they were going.

I did not improve the piece. On getting home I put a note in the book to say that the old shape was satisfactory and that one spare should always be kept fired. It is on the rack now. In the afternoon the belt going by the slot makes a pulse of shade across it. Sometimes a child comes in dripping, with a paper in a silk cap, and stays to fit the edges one against another. The left-handed one is a favorite because it will not go with any of the rest.

That is where I would be, I think: upstairs from the people who make things, at the receiving end of the accounts of how they fail to fit. On warm evenings I would take my supper out on the landing. The window of my room is quite easy to reach from there. It opens over the foot of the bed, and although there is a door not four paces off, I have got into the way of going in through it.
