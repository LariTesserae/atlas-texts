I would go out much too sure of what a hen looks like. I would find a loaf-shaped stone almost at once, get down on the seaward side of it and see a hen. There would be the slope of the back and the little head held well forward. I would be halfway under the slab again before it occurred to me that I had supplied the beak.

“Was there a hole under her neck?” he would ask.

I hadn’t seen a hole. He would consider this. It might have filled up. They did, from time to time. Or I might have found another hen, which seemed to please him just as much.

I think I would stay on the beach a while. There is a trade I could take up there. I would sit above the reach of the wash and mend the shoes people took off before going in. The first would be my own: the leather over the little toe would have parted where I had dragged my foot under the slab. There is a particular satisfaction in a repair to which one can immediately entrust one’s full weight. I would have an awl and two needles and some ends of good waxed thread, and people coming out with their feet pink from the spring would stand on my folded coat while I worked. From where I sat, most of a person would be outlined against the sky. Their feet I would get to know quite well.

It would be some time before I had anything further to tell him about the hen. There would be other news. A boy had lost a front tooth biting the knot in a bootlace. Someone had come down the beach carrying a pane of glass; you could see all the way through it except for the green edge, and the man carrying it had been at considerable pains to get it over the stones although there was nothing, as far as we could see, to put a window in.

“Did he come back with it?”

He did. This struck us both as a good deal stranger.

Eventually the man on the shelf would ask me to show him what I did to a shoe. I would bring in a sandal with a loose strap. He would put out a finger to feel for the point as it came through the leather. He would be very quick at it. I would let him do the second stitch himself, holding the sandal upside down for him, and we would get the threads so thoroughly crossed that for a while neither of us could take his hand away.

It would be on one of these visits, with the two needles lying in the tipped bowl, that I would notice something about my own hand. There would be a little nick beside the thumb, an old cut from the awl. Looking down past it into the water, I would see, quite distinctly at the bottom of the basin, the cloudy nail of my great toe. This would be odd because my foot would still be in its boot, tucked up on the shelf. I would move the toe. A little cloud of lime would lift from the bottom.

I don’t think I would turn into another of him. For one thing I would still have to go out in the evening and carry my tools up the bluff. But after that I would sometimes feel, sitting at my work on the beach, a steady warmth along one shin, no wider than a hand. Once, pulling a particularly obstinate needle through, I would hear him say quite close to my ear, “Mind the bowl,” and ease my elbow in time. I would come to understand that when he made room on the shelf he didn’t necessarily take all the room back afterward.

In winter a sea would come up that did reach the drain. In the morning there would be a bank of weed outside the slab, full of small hopping things, and halfway up the beach the loaf-shaped stone would be lying on its side. It would have a pale clean stripe around its middle and a hollow underneath, lined with minute hard white spirals. I would sit beside it to eat my bread. From that low down I would see, farther along the beach, a quite inconsiderable hump of stone with a long piece jutting out of it, and under the jut a hole bright enough to put a needle through.

I would go in without finishing the bread. He would already be on the shelf. Something in my knee would have told him I was coming.

“Well?” he would say.

I would have to tell him first about the other stone, the overturned one, and its little white worms in their hard houses. I would have to use my hands. He would hold the bread for me. Then I would get down on the scooped step, with my chin just clear of the shelf, and lay my hand flat and lift one knuckle a very little way. From where he sat there would be nothing to see but a cobbler’s hand, puckered from the wet. I would feel him shift, and feel the stone give a little under my knee. Presently his cheek would be down beside mine. There, between the knuckle and the shelf, would be the thread of light I had gone out for.
