At the public bell on the last street before the stakes, there is a place for a fourth head, provided the head goes in sideways. I should know. I was the fourth child at the making of it. The potter had us stand with our chins on a cord while she built the ribs between us. My brother held his ears out with his fingers and got a very handsome allowance on either side. By the time she came round to me there was not much clay left on the board.

I am the one in our family who works in clay now. I would like to say that the potter took me on because I held still, but there were two years in which she took no notice of me whatever. Then she wanted somebody with a flat shelf at home to look after a board of drying handles. Ours was flat. We had to sleep with our knees up for a week, and my brother broke one anyway.

There is a good deal to the work besides bells. A shallow dish for a sleeping shelf ought to have one flat side so that a person feeling for a drink in the dark can push it against the wall instead of chasing it into the street. The little cups in which the carriers take embers down the mountain have two sets of holes for the handle. Coming up they use them for blackberries and like them to hang the other way. I could pass a quite happy morning putting the second holes in cups. You brace the wet wall with the ball of a finger and turn a hollow straw through it from outside. A little cylinder of clay comes away in the straw. My daughter used to want these cylinders for the legs of animals. There was a box of them on our shelf long after she had lost interest.

It was my daughter who took me up to the new street to see about a bell for the people laying cane. They had been working in pairs, one working and the other on his way to or from air. She was doing the sums for them. It turned out that most of what they were being paid for was the journey. She had run a cord to show where the mouth should hang. I caught hold of it and found that it led straight to a willow stake with a fork just where a person would put an eye.

We spent some time moving the cord. By the time we were done we had it round the stake twice, and through my armhole, and she was laughing so hard she had to go back to the old bell. I stayed to look at the fork. The two branches were sound, with a good skin of stone on them. If we hung the bell beyond it, instead of short of it, there would be a rest for the back of somebody’s head.

In my master’s time we should have had the cane layers in to stand for the ribs. My daughter suggested taking the shapes off them at their work with a piece of soft wire. We got one man with his jaw set forward to hold a length of cord in his teeth, and a woman with a big knot of hair worn rather low. I allowed for the knot. Of course the first thing she did when she came up inside the finished bell was to take her hair down and wring it out. She felt behind her with one hand.

“What a good place for the soap,” she said.

I go there sometimes now that the street is open. With my heels on the new rail I can lean back against the fork and see a little way out through the mouth, as far as the hands of the people going by. I know quite a number of them by their hands. My brother always runs his along the rail, lifting it at the joins and setting it down on the far side. He came by the other day with his grandson, who is just beginning to go visiting. I heard the slap and moved over. First there were eight fingers curled over the edge of the mouth, and then a wet forehead against my chin. My brother put his face up on the other side.

“Roomy,” he said, trying it with his ears.
