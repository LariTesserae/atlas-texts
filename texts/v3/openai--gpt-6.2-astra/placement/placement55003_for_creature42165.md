I should be on the underside of the shell lintel. There is a nick there, made when the shell broke, with room for my head and the first three rings of my body. The rest hangs down into the doorway. A sowbug coming through backward brushes me up against the shell. After it has gone I take some time to come down again.

I am a grub, nearly as clear as the drop on the clay, except for a brown head and a little crooked length of green inside. The green comes from feeding on the shell. What looks white from the floor has, in the hollows of its upper surface, a short wet growth the color of the inside of a bean. I go up there at night. I have no occasion to cross the plain. In fact there is a difficulty about getting off the shell at all. I go along by making fast with my mouth, drawing the hind end of me up to the head, and making fast again. There are places on the shell where I can do this. On a loose crumb the effect is merely to bring the crumb nearer.

For some days my chief acquaintance in the room would be the springtail skin on the lacewing’s back. When the lacewing comes to the heap, the skin brushes past the door a little above the level at which a sowbug goes through. Sometimes the empty sleeve catches on the shell. Then for a moment I have hold of one end of a long tugging white thing, under which the lacewing patiently goes on sorting crumbs. Once I get the cuff quite into my mouth. There is nothing to be got from it. Still, when it comes past again I take hold.

The day the leaf gets out into the field, the light comes into the room differently. It used to reach the heap in the afternoon by passing between the seed leaves, which gave the heap for an hour or so a fine bright parting. Now a little of it comes down the stem. It is green light, and does not set until very late. A sowbug brings a string of it through the door and wipes it off along my side. By morning there is a thin green rail from the nick in the shell to the top of the heap. Going down it, I can keep my mouth fastened while I try the ground with my tail. This is how I first come to walk on the lacewing.

The surface is a good deal steadier than the heap, the crumbs being held on bristles, and there are passages between the crumbs that keep their damp all day. In one of them I find the green purse. There is still a meal in the hinge of it. I put my head in to feed and when I bring it out the door is a long way off. The wall is going by, with the feet of springtails showing through it. Then there is a tremendous climb, impressive chiefly for the number of things that come sliding down past me. A chalk grain goes into the purse and I cannot get my head in there again.

At the top there is something I have not felt in the doorway. All along one side of me the damp goes off at once. It comes back, then goes off along the other side. The lacewing is standing on the rim of the leaf with its head up in the wind. Below us the small green louse has edged in close to a rib. Above us a sheep’s lip goes along the track, taking the heads off the grass with a noise like somebody drawing a stitch tight. The leaf dips. The bit of shell to which I have made fast dips with it; the rest of the lacewing steps away.

It is a very small bit of shell. I must have had it loose under my mouth for some time. For the rest of that day I carry it about with me, going no faster, on account of its weight, than the shadow of a hair across the leaf. By evening I have come to the place where the stalk begins. There is a nick there, too, and in it the wind only touches the last two rings of me. I let go of the shell. It slips down the stalk and falls into the room.

I stay in the nick. There is food enough where the leaf has scraped passing through the roof, and after a few nights I no longer need food. My skin comes loose at the mouth and draws a little way back from it, as a stocking comes away from the toes. Within the stocking, folded narrower than one would think possible, are several things for use in the field. Two of them will be wings. As yet they have no more spread than the empty sleeve I used to catch in the doorway. They lie wet against my sides.

Down below, a sowbug has got the fallen chip of shell into its load and is attempting to take it backward through the door. The chip is a shade too wide. Each time the sowbug pulls, one end of the lintel rises and settles again. Even up here, with the stalk for a great green sounding board, the noise it makes is only a tick. But I have spent most of my life with my head against the thing that makes it. Long after the chip has turned edgewise and gone through, the soft wing on that side of me goes on stirring in its fold.
