I think I would be found first by touch, in the part of the white tube where it goes through a leaf. A finger worked in there would feel a row of hard little humps, much like the teeth of a comb. They would all lean away from the finger. If the finger were kept still long enough one of them would lean back and rest against the nail.

A single hump is the end of an animal. The rest of it is a buff-coloured pleat tucked into the waist of the tube. I would have a hard end towards the leaf and a soft end towards the white, and a mouth on either side, so close to the base of the pleat that there could be very little pleasure in opening it. The food has to be brought almost inside. There is a good deal of it when the stalk is up. At every small check in the ascent of the flecks a little liquor works out through the white wall. What it tastes of depends on where the top was laid the evening before. There are evenings after which the whole row feeds, and evenings after which we keep our mouths shut and let the stuff run down among the roots.

My favourite part of the day would come a little before the stalk appeared. There would be a fullness on the soft side, gentle at first, sufficient to iron out the pleat. The hard end would lift clear of the leaf. For a few minutes I could turn quite freely in my place. This is when we change neighbours. One creeps a short distance round the waist, meets another coming the other way, and the one with the softer end goes inside. The skin we have rubbed loose during the day stays behind in a tidy row in the grain of the leaf. A flat animal sometimes takes a turn around the waist immediately afterwards. It gets very little from the skins themselves but quite a meal out of the yellow that has grown in their shelter.

From the inside, the giving way of a lake would not be a great noise. It would be the loss of that little interval in which it is possible to turn. The fullness would come on too fast. My soft end would be pushed deep into the grain and my hard end driven back against the white wall. All around the tube the same thing would happen, the humps turning inwards until there was scarcely room between them for a fleck to pass. I should like to have seen this once from outside. I imagine a short string of dark commas coming suddenly clear through the stretched white skin, and the skin going slack above them. Below, where the pressure was still on, a new knob might begin.

I would have no feeling for that knob. It could grow out until it reached the next fold, rise into the sun and be the stalk by which a traveller judged the movement of the plain. My acquaintance with it would amount to one bad afternoon when I could not get turned round. What I would remember remarkably well would be the feel of an old neighbour coming back into place beside me. One has a nick in its hard end. In the dark the nick goes over my edge with two small knocks instead of one. After a long disturbance I would put my mouth out on that side first.

There would be one chance to see me whole. At the eastern cut, before the two knobs had grown much, the white wall would draw away from the eleventh leaf. For a moment there would be light all the way round the waist. Some of us would go with the tube and some would be left in the little sockets in the leaf, opening and shutting on air. I think I would be one of the latter. The light would hurt on the soft side and feel wonderfully warm on the hard. I would inch sideways along the exposed grain, a buff fleck with one polished end, going so slowly that the shadow of a runner would overtake me and leave me in the sun again.

It would take me until dusk to reach the scar. By then there would be moisture enough on the white for my soft end to hold. I would settle in the angle under one of the knobs, without much purchase yet, and wait. Well after dark, as the knob swelled against the first of the new leaves, something would come edging down the other side. I would feel along it with my mouth. Two knocks. There would be room.
