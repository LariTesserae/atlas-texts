I would come in with a hand on the lowest step, I think. I would have got my feet down all right and be trying to get the rest of me round to face the light. The moth would walk out between my fingers. For a moment I would keep my hand where it was, taking more and more of my weight on the other arm, for fear the step had a second loose piece of that kind.

But it is the underside of the hand I can feel, the damp of it coming down over a place that has been dry all day. There is plenty of room between the fingers. I go over the edge and stay there. The hand goes away with a little grey on it that might as well have come from the stone.

Even that is rather late to find me. I would have been up in the turf since the autumn. My house then would have fitted inside the hollow of a bent finger. At the narrow end of it I could turn about, provided I put my head well down and let the roof come along with me a little way. Some nights I used up the first of the darkness making good the place where I turned.

A sheep standing over the tube brought the roof down very gently. There was time to get flat. It was when the foot lifted that things went wrong: the earth went up and a damp crumb stayed behind, adhering to the silk and making a low place that caught on my back each time I passed. I bit it free and took it out after dark. A fair part of the night might go in getting a bit of the hillside from one side of a grass stem to the other.

Down there I had the sea chiefly through my belly. A blade of grass held in the jaws gave a small kick which had nothing to do with the work of cutting it. If I let go and took hold farther up it did the same. I learned to go on cutting. A pull on the silk was quite another matter. That came the whole length of the house, and before the grass had fallen out of my mouth I had set my hind feet. For a little while the house was very long and narrow. Then it was much as before, except for a greater ease in turning, and a little of the evening showing where no evening ought to show.

I think that is what I would carry into the winged part of my life: a nice distinction between things that can be allowed to go on and things that require the feet. I should have no particular interest in knowing there was a sea. On the back of the pebble, where the blows are tremendous, I would attend to a single hair caught under the edge of a wing. It takes a sideways movement to get it free. There is a pit just there in which to put a foot and make the movement without coming out of the groove.

As for the yellow, I would know very little about it. It would be chiefly other moths that were yellow. One would open a little way ahead of me on the grass and for an instant there would be something out there of quite another order from grass or stone. By the time I reached the end of my blade it would be gone. I would have the same two warm scraps folded away down my own back and go to considerable pains, at the fork in the root, to get them through without rubbing.

You would find me most readily just there, backing out because I had tried the fork a little too upright. The moth behind also has to back. For a moment we stand on the pale root facing the wrong way into the hill, in a noise in which a slab might fall without being heard. I take a fresh hold a hairsbreadth farther round the root. This time there is cool grass against one wing and nothing whatever against the other, and room to go on.
