I would be on my belly on the pump house step, with a folding rule in the water. From the roof the street looks level. Down here one curb comes out of the water a good while before the other. I want to know how much water there still is against the grating when the first cobble shows. I have one arm down the hole where the bars are missing and the other bent under my ribs, which is quite a comfortable way to lie until somebody opens the pump house door.

I read the height boards for the works. There are ten of them in my round. In the office there is a drawing with ten black strokes on it, beautifully straight, all the same length. Out on the pans one of the boards leans back so far that a redshank can walk up it. Another is plumb but has gone down five inches with the wall it is bolted to. I carry the corrections on a card in my pocket. The card has been wet and dried so often it has taken the curve of my leg.

There used to be a board on the step. The bolt holes are still there. Until a new board comes I take a depth off the sill with the rule, and this is how I come to spend more time than most people with my ear against that stone.

What I hear one evening, well after the motor has run down, is a small double knock. It comes distinctly through the stone. I know the loose shell in the drain: that makes a noise like a button going round in a tin and can keep it up for minutes. This is two knocks, a little apart, and nothing after. I put a hand down and try the cut ends of the bars. Both are quite firm. When I sit up my shirt has a print of the doorstep on it, down to the dry white threads where the stone is cracked.

It takes me most of a week to find the tile. By then I have tried everything near the pump house that a man with a hook can decently get at. I find it in daylight, with the scullery floor barely awash. My boot comes down on one corner and the other comes up and lets out a little sneeze of black mud. Six or seven shrimps shoot out across the white. When I take my foot off, the tile drops onto a lump of old bedding mortar, rocks over, and strikes the floor a second time. I can make the noise as often as I like. What I can't do is hear it from the step while I'm making it.

The man who got his sleeve wet offers to come out with me. He stands on the step while I tread on the tile. He shakes his head. We change places. He is a much heavier man than I am and I can plainly hear his boot squelching and the change in his pocket. Of the knocks I hear nothing at all. We go in and put the kettle on. He thinks the eel lifts things to get at what's under them. He has kept bait in a bucket with a saucer for a lid and known an eel to get the saucer off. He doesn't insist it was this eel.

After a while the new board comes. It is white enamel, long enough to stand clear of the highest tide, with a considerable blank bit at the bottom. We bed it on packers so the back won't bear against the belly of the wall. It can be read standing up, from halfway across the bund. This saves me getting wet and is reckoned a great improvement, especially by the woman who takes my round on Sundays.

I'd still be there some evenings. There is a part of the day after the figures have gone in when nobody particularly needs to know the height of the water. I'd sit in the hollow on the right with my heels just clear of it. Once, in that light, I'd see a shrimp come backwards through the missing bars, then a second shrimp, then nothing for so long that I'd be looking out at the lamps when the eel herself went through. There would be a little lift of water under my left heel. If I were quick I could put my foot down and feel her. I'd keep it where it was.
