I think I would be the girl who owned the red boots. I would be on the wrong side of the cloakroom pane for most of what happened. The children coming round by the kitchen would want to know which boot the bird was beside, and whether it had dirtied either of them. I would say I didn’t mind, but I would look, afterwards.

The boots had belonged to my brother. There were two holes for the prong of each buckle, the proper round one and the one my father had made with a hot skewer. He had done the job with me standing in them in my winter socks. By June they were loose whichever hole I used. On a wet day I liked the way a boot that was too big went down flat on the water and sent a little fan of it ahead.

For some days after the dipper got out I would lay one boot on its side, with the buckle against the floor. I had an idea the bird might have wanted to go inside and been put off by the height of the top. The woman who opened the school kept standing it up. Neither of us mentioned this. Then we had the long holiday, and by the time we went back I could hardly get the boots on.

I would go to work eventually at the counter for lost things. There would have to be one somewhere on the car lines. Ours was at a small exchange where people changed for the laundry district. It had a window onto each platform, so that a person could ask after something without going through the barrier and having to pay again. People would sometimes come to the wrong window to report a loss, as if the thing, travelling on, ought by now to be on the other side.

Gloves were difficult. We laid the single ones palm up in a shallow drawer. People recognized the fingers more often than the backs. There was a fish slice, collected within minutes by a man still wearing an apron, and a set of false teeth that nobody ever came for. The most frequently lost thing was the bag with the dry socks in it. It was almost always left on the homeward journey, with the wet socks in it instead. We had a little line behind the counter for those. By closing time there was sometimes a very fair smell of people in that room.

One spring the woman from the school would come to my window. I would know her quite well by sight; scarcely a week would have gone by in all those years without our passing somewhere. She had lost a short folding rule. She could give me the make of it and the length and the colour, but she gave me another particular first. The brass joint in the middle had been mended with a copper rivet. Her father had done that. With the joint open the rule was a trifle crooked, and had been so as long as she had used it.

We had no rule of any kind. I took down her name. She was going below the cloud to stay with her sister for a month, so I took the sister’s address as well. At the other window somebody was knocking a coin on the glass.

“I kept hoping it would come back,” I said.

She thought I was talking about the rule. I had to explain about the bird and my boot on its side. It was a poor time to have chosen for this, with the coin going and her car visible already on the wet flats. But she put her bag down.

“Was that what you were doing?” she said. “I used to put it right so you wouldn’t have a damp foot. That floor got washed every morning.”

The rule would turn up in August in a consignment from the lake exchange. I have no idea how it got there. Somebody had folded it up wet, and the figures from one face had come off faintly on the other, so that there were inches you could read and inches you would have needed a mirror for. The copper rivet was in very good order.

By then she was home. Instead of sending the rule on the car I took it to the school before my shift. She was at the east steps, crouched down with one hand feeling under the bottom tread. There was a considerable stream there again. I had come prepared to show her the reversed figures, which I thought rather remarkable, but when she had the rule in her hand she opened it without looking and put it straight to work. I stood on the paving holding her bag. From where I stood I could see the white knob well under water, and a long green hair growing from it, combed out in the direction of the bakery.
