I think I would be under the shelf, on the floor, a little to one side of the place where the drop comes down. In the good light there is very little to see there. An old wash of chalk crosses a fine black groove; one can see the groove through the chalk, except at one place where the white has a turned-up edge. I would be easy to take for a flake working loose.

When the belly of the drop touches the floor, the first water goes past on either side of me. Then it gets under the edge. The white comes up like a single wet scale and there is a clear animal folded beneath it, the length of the scale and much too long to have fitted there. I have a row of little pits along one side. I put that side down in the groove. By the time the whole drop has left the shelf, I am laid out along the bottom, with the white piece standing almost on edge in the current. It is the lid of the place I keep, not a part of my back. The same lid does for many days.

There is food in the shelf water. All day things too fine to see have been coming to rest against the skin of the drop. When the skin lets go, they come down in a wrinkle which travels some distance along the floor before coming apart. I take what sticks in the pits. There will be time to shut up and work it all along to the one deep pit, which goes in. For the present the pleasant thing is to be long. At the far end of me it is cool already and here by the chalk I can still feel the warmth of the dry floor giving out through the water.

Once there is a catch in the middle. Two lips of a foot have closed on me. The little one from the shelf has got its back wedged under the upright lid, and for all its taking hold and letting go it only slides the length of its own pleat and back again. I shorten on that side. It is what I do if a sharp grain sticks in a pit. The lid tips, the back comes free, and for an instant the two of us are stretched out in the same groove, going different ways: I am drawing in towards the shelf and the young one is going backwards towards the stone. Then the water is too shallow to carry anything. Where the grazer went there is a wet thread with a dry division appearing in the middle.

I do not get as far as the pasture. Even at full length there is the breadth of a pebble between the last of my pits and the first hairs. Something of it comes to me, though. In the morning, when a tap sends a shiver back up the groove, a length of stripped hair sometimes works loose and arrives at my lid. It is wonderfully fine, with a little knob at the end that the mouths have all passed over. I have three of these under the chalk now. They keep it from sitting quite flush with the floor. In the long interval after the taps, when all the exposed glass is hot again, there is still room under that edge for a seam of water, and in the seam, folded small again, room for me.
