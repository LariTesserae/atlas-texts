I would be under the shoes, though it would take a particular sort of shoe to get me there. A smooth sole would put me back with the wash. I would need one of those old heels built up in layers, with a place at the corner where a nail had let go. A person coming out of the bath sets a wet foot on the warm step and steadies a shoe with the other foot while getting into it. Quite a little water changes hands.

I would be a young hem, still green, with the use of my foot and very little else. The split in the heel would feel much like the cut in the stone I had just left. Both have a ceiling close enough to lean one's back against. There would even be something to eat. The person who owned the shoe had a garden, or had walked through somebody's garden, and there was a good paste of earth in the split. I should be busy with that and miss altogether the passage up the stairs, which would afterwards be the most remarkable thing about me.

The shoe would come off again at a kitchen door. It would stand on a flat stone considerably hotter than the step. As the edges of my drop drew in I would go farther under the heel, into the little dark the nail had made. Here I would do very well until somebody took a brush to the shoes in a yard where a tap dripped into a horse trough. The last of the dirt always wants a good knock against the side.

There is a world of difference between falling into water and having water come up about you. In the bath one is ordinarily aware of one's foot first; there is a cool loosening all round its edge, and by the time the water has one's back one has a fair hold. In the trough I would touch bottom on my back. It would take much waving of the folded thing at my head to turn me over. Then the eating would be extraordinary. Nobody had scrubbed under the ball of the float, where there was a green fur with brown dust caught in it, and a snail had been through and laid open the fur in strips of a convenient width. I would go along a strip till the snail's turning was too sharp for me and then make my own way.

In time I would put out arms. There would be a difficulty here that none of the hems below had met with. In the still trough a fringe soon clears the water on either side of it; after a while one may stand quite handsomely spread and get nothing. The best place would prove to be just under the horse's end. Twice a day a soft great muzzle would come through the roof of things and the whole trough would move towards it. Even after the horse had finished there would be food going by for several minutes, shaken off the sides by the slap of the float. I would work my way towards that end. By the width of a brass peg in a summer is not very fast, but the horse would live a long time.

I think that is where you would find me: a pale rag on the iron just below the mark left by the water in a dry August. There would be young in my pocket. What would bring them out in a place without the great lifting bubble I cannot say. Perhaps after a hard frost, when a sheet of ice an inch thick came loose from the sides and rode up on the new water from the tap, the stillness under it would answer. A horse can put its nose through such a sheet with a noise quite disproportionate to the small hole it makes. There might be green on its whiskers when it lifted its head.

But a hem could spend its whole life in that trough without knowing it had come up into daylight. When there was nothing eating or drinking and the float lay still, there would be the blue depth overhead with clouds going through it, familiar down to the small quick interruption of a bird. The only novelty would be at night. Below, when the bars go out, they go out altogether. Here on a clear night there would be a quantity of little lights past the ends of my arms. I would do the usual thing with the fringes, drawing them in very carefully on one after another, and in the morning they would all be gone.
