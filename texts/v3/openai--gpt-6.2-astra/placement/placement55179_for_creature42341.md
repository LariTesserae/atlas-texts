The person in the doorway has the easier part. I know this because I have done it. There is a nick for the finger, a bowl for the weight of the rump, and no particular difficulty about keeping still except that presently the foot on the bottom goes to sleep. Out in the water a child is attempting, for the first time, to come back to you. You must on no account help by leaning forward. I used to think the important thing I did in that house was to stay put.

There was a third place in our house, though nobody had worn a hollow in it. It was a triangle behind the left-hand slab, just big enough to put a cup away in. We kept in it a cup which would not stand up. A little piece of the green lining had dried in a curl under the base. It went round and round on the floor, making a noise like a tooth being worked loose. Our child thought a great deal of it. When the time came to go out she had it tucked up under her frill, where it made a very conspicuous lump. We pretended not to notice.

Her first trip went quite well. The other grown one took her out as far as the black band, let her eat there a little, and turned her so that she could see the door. My foot had gone to sleep. I had a good long time to consider what I should do about this if the rest of me were suddenly wanted out on the plain. Then four pads, quite cold, came down on the back of my hand. She had found me with the wrong side of her fingers. I turned my hand over and she was so pleased with the considerable improvement in the fit that for a while she went about trying both sides of everything, including her food.

It was later, when we had no more use for a roof, that I began going back to the houses. Not to ours particularly. A roof is a good place to do a small job with both hands. I had got into the way of taking broken cups out of the water. They catch against the feeding edge and make a sore place if you have to travel far with one. Most can be made good by wetting the broken edges and rolling them over together. They come out smaller, sometimes too small to drink out of. I put these in the triangles for the children.

One afternoon I came upon a house where the two people on the floor were sitting side by side. It gave the place a quite different look. They had a new child, still mostly a swelling on the breast, and they were taking turns at putting a hand into the empty bowl. The fingers went down into it a good deal farther than the depth of the stone. One of the two showed me how far, with a wet mark a little above the wrist. They had come a long way and the little one had begun to slap in its sleep.

I sat in the doorway while they ate. There was a wedge under one of the uprights. I had often cleaned such things; like everyone else I was careful to clean away from the point so as not to work anything farther in. This one was black and had a pink line through it which continued, a finger's breadth to the left, in the stone of the floor. It had come from there. Indeed, when I put my nail to the place I found the shallow trough the exact size and shape of the wedge, wet at one end. A little brown water breathed in and out of it.

I would like to say that I understood then what the wedges were for. What I understood was that if I lay down I could drink the water without the trouble of getting out my cup. It tasted of the yellow stuff. There was quite a good seam running under the house. I spread myself over it, and in the course of feeding put a little of my weight on the blunt end of the wedge.

Behind me somebody laughed. The hand in the bowl had met the bottom with a smack.

We spent most of the afternoon finding out how I had been lying. It is surprisingly hard, once you have got up to see what is funny, to put your weight down in just the same way again. In the end it took all three of us: one with a hand in the bowl, one on the wedge, and one lying outside with the pads well apart to feel what the water was doing. We had to wait for the falling rain to find out that we had mended the seat at the expense of the doorway. The sill began letting water in. Our several thousand lips had a fine meal that evening and the child slept in the pleats as before.

I do that sort of work now. There are houses I can do nothing for. There is one in which the bowls keep the right distance in all weathers but anything placed between them turns up under the roof, adhering there quite gently until the rain changes. The children brought up in it have a curious upward sweep to the arm when they hand you something. They are perfectly competent out in the wet. I was asked by the family to leave the house alone.

I am getting heavy for going up under a sill. These days I generally take the part outside, with my cheek to the stone and my fingers spread. The one who works the wedge is quicker than I ever was. She has a little cup, hardly big enough for a snail, which she sets spinning on the floor before she begins. From the slight catch in its revolution she can tell which upright wants attending to. I have seen her do this in the dark. When I ask how she knows to do it she sets the cup going again and puts my finger in its path. I feel a tap, a fairly long interval, and another tap precisely like the first. She keeps my finger there a little longer. It is pleasant under the roof; there is no hurry about getting up. After a great many turns I begin to feel what she means.
