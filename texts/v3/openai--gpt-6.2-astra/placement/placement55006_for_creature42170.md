I think you would find me at the far end of the shelf, the one that has grown out into the marsh. From the doorway it looks rather too narrow to walk on. I should be able to walk on the underside, which is wider. There are hollows there where the bark has come loose in long strips, still fast at either end. In the middle of the day you might lift one of these strips and see two black soles and a quantity of dry brown fur. The soles would draw up out of sight. I should be awake by then, but in no hurry to come out on your account.

I should weigh about as much as a rail's egg and be rather longer, even without the tail. The tail would be nearly bare for an inch at the tip. I should sleep with this inch hanging out of bed. When water touched it I should wake and move a little nearer the ring. A heavy cow stepping on the path could have me up and moving three or four times in the course of a perfectly dry afternoon. By evening I might be well inside the doorway, with a long walk back to supper.

Along either side of the shelf, where it lies in the water, there are rafts of seeds. Each seed has a little bladder at one end and a white curl of meat at the other, wrapped up rather firmly. I should fish them up with a forefoot and eat them hanging by the hind feet. The blade of a front tooth would fit just inside the seam of the wrapper. With a good seed both halves would fall away at once, leaving the curl of meat in my mouth; with a bad one I should have to turn my head sideways and saw. From inside the ring, on a still evening, it would sound as though someone had begun a very small job of carpentry and kept mislaying the tool.

There would be a place on the shelf particularly good for eating them. A twig once grew out sideways there and was bent down, perhaps by the passage of a beast before the marsh came in. It has taken root at the tip. In the crook the water runs quite briskly even when the rest of the marsh appears to stand still. Seeds turn round in it, presenting first the bladder and then the edible end. There would be room for one of me in the crook. There would generally be two.

Much of my evening would go in getting past a sister. By daylight we could sleep six in a bark pocket with hardly a foot showing, but at supper the sight of her haunches ahead of me would be more than I could bear. I should set my forehead against them and push until she reached round to bite me, whereupon we should both fall in. We should climb out by different routes. She would be there ahead of me.

It would be possible to tell our crook from all the others, even in our absence. A little way downstream there is a patch of seedlings that comes up white every spring, the seeds too closely packed to open their leaves until the middle ones have died. These are the ones we drop unopened. Farther off there are green patches with quite as much water between them as a small animal can shake from its coat at one sitting. One of those patches has begun to hold mud. In summer a rail stands there to wash, spreading her toes over what was, when my mother was small, a rather trying stretch of open water.

Of the ring itself I should know very little. I should know the dry way in, and the taste of one of the knobs by the floor, where a salt damp comes out of the wood in hot weather. I should never have occasion to see the arches all at once. Nor should I see the other basins coming into the light. About then I should be underneath the shelf with one heel hooked over my sister's heel, both of us reaching down. The shadow of the shelf would be full of pale things turning. For a moment, as the sky cleared behind us, we could see which end was which.
