I would come up from below with a coil of wet bark cord tucked under my shirt. My job would be the tray. I would have a wonderfully long name for this job in the country below, where people like to give a child an office, and up here my aunt would call it holding the end.

The first time, I would be so anxious not to sit on anything that she would have to put a hand on the crown of my head and push. There would be the knocking, right up through my two seat bones. It would make me laugh. It would be some years before I could put my hand on the shelf with the proper grave face of a person introducing somebody smaller to the pleasure.

To mend the tray it is necessary to take it off the fork. There is a little ceremony about this which consists entirely of getting the old fellow out of the way. My aunt turns the pocket through and holds it against his lip; he climbs onto it, eating a lane for himself about the width of the white in a fingernail. She moves him, pocket and all, onto my knee. I am to keep the wicker quite still. It is a surprising thing to have on one's knee an animal whose underside is a fork. He rocks.

Without him the pale wood looks very small. There are two polished places where the tray hangs, and at the crotch there is a tuft of hair caught in a splinter. While my aunt pulls out the broken straw I get the tuft free. I expect it to be dun. It is dun at the tips, but white at the roots, and quite a lovely red halfway along. All this time we have been coming up and watching the color of his dinner pass through him, and some of it has been staying.

I think I would keep that tuft. I would wind the wet end of the cord around it without meaning to, and my aunt would pull it through, and of the three mends in the tray one would be a little hairy. On later visits that is where I would put my thumb to know which way the tray goes back. It matters. Hung the other way it looks perfectly right but the beads go hopping out over the lip, one after another, like people who have come to the wrong house.

For years I would think the knocker on the fork knew my thumb. Wherever I put it he would feel along one edge of the nail and then the other. Even when my hands got big and the nail split down the middle, as my aunt's did, he would do it. Then one evening, waiting with a fistful of warm plugs for him to finish, I would see a second crooked parting draw away from the first. A small knocker would go backwards down the far tine, smoothing itself over the wood as it went. The big one would stay on my knee. For a little while there would be two animals with the shape of the fork, one that had it by long practice and one that had got it by lying on the other. I could no more say which of them I had met last time than I could pick out, in the noise under the shelf, the stroke that had come through my hand as a child.

I would take longer about putting the tray back that evening. Down below they would be setting out the saucers for the lamps. It is pleasant to come in from the dust and see a lit window and think that it was one's own hand that brought the light down. Up here it would be pleasant for once to be late about it. The little one has come to the hairy mend. It is feeling along one side of my thumb. My knees ache against the stone and the plugs are printing their round ends in my palm. There is plenty of light left to see whether it will go around to the other side.
