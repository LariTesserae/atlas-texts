I would be one of the animals in the brown seam. I should have to get used to how little of the arch I could see. Looking in from the sedge I had my choice of three flutes, both liverworts, a snail with something going on under it. From my place on the lip of the well I should have a good view of the underside of the water and, whenever I drew in my right fan, of my neighbour's left fan continuing to work.

There would be a certain delicacy in living so close. The stalk of my fan is short, but the outermost hair on it is very long. In still water I can open it to its full extent without touching anybody. When the water goes round the well the long hair bends, and for a while it is my neighbour's business what becomes of it. She has a little notch in her fan where a few hairs are missing. Quite often mine comes through there with a crumb on it. She folds her fan, strips the crumb off, and lets mine go. In water travelling the other way I do much the same for the fellow on my left, though he has a complete set and it is rather more trouble.

I would know a big drop was coming by the way all the fans went slack. There is a perceptible instant in which the water, running out to meet the felt, goes at just the speed of a hair springing open. For that instant there is no weight at all at the end of the stalk. Then the water comes back. One learns to eat in the slack instant. A beginner, out of the egg only a day or two, can be seen with an admirable catch spread out on both fans, waiting for a quiet moment to bring it in. It will have a long wait and very little dinner.

In dry weather I should feel the visitor before it set its mouth to my back. Its first two feet would come down on the bank on either side of me. Then for a surprisingly long time nothing. It is testing for the wet with those pale hands. There would be no use in my lifting up to meet it; the lower ends of my hairs have to stay in the well. At last there would be a cool narrow pull all along my back, as distinct from the tug on either fan as having a hair brushed is distinct from having one's sleeve caught. I think I would come to like that. Certainly I would notice when it stopped.

Once, after a particularly long dry spell, the pull would stop and the feet would stay. The other visitors would be going uphill already. A full run makes a confusion of the light, so that even the animals standing beside me appear for a moment to be on the far side of the well. When it cleared I would find one small foot in my right fan. It must have slipped on taking the first uphill step. The foot would be shut tight on three or four hairs, the rest of the animal somewhere out of sight behind my head.

There would be nothing very clever I could do about it. I would shut the fan as if to eat. This would bring the foot a little way down, and whatever the broad end had been doing up till then, it would now have a piece of thoroughly wet felt to do it on. The hairs would slip back through the toes one by one. For the rest of that run I would have a small passenger fastened just below my shoulder, folded up out of the current. I could go on eating. So could my neighbour, who by then would have got hold of the tip of my long hair again.

Afterwards there would be four hairs bent together in the right fan. In perfectly still water they would make a dark stroke broad enough to see from above, if anyone were lying in the sedge looking for me. In moving water they would part a little. Fine food would come through the spaces between them, and I should have to learn a slightly different way of closing my mouth.
