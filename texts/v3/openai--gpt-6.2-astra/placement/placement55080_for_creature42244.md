I would be under the fig, but not as far under as the flies. There is a skin on the side away from the split which is still purple and quite firm. It stands out a little from the pulp, like a blister. I would be the woodlouse in that blister. To get in I have to go past the place where I mean to stop, turn round in a wider place, and come back. From outside there is nothing much to see of me except, sometimes, one feeler going along the ground in front of the fig and then along the skin, as if to make sure the two are still separate.

It is the leaf showing through the fan of silt that brings me out. The green has gone from it now. On the underside it is soft enough to eat, except for the little cords that used to carry water through it. I work between two of these for a night or two. By day there is a long window in the leaf, with a film over it so thin that a grain of pale earth beneath shows pale and a grain of dark earth dark. Then the fern lets down its drop and the window is gone. I come to the edge of it in the dark and put several feet through before I find the cord again.

Of the frog I would know chiefly the hand. When it sits at the split its nearer hand rests on the piece of skin that makes my roof. Four small pressures come through, not all at once. If I am on my way out I stop. Sometimes the hand lifts to wipe the pulp off the mouth and I go a little farther, and when the hand comes down again it is behind me. We can spend a good part of a damp afternoon like that, the frog taking what comes out of the fig and I eating what the fig has come down upon.

Lower down, things are changing rather quickly. There was a little hard knob on the underside of my roof that I used to have to dip my back to pass. Now even with my belly to the earth I catch on it. I have been carrying pieces of leaf in with me, not on purpose; they stick to the edges of my plates and come off when I turn in the wide place. The turning place is nearly full. On the afternoon the fly opens its wing beside the frog’s foot I am outside for good, though I go on trying the edge of the skin here and there. A feeler still goes in quite easily. The rest of me makes the whole fig stir.

I would find another place by the projecting leaf. Its tip has dried and lifted, and the drop, coming through the hole I ate, has washed a little earth away beneath it. There is room to turn round without any special arrangement. For the first night I keep well back from the hole. The next night I sit in it. My back fits the long window fairly well; the two cords run one on either side of me. When the first tap comes on the fern I draw in my feet, and for a little while the rain falls on a leaf with a sound part in it again.
