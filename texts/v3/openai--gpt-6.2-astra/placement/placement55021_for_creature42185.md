I would have one knee down in the old stream and my other foot in the new one, trying to get a straight measurement between the two without doing a mischief to my trousers. I would be the person sent out to settle the question of the shed's address. It might seem an odd thing to settle in advance of moving it. But there is a little money belonging to each street for the use of its inhabitants who cannot pay for silence, and as matters stood the shed was about to leave the district where most of the bad teeth were.

I carry a wallet of rubbing paper, a length of cord which will not stretch in brine, and a list of householders entitled to sign things. Eda's name is on it. Her father's is not, since the list allows only one householder to a house. This has saved the city a great deal of paper at the expense of having the wrong person available at almost every door.

The first time I came, it was her father I found. He held the end of the cord for me. I asked him where the old stream had run before it ran under the shed.

“Which way am I facing?” he said.

I told him uphill.

“Then through your lunch.”

I moved my lunch. There was a stud under it, a very early one with a hole in the head where a second stud could be screwed in if the water came back. I had seen pictures of these. He let me take quite a long time over the rubbing and didn't tell me until I had finished that it was the cap of an old gas pipe. We ate some of the lunch together. He was quite as disappointed as I was about the stud. He'd thought it was one too, until the words *east kitchen* came up on the paper.

I expected to be there for an afternoon. In fact I came back for much of the night. What held us up was a difference between the plan of the district and the words by which the money had been left to it. On the plan the boundary went along the old stream. In the words it went “by the shortest wet way from the kitchen step to the place of washing.” There were still people willing to swear to six different places of washing. Nobody had thought there could be any difficulty about the kitchen.

I got to know the people who used the shed quite well from the knee down. It was necessary to work low, to see the reflection in the water, and I did not think I ought to look up when the inner door was working. There was a pair of split boots that nearly always went in together and came out ten minutes apart. There was a child in a man's fur slippers with string at the ankles. One afternoon a woman stood beside me a long time in a very fine pair of shoes, the salt eating the polish white. Eventually she said, “Shall I have to go somewhere else?”

She was under the impression I had come to see who could afford the fee. I stood up then. There are disadvantages to doing public business on one's knees.

It was Eda who found the kitchen. One day she came back from the ferry with her skirt pinned up on one side. Her son had nicked her with the scissors, taking them from her after his haircut, and she didn't want the little triangular flap to get caught and tear off. With the hem turned out there was a strip visible of what had been the margin of the plan. There, in rather better ink than had been used for the streets, was a note to the printer about a set of steps that had been put in backwards. The uppermost of them was under the bench where her father sat.

We found all three, still going down. The bench had been bolted to the top one and the other two had been filled up with broken cups. People had evidently gone on throwing things out of the kitchen door for some time after there was no kitchen. From the bottom step a tongue of water no wider than my cord ran straight towards the new site. It made no difference which place of washing we chose. For the first few yards the shortest wet way to any of them was the same.

I was able to enter the shed at its new address without changing its street. The woman in the good shoes never asked me about it again, but after that, if I was in her way, she put a hand on my shoulder and stepped over the cord.

There was a job for me on the day of the move, which was to carry the bench. It was a two-person job. Eda's father took the light end, the end where the grain had opened up and a good deal of the wood had been worked out by the seats of people's coats. We walked behind the shed. With its weight off it and its six feet bound up in cloth it looked so like a piece of household furniture that people kept coming out of doors to advise us how to get it round the corner. Eda went ahead with the dish held against her stomach. She looked back at every turn, although hers was the one load in the procession that nobody could very well drop but herself.

At the new site we set the bench down before we set the shed down. Her father sat on it and tried the distance to the treadles with his feet. He had us shift the whole shed the width of his thumb. This struck me as a very considerable use to make of a thumb, but the men from the exchange did it without remark. Then Eda went underneath, and for a while nothing could be heard of her but the regular sound of one saucer being put down while another was picked up. Her father and I sat outside. The knocking here was very full. I had got used to the old street without knowing it.

I thought he might say something about the difference. Instead he felt along the underside of the bench and took out a key. He handed it to me and pointed back the way we'd come.

It was the key to the box for the silence money. The box was still on its post at the old site. I went back with a barrow to fetch it. There was a man standing there with a coin in his hand, looking at the six round marks and the dry place in the middle. He had come off a long shift in one of the bridge piers and had slept through all the notices. I showed him where to go. He put the coin in the box first, out of habit, and I heard it fall about twice as far as I would have expected.

That would be my place, I think: a little to the rear of the thing that is happening, coming along with its address and the box that belongs to it. I would go back sometimes to the steps. You could sit there now that the bench was gone, if you took a folded coat against the edges of the cups. At first I went with the cord, in case the little tongue of water had altered and the papers needed doing again. Afterwards I left the cord in the office. From the bottom step I could hear two callers, quite far apart. It took a number of visits to establish that the second one was not an echo of the first. I did not put a stud there. I took Eda's father down one evening and let him sit in my place.
