I would be the woman who tried to take the garnet house off the map. Not the woman who wintered there. I knew her a little. She had given me the depth of the new step by laying her thumb against it and making a mark with her teeth on her thumbnail. By the time she reached the station the mark was a poor one, but it told me more than a figure would have done about what she had been doing down on her knees in that cold room.

We printed the houses as little squares with two breaks, one for each door. You could tell, before you set out, whether at the end of the day you would have to walk round a house to get in. We were rather proud of that. The squares were considerably bigger than the houses; drawn to scale, a house would have gone into the white middle of its own sign several hundred times, and anybody relying on the map alone towards evening was going to be glad of the peach light on the roof.

My work was mostly in the station. To take a house off, you filled the cuts in the printing block with a compound of wax and powdered stone, then rubbed it down flat with the ball of the thumb. Over the years the garnet belt had got quite smooth under my thumb. It was the part of the country I knew best and I had never been there.

What took me out at last was the question of the roof. If it no longer held level, a walker counting three lights from the east would count past the garnet house and go looking for a bed a good twelve miles too far on. I went with the winterer, who wanted a little brass cup she thought she had left there. Given a choice of errands, I would have taken hers. There was an end to it.

We came up from the east in good time and sat on our packs while the sun went down. Two roofs took the light. Then, much nearer us, in what I had been taking for an empty stretch of gravel, there was a short peach stroke, a space, and a second stroke a shade redder than the first. I could have covered the two with the tip of a pencil. One went out. While I was entering the time of this, the winterer put a hand on my book. I looked up. The first stroke had come back and the other was gone.

The roof had broken over the step. Each half was keeping its own level as best it could. It was quite a good light to walk by. Even after we had lost it we could pick out the house by a faint difference in the darkness, as you can go on seeing a little way into a room where a lamp has just been put out.

The cup was where she had left it, upside down to keep the grit out. It had taken a slight oval from being stepped on. She stood it on the high part of the floor and poured water into it, and we drank in turn from the narrow end.

I had brought a straightedge and a little lamp for examining the break. The young hems were gone. In one place, however, the lamplight showed a quantity of fine paired marks running up the face of the step. They did not go straight up. They went slantwise to a place where a flake had started out of the stone and where, for something the length of an eyelash, there would have been quite a comfortable landing. Above that the stone was too coarse to take a mark. I was getting my lens out when the woman said it was not necessary to hold the lamp for her any longer. She had got her bed made.

In the night I woke with a wish so plain and particular that for a while I mistook it for thirst. I wanted to put my hand on the low floor and my other hand on the high floor when the load went through. My bed was on the low side. It took very little moving. For a long time nothing happened except that my wrists grew tired. Then the heel of my right hand took a smart tap, and a little later the stone under my left hand sank away. Between the two there had been time enough to feel the nap of grit against both palms. I had never felt that in a sound house. There the beds and the cups and your own teeth all went light together.

“Different, isn’t it,” said the winterer in the dark.

I asked her if she still put a finger out.

“Where would I put it?” she said. It was an ordinary question. The break ran right through the neck rest. In the light of morning I saw that she had made her bed with her head at the other end.

On the block at the station I filled in one of the two doorways and cut a fine line across the square. The first proof had a black smudge where I had rested the side of my hand. On the second the new sign came out well: a house you could still get into, with something the matter inside. In the margin, where there was room for several words, I put *roof shows twice*. After that I went out more often. There were houses we had been printing from the same cuts for ninety years.

I have the oval cup now. She gave it to me for the washing water on my bench; it makes a good lip for wetting a very small brush. Sometimes in cleaning a block I draw the brush along the garnet belt and a little water stays behind in the cut across the house. It takes most of the morning to dry. If you were looking for me that is where I would generally be, at the west window for the sake of the light, tipping the block this way and that to see if the middle of the little room was dry enough to print.
