I would be under the rail’s foot. A pad of grey silk no wider than a scale on its middle toe is all that can be seen of my house from that side of the bank. The scale comes down on the pad, turns a little, and lifts away. Inside, a knot of us let go of the ceiling.

I am a little brown spider. There are six of us in the hollow left by a fig twig, all from one batch of eggs, though the two nearest the mouth are nearly twice my size. A burrow full of spiders need not have been made by spiders. There is a grub at the far end still eating its way into the twig. We follow it at a distance of an inch or two. The part it has finished with is a good fit for our mother; across the rest of us it is quite a roomy tunnel. At night the grub stops eating. You can put a foot on the bare wood and feel the small shock when it starts again.

The silk pad is the end of a line. It goes up over a splinter and down to where I sit, with one foot crooked round it. There are lines out from most of the holes on this face of the bank. My elder sisters have those that go into the liverwort. Mine ends on a piece of bare bark where nothing much goes by except the rail. I have had the rail on my line eight times this morning. Once I had a springtail, and was so well used to letting go that I let that go too.

Towards evening the bank begins to pull on all the lines at once. A great weight has settled somewhere overhead. The twig opens along its length, very slowly, admitting first a green light and then the actual edge of a leaf. One of my sisters goes out along the edge. The others go after her. For a while I stay on my splinter with a foot round the slack line. There is a sweetness in the tunnel now. The split has run on ahead of the grub into live wood.

When I come out I find we are on the underside of the bank. The leaf that came into our house belongs to a shoot growing almost flat against it, its upper surface scarcely the thickness of a beetle away from the roof. Out beyond its tip there is nothing near at all. A moth passing under me shows first the brown of its back and then, a long way off, the white of the same two wings. My sisters are already paying out silk into that air. One goes a little way down on her thread and lands against a root. Another hangs turning until her thread inclines enough to put her on the same root somewhat lower down.

I go back a little way along the shoot. There is a place where the stalk of the leaf makes a snug angle with it, and in the angle is a gnat trying to get its long legs round to the side where its wings are. It has come up wet from below. I take it just behind the head. It is the first thing I have ever held that pulled hard enough to make me brace my back feet against something.

By dark I have a line from the stalk to the roof and another going out towards the tip. Nothing from the walk above can set a foot on either. The rain when it comes makes a low place in the middle of the leaf; all night there is a little run of gnats going down to drink at it. Above them in the dry, with the sucked skin of the first gnat still sticking to one of my knees, I have more to attend to than I can manage. A rail knocks twice. The sound comes quite clearly through the stalk. I keep my foot where it is.
