I would live three layers down, in what used to be the clockroom of the south tram depot. There is still a round window between it and the ticket hall. It is a little too high for a person to look through, having been put there for people to look through from the other side and see the time. Once, standing on a chair to mend the blind, I looked out just as a man looked in. He glanced at his wrist and settled down with his paper, apparently quite satisfied.

My work would take me out in the mornings. When a barber finds a sound above a ceiling, he puts his blue ring round it, but he has generally got five or six more ceilings to do and cannot stay to find out whether the sound is still there. I would be the person who comes back. There is a folding set of steps for the purpose, with felt on the feet, and a book ruled in three columns: place, date, what was heard. The last is quite wide. One of my predecessors has written, in a beautiful hand, *Something going about with something in its mouth.* I would have liked to ask her how she knew about the mouth.

Some are easy. A little run that ends in a hard click is a button dropped on the floor above; presently one hears it all over again as the owner, feeling under a cupboard, knocks it farther in. Some are difficult because of the interest people take. The clerks of the South Rates Office once all held their breath for me at the same time. There was a minute noise in the ceiling, certainly, but after a while there was also the noise of seventeen people in considerable distress. I asked them to go on adding. It was easier then to hear the small thing at the end of its run, turning round. We left the ring where it was.

I think I would come to know the city chiefly by the tops of people's furniture. On the high shelf of the instrument shop there is a little orange shaving of copper curled up like a sleeping shrimp. In the mayor's dayroom, on top of a tall case containing the original survey, somebody has been keeping pears. I would have a flat place on one ear, and very little blue chalk of my own. The mark has nearly always been made by the time I get there. The useful thing I carry is a clean cuff to rub it off with, if it proves to be a button.

For years the clockroom would give me nothing to put in the book. It would suit me pretty well: the blind down at night, the late passengers talking on the far side, and the stir of air each morning as the depot roof slid back. Then one night, with my head in the white growth beside the bed, I would hear a long, laborious scraping. It would stop when I sat up. The next night the same. By the fourth night I would have got the steps out, moved the bed, and spent the best part of my time off with one ear to various parts of my own ceiling, hearing nothing whatever.

It would take a change of sheets to discover the cause. Between the bed and the wall there would be a young doorback, already quite broad, trying to pull a corner of the blanket down into the gap. It had managed perhaps a finger's length in four nights. The trouble was that I slept on the other end.

I would give it the blanket. There are other blankets. For a few weeks I would sleep rather badly for listening, and then go back to sleeping well. Occasionally someone waiting for the first tram would see a pink foot up against the clock glass, the ledge inside being just wide enough for a doorback to lie on in the early warmth. I don't suppose I would ever see it there myself. From my side of the wall, standing anywhere in the room, all one can see of that ledge is the underside.

There would be one entry out of order in the book, made some weeks after the fact. *South depot, old clockroom. A blanket being taken.* I would leave it at that. It is good, reading back through a long winter of ticks and soft rubbings and the occasional unexplained bump, to find an entry in which somebody got what they were after.
