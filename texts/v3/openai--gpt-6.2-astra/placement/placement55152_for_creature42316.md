I think I would come into it in the mouthful of water the cat brings up with the fish. Some of it goes back into the pool. Some goes down her throat. There is a drop big enough to hang for a moment from the lowest whisker, and when that falls it lands well up the bank, where no water has fallen for days. A little flour of clay rises round it. By daylight there is a dark pellet there, hard enough to pick up whole.

It is on that side of the pool I would find myself, just above the reach of a cat sitting on her haunches in the water. There is a round hole in the clay with a sill so narrow that a bird coming home to it needs to have its wings open until its toes are well inside. The kingfisher that made it has raised a brood and gone. For some weeks nothing much has happened in the hole except that a spider has put three threads across it and lost one to a falling leaf.

I would be the hole, I think. Not quite straight, as it happens. A little way in there was a pebble too firmly set for the two birds to shift; each, digging in turn, went to the left of it. Beyond that elbow there is no light at all. The passage inclines a little upwards to the chamber. In there are the fine bones of fish, all laid down when there were five warm bellies to keep them pressed flat. One scale still adheres to the roof. It got there on the crown of a young bird that was very nearly too big for the place and not yet ready to leave it.

From where the cat sits washing, I would look about the size of one of the dark spots on her shoulder. She has had a paw in me twice. The second time she tried lying over the lip of the bank and reaching down, which got her claws as far as the pebble. There was nothing for her either time. But the scratches are there, quite sharp beside the rubbed place where the birds used to turn.

Tonight what comes in is the moth. For several circuits it passes my entrance at just the same distance. Then it alights on the clay and walks in backwards a little way, till its wings are under cover and its two feelers project into the night. There is room behind it for the whole of its flight many times folded up. It uses less than the length of the cat's claw.

When the bend fills, the water will stand at my door a good while before it comes in. Then there will be a curious hour when a little fish can swim in as far as the pebble and a cricket on the dry side of the pebble need not move. After that the chamber will take water, all but a thumb of air against the roof. The scale may come loose then. What the bird carried in on its head will go out by the same crooked way, turning in the dark, and flash once in the open before it sinks.
