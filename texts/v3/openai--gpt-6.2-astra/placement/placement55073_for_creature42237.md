I think I would come in with the man who takes the Sunday shift. He has been told about the loose tread. Going down for the first time he puts rather too much faith in the one above it and lands sitting on the steps with his lunch still tucked under his arm. From there he has a good view of the underside of the kerb. There is a bubble running along it, silver and flat as a sixpence. It goes uphill where to his eye there is no uphill to go.

I should like to stay with him, because he will have to decide whether to mention the fall, and because of the care he takes, before getting up, to feel through the paper for the corners of his sandwiches. But he goes into Number 8 and shuts the door. Down here the small draught from the closing of it pushes the bubble free. It comes out through a ring of something that looked until then like a stain on the stone.

I would be in that ring, I think. There are perhaps forty of us, little grey shrimps bent nearly double, lying on our sides in a wet seam. We are considerably smaller than the prawns at the moth. A bit of leaf has been drawn into the seam stem first and we have eaten the soft green parts out of it as far as we can reach. The veins remain, rather a handsome piece of work going on in the dark. There is one vein between my body and the stone. When the pumps run I can feel it sawing very slightly across my back.

My particular accomplishment would be getting round the end of that leaf. It is not easy. On the street side there is plenty of room, but something takes you there; nobody who has tried that way is in the seam now. On the land side there is a space the thickness of a coat of paint. I get one feeler through it and then, by drawing my legs up, the other. It takes most of a Sunday to get as far as the first pair of legs. In the meantime I eat. There are places on a leaf where it is better to be than at the edge, and I have found one of them.

When the eel moves, the difficulty solves itself. The stone comes off my back and for a moment there is room for a hundred of me. Along with three grains of blue grit I fall through the floor of the seam. It is quite light below. There is the round white underside of the tile, and close against it a skin the colour of the wet paper round the Sunday man's lunch. A long fold goes down the skin. It draws the water after it and draws me too, past a crab claw with nothing in it, into a pocket in the sand. By the time the stone comes down I am out of its way.

This is where I would find myself: on the back side of the spoon, where from the boat you would put the eel's throat. There is a little brown stuff on the glaze that comes away readily. It has never been worked over. Above me somebody has opened the pump-room door and is calling to know whether anybody saw him slip. There is no answer. I set my feet well apart on the curve of the tile. At each pull with my mouth I slide a little nearer the bottom, which for the present is just the direction I want to go.
