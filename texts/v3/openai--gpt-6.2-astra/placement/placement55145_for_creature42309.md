I think I would live at the turn in the goods lift, six floors below Ada. A basket going from the orchard to the ground has to change cars there. The upper car is long enough to take a pear wire rolled on its drum; the lower one is nearly square, having been built for stone. My room would be in the angle left over when the two shafts were brought together. I would have a long kitchen and a nearly square bedroom, with a door between them that would open only when both cars were away.

I would mend baskets. There is a short time, while the baskets stand empty at the turn, when you can see what ails them. Loaded, they all look much alike. Empty, one stands on three corners, another lets daylight through its bottom, another has a fine upstanding splinter just where a man will put the quick of his thumb. I would have a little tank of willow soaking under my bench. The water would have to be changed on Tuesdays; on Wednesdays the woman in the ticket office would come by for it, since her cuttings strike particularly well in water that has had willow in it.

At first I supposed I would know a great deal about the traffic, sitting there all day with the loads going by. In fact I would know a great deal about their undersides. I could tell Ada’s baskets by the little flat on one runner where she puts her foot to steady them on the scales. I would know which households received peaches by the scraps of print that lodge under the binding. Once I would spend the better part of a morning trying to put a timetable together, only to discover that the 4:10 which left my left knee arrived at my right the summer before.

I would know Ada herself rather less well. She would come down now and then to have a handle moved. Her left hand has begun to close a little more reluctantly than her right, and it is easier for me to slip a handle two stakes along than for her to explain this at home, where somebody is sure to fetch a bottle and rub her hand until it hurts. While I work she would sit on the end of the willow tank and read the notices for the lower galleries. The notices are several years old. She likes to see who was going to marry whom.

It would be one of the baskets I had condemned that brought the girl down to me. I had cut its handles off, which is our way of preventing a bad basket from making one more trip, and set it on the upper car to go back for kindling. The girl would arrive with it over her head. Without the handles there was nothing much to hold on to, she would say from inside it.

What she wanted was a shallow tray to put beneath the orchard. There was a pigeon on one of the bars with string wound round its foot. It had lost a toe already. The girl had no notion of catching it, but she had seen how the string caught on a rivet each time the bird moved sideways along the bar. If there were a tray of earth over the rivets it might at least be able to walk to the warm end with the others.

I would start to tell her that a tray full of earth is a considerable weight to carry down a ladder. Then it would occur to me that she had come six floors with a basket on her head, and probably knew what she could carry. We would take the bottom out entire, leave a lip two fingers deep, and run wire through it in four places. For earth I would give her the sweepings from under my bench, which are mostly willow bark but quite pleasant underfoot. She would go back in the lift to the orchard. I would keep the sides of the basket. A condemned basket often has perfectly sound sides.

I should like to say the pigeon would use it. What I think would actually happen is that the tray would remain empty for three weeks, after which a pair of quite different pigeons would begin a family in it. I would hear this from Ada, who would also tell me that the string had come off of its own accord and was now hanging from the rivet, out of reach unless you went down two rungs and leaned to the left. The girl had decided to leave it there. It made a useful indication of the wind.

In time there would be people who knew my floor chiefly as the place to change cars, and people who knew it as the place where they had a good piece of willow put in and could get their hand under a load again. I would like both sorts to feel free to come through the kitchen when my door was stuck. I would hang the cups low. From the bedroom, lying awake between the two long journeys of the night cars, I would sometimes hear a cup settle back on its hook, and then the much smaller sound of the next cup along, touched by its rim.
