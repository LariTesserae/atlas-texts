I think I would come down in the box as a seed. There would be a nick in the moss cushion where I fell. For a day or two the two lips of green would be held apart. Then the cushion would swell and there would be nothing to show where I had gone.

There is brown water in a moss cushion but there is air in it too. I would go wrong on my first attempt at a root. It would come out into air between two whiskery brown stems and hang there with a bead on its tip. The bead would get quite large. Its lower curve would touch a stem, the bead would run off down the stem, and my root would be left lying against a wet surface almost as good as earth. From the upper side of the root a great many hairs would come out and fasten me to a single dead moss leaf. It is surprising how little purchase is needed to lift two leaves into the light.

My first leaves would be smooth spoons, one a little out of shape from having had to come up through a fork. Nothing in the box would eat them. The springtail, arriving at the fork by a route of its own, would find a tight turn there which had formerly been easy. It would go over my leaf instead, and leave a little of the damp on its belly in the hollow of the spoon.

I would like to be a bittercress. There is generally one in the pot of something one has gone to a nursery expressly to buy, and it is quite possible that my parent would be up on the pavement in a pot of yellow wallflowers standing by the timekeeper's door. But down here there would be no need for a name until my third leaf came, with a round lobe at the end and a smaller lobe like a thumb to either side. Then somebody might know me. From above I would look much too big for the place. This would be an effect of the familiar shape of the leaf. One expects to be able to put a finger under a leaf like that and turn it over; two of mine together would scarcely cover a fingernail.

I would have a particularly good hold by then. One root would have gone through the hole in a washer and turned up again, and from its elbow a fine white root would hang in the room beneath. The shrimp on the ceiling would have to put a feeler to either side of it. I would feel very little of the pump in my leaves. Down in that root it would be another matter. A grain of rust lodged between the root and the washer would keep working at the same bit of skin, until a slight pad formed there, brown at the centre and white all round. Even if somebody took me out and washed me, that would be a piece of the works I would keep.

I don't think I would manage much of a flower. I would put up a stalk which in the ample light under the glass would have no occasion to grow taller than the fern. Four white petals would open rather unevenly, one still folded along the middle when the others were quite flat. Seen by the person kneeling on the pavement they would be a second bit of enamel. The way to tell would be to move one's head a little. The square of wire would shift across the white on the bottom before it shifted across the white on the stalk.

There might be just one pod. While it was green it would point across the saddle. As it dried it would turn a little towards the wall; one afternoon its two sides would curl back so sharply that all my seeds would be gone before the stalk had finished shaking. Most would strike tile and go straight into the water. One would stick halfway up the bolted foot, in the sweat, and come down very slowly, travelling about the width of its own brown back in an hour.

By then my lower leaves would be lying flat on the moss. The stalk would come away at a touch. My white root in the room under the washer would last rather longer. It would soften at the elbow first, and the end which had hung clear of the floor would begin to trail past the doorway. There is a shrimp there quite strong enough to pull it in.
