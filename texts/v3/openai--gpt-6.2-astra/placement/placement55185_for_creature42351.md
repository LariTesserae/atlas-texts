I would be the woman with her arm in the roof. My ring has left a greenish mark all round the leg of the duckling. I rub at it with the ball of my thumb and discover it is a thread of moss. The child is impatient with me. There is a mother waiting.

We tip the basket at the low end of the yard. The duckling goes out over the lip of it on its belly, its feet working behind it to rather better purpose once they touch earth. Its mother gives it one hard look. Then they all go off round the corner of the house, eleven quite dry and one with the nap still stuck down on the back of its head.

There is a little gravel in the basket. So that is where she has been. I have been thinking, with my arm in the dark up to the place where my sleeve is let out, that I must speak to her about coming straight home. Now that I have my arm back there is a stiff throbbing in it and I let her take hold of it above the wrist and straighten it for me, very slowly, the way her aunt does. By the time it is straight we are talking about something else.

I patch roofs. It is work that falls to the person on the higher street, who has only to open a gate and walk out with a spade, rather than to the person who has to live under the result. There are twelve of us along the rim who do it for the first shore. We have our differences. There is a man at the far end who likes a good thickness of yellow iris on a roof. He says you will never see a slip where those roots have knit. That is true. You will see a man indoors in April holding a basin under a yellow iris.

I know the duck hole well enough to put my hand in without fear of breaking through anybody's ceiling. Behind it is the turned-up end of a timber knee, and behind that comes the little space where the rafters change direction over the bread oven. I could have told the child where the duckling was. What I could not do was get it to come out. It took the bright movement of the ring, or perhaps it took the sound of another person arriving. A foot coming down on turf makes a much greater noise underneath than you would suppose.

After supper she shows me the map. She has drawn the boy's house very large and the meeting house scarcely bigger than a bench. I see that the sizes have to do with how much they know. There is a narrow crooked space which I take for a lane until she puts the side of her hand against it. It is where the paper was laid on the lip of the inlet and got wet. They have drawn up to the mark on either side.

I offer to put in the oven and the timber knee for her. She gives me the pen but keeps one finger on the place.

“Have you had your head in?”

“My arm.”

“Which one?”

She takes the pen back. Beginning at the mouth of the hole she draws the length of my arm, narrow at the wrist, broad at the place where the sleeve was tight. At the far end she makes a little circle and goes over it twice. I think it is the duckling at first. It is my ring. The rest of the roof she leaves alone.

In bed that night I can feel the knocking again, though it was scarcely enough to be felt at the time. I turn the ring round. There is a place on the palm side worn so thin it has a little give in it. I have been taking it off to knead bread for years now, and putting it on to handle a spade, which is the wrong way about if you want to keep a ring.

In the morning the child goes down with the eggs. I go out to the eaves to put back the piece I lifted. The hole wants closing; there will be rain through it otherwise. I bed a flat slate in over the oven, with its lower edge well out, and lay the flap of turf back over that. There is still room for a duck, though she will have to turn round to sit.

From three shores down a sleeve goes in and out of my sister's door. I cannot see the basket at that distance. I know it is my child by the way the door stays open after her: a moment for herself to go in, and another for whatever she has brought.
