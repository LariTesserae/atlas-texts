I should have come up with the pears. There is room under the twist of paper at the stalk for something considerably larger than I am. Three of us once came up under the same twist. For the greater part of the journey the only inconvenience was a gradual tightening of the paper as it took up the damp. Then somebody in the yard took the crate off a barrow and set it down on its side. What had been the floor of our little compartment became a wall, and a very ripe pear came against the door.

I got out in Linnet’s kitchen. The other two, I think, went into a tart. It need not have been a disaster. We are quite good at leaving a thing that is getting hot, provided it gets hot on one side first.

Down by the river we live chiefly in the joins of things. A leaf pasted by rain to another leaf makes a house with two good walls and a way out almost anywhere. When the sun gets round to it the whole establishment comes apart. We gather what we eat from the two surfaces as they separate. There is a pale bloom there, too thin to take a thumbprint, which does not occur on the outside of the leaf at all. Paper against a pear makes something very like it. I had no particular intention of leaving home.

For a time I lived under the saucer. The dresser leg stood a little off centre in it, with the result that one side of the saucer bore hard on the flags and the other stood clear by about the depth of a sixpence. I could go in at the wide side and walk round until the fit overhead was to my liking. On Sundays the sky went away. By the time the woman had attended to the range and put the saucer back, the floor was dry and the best part of a week’s housekeeping had to be done again. I tried living in the saucer instead. The animal upstairs sent down a quantity of grit in the night, preceded by a single larger piece which struck the china sharply. After the first night I knew to get under the lip when I heard it coming.

I am rather like one of those bits of grit myself until I move. I have two broad forefeet, each with a little fringe which I can lay flat or stand up like the brush on a dustpan. They are of use in gathering the bloom. They are also of use to me in knowing how much room there is on the other side of a narrow opening. The rest of me will go where the two brushes have gone abreast. I have sometimes had to wait for digestion to make this true.

What took me out to the lane was the smell on Linnet’s can. She kept it just inside the kitchen door and there was a dark scrap of moss caught in the crimp of tin behind the spout. I had been living in that scrap for a day or two when at dusk it began to rain from underneath. We went a considerable distance at a good height. At the farthest extent of the woman’s arm the scrap came free and I arrived in the middle of the crossing.

Under moss there is no need to go a day’s journey to find the next leaf. One has only to turn over. I did very well there. By spring there were numbers of us, enough to leave a whitish line along the lane where we had cleaned the underside of the red growth. Linnet saw it one evening when she lifted a corner to look for a missing teaspoon. She rubbed a little of the line between finger and thumb, smelt it, and put the corner back. The spoon turned up in the oats.

I could tell her something about the right of way. From her kitchen the crossing appears to be one continuous piece of moss. In fact there are two pieces, which meet a little nearer the empty house than hers. They grow across one another there like the two sides of a shirt put on in a hurry. In going one way a beast has the edge of the overlap running smoothly down its back. In going the other it has to get its nose under a hem. If two beasts meet a little to either side of that place, the one which has not yet passed through the overlap can generally be felt to give a little push. Then there is a very curious half minute in which neither goes forward and the whole roof gets slowly higher. We lay our brushes flat. It is no time to be caught in the nap.

But I could not tell her which will back out. I have been brought the whole way to the hedge on the forehead of a beast that by every appearance had the easier passage. It went backwards with such care that not so much as one of us was brushed off. At the edge it sat down, passed a hind foot over its nose, and waited until the other had gone by. Then it went in again. There was plenty of room in the hedge to go round.

In the last dry winter Linnet was away for eight days. Her sister had taken ill down below. The boy attended to the dresser and ate his meals at the laundry, and for the first three evenings it did not occur to him that there was anything to be done about the lane. The moss drew up. On the fourth evening he saw from the kitchen two bare strips of mud with a high red roll between them, like the ridge down the breast of a plucked fowl. He took out a pail and emptied it over the roll. Most of the water ran straight down the lane and in under the door of the laundry, where somebody called out to him to have a little sense.

He came back later and lay down full length with his cheek on the flags. I knew the feel of him; he had done much the same thing on the school stone, and a fair number of us live along the sandpit now. He put two fingers under the crisp upper edge. Then he went indoors and brought out the twist of paper from a pear. He opened it, wetted it at the pump, and eased it a little way in. It lay there all night with one end trailing in a saucer of water. By morning there was a dark patch the size of his cap. He moved the paper along. By the time Linnet came home there was a narrow passage all the way across, quite a good passage if a beast was willing to keep its elbows in.

She was pleased with him. I do not think she ever quite understood the expense. For eight nights the kitchen dresser had stood on his best folded neckerchief, and when at last he drew it out there was a small pink hole through all four thicknesses.
