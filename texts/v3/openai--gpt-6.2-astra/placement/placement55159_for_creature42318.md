I would be the person who crosses his own name out in the visitors’ book. The girl lets me do it because I take the book away with me.

It comes to the bindery on the ninth street about once in two years. While it is away she has a board with a clip on it, and the sheets from the clip have to be sewn into the book when I bring it back. A book which goes on being written in during its own repair presents a small professional difficulty. You can never trim it finally square. Somewhere there is always another leaf coming down the stairs.

My shop is in one of the plated streets. When I am knocking a stack of pages level on the bench, the woman underneath sometimes comes up and asks me to do the next lot over her kitchen. She is beating something and likes company. We have never got the strokes to coincide. By the time she hears mine her spoon is up.

The first time I took the visitors’ book apart, I thought the girl had been ruling lines in it with something greasy. There were pairs of little grey tracks, particularly on the pages with few names. They were just too far apart to have been made by the two points of a bent hairpin. I tried bread on one of them. The grease came off and underneath it, pressed into the paper, was a succession of minute slanting marks, like the stitching on the inside of a glove.

I was a considerable time discovering that the marks were in my own book also. Mine is a daybook. It lies open at night to let the paste on my fingers dry before I shut it. The light at my window is good enough to work by and quite useless for seeing anything in shallow relief. It occurred to me to take the daybook down to the landing, where one can get practically the whole sky on an edge of paper without getting any of it on the face.

“Moths,” said the girl.

She put the two books side by side on her desk. There were the same tracks in both. In mine they went straight over the writing. In hers they went round a name, or broke off altogether and began again on the other side. We waited for a moth. Nothing came but a cricket, which she picked up in the hollow of a key and carried to the stairs. She said it would put us back a good hour to leave him there.

I used to think of myself as the one with the tools in this acquaintance. I had a lens on a joint, needles with a bend for getting behind a tape, and a little sprung clamp which would hold a single page up by a corner without making a mark. The girl had a pencil. But she also had the hours from the morning lift to the evening lift and had found out how to occupy nearly all of them without disturbing what went on in the intervals. An hour with her was long enough for a thing which had been happening under one’s nose for years to happen once more.

The moths came up the underside of the desk. The first put the tip of a wing over the edge and kept it there while the rest of the moth came round. The second followed the first so closely that for a moment it appeared we had got a four-winged one. They walked across the blank portion of a page. At the downstroke of a capital B they separated, went one on either side of the name, and joined up at the tail of the last letter.

My entries are in pencil. The visitors sign in ink. It would have been quite satisfactory to leave it at that, but the girl turned back six months and presently found the name of a man who had brought his own pencil. The tracks went round him too.

“Feel,” she said.

I closed my eyes and passed a finger over the signature. He had been one of those people who know a pencil makes its mark by pressure and wish to be on the safe side. I could have read his name on the back three leaves down. On the face there was a furrow, with a ridge of displaced paper on either side. It took the jointed lens to see it, but a moth walking with its wing tip just ahead of its feet would have come to something very like a street with the floor up.

We tried making furrows with my needle on a waste leaf. This was the part at which I ceased to have much of an idea whose assistant I was. I made a square round a pair of moths. They went over it without the least hesitation. I made a very deep furrow. They followed it for a bit, one on each bank, and went off about their affairs. The girl wrote her name. They went over that too.

We had the answer the following winter, when a lift party came down in stiff new jackets to look at a wheel they had subscribed to. Each man signed, and the book lay open all day under the little brass weight on its cord. At dusk the girl called me down. A moth was walking along the downstroke of a capital B. It went all the way to the foot, turned, and walked back up it. Its companion stood with the tips of both wings touching the first moth’s hind feet whenever it came back within reach. Under the lens one could see a little darkness on the lip of the furrow. The moth was taking it off.

The men had come down from the wheel by way of a staircase with a newly dressed leather handrail. They had steadied the leaf with the same hands they had used on the stairs. The moths weren’t avoiding the names. They were going round the edges cleaning them. There was nothing on my hands but dry paste, and on the girl’s, ordinarily, nothing at all.

There is a piece of calf leather folded over the spine of the visitors’ book now. I dress it very sparingly. It is a good place to put a thumb when bending to sign, and the board no longer cracks at the hinge. If in time the moths learn to go to the spine directly we shall have to think of some other way of finding out where they go by night. So far they still come up over the edge and set off together across the great expensive field of paper.

I am in the book myself some forty times, more than any stranger. The girl enters me when I come down to see her even if I have only come to get my clamp back. She writes my name small so as not to use up the town’s property. On leaving I put a line through it, except once when I stayed talking to her and we came back up together in the dark.

That entry is still open. I came across it at the bench the last time I had the book unsewn. My first inclination was to put it right. Then I saw that on the line below, in the narrow space usually left for the time of return, she had drawn my clamp. She had got the spring exactly, including the odd half turn I put in it to keep the jaws parallel. There was even the little square of blank page held in its mouth. I had never seen my clamp look so much like an implement worth having. I set the leaf back in its gathering and passed the needle through the old holes.
