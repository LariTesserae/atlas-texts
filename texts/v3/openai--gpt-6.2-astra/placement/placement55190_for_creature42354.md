The place I would try first is the buttonhole under the station floor. A little bast caught in it might tell a good deal. Draw on the bast just after a delivery and it comes away freely; draw on it just before one and there is a turn in it which no amount of pulling will undo. Yet the parcel now goes nowhere near the loop. The exact instant of its going by is still available down there, like the warmth on the underside of a pad after the reel has lifted.

I think I would have a use for that instant. There are fittings which cannot be tested on the bushes. A canopy knuckle, for example, ripens two eyes at a time, one inside the other; it takes the weight of a canopy to pull the eyes far enough apart to admit a pin. If the pin is offered early it goes through one eye, slips past the other, and comes out in precisely the right place on the far side. A season may pass before the canopy is called upon to turn. Meanwhile the ends of the pin have flowered and there is an excellent selvage all round the joint.

Some way inland of the buttonhole there would be a little bed for trying these knuckles. No canopy, only a strap running under a floor. At the passage of the parcel the strap would tighten, a knuckle open and a pin of floss go through. The parcel would ride the easier for it; it is a good thing to take a little of the spring out of a floor where a belt delivers onto it. Most of the floss pins would come out round. One in a great while a pin would come out with a flat, shining cheek. That knuckle would be put back on the bush for another circuit.

For a long time I might be mistaken for the bed. But once, when a knuckle was taken off for use before it could be tried, the strap would tighten all the same. There would be a small hard crease in the floor. After two or three such creases a fitting of quite another gauge would be brought there, something for which no test had ever been provided. I would be interested to find out what it did under a pull.

That is nearer where I would be: in the bringing of the second fitting. The strap and the station could manage the knuckles between them. It would occur to me, with a whole empty circuit before the next knuckle ripened, to put a thing in the strap crosswise, or by its growing end; not enough to spoil it. A plug of cork pulled lengthwise parts down the middle into two brushes so fine that when the pull is taken off not even oil can find the parting. I would try that more than once. Eventually there would be a district in which a severed filament of bast was mended by threading the two ends into a cork while it was under tension. In the district beyond, the ends would still be grafted in the ordinary way. I doubt I would follow the corks very far.

I would like the new incline, particularly while the teeth were soft. There is not much one can put against a tooth that will not determine the shape of it thereafter. I would leave the underside alone. Up on the ridge, though, it would be possible to lay a strap with a little slack in it and wait for the ground to take up the slack. At first all that would happen at the trying bed would be a faint stirring among the used pins. Later a pin might roll the length of its own shadow. It would be a long business getting anything up that hill.

By the time the old incline reached the parapet I would have a reel there, a small one, such as grows at the very tip of a bush at the end of the crop. Each time the ridge lifted, the strap would carry the reel forward a little. Each time it sank, a lip of floss would keep the reel from rolling back. There would be no delivery marked for it anywhere. On the last circuit of the old teeth the reel would come gently over the crown of the ridge and settle into warm tow on the other side.

Perhaps the attention that steadies the great reels on the turns would be there too. It has had, so far as I know, no experience of letting one run downhill. For several circuits beforehand I would leave the strap easy, so that under the small reel, still sustained against the slope, there might be felt the gathering, quite harmless inclination to go.
