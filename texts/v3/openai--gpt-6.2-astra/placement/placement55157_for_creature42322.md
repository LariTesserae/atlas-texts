I would be one of those that go round. You could pick me out by my left hind print, which has a little comma at the end where I turn the foot to get it off the ground. In the late afternoon, when we are getting up, the same foot sometimes stays behind on the sill. I have to sit down again and fetch it. This is how a pink-soled youngster, out on the street for perhaps the fourth time in its life, comes to be in the grove ahead of me.

It has chosen a fine purse, quite fat, at the end of a long stalk. Each time it gets a nail into the seam the stalk gives a little. It will have that purse open by morning. I sit down with my back to it and attend to three less handsome ones growing close against the stem. There is some advantage to knowing which part of a plant will hold still.

After a while I feel a small warm weight against my back. The youngster has contrived to brace itself. For a few moments we sit tail to tail with the stalk bent over its shoulder, and then there is a quite extraordinary quantity of seed on the ground. A seed down here is the color and very nearly the size of a grain of grit. We find them with our lips. Sometimes two pairs of lips arrive at the same grain, in which case it is generally grit.

I shouldn't know the town from the description of its towers. My way home goes by a wall that is cool against the whiskers, a place where a draft parts the fur on my chest, and a narrow interval in which it is necessary to go very fast on account of an owl. A good part of this way is available to me with my eyes shut. Tonight, coming back with dust on my lips, I put my face into the draft and find a wall there. The builders have been at work for days closing the interval, leaving at the bottom a hole just large enough to put a hand through. There is still a beautiful draft.

I try the hole with my hand. So does the youngster, who has come up beside me. We sit for a time feeling the air from a street we cannot get into. Then the youngster goes off along the wall. It has spent a good deal of its short life going along walls to find out where they end. Presently I feel through my forefeet the little double knock, and put my cheek down. It comes again, from surprisingly close. There is a way through two bounds back, under a ledge. I must have passed it every evening for a season. It smells of owl, but so do any number of places where an owl would not fit.

On the other side we are on the oval. The youngster goes straight up the low step and I go round it. By the time I reach the sill it is well inside, a gray back among gray backs, with just room beside it for another. I turn twice in this space and settle with my nose in somebody's warm neck. My left foot sticks out into the last of the light. A little hand comes out, takes hold of it by the heel, and draws it in.
