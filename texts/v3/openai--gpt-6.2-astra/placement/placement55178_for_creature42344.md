I would be under the enamel chip. There is room. The gray band makes so sharp a turn there that the chip, which looks as if it lay flat, rests on the stone at only three points. The green between is a ceiling. It is a very good green when the sun gets in at one side.

I would have come up through the first staple, I think. For a long time after coming up I would go on putting an arm overhead before venturing forward. Below the shelf this is quite a sensible way to travel. The silt gives under a sucker and comes away with it, whereas the roof is sound, and has seams in it that a small arm can follow for a considerable distance without once losing its hold. Here the arm goes up and up and presently makes a little dimple in the light. A bird can see that from the next tree.

The chip would be the first roof I found that felt right. A leaf gives an alarming amount when taken hold of from below. Under the chip I could draw myself up off the floor altogether. Something walking past would see daylight clear through the place where I lived.

There would be the three limpets to get used to. One of them comes down over the edge at night and grazes a little way along the green. I know it by the cold place it leaves when it goes back. The other two I know chiefly as weight. In the first weeks I can take the whole establishment with me. I go forward until my eyes are in the light, fasten two arms well ahead, and bring the shade up over my eyes again. In this way we do nearly the length of a fallen leaf in a morning. Across a groove is difficult. There is a moment when the front edge drops and all four of us must go down together, whether or not I have found a good place to put my feet.

By and by the limpets put on so much shell that I have to leave the chip where it is. For several mornings I pull as usual and succeed only in drawing myself out from under it. There are worse places to have stopped. Just uphill is a stand of weed no higher than the nap on a coat. It catches things coming down the groove. I can reach it without showing an eye.

One noon, feeling about in the weed, I take hold of the end of another arm. It is so very small that at first I take it for part of something good to eat. Then it takes hold back, in six or seven places closer together than the grains in the stone. For a little while neither of us does anything. The water from the middle staple has reached us; it is cold along the floor and still quite warm against the green overhead. Presently the little arm goes on past mine into the house. There is a small difficulty getting the eyes in. After that there is no difficulty at all.

I sleep that afternoon with two arms outside. The next day the small one goes away under a transparent scrap of fruit skin, a poor choice as far as keeping the light out goes, but wonderfully easy to move. I can follow its progress a long way by the pip that is still attached.

In time there would be too much of me for the chip. I would go out one morning after food and get only my arms back in. I expect I would stay there most of the day all the same, holding on to the familiar green with the rest of me in plain sight, darkening whenever a shadow went over. Toward evening the woman would set her mug on the lip. There is a worn place underneath it, a shallow arch where the mug rocks when she puts it down. Out of that arch would come a thread of water appreciably cooler than the water I was sitting in. I would let go with one arm to feel how far it went.
