I think I would live under the floor of the third house. Not under the whole floor. There is one board, between the water jar and the door, that has had its end replaced twice. The new piece is quite short and gives a little under a heel. I would be in the water under that.

For a long time I would know the people of the house chiefly by what they spilled. Bean water makes a cloud that takes the better part of the morning to go past. Plain water comes down in a single clear string, drilling a little pit in the silt in precisely the same place each day, because the dipper has a crack and is always hung on the same peg. Once a pot of milk went over. For three days afterward there were blind shrimp hanging by their forefeet from the underside of the board. When the keeper walked over them they all let go at once, and the bottom of the pool received a soft pattering, as of somebody applauding a long way off.

I would be one of the things that came for the milk and stayed. There are small fish under the islands, almost all head, with two fans just behind the gills that they use for getting over the sills of rock between pools. In a deep place they swim on their sides. This puts one fan uppermost where it can feel the ceiling. A fish that has spent its whole life in shallow water will go over on its side just the same if you put it in a bowl. It is a disturbing sight until you put your finger in above it. The fan takes hold of the ball of your finger and the fish tows itself about quite briskly, taking you along.

My pool would have a piece of broken crockery in it, blue on the inside. When the mud was stirred up I could find my way to the crock by the feel of its glaze against my fan. Behind it the water hardly moved. There I could finish things that took a little eating, the leg of a beetle, for instance, which has to go in the right way. There would be other fish. We would quarrel over the crock, especially on days when there was nothing in particular to eat behind it.

I shouldn't know about the river. I should know something about the bells that the people in the houses don't. For a little while before the skins move in the bean bowl, water comes *back* over the sill at the low end of the pool. It brings things from several rooms away. A little foreign mud arrives, reddish instead of black. Sometimes there is a taste of the fine weed the snails go out for. That would be my occasion to leave the crock. I would get over the sill while there was still enough water to cover both eyes and work my way down against the current, eating what came up.

One evening I would come to the underside of the lip. I don't imagine I would see the town. There would be so much light in the last inch of water that the small black shape of a snail, going along with its horns out, would be as much as I could distinguish. I would feel ahead for the ceiling and put my fan against something yielding. It would be the loose foot of an underleaf. For a moment each of us would have the use of a very odd part of the other. Then the foot would close a little, and I would go backward in a hurry, as these fish can, by shutting both fans hard against the face.

By the time I got home another fish would be behind the crock. I would have to lie up in the dipper pit with my chin in the silt. Overhead the keeper would wash her feet at the door and set her shoes upside down on the jar to keep the mice from eating the laces. The board would come down a little way, then rise. After a while the shrimp would begin climbing again. I would lie there in the dark opening and shutting the fan that had touched the foot, getting the ordinary water back between its rays.
