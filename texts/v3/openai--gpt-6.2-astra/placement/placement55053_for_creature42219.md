For the first few evenings I should be taken for somebody who wanted to get past. The street is narrow, and the people on the benches sit with their knees well out. A knee would draw in, then another a little farther along. Somebody would take a jar by both handles and lift it clear of the paving. At the far end of the street there would be a space just large enough for a person to stand in, and nobody standing in it. After a bit the man with the jar would put it down. It is disagreeable to hold something heavy for a person who shows no sign of hurrying.

I should know very little about this from my end. I should have a quite distinct idea of the distance between two knees, rather less of an idea of knees. The underneath of the jar would go by, with its little ring of red grit. Then I should be against the warm hollow in the bench where somebody had been sitting. The warmth would last me until the water drew apart.

I think it would be the girl who found out how to make more of me. She likes to go along the backs of the benches, setting one foot exactly in front of the other. Where a seat has cracked through, people put out a hand for her. Generally she slaps it in passing. One evening, for the pleasure of giving somebody a fright, she would put her whole weight into the hand.

It would be mine. For a moment I should have an arm all the way to the shoulder, with a pain high up in it such as a person gets from lifting a child he remembers as being considerably smaller. She would step down and straightway ask to do it again. This time there would be no hand. There would be two damp finger marks on her sleeve, of which she would be rather proud.

There were places in the catchment where a great many people had had to make room for one another. At the turn of the kiln stair the wall bellies out to accommodate the back of a jar. A person coming down with a board of unfired cups could not see his own feet there. Quite a number of left shoulders had acquired the habit of going flat against that wall. At one of the mouths in the red street, whoever leaned in to skim the water was held by the back of the belt. The glaze beside the mouth is worn away in two patches a good way apart. They are the marks of the feet of the one who did the holding. There was a knack to letting the belt out a little, against one's better judgment, when the skimmer said a little more.

On a night when that stair and the inspection mouth were joined I could carry a board round a corner without losing a cup. I could also let a person headfirst into a jar almost to the backs of his knees. I should have liked to be asked before doing either. It would take a while to get this preference across. The first word I could manage would be *steady*, a word of surprisingly little use in the circumstances. Everybody who heard it at once let go.

My place, once I had some choice in the matter, would be at the west bench. There are people who come down too late and have to go the last few yards on their hands, dragging a foot which, though present to all appearances, will not take any weight. I like the little adjustment a person makes on finding that what he took for the end of his walking is only the point at which he will have to walk with somebody else. We generally go a yard or two farther than strictly necessary. It is pleasant to have the same pace.

The old woman would have no use for me in this capacity. She had managed her foot before there was glaze on the west bench. But she would let me play her game. There is a throw in it that has to be made from a shut fist, and the first time I tried it the grit went out through the wrist. She did not allow me to take the throw again. On the other hand she spent most of the next evening finding me pieces too big to fall through. They make a different, deeper sound on the paving. By that sound, on very wet nights, the man she looks for can find us without having to peer under every bench.

I should like best the part of the morning after the game, when nobody needs a hand any more. We put the bits away under the little stool. The old woman goes through the business of undoing her sandal. Sometimes she props her short foot on my knee to do it, and I feel the impatient tug and then the curious ease of a strap coming free, although there is nothing to come free of. She goes before I do. For a little while I sit there with one knee higher than the other. Then there is just the stool, and under it the pieces she chose, too large for the water withdrawing down the joint to carry away.
