I think I would live at the hollow that gives back two knocks for one. From the old swimmer’s room it sounds as if the seed has struck twice, first with its full weight and then, having bounced, rather lightly. But a seed carried along a roof does not bounce, and the second sound comes even when the seed has stopped.

There is a small animal fixed to the underside of the roof there. I would be that animal. My shell would be much like a grazer’s, except that the two edges would no longer meet. The upper edge grows into the felt; the lower hangs a little way down in the water and has a lip fine enough to show brown light through it, if there were light. A seed coming round the bend strikes the shell and tips it up. The second knock is the lip coming back against a grain of hard stuff lodged in the felt. On the room side of the leaf this is considerably the clearer sound of the two.

What I get from the arrangement is a meal. There is a fringe in the opening of the shell which the current ordinarily plasters flat. For the instant the shell is tipped, water goes through the fringe sideways and leaves a line of food on every hair. It takes about the interval between one seed and the next to draw the hairs through my mouth. When two seeds come close together the second does me no good. To the swimmer with her cheek above me it sounds like quite an event.

I would know her chiefly as a weight. The young in the room make a pattering which is felt everywhere at once; her weight comes down on two places, with a long untroubled stretch of roof between. Under the nearer place the grain against which my shell strikes is driven a little farther out. The stroke is shorter then. I grow accustomed to feeding with her at home, to getting the fringe out quickly and taking what there is, and on the first day she goes to the court I miss nearly every seed. My mouth closes while the hairs are still on their way in. By the time she comes back I have it right for an empty room.

Once a seed jams under the lip. For most of a day I stand wide open, with my fringe streaming uselessly out behind me. There are knocks all along the gallery, none following quite as it should on the one before it. Towards evening one of the young gets its nose down the crack from above. It is after the green coating; I can feel its little jaws taking hold and slipping. The seed turns perhaps the thickness of a hair at each bite. Eventually it presents its narrow end to the water and is gone. My shell shuts on the bare grain. The young one shoots backwards and for a while there is the pattering overhead, extraordinarily fast. Then it puts its nose down again. There is still a little green on the lip of my shell.

As the roof thickens I would go on in much the same place. It is a disadvantage of being fastened by the upper edge that one cannot follow the good water. But it takes a long time for a gallery to flatten altogether. Even after the seeds have found another way, fine things come through, and a thread of root, moving up and down with the swells of the felt, lifts my lip often enough for a living. By then the old swimmer will have gone west. There will be days when the root lifts and does not let go for a long while, and days when I knock steadily in a room where nobody comes for seeds.

I like to think of the last youngster to use the crack. It would have to be very small. It lies belly down where the two leaves have grown nearly together, trying with the whole breadth of its face to get at a green speck just below. From where the old one used to rest it looks as if there were nothing there but a pair of eyes laid on the felt. Down here a single hair of my fringe has got into its mouth. I draw the hair in. The youngster holds on, not hard enough to hurt, and for a moment I can feel through that absurdly fine attachment its two fins going in the upper water.
