I would be the aunt who stayed to see whether the house had room.

It was my sister’s first visit to it since she was carried out under a white shell. She knew the knee for the belts. What she did not know was how far back the bed had stood. We put her roll where she thought it ought to go and lay down on it together. A hem came in, walked the length of us, and tucked its chin into the gap between our two necks. When I tried to get up, all three of us moved.

“Not there,” my sister said.

She had six or seven weeks yet. The others were going on to the fork, where there would be women who remembered the house in use. I said I would stay and try the bed in different places. My sister left me the roll. Once out of the trough she was hard to pick out among the others. I had grown used to knowing her by the way she walked, and lately that had changed.

For a few days I did the usual little jobs. The thong of the adze was grown in at one end; I worked it loose, very carefully, since I had nothing half so good to replace it with. I opened the mouths of the notches with its corner. There was meal enough in the seat to keep somebody for a week, but somebody would shortly be needing it. I got my supper by following a hem and putting my bare foot over the next check along from the one it was attending to. A hard lip disturbed at one hole backs out of another. The hem and I did fairly well, though not generally on the same evening.

What I needed for the bed was the weight of a person who would stay still while I got up. I took the roll down to the seam and brought it back full of water, the ends shut with a twist of cord. Laid across the slope of the floor it made a tolerable person. As I stepped about the house a bubble went from one end of it to the other with a little noise like a tongue coming unstuck from the roof of a mouth. I got rather fond of that noise. At noon when everything else was too loud I could lay two fingers on the hide and feel it go by.

Nowhere was quite right. Close under the belts was best, except that there one had to get out on the wrong side. In the middle of the night a woman accustomed to getting out on the other side would put a knee on her child. I began to think we had the wrong house. Knees grew wherever a prop had once bitten into the wall. One had no more reason to recognize this one than to know a dead animal by the bend it had left in the grass of a tray.

About the fifth morning I woke with a cold foot. The roll had leaked in the night. A thread of water was going along the floor, away from the door, which was odd because it was uphill. It ran to the foot of the back wall and there it disappeared. When I put the blade in after it I found the wall had a loose lip. Behind it was a space big enough to take my hand laid flat.

I had imagined a house getting old as people do, settling down on what was underneath it. This one was still in the act of folding. The old back wall stood a good arm’s length behind the one we saw. In between was a very dry place, full of the cast coats of small animals. I put my arm in to the shoulder and felt something I knew at once, although I had never been in that house before. It was the place for the lamp. A shallow cup with a runnel down one side, where the fat had got over the edge one time and the wall had grown round it. My mother used to give us that shape in the palm, telling us about the night my sister was born. All these years I had thought the runnel was part of a person’s hand.

I might have taken the adze and gone through. Instead I sat there on the floor with my arm up to the shoulder in the house until my hand went to sleep. I should have liked very much to see that cup. There were probably the marks of a burnt wick in it. But a house a woman remembered being born in was one thing and a house she could put a baby down in was another. Presently I took my arm out, with a good deal of small dry crackling, and went outside.

There had been a house beside this one once. It was a rise hardly higher than my knee, with one steep side toward the trough. On that side I found the old sill. I set my heels on it and felt no mouths at all. That is fairly unusual; in a place where people have come and gone for a long time the layers can grow quite solidly together. I unrolled the hide along the sill. There was room for a woman and a very small person and room beyond them for the feet of someone coming in. All it lacked was a roof.

When my sister came back I had a strip of new rind up on the borrowed fork and two little side walls just beginning their downward reach. It looked absurdly young beside the birth house. She went into the old one first to feel the lamp cup, and came out with dust up one arm and a look on her face I was glad not to have missed. Then we lay down in the other. She turned her back to me. After a while I got up. I went down to the seam and had a drink and came back, and she was still asleep.

That is where I would find myself, I think: outside the house, late in the day, with a roll to mend. There is a trick to finding a hole so small it lets water through but shows no light. You put the hide against the inside of your wrist and breathe on the other side of it, moving it along a little at a time. It takes a while. There would be no particular hurry. For once it would be rather pleasant to sit at a door and let a hem go by with its own supper.
