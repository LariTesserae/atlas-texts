I would be a little way round the turn, beyond the reach of the grandmother's hand. I would have come in on the stalk. When the old male laid it down he rubbed one side of me quite raw against the roof of the hole. For a day or two afterward bits of the roof stuck to me, so that when I moved it looked as if a patch of leaf had come loose and was going slowly uphill.

I would be one of the snails that clean the flowers. On the old road there is a snail at the bottom of nearly every bell. There is a ridge inside, below which the rain that blows in at the mouth can't drain away. I used to go round the ridge eating the soft white deposit the water left as it sank. A good bell kept me for most of a season. I had a shell the shape of a cupped thumbnail, with a pink line for every time the road had flowered since I was small. The last line stopped halfway round. In the hole there was nothing pink to eat.

It was a very good place otherwise. The hen's heat came through the stalk. There were things growing on the older lengths of it that had never grown in the bells, little crisp knobs that came away whole if I got my mouth under them. And on most mornings there was the boy's arm. He wore a band of plaited grass at the wrist, for the hen to put her claws into. It held a considerable quantity of salt. He had been counting eggs for some time before he found out about me. One morning he drew his arm out rather faster than usual and there I was on the band, drawn tight against his pulse.

His first notion was to return me to the bells. He was particular about putting things back. When he was very small he had put a ferrule back up the spout and been disappointed to get it again immediately. But there was no way to the flowering road except the way I had come, and when he set me on the new stalk with my head pointed out, I went round underneath it and made for the eggs. He let me go. After that he turned the band a little before he took his arm away, to ease me off without hurting my foot.

I think I would have stayed there. But a hen does not lay indefinitely in a place as satisfactory as that without eventually taking an interest in what she has laid. One morning the boy put his hand in and she pressed her breast hard down on it. He could feel a small sharp blow in the palm of his hand. He stayed until a wet head came through the shell and lay along his forefinger, with its two eyelids sealed by a shining thread. It took most of the day for the others to come out. The hen went down the notches with six chicks under her, a descent managed almost entirely by the boy, who had to stand on each notch in turn with the front of his shirt held open.

For several nights the little chamber was cold. I went as far as I could along the rooted stalk. Out in the air it had begun to put out the low swellings that precede the bells, and I lodged in the cleft of one of them. There was not yet much of a sheltered place there. Sometimes a drop went down between my shell and my back and I had to come all the way out into the weather to get rid of it.

The grandmother could see me from the shelf. Indeed, for a long while I was the only part of the new growth she could see, the stalk being light against light and my shell still quite pink. She sat under the eaves to shell her nuts and watched me go up and down what appeared to be a short piece of nothing. Once, while I was perfectly still, she saw me go sideways the breadth of her hand. She set down the colander. Next evening at the same hour it happened again.

It was the hen. She had got into the habit of taking her family out along the stalk at dusk. It was much too slight for this. The chicks went in single file, each leaning a little to the side opposite the one ahead, but at the tip there was the difficulty of turning round. For this the hen had to get under the stalk and hang by her feet, while the six stepped down onto the soft part of her belly and up the other way. The grandmother watched the maneuver through twice. On the third evening she took a switch out of the colander, split it with her teeth and slipped the split end over the stalk where it left the shelf. The other end she stuck under her seat. It was the first work she had done on a road in eleven years, and it left a hole in the colander through which the smaller nuts thereafter escaped.

By the time my bell opened, the wood had grown fast round the switch. There was now a firm place at the turn where even the hen could stand upright, and she stood there in the mornings after the chicks were grown, sometimes on just one foot, with her neck drawn in. The boy could see her quite clearly from below if he lay on his grandmother's seat. Looking up into the bell beside her he could also see my foot, a pale oval going round and round, interrupted at intervals by the shadow of my head. The ridge had come in nicely. After rain I could make the circuit without once having to cross a dry place. My shell had begun to grow pink again, with a little clear window in the last line through which, in a good light, one could see my heart.
