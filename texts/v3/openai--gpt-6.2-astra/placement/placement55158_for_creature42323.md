I think I would live in the rut. The minnow would have four inches of water overhead and room to turn end for end; I would be considerably farther down, in the dark among the gravel. What I would know of the minnow would be a little dust coming down through the roof when it turned.

In the morning a person poking about there with a straw might find a curious stick. It is as long as the last joint of a finger and seems at first to have grown a coat of grit on the immersed side, as things do here. Laid in the palm it proves to have scarcely any stick to it. There are three lengths of rush, a flake of black peat, a great many grains of stone admirably fitted together, and, sticking out rather far on one side, the wing case of a beetle. Put it back where it was and presently six legs come out at the thick end. Then I set off very slowly in the direction in which I was pointed when picked up, which is unfortunately the wrong one.

I would be a caddis larva. The house would be mine in a rather more intimate sense than the brick shed is the pump keeper’s. I could back up in it until no part of me showed, but I couldn’t turn round. To change my mind about the direction of travel I would have to take the whole establishment with me in a wide sweep. This is quite a business in a wheel rut full of old grass. The wing case in particular, a handsome piece of work with fine parallel grooves down it, catches on everything.

There is plenty to do below the pump house. An alder leaf has come up from somewhere lower on the mountain, perhaps in the tread of a boot. It lies under the gravel with just a frill of its edge showing. I have been eating that frill for two days. When the water falls I go down with it into a wet crevice and fasten the mouth of the case to a stone with silk. The enormous noise of the pump reaches me there as a little stirring of the soft skin of my back. When it ceases a grain of sand occasionally goes on slipping for a while, and comes to rest against me.

I should have no notion of the country as a height. Indeed I should have very little notion of height at all until the afternoon the dipper finds me. Its bill closes on the projecting bit of wing case. Up come the rushes and stones and the silk lining and myself, all beautifully held together, better held together than I have any wish for just then. For the length of one swing I am clear of the water. The bird strikes the case against the rail. It has done this before; it means to have the soft part out. But the beetle wing comes away, and I go back into the rut with a small square of daylight in my bedroom wall.

For some time after that I stay quite still. Water comes in by the new opening. There is a thread of it against a place where I am accustomed to the touch of silk, and this, once the bird has gone, is a more considerable matter than the bird. I feel about on the bed of the rut. A good deal of what is within reach is either too round or much too large. At last I get hold of a flat red chip, draw it up to the opening, turn it, let it go, get hold of it again.

By evening there is a piece of the pump house in my wall. Anyone leaning from the step might see it going along below, a speck of brick moving by fits between the rails. I am on my way back to the alder leaf. I get through the grass rather better now.
