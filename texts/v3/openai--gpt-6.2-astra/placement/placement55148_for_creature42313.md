I think I would be up at the mouth of one of the shafts, eating the other half of a pear. The noon relief is allowed twelve minutes. It takes four to get the hatch open in winter, but nobody has ever succeeded in having those four minutes reckoned separately.

There is surprisingly little to see of the basin from up there. The silver lining gives back the sky. Lean over the rail in a red cap and you see, very far down, what looks like a red seed at the bottom of a glass of water. You cannot see the workshops or the people who have gone out to sit in the sun. You have to take them on trust, which is one reason for the rail. A previous keeper, wishing to know whether he could see his wife, lost a very good pair of spectacles into the winding works.

My actual job would be keeping the mouth free. A dead grass stem caught across it in October can throw a line across a machinist’s rule until March. Live things are left alone. There is a distinction, in the regulations, between an obstruction and a visitor. A spider is a visitor for as long as she is in her web. Once she has gone I may take it down. This involves me in a certain amount of standing about with my face six inches from a spider, to establish which of us is on his own time.

I would know what made the shadow that Dav saw. Or rather I would have something to tell him about it, which is not quite the same thing. There is no wire over that shaft. There used to be, and the two porcelain cups it was strained between are still there, full of rain. What there is now is a long spray of mountain ash which dips over the mouth when a bird lands on the end of it. The bird goes down through the light facing forward and comes back up facing forward, without doing anything one could reasonably call flying backward.

I should like to tell him this in person. For a long time I would not know there was anybody to tell.

Then one day an inquiry would come up in the tool cage. Not from Dav. It would be from the furniture man, wanting to know whether the proposed school might count on direct light at the teacher’s end between eleven and twelve in February. There would be a fair copy of the plan with the inquiry. While puzzling out which end was the teacher’s I would discover what appeared to be a drain, very carefully ruled in blue, with a little heap of pencils at its outfall. I would turn the paper over to see who had drawn it.

The teacher could have her light in February, but some mornings she would have it full in her face. I would mark the patch on the plan with the wash we use for sunlight, a yellow so weak that while it is wet you think you have left it out. In the margin I would draw the two cups and the branch between them. No bird. A bird on a plan looks too much like a promise. Underneath I would put, *Occasional moving shade.*

That might be the whole extent of our acquaintance for some years.

But when the school opened I would go down. I would have an excuse: somebody ought to see whether the yellow patch fell where it was supposed to. In my absence the relief would sit with his boots on the rail and eat the pear right through, pips and all. Downstairs the children would already have found that if you dropped a pencil crosswise in the trough it went quite smartly to the teacher, whereas if you laid it in point first it stayed there and the next pencil had to come and fetch it. A large man from the works, standing at the back with a rolled drawing in his hand, would be watching this trial with some anxiety.

I think that is where I would find myself in the end: standing beside him, both of us a little in the way, while the schoolmistress tried to get on with the register. Halfway down the list the room would darken. Across the board would go the shadow of a spray of leaves, every separate notch in them visible, and at the tip of the spray a little wedge of tail. The man beside me would put out one hand as if to stop anybody rubbing the board clean.

“Is that a wire it sits on?” he would whisper.

By then the ash would have grown considerably. I would have to look.
