I would come to the shelter stuck to a piece of the peg. It would be a good while before there was any reason to distinguish me from the wood.

There are several young ones in the black place at the heart of the peg. The hole is too narrow to admit grit of any size, but water works through it. Their lips lie one above the other in the opening, the smallest at the bottom, where the water comes in by a split below the level of the road. In such a place a young one grows long before it grows broad. Mine would be a poor shape for going about on a ridge. I could reach a considerable distance ahead of myself but had very little to put down on either side.

When my bit of wood came away, I would draw into the split in it. Of the journey up the road I would know chiefly the difference between the blows on the end of the chip, which shut the split, and the blows on its flat side, which opened it. Toward the end there would be a long easy turn without a blow. That would be the lower animal turning the chip to offer it. The old one generally takes a splinter by the sound end, leaving the soft end free to be pressed into whatever unevenness there is in the joint.

This time the unevenness would press back.

I think the old one would try me the other way round. It has a use for a piece of wood and no particular use for another animal. But when its lip closed on the soft end, mine would close on its lip. It would put down the wood and draw away and find it had taken me along. There would follow a business quite familiar on the ridges, though it cannot have happened under that shelf for years: a little one trying to get into a pleat already occupied by the flesh of a big one. The big one has only to fill its bag a little fuller. Out I would come, right in the joint this time, with the wood behind me and the open mortar ahead.

It is a fair place for an ill-shaped animal. The mortar has fallen out in cups where there used to be small round stones. They are too shallow for a bag, but by putting my middle down in one cup and a thumb in each of two others I could let a good length of lip hang free. Sand goes round under the shelf at the far wall. There is no fall of it there, only an occasional grain coming uphill, rolling a little and falling back; I could clean the same grain three or four times before it got away. I would be hungry much of the time. Then from behind would come a pressure I knew from the peg, the closing of a split. The old one would be putting in another chip. Against the end of the last chip a little dirty water would well up, thick with what had been growing in the wood since it was laid there. Enough to eat, with nothing hard to get rid of afterward. I would put my whole lip over the end.

So for a season my life would consist in being moved very slowly toward the far wall, well fed at one end and obliged to find a new hold at the other. I wouldn't know how little mortar remained. The first I would know of the wall would be the cold of the narrow upright joint behind it. Water comes down there even when the lake is slack. It goes under the shelf and out by the door, carrying a little lime, which accounts for the pale streak down that side of the doorway. I would put my bag up the joint and fill it. After the low cups it would be a wonderful thing to be held on all sides at once.

There is a difference this would make to the shelter. Until then, at the first knock the old one has always had to draw in the whole of its lip. What it holds it must draw in too, or drop. Now it could give me the far corner to hold. The first time would be by accident. It would be cleaning the last grains off the shelf, reaching farther than usual for one that had lodged at the wall, and I would take the corner for something being passed. At the knock it would pull. I would fill my bag harder. Between us a strip of skin no wider than the splinter I came on would stretch very tight across the front of the pale line.

For those few minutes I would feel the running of the lake as none of the others in the shelter feel it. The old one has the shelf against its back; the one below has the road. I would have the trembling of the old one's skin in my mouth. Every pebble striking the slab roof would give a separate tug. In the middle of the rush there would be an interval of perfect ease, though the roof was still humming. Down on the road the lower animal would have reached up and taken hold halfway along.

I would let go then. There is no need for three to hold what two can hold, and there is something coming down the upright joint with its feet folded under it. It would be the first white seed I had ever touched. In the peg there was no room for anything so broad to turn. I would clean one foot, turn it with some difficulty, clean the next. Below me, within easy reach, the two lips would still be together. There would be a choice of directions. I think I would keep it a little while.
