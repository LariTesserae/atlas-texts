I would be the one who walks the things that will not go by street. A tooth generally makes the journey in a little twist of leaf with the name on the inside, though after my first year the woman at the western exchange seldom troubled to put in a name. She would say, “She'll have moved her table by now. Try the warm side of the house.” She was nearly always right about the table. She had made most of the journeys herself before her ankles got stiff.

There is no great distinction attached to the work. Anyone going east will take something. What you get with me is that I will come as far as the chair you happen to be sitting in, and will wait while you put the tooth in your mouth and try it on something. Once I walked a tooth back the whole length of town because, although it fitted perfectly, the man was quite sure it had never been his. We were halfway through the afternoon of the following day before the woman remembered he had a brother.

I sleep in both exchanges. I have a little difficulty on waking in knowing which one I am in. The benches were grown to the same pattern, with a hollow underneath just long enough for a person who doesn't mind bringing one knee up; in both there is a smooth place against which I have been putting that knee for years. Usually I put one ear out against the end of the bench. At the eastern end there is a seed hutch on the other side of the wall. Even when nothing is happening to the seeds they make a close, busy sound, like a person considering what to say with a mouthful of food.

For a long time I supposed the two smooth places to be quite independent. Then I tore my knee going over the sill of an oven where they had forgotten to cut back the new growth. The woman gave me a square of the stuff they put over a lens to carry it, very soft on one side, and I bound it on over the dressing. That night it stuck a little to the underside of the western bench. There was a tuft left behind, too far in to get at without turning over.

At the eastern end the next night I felt something tickle the bare square above the dressing. I lay for some time trying to get it to do it again. It was a small thing to have walked so far to find.

After that I looked more carefully at the benches. Nothing else about them matched. On the western one there was a burn the size of a thumbnail where somebody had rested a meal scoop. Under the eastern one several children, by the look of it in succession, had tried to draw a bird as it must appear from below in flight. They had all had trouble with the feet. Only in the place where I lay did the grain run alike, and then not for the whole length of me. My head goes in at whichever end is out of the wind, but on account of an old shortening in the leg it is always the same knee I bring up.

I told the woman. She came down rather laboriously to have a look. I thought she would be interested in the possibility of sending things after the street had finished for the evening. There are few things small enough to go on the smooth place, but a tooth is one of them. Instead she put in her arm, swept the place clean with the side of her hand and said, “You can have the rest of that stuff if it's still rubbing.”

It was she who needed the benches in the end. Her ankles got very stiff indeed. She could still get to work, a distance of two roofs, but some mornings by the time she got there her ears were lying open on her shoulders, the way a person's will when she's had to attend very closely to where she sets each foot. She disliked sitting down in that condition in front of people who had come to discuss their teeth. Apparently there are things one doesn't care to have heard quite so well.

I took to sitting at the eastern end with my bad knee against the place. At an agreed time she would reach under and lay two fingers there. There was no sensation of fingers. What I felt was the relief of moving my knee a little way after having kept it still too long, even though I hadn't moved it yet. I would then go out and tell the people waiting at the eastern end that she was in, and we could begin. On mornings when she couldn't make the walk I would do what I could on my own. We managed very well. I got the brothers straight.

I still walk most days. Whatever it is that passes between the benches, we have never been able to persuade a tooth to use it. Once, at her suggestion, I lay down at the eastern end while she had a young man at the western end put his own knee to the wood. Nothing happened at all, except that the young man, on getting up, found a missing lens and was warmly thanked. It seems to require a certain amount of having been uncomfortable in both places.

What I like best is the walk back with an empty pocket. I can take off my shoes a good way before the smooth ground begins. Near the last roof on the western side there is a place where, at the right hour, I can feel the woman getting up to go home. First there is the hard end of her stick and then a pause, surprisingly long, before she trusts the first ankle. I used to hurry so that I could be there to offer an arm. It made her put her foot down sooner than she otherwise would. Now I stop where I am and get the shoes untied from one another and shake the powder out. By the time I reach the bench she is usually well on her way. There is a little warmth left in the seat, and all that space underneath it for my things.
