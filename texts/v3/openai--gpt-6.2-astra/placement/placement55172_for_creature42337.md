I would be one of the little dun birds. In particular I would be the one that has learned to go under the basket. There is room, where the bottom has pulled away from the side, for a bird to put its head in and take a grain from between the two thicknesses of the weave. It is a good place to eat while there are six or seven heavier birds inside. When they all get out at once, the basket settles on its other corner and you have to get out too.

I would know the woman chiefly by her hands. One of them has a split in the nail of the thumb that catches the fine wool on a girth. She has a way of freeing it with her teeth. The hand has to come up past her face to do this and it used to put me off the trough in a great hurry. I can sit through it now. The boys have hands rather like hers, particularly the younger boy, but a boy can take a perfectly ordinary hand that has been lying palm down for some time on a stone and be halfway across the trough with it before a bird has properly left the ground.

For most of the day I would have nothing to do with any of them. There is a short fly with rusty wings that sits very close on the cushion plants, too close to be picked off among the prickles. Four sheep walking abreast through a patch of it make a good morning for a bird. You work up the outside of the last sheep, taking what comes out beside its foot, and when you come level with its eye you let the whole of the sheep go by and begin again. It is quite possible in this manner to get a long way from home without ever having set out for anywhere. There are days when the woman turns the baskets and none of the birds that come are me.

Once I would go down the stair. A thaw has been followed by a freeze and there is a skin on the trough that gives under my feet but will not break. Below, in the sun, water is running out of a spout into a shallow stone dish. I can see the rings in it from the uppermost step. Going down is no trouble. Coming up ought to be no trouble either, except that when I have drunk I fly toward the greatest piece of light, which is straight across the street. I come up on a landing among skeins of yellow wool. A woman there puts her plaiting between her knees and waits. I try again, and this time fetch up a whole story lower, on the projecting end of a lintel. From there the sky looks hardly wide enough to admit me with my wings out.

It is the younger boy who gets me up. He is coming home with the empty water pot, knocking his knuckle against its belly to hear the difference now there is nothing in it. I see him take a turning where there appears to be no turning at all. I go ahead of him a little way; he comes on; I go a little farther. It takes the two of us until the trough to discover that he has been driving me. He puts the pot down and goes back a few steps to see whether it will work again.

After that I would drink below quite often. There is a minute or two late in the afternoon when the end of the spout casts a shadow clear across the dish. Midges collect in the shadow, and you can stand on the lip and take them as they come round. The woman at the stair head, seeing me come up from this, might reasonably suppose that I had a nest down there. In fact my nest would be well out on the plain, under the lip of a stone that rocks if a sheep puts its foot on the high end. I have built at the low end. This is my second year there.

In the evening I would come back to the shelf. The stone the woman sets against the basket is a piece of old gutter, with an almost imperceptible hollow along one face. She puts the hollow inwards because the other face sits better against her palm. Even with the stone in place there is a way through. You turn one shoulder down, draw in the near foot, and come out on the shelf with a little white on the ends of your feathers. The other birds, hearing the scratch, stop feeding and look over the edge. All they see is an ordinary bird going off along the gutter with a grain. There is a moment before they get their heads down again in which you can go back the same way.
