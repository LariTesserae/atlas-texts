I think I would be one of the young blackbirds, though not the one on the slipper. I would be three stalks back, in a lane from which I could see the slipper and very little else. I would see the other bird put its foot on the dark stuff without coming to grief. Before I had quite made up my mind to try it, a hand would come down out of the bright part of the currant bush and the foot and the bird and the berry would all be gone.

At that age I would still occasionally open my mouth at an older bird that had something good in its bill. It is a habit that lasts a little longer than the corresponding habit in one's parents. I might do it to the bird with the currant. Then I would have to look for one of my own.

My way to number eight would be quite a long way. There is a stretch of white stone outside the door which a blackbird can cross in two seconds, and which I would never cross if I could help it. I would go down into the dry saw cut, run along it with my tail sticking out, and come up among the chips on the far side of the chair. From there I could get as far as the woman's right heel without once having to stand out on the white. The currants that fall on that side are mostly the bad ones, which is fortunate. A sound currant gets away from a young bird rather easily. A split one stays where you put your foot on it.

I would come to know the chair better than the woman. It has one leg with a cap missing; when she brings it out, there is always a little business of finding a place for that leg. For a while all four legs are dangerous. Then they settle, and for the better part of an hour they are as good as stalks. Once a berry would run into the hollow at the bottom of the bare leg. I would spend much of that hour getting it out again, going away and coming back, although there were half a dozen perfectly accessible berries within reach. The next time the chair came out I would look there first.

On a shutdown morning it would be possible to hear a currant fall. I doubt that this would be of much use to me. There would be the little tap of it, and then the slide of the woman's skirt against the chair as she leaned over to see where it had gone, a considerable sound. I would stay in the cut. By afternoon she might have taken the chair inside. There would be four places in the dust where its legs had stood and, at one of them, the currant, with a little grey collar round its split.

That is where I would find myself, I think: out on the stone after all, in the square the chair had covered. From her kitchen the woman would be able to see the whole of me for once, the brown flecks still showing on my breast. I would be looking at the ground, turning my head to bring one eye very close to the small round place where experience had taught me there was a hole.
