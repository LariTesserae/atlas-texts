I would live on the south stair, in the third tread down from the kitchen. It is a wooden tread fitted between two courses of the red stone. On the kitchen side it has been scrubbed dead white. Underneath, a curl left by the joiner’s plane is still attached at one end. I could sleep in that with a little of me over at either side.

I think I would have wings. There are things worth crossing the square for, but it would be a long walk for something my size, particularly with a grain of salt. A grain of salt is about the most I could carry. Up on the third tread there is plenty, trodden into the end grain and sometimes coming free in a lump with a little butter on it. Down below there is water. I would spend a good deal of my life getting the proportions wrong.

Early in the morning the kitchen door opens the width of a hand. The woman who lives there has a habit of sweeping toward a door and then being surprised by the sill. She tips most of the sweepings out into a pan, but what falls my way is enough. In the first draught of warm floury air I come out of the curl and open my wings to their full, rather disappointing extent. They are grey fore and aft, with a strip of clear wing between, so that if I settle crosswise on a crack a short piece of the crack shows through me. I have a mouth like the loose end of a watch spring. It takes me some time to eat butter with it. By the time I am done, there will be the first *tok* below.

From the stair the sound is quite different. There is the great note everyone hears, but before it there is a little upward shove of air. With my feet still on the wood I can feel it fill the slack of my wings. Let go then and I can get as far as the drinking pipe almost without working. Let go too late and I am in the kitchen, where it is necessary to spend the morning on the underside of a chair. I have done that often enough to have a particular chair.

For a long time I would know the old animal principally as a difficulty with landing. A minute ago there was a good piece of floor there; now the floor has a temperature, and a join in it closes over the very tip of a foot. I pull free and stand a little off. There is no pursuit. Once, at the pipe, I put my mouth to a bead on her back and the bead travelled away under my weight. I followed it over three or four lengths of her before it went down a fold. After that I could tell her at a little distance, even stretched quite flat. Water on the municipal stone has an edge to it, like the edge of a fried egg. On her it stands up round.

It would be one of the young ones that came up the stair. I would find it there after a night in the kitchen, my own white curl occupied at the open end by a blunt chin. The rest of the animal would hang down the riser with a turn in it, like a length of soft solder somebody had started to put away. It would have been trying the wood with its mouth. There would be two damp strokes and a pause and two damp strokes, all the way up from the joint with the stone. Nothing to eat in any of them.

There is room at the fast end of the shaving if I go in with my wings shut up hard. We could spend the heat of the day like that. On the other side of the tread a chair goes back and forth; a knife dropped flat makes us both draw up small. I am accustomed to the cool of the wood returning by degrees where the sole of a shoe has been. This little patch stays warm. Towards evening it begins to slip. A foot takes hold of the shaving, the shaving uncurls half a turn, and for the first time in all my summers I get a look straight down from my bed into the square.

There goes my lodger with a flake of white wood on its chin. It makes much better going down than up. Below, in the little rain, its mother lies aslant the lowest step, wide enough already, but there is room. By the time the bread flat passes they are off the stair.

I would keep the bed, though it would never spring quite back. In cool weather I would have to go farther in. But on a close afternoon I could lie at the open end with my mouth rolled up and my feet all comfortably caught in the grain, and see the rain come down. From most places in the square you have to wait for the first drop to strike. From there, looking slantwise into what light the steel cupboard gives, I should see a whole street of drops hanging by their necks, and then the little stretch there is in water, before it lets go.
