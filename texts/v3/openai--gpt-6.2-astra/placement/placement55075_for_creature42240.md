I would have a bench at the station, on the landing where people waiting for a car can still feel whether it is worth going down the last flight. I would put pockets into clothes. There is enough business for one person. A station porter who has had to sit on his ticket punch all the way round the curve will pay quite well to have it come to rest somewhere else.

There would be a strip of chalk on my bench and a drawer of things for trying pockets: a child's hand lamp, a luminous rule, a twist of the paper salt comes in. For a good fit I would want the thing itself. The dummy punch in my drawer has never worn a hole in anything. It lacks the little upturned corner that the porter has been meaning to file off for eleven years.

The excursion coat would come to me folded small, under the boy's arm, with his grandmother a little way behind him. I would know the coat by the pocket mouths. The left one is stretched wide enough to take a fist, the right hardly wide enough for three fingers. This has been the case through several changes of child. A child who ordinarily keeps things on the right soon discovers which side of the coat is prepared to do business.

He would want to know whether anything could be done about the cuffs. I would turn them back and show him the inside, where a succession of remarkably economical people have already spent the allowance.

“And if you took the pockets off?”

“Then we'd have sleeves,” I would say. “We could make very handsome sleeves.”

He would put a hand over each pocket.

His grandmother, meanwhile, would have taken the seat by the wall. There is usually a little company out there for anyone who wants it. On the far side of the upright is the station tea room; you can feel a spoon go round in a heavy cup. She would be sitting with both hands tucked under her arms. Once, coming in off the strip, she tore one of the tender places on a broken wire. It has healed, but ever since, the first few minutes indoors have been uncomfortable, when the hand gets warm all through.

It would be the boy who thought of putting pockets for hands into the skirts of *her* coat. She would object that she had two quite serviceable pockets already. She would empty one to demonstrate. A bent tag, a thumb's length of ceramic tube, something wrapped in paper because it had points at both ends. We would wait for her to come to her own conclusion.

This is work I like. With the coat on and its owner standing easily, I would ask her to let her arms hang. The boy could mark where the thumbs came to. Then I would make two slant openings a little above the marks and tack in soft bags, deep on one edge and shallow on the other, so the hands could rest without having to close. I would get her to try the stairs and sit down and get up again before I sewed anything fast. People have their own ideas about where their hands belong. Some like them so far forward that in a head wind the skirts of the coat blow out behind like a pair of doors.

While she was on the stairs I would let the boy turn on my bench light and look inside his left pocket. There are usually initials down there, done in pale thread before the bag was stitched into the coat. In this one there would be a tram with square wheels, a house such as nobody has ever lived in on the Table, and, close to the seam, a very creditable likeness of a shell animal, its feet made of a row of stitches with the connecting thread picked out. Nobody has ever asked me to put these things in. People do a little work of their own before they give the coat up.

I think he would want the needle. I would have to show him how to hold the bag clear of the skirt. We would have time; she would be giving those pockets a fair trial, perhaps going all the way down to the platform to sit on a cold freight box and see how they did there.

By the time she came back he might have made half a dozen small red stitches in the red cloth. Under my bulb there would be scarcely anything to see. Run a finger over them and they would be quite distinct: one, a space, another, the spaces getting longer. I would ask if he wanted the thread cut.

He would lay his hand along the rest of the pocket, judging the room he had left.

“Not yet,” he would say.
