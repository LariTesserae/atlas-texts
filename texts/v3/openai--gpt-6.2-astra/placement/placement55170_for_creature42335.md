I would have taken the pinholes for the work of a listener. I would have put myself there, out in the blue with my stomach showing, trying to get an old sound clear. But there is something to be said for the other end of the business. A tooth does not cease to feel because it is making most of the noise. I think I would be the grinder on the pebble, the one with a little of the brown still showing. I would know the shadow animals chiefly by weight.

The first one to turn over in the morning takes the weight off one corner of my lower lip. I can get that corner forward a little. Then the others begin to turn over and for a while I can scarcely take two bites alike. This is a considerable nuisance. My teeth are new; there is a sweet place in the pebble, scarcely wider than a thread, where the two grains part cleanly, and I keep losing it. Then for several mornings there is one that does not turn. It has got too long inside. There is a hard ridge against the lip where there used to be a warm round belly. I get used to that too. When the shadow comes and it steps out, my jaw goes sideways into the empty coat. For the rest of my life there is a small slantwise stroke in my chewing, of no use at all on a pillar, very useful once for getting past a knee.

I would have no very clear notion of becoming big. There would be a day when I could no longer get the pebble between my teeth without taking in a quantity of grit from either side. There would be a rather bad time after that. I would go by night, taking my collar with me, the coats on the underside wearing away as I went. A tree would be the end of my travelling. Its first shoot against my belly would feel very much like the beginning of another knee. By then there would be yellow stone coming up through the slate so close to my mouth that to take hold of it I would only have to let my head hang.

From down there the celebrated brightness of the backs would not amount to much. I would know afternoon by a patch of warmth creeping along the roof of my mouth. Birds would be six sharp pressures followed by six more, and occasionally by the rather agreeable sensation of all twelve letting go at once. Of the tree I would know an astonishing amount. I would feel a dry place in it a hundred feet away. Just before the morning patter, the shoot in my belly would draw tight enough to lift a fold of skin; then the first drops would go into the leaves and it would slacken. I think I would come to like that minute best, when I had had enough of the night's cold and the tree had had enough of me, and for a little we could both let go without coming apart.

It would take a long life to notice anything as light as a hair set down on stone. Probably I never would. What I might notice is the loss of a very little of my own sound. There is a high shiver that runs out through a blade of slate and comes back into the left tooth. I have been getting it back for eighty years. One evening it comes back dull. Clear, then dull again. Something is touching the blade far out toward its tip, at the very limit of what can be felt with a mouthful of stone.

I wouldn't know which of them it was. By then there might be no more left of its coat than a brown scale caught in a crease; there might be nothing left at all. But I would try the slantwise stroke, the one that leaves room for the knee. Out in the blue the hair would lift. I would get the whole shiver back for an instant before it came down again. And that is where I would be, head down in company, with the sun still quite hot on the hindmost plates, taking a long time over a piece of stone that had given way several bites ago.
