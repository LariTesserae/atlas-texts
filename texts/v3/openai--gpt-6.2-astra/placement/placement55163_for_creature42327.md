When the daughter calls for a wedge, I am near enough to hand her one. It is what I am using to hold my door open. For the rest of the afternoon people coming to see me have to lift the door with both hands and duck under it. A small girl finds this so much better than coming in the usual way that she goes out backwards and does it twice more.

I go along beside the bedroom. There is a place prepared for it at the frog end of the street, a scraped rectangle with a little standing water in it. The carters ease the room down. Water comes out from under it on all four sides, and the old woman, who has been looking straight ahead throughout the journey, leans over at last to see where she is. There are no frogs to be heard. It is the wrong part of the day for them. There is the sound of a man at some distance beating a pair of trousers against his leg to get the nap off.

I get down on my stomach and look at the join. One of the daughters stays inside with her mother; the other gets down beside me, still wearing the good combs she put in her hair for the move. We breathe on the gap. Where the two cut faces are taking to one another, the breath stands in beads. At the near corner it goes straight in.

This is the work I would have here. I look after the underside of things that have been moved. I am a fairly broad person for the purpose, but my head is narrow, and I can keep one eye shut in water without shutting the other, a faculty of less use than you would think. My best tool is a length of yellow tape, hemmed for me by the pocket maker, with a small rent near one end which has never been a hindrance. I feed it in at the corner. The daughter goes round the room after it. She calls when it comes out.

“By the pillow,” she says.

“Well, pull a bit.”

The tape travels through my fingers. There is a pull in the other direction also. I let go, and a length of the tape disappears under the room, then a little more, then the end with the rent. The daughter holds up both hands to show me she has nothing.

We find it in bed with the old woman. A loose curl in the floor has taken it up through the mattress like a stitch. It lies by her hip in a yellow loop. She has put one finger through it.

There are several ways to make a room lie flat. You can weight it overnight, though you must take care what you weight it with; a room on which three full water jars have stood will sometimes go on having three cold round places for the rest of its life. You can cut away the curl. Or you can get everybody out and begin again. I sit on the edge of the bed to consider which to do. Under my weight the loop shortens, and from the floor comes one remarkably clear frog note, a low *uk*, as if somebody beneath us had been handed a parcel heavier than expected.

The old woman tightens her finger in the tape.

“Do that again.”

I stand up and sit down. Nothing. One daughter tries, then the other. We are all three sitting in a row on the edge of the bed when the note comes again, this time from well out in the street. The curl under the floor runs into one of the long hollows from which the frogs call at evening. We have been hearing, at rather close quarters, a single frog trying his voice. Presently another answers him. You can feel it particularly well through the carved comb if you happen to be the daughter lying down with your good hair pressed against the pillow.

We leave the corner as it is. I cut my tape, since it is not coming out again without bringing some mattress with it. The two ends that hang down beside the bed are just the thing for drawing a pair of slippers up off a wet floor. The old woman has me try this with hers before she lets us go.

On the way home I get my wedge back. It is warm from carrying the weight of the bedroom and has acquired, on the flat of it, a number of very small square impressions. I spend some time trying to account for these. Then at my door I see the little girl sitting barefoot on the step. She has used the wedge, in its absence, to stamp a pattern on the damp sole of her foot. These minor disagreements about when a thing has happened are commonest at the two ends of a frequently travelled street. I give her the wedge to finish the other foot.

By evening there is a boy waiting to see me. He has a wooden spoon and a nearly whole house of grit. He would like to know if the house can be put on something else. There is a place in the bottom of it, he shows me, where a person holding it up to the light can see his own skin shining pink through the sand.

I fetch the cut-off bit of yellow tape from my pocket. We lay the little house on it and slide the tape to and fro until a loose thread catches in the hole. It holds quite nicely. He does not want to take it away yet. He would like first to see what it is like to walk towards it from a distance.

So I stand in my open doorway holding the strip of tape across my two palms. He walks to the end of the street. I can see the white soles of his feet as he goes, and beyond him the last of the sun on the freight ribs, which have not quite subsided. He turns. It is a long way back for the sake of something which, until he is almost upon me, he cannot possibly see. I keep my hands still for him.
