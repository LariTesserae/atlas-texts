I would be in the rough flue, at the inch where the two cuts fail to meet. The ledge is just deep enough to keep a little of whatever goes past it. For a long time what it kept was yellow meal and the pointed ends of leaves. Then a piece of stalk came up on the draft. It lay rolling a quarter turn each way on the ledge until its broken end caught in a chisel mark. There were eggs in it. I would be one of those.

Coming out would take me most of a day. I would brush the stopper until the meal had packed itself behind me more firmly than it had ever been packed in front. At last I could go neither in nor out. The thing that freed me would be the fall of the vane. The stalk would jump a little in its groove, and I would find myself standing on the outside of it with my brushes still going.

There is food even there. The ends of leaves have a little oil left in them, thickened to a gum that comes off on the brushes in strings. I would learn to put a foot on a string and pull my face away. At a certain distance the string breaks and the part on the brushes draws up into a bead. It would take longer to get my horn on this diet. Well after I had reached my length I would still have a clear place at the small of my back.

I think I would have a use for the chisel marks. Each stroke left a little shelf at its lower end, not big enough for a foot but big enough for one toe. I could go quite far down the bore on these. Where the men had changed places at the work the strokes changed too; for a few inches I would have to travel with my left side leading instead of my right. At the mouth they peter out altogether. From the last mark I could reach into the room with my forefeet and find nothing.

What would bring me down at last would be a whole red blade, torn loose and taken tip first into the shaft. The tip would go by me. The teeth would go by. I would catch it by the pale base, where there ought to be food, and my one toe in the cut would come out as easily as a stitch. For a while the two of us would go up. Then, with the change of air, we would come down much faster. I would land deep in the bed, still holding on to my piece of leaf among all the others that had not come loose.

Here I would do nearly everything wrong. At the touch of another foot I would draw my own in, as if a bit of grit had struck it. Given a fresh blade I would pin it down and pull away from it, and all that good oil would come off on my belly. But I would know what to do when the stone fell. While the others were lying close in their folds I would be out looking for what had shifted.

That is where I would be easiest to find: not up the rough bore, once I had got down from it, but at the bottom of the garden just after the report. Lift a spent leaf there and underneath would be a small beast turning over a chip of stalk. It would have a thumb of clear horn on its back, through which you could see its last meal pass. If the stalk were empty it would put it down. If there were meal in the end it would work the piece about until the meal faced out into the open. Then it would put one foot on the stalk and stay. There is a very slight stirring, quite different from the trembling of the masonry, when something inside has begun to brush.
