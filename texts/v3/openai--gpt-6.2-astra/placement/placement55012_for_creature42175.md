I would be the person who takes the pegs out. Putting them in is work for a pair, one to hold the root in its new position and one to set the fork astride it. Taking them out is generally work for somebody with a whole afternoon to spare. I have afternoons. I have a gap between my front teeth from a peg that came out much more easily than the previous one.

There are some that will never come out. The willow has grown up between the tines and joined itself over the top, leaving a knob of antler showing on either side. These I feel with my cheek. If there is an edge on one that would open the skin of a passing shoulder, I rub it down with a piece of rough stone. Otherwise it is pleasant to find them there. My father set a fork the wrong way up under the sill in the upper court before he had anything to do with my mother. He showed it to me once, and the underside of that sill is the one part of town where I know my way better upside down.

I would live in the basket court, in the room the hinge maker has for this year. It is a room meant for two. We have strung our nets at different heights so that whichever of us is awake can get to the door without putting a foot into the other's stomach. The first night we had them the other way about. I learned something then about the vigour of a woman who does not otherwise stir in her sleep.

I like having another person's things in the room. I had thought the sound of the little screw lid would vex me. In fact it is a comfort to hear it take the thread on the second try, as it nearly always does. There is one thing I have wanted to ask her. If she has grit behind her ear and uses the fine end of the comb to get it out, how does she keep the grit from going in on the other side of the same pleat? I have never managed that with a comb. I have been waiting for her to do it while I happen to be there.

On the afternoon she goes home by the rails with the boy, I am coming the other way with four good forks, a broken one and a nice length of root bark which came away in my hand and may do to mend the heel of a net. I see them in time to go over them. The boy has her hand held rather high, as if it were something he had been told not to get wet. At the turning by the basket yards he transfers it carefully to his other hand before he feels for the ribs. I can see his lips moving. He is counting them. There is no need whatever to know how many ribs there are, and now that I see him doing it I find I want to know the number myself.

I wait until they are round the turning and put the forks down. There are nine. Or there are eight, depending on what you think about the last one. It is a little raised place where the binding passes under itself, which I should never have counted by daylight. With my eyes shut it makes as good a rib as any of the others.

I pick up the forks. Later, sitting on our sill with our legs out, the hinge maker will say, against my ear, that all the time she lived in the western court she had the distance wrong. She had thought it was much farther from the public beds to the west turning than from the turning to her door. It is almost exactly the same. I will ask if she counted the ribs. She will want to know what ribs. It will turn out that the boy had been telling her something the entire way, with his thumb against the inside of her hand, and she had been attending to that.

For now I go a little way along their street. At the next branch there is an old fork I have tried three or four times in as many summers. I hook a finger under the lower tine and give it the usual unhopeful pull. This time there is a soft give deep in the wood. I shift my face out of the way.
