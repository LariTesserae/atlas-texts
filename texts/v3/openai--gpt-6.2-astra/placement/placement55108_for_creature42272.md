At first I would have sat on the square of turf. It looks to have been left for precisely that purpose. But a hand can feel a foot scratching through it; a stoat asleep under it must be able to feel a man getting comfortable. I would have got up again and sat on one of the flakes, and had the grit on my trousers for the rest of the day.

From the flake the cup is almost directly below one's knees. There is a little movement there even after the water has stopped: a fleck of wood going round the lip, in water so still one can see the shadow of the fleck keeping pace with it on the bottom. I think I would turn out to be the piece of wood the fleck has come from. I would still feel quite long and substantial at that end. It is only from the flake that one can see the light coming through me.

I grew somewhere below the cloud. For a while after I was split there was a great difference between my two sides. On one there were the little tight scales of bark and the clean places where the thumbs had pushed them off; the other was white, and full of splinters lying all one way, so that the first person to set me up here ran a finger down me quite safely and got a splinter drawing it back. The roof water ran pinkish for a day or two. Now I am very much the colour of the stones that hold me up. A bird coming to drink puts one foot on wood and one on rock without making any allowance for the difference.

There is a particular drop I would know. It comes well on in the morning, when all the others have done the work of wetting me from end to end. I take a blow at the thick end and almost at once am lighter at the thin one. The drop that struck me is still a good way up the grain; it is yesterday's bit of beetle wing that goes over the lip. By noon a whole finch scarcely weighs on me more than that first blow. It stands well back and runs its bill along the wet. I have a little sawn-off spur there that is just the right size for its hind toe.

The stoat is another matter. Her two feet on the cairn tip the upper stone by the breadth of a hair. For a moment the water goes down my other edge, which is still rough. It makes a dark crooked line, ending nowhere near her mouth. She takes the bead that was there already and goes off with her cheek wet, and for half an hour after she has finished washing there is this further, rather exact account of her weight drying in the sun.

I would like the hours when there was no water, too. Once, toward evening, one of the young ones would come as far as the spout. From below, my thinned end must look a little like the feather they keep having trouble with. It would reach up and try me with its milk teeth. Not much of a bite; there is still spring enough in me to give under its mouth, and it would lose interest when I sprang back. But in the morning, after the last drop had hung and gone, there would be two much smaller drops left, side by side, in the dents.
