I would probably be on the fallen piece of roof. It would be a poor place to get any sleep. Even before the light came over the wall I would feel a foot come down on my back, the toes taking hold separately, with a little extra pressure from the hind toe as the bird went on. The white-toed bird would weigh exactly as much as the other one. For a long time I should have no notion that one of its toes was white.

I think I would be something growing in the groove, too thin to be distinguished from the polish. In the parts on which the feet actually fell there would be very little of me; the better part would live along the edges, in the minute shelter afforded by a grain standing up out of the stone. The damp left by a bird coming from the pond would be enough to last me until the following morning. There would be good years marked in the shape of bird feet, three long reaches of prosperity with lean country in between.

My nearest approach to hearing the river would come in winter. Water in a flaw beneath the slab would freeze and lift one end of it off the shingle. For a few days it would rest on two points only, and the whole piece would give a little at the knock of the pebble overhead. I would not know that this was happening a long way up. It would be something the stone did when it was cold. When the ice went out, the slab would let itself down with a movement so slight that the birds would step on it without checking. There would be grit in all my folds for weeks.

But I would also be down by the pond. Whatever I was, a little of me would go away on a foot from time to time. At the pond's rim, where there was water enough, I would grow quite differently, in loose brown scallops, with bubbles caught underneath. A bird would put its foot through me without feeling a thing. And there would be a little of me high up in the mud beds, taken there in the best possible condition, packed damp under a claw. Up there I might put out a stiff cup no bigger than the end of the bird's nostril. Cloud would gather on its rim. All summer it would hold a single round drop off the ground.

I would have no way of putting these places together. On the slant, the sun would go off me while it was still warm at the pond. On the broken roof I might be dry for so long that a grain of me came off entire, all its edges curled inward, and went rolling down into the groove. A young bird would find it there and try it in its mouth and put it down. For that much of my life I would have been quite warm.

It would be difficult, all the same, to say quite where I ended under the last roof. The fine dark saucer would be in part what I had made of the stone and in part what the stone had made of me. A dry leaf dragged in by its tip would catch on one of my rough places and stay, though the bird that brought it had given it up two inches short of where it meant it to go. The young would finish the work by backing into it. A little moisture would come out of their feathers overnight. By morning the leaf would lie flat.

When the three of them stood abreast at the entrance I should feel only the outside toe of the smallest. The others would be on bare stone. Then they would shuffle back and there would be a much greater extent of warmth than the feet had promised. In the dark, under the weight of a breast that could not yet be held up for very long, I would begin to open.
