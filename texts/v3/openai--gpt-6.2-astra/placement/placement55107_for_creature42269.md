I think I would live under the street at the yellow turn. From there the round doorways would be out of sight. I would know one of them by the moth scales that come over the edge whenever a dun animal squeezes past it. A scale is a handsome thing seen from underneath. It has ribs, and a short stalk at one end, like a fan made for a hand with only one finger.

I would have a use for these. There is a sort of fly that spends all but the last morning of its life in a movable tube. I would be one of those, still a good way short of the morning. The forepart of me would be brown and hard enough to go about in daylight; the rest would have the consistency and much of the character of the white of a poached egg. It is the business of the forepart to find covering for the rest. Down by the basins the tubes would be green, made of shavings taken off the floor. Mine, under the turn, would have a good deal of blue in it, and a piece of white cuff so disproportionately large that I should have to back up a little before going round a corner.

The place I have in mind is just where the smooth yellow stuff ends and the living green begins again. Two drops out of three go straight down the gutter there. The third comes round underneath. It hangs for a time, getting rather square across the top with the weight of the water in it, and goes on down the wall. There is a fine brown meal in this water. I would come halfway out of my tube and comb the meal out with my front feet. When there had been no rain for a long time I would push along to the sleeping shelves and do the same thing with the moisture that collects on the narrow edges of the tails. The sleepers would draw them up, one after another. From the square it would look as if somebody were slowly unpicking the street.

What I should like about the turn is a thing that would probably be a great nuisance at first. The scrape in the round room comes through there with such particularity that my tube, which I carry slung from two little hooks at my waist, would answer it. The white piece would strike the rind. I would draw in my head; presently I would put it out; the sound coming round by a longer way would make me draw it in again. There might be scarcely any feeding done that day. But in time I think I should learn the interval. There would be time after the first scrape to go as far as the lip for a fresh scale and be back with it before the second. An old tube, split down one side and left sticking to the wall, might show a run of six or eight blue scales, very regularly spaced. Someone finding it might suppose the fly had a taste for ornament. In fact it would be the measure, taken several afternoons running, of how far a little soft animal dared go.

On the last morning I should come out backward and leave the tube where it was. For a while there would be the difficulty of the wings. There is no call, in the life under the street, to provide for anything so wide. I should have to walk out onto the yellow turn with them still wet, and stand in everybody's way.

I count on the dun animal with the white cuff here. I think it would stop for me. There is a little level place between its pink front soles where I could stand while my wings hardened, with the traffic going by on the low side. Then I should fly, not up the great shaft of light, which from where I stood would scarcely show, but across to the opposite pavement, a distance I had gone all my life by way of the underside and had never once seen entire. Behind me the dun animal would take the last three steps to its shelf. Much later in the day, when the sound of its stopping came through the turn, there would be a small knock under the street. It would be the white cuff on the empty tube, striking the wall as usual.
