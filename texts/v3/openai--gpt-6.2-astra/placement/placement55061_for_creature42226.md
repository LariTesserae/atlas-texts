I would come down the salt stair with a strip of ash which I had been soaking all night in the trough. My sister would have told me that the foot of the bucket was going. She could put the end of her nail between the lowest hoop and the wood. I would have said that this was what came of leaving it empty, and she would have said that it had not been empty in thirty years except when I had charge of it. We are both old enough to get some pleasure out of this.

I would expect to have the room to myself. From the door there is a good deal to support this idea. The yellow place is bare, the bucket is full, and the only movement is a little rocking of the strip of daylight in the last dish. Then, in stooping for the handle, I would see the crab sit down. It would remind me of pulling a nail out of a joist on the strength of which you have set both your feet.

I would go no nearer. I have seen the eel before, though I have never seen its two ends at once. Once I saw what I took to be the whole animal going over a saddle and, when it was gone, a considerable further length of it went over. My sister likes to tell me I felt it first through her ribs. She has shown me how she held me, on the left hip with her right hand laid over the outside of my knee. I remember a red button I could turn, which she does not remember. Of the eel I have no recollection at all.

To fit the hoop I would have to empty the bucket. It is a curious difficulty to be surrounded by water and have nowhere you care to pour a bucketful. I would take it up two treads above the pasture and tip it out slowly against the side of the stair. A little brown leaf shut up like a purse would open there. The rest would find its way down to the dishes, and the eel would go under at the first thin lick of it. The crab would stay where it was, passing one claw over the other.

There is a way to hold a bucket between the knees, bottom up, which leaves both hands free and is quite comfortable until you have to get up again. I would work the new ash down over the staves and tap it home with the handle of my knife. At each tap there would be a very small answering knock under me. After a while I would stop to listen and hear nothing, and begin again and hear it again. It would take me most of the job to discover that it was the old hoop, which I had slipped over my shoe to keep it out of the brook.

By the time I had the bucket back under the spout there would be no point in going home. I would sit with my heels on the dry tread and watch the fresh water come. For the first minute it makes quite a respectable noise. Then it strikes water instead of wood, and you can hear the street again. A man up there would be having trouble with a wheel. He would lift his end of the cart, ask somebody if that was enough, and have to lift it a little more.

If my sister came looking for me she would see the top of my cap level with the street through the grating by the stair. She would know better than to call down there. She would come the long way round and sit beside me. There is room if one of us sits a tread higher. Presently she would put a finger under the new hoop to try it, and presently, without either of us having seen it arrive, there would be the eel’s head in clear water and the transparent fin going to work on the pink bit of shell.

I would be the younger of the two people on the stair, though from the street, looking down at the two crowns, I doubt you could tell. When the bucket was full she would take one side of the handle and I the other. We would have to let go at the square door, which admits one person carrying a bucket but not two people carrying it very easily. She would go through first and turn. For a moment the whole weight of the water would hang between us in the thickness of the wall. Then she would have it.
