I would have a belt buckle nobody could remember renting. It would be in the breast pocket of my apron, wrapped in a corner of rag to keep the tongue from sticking through. We would have spent an unreasonable part of the morning discussing it. There are not many loose buckles on a mountain.

I would be the one the road crew leave at the square hole to clean the rake. It is a job that takes two hands but very little strength. The man on the handle stands over me with his feet well apart. He makes a pull of about the length of a loaf of bread, no more. Any longer a pull and the head comes up with a weight of red mud which drops off entire before we get it through the hole. I have to catch the iron by its shoulders, never by a tooth. For a few seconds we are holding between us a piece of the bottom of the ditch. It comes apart very readily. There are dead leaves with their stalks threaded through the buckle. That argues for its having lain there some time. On the other hand the tongue still works.

My father did this part standing on the bank. He put the rakings on the road and spread them with the side of his boot. I have only the top of the stool to work on, and there are things in a rakeful which ought not to be spread with a boot. I keep a white enamel plate on the stool and a dipperful of the shelf water in the plate. In a good pull the stuff on the rake lets down two brown fish before I have even touched it. Against the enamel they turn nearly as pale as the ends of the roots. The eyes stay gold. It is a poor colour for anything wishing not to be found in a plate.

I used to tip them all back through the hole. One year a boy who had come up to carry the handle on the way down asked me how I knew which side they belonged on. He had been at the doorway watching the hems drop from under the banks. He thought some of them might have got farther than they meant to go. I said if he ever found himself able to tell which direction a fish in a plate had meant to go, he was welcome to my apron and the money in the pocket of it. But after that I took to setting the plate a little aslant at the edge of the opening, with the low rim dipped in the ditch. A fish could leave when it liked. Most went at once. Once one stayed the whole of the rest break, facing the place where the enamel had chipped and a black quarter moon of the iron showed through. We ate sitting quite still, watching it do this. At last the boy leaned over to put a crumb in for it and his knee knocked the plate in.

That was the end of the plate. You can see it on clear mornings, sunk on edge in the mud. People looking into the hole take it for a marker put there by the rain man. A white thing that shows more or less of itself according to the depth: it does as well as anything. It has even found its way onto the sheet we have to fill in. *Water at plate on arrival.* There are three little boxes for above, level, below. For two years now I have had to put my cross outside the boxes and write *leaf over it.*

I use my hand these days. After a few pulls the skin on my palm gets soft enough for the marks of the teeth to stay in it. I lay it flat just under the surface in the ditch. It takes a little while to lose the feeling that I am holding something, even with the fish a good way upstream. Then the hand comes up empty and considerably cleaner than the rest of me and I have to dirty it all over again.

At the end of the hour the man on the handle and I carry the rakings round to the uphill side of the booth. There is a bed of cress coming on there, quite a decent bed now, from bits that broke off on their way through the floor. We have tried it with bread. There is too much iron in the water here; the first bite is all right and after that your teeth feel like the teeth on a key. Still, it is a nice green thing to come upon round the back of a building. Little bare tunnels are beginning to form under it. I put a finger in one last year and felt on the tip of the nail a distinct push, then another. I had no business putting it in any farther. I went round to the door and got my apron, and by the time I had come back the water had risen and the stems were standing up separately, without any sign of a way in.
