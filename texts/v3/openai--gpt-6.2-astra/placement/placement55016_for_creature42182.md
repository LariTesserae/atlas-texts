I think I would be in the well beyond the bluff. Not one of the dozen the man brought up. On the way down, his cup knocked a little skin of lime off the brickwork and turned over, and one worm went past the lip on the outside. That would be me.

There is quite a good ledge a hand below the usual level of the water. When the shaft was lined, some mortar squeezed out between two bricks and set in a sort of trough under the mason's thumb. For years bits of things have been fetching up in it. There is the wing case of a beetle, still green on the underside, and a shaving of wood gone so soft that a bristle put into it leaves a hole. The streak of brown that came with me hung against the brick for a while after I had got in under the shaving. When the cup went up again it took the brown with it.

For a worm so lately hatched there was food on the beetle alone to last a considerable time. I went round the turned-in edge eating the fine stuff there, and came in the end to my own workings, already furred over and good to eat again. By the time I had the girth to shift the shaving I had little use for anything beyond it. My back rings fitted a fault in the mortar. I could let the rest of myself go slack. Out in the mere there are acres of mud where a worm never gets to do that.

I would know the man chiefly by the water he took. On washing days the ledge came bare by degrees. There would be a jerk and a wash, a rest, another jerk, and finally the wash would run off my shut gills and leave a grain of grit between them. But sometimes the water went down quite smoothly. Then, through the front of me buried in the trough, I could feel a fineness of movement which did not come through the gills at all. The grains resting against my skin kept lifting off and coming back a little further along. It took less effort to eat when they did that. I suppose that would be the great note, where I lived: a meal that needed very little work.

In time there would be young in the thumb mark. They would get into places I had never been small enough to enter, even on that first day; under the beetle's wing case there was a second skin, quite close to the first, and for a week it would look as though the green had come alive again. I would have no means of keeping them on the ledge. One afternoon there would be a drawing out of water, much longer and gentler than the lowering of a cup, and the two skins would lie flat together. A few young might get as far as the iris beds. A good many, I expect, would go no further than the next brick down.

It is a narrow habitation to have come an hour's walk to find. Still, I like to think of the man coming out after dark with his saucer, having at last got the flake of enamel loose. He tips a little water down the well. It strikes the wall well above me and divides round a brick, and the two trickles join just where the mortar sticks out. For a moment the ledge is awash with the smell of the place I hatched. I back out the breadth of a bristle. There is silt coming down, a little food, something alive that catches on the shaving and holds. Then the saucer scrapes on the coping and the drop on the end of each gill hangs still.
