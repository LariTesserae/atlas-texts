I would be in the scratch. For the first afternoon I would be very difficult to distinguish from the black meal. When the feet went down into it I would come up a little way on the leading fringe, turn over, and fall back in much the same place. It would take a run of six or seven strokes to see that I was getting somewhere. Each time I fell I was a little nearer the copper.

There are a number of us in the bank. In a side light we look like short lengths of dirty cotton laid along the husks. One end is rather flatter than the other. If a young carrier gets hold of the flat end we come away readily enough, and sometimes a carrier will take one of us half the length of the fault before setting it down to get a better purchase. By the time it has got its feet round to the other end there is nothing there. We can travel quite fast in a crease. It is on the open stone that we go so slowly a foot can sweep us up.

My underside would be all little clinging pads. On top I would have a single ridge running lengthwise, soft enough to take the impression of whatever lay against it. My food would be the white inner skin of a bean shell, which comes away, if worked at from the broken edge, in strips considerably longer than myself. There is no use going after a shell while it is rolling. I would live for days on the one wedged in the copper, going out to it from the scratch in the long spells between weighings. I might in fact be one of the reasons the shell eventually came free. The white animal would get most of the credit for that, being visible.

But in the bank there would be another use for that way of feeding. A dead bean and an empty shell weigh almost the same to a pair of the little carriers. Both go up on the bank with whatever else happens to be on the fringes. A live bean, if it is put down on its back, works its legs and presently finds the passage. A dead one stops where it is put. At the next weighing the finer matter lodges round it. By the third or fourth there is a quite solid little door where the wind used to go through.

That is where I would do well. The pressure against my ridge would keep me in place while I worked. I would eat a way in along the hinge of the saddle, thinning it until it lifted. Behind me would be a clean slit, black to look into. On the next fall of the tread a whisker of cotton on the far side of the bean would stand straight out. A very strong current, for such a little hole. Sometimes it would take the saddle off altogether. The young would pick it up and set it crosswise somewhere else.

I should have no particular attachment to the white animal. If the tread were taken up for repairs and the bank laid open, I would follow a broken shell as readily into a weighman's trouser cuff as back under the sill. But barring such an upset I would spend nearly all my life within reach of those feet. I like the pressure when they take the bank. Everything fits then. A husk that has been joggling against my back all morning comes to rest and for the space between the knocks I can get both edges of my mouth under the skin and draw off a proper strip.

On Sunday, with no pressure to speak of, I would go out a little. There is still something to eat in the scratch after the shell has gone: a narrow white shaving taken off the rim where it dragged over a high grain. It lies lower than the hairs can reach. I would have to get my flat end underneath it and go along almost doubled over, eating backwards. From the loading floor nothing of this would be visible but a white line slowly coming unstitched from the stone. Where the line ended I would straighten out. The feet would pass over me once going out and once coming home, and on the homeward stroke I would catch hold.
