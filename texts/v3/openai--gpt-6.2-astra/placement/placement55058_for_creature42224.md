The scales on the backs of my hands would be a considerable nuisance to me. My hands would be the only parts I had above ground. I would be lying just under the nap, with my face in the cool hollow made by a root hair and my two wrists coming up through the same hole. From the height of the seed eater they would look like a pair of little brown leaves. If a scale fell across both of them I could do nothing for some time. Eventually a hind foot would go ploughing through the heap and take the scale away.

I would live at the very limit of the quiet, where there is still beating enough to lean a hair over, but not enough to lay it flat. In the evening I would feel about for a hair bowed down with grazers. One hand would take the tip and draw it underground a little way. The other would work down it against the grain. There would be a rough place on the heel of that hand, with a hollow behind it; after three or four strokes I could put my hand to my mouth and eat. You might watch a great many red arches let down and spring up before noticing one that let down quite as usual and came up green.

I wouldn't have made the hole myself. A young runner would have come out of it in the spring. There are a great many animals under the plain that can keep a hole open, and rather few that can begin one. Mine would be quite stout at the mouth from the pressure of my wrists. Lower down, where I turned over to sleep, it would be lined with the soft ends of those tubes that come up on the wind. I would have taken them for no better reason than that things with a flare to them are easy to catch. A thread of pale tubing would show whenever I stretched too far and brought a little of my bedding up with me.

For much of my life I would know the seed eater by weight. There would be the four separate dents, and then the broad shallow depression of the breast coming to rest, squeezing the roof of my hole so low that my hands opened of themselves. In the brief stillness afterward there would be time to collect grazers from the tips of six or seven hairs. One day the weight would come down in the usual way and lift again, but a quantity of lesser weights would remain. For several evenings I would eat very poorly. A chin thrust under a promising arch can undo a good deal of patient work.

It would be one of the young ones, I think, that would first bring me altogether into the light. It would get hold of a hair just as I did. There would be a tiny pull from my end and a much more decided one from its end, and out I would come, a little grey twist no thicker than the hollow in a seed. For a moment we would both hold on. It would be odd to see, on a body so large that I had taken it for a change in the weather, the small unequal motions by which I got my living.

Then the hair would slip. The youngster would sit back on its haunches chewing, and I would be left on the surface with all four of my feet tucked up against my belly. They would be white, very soft, quite unfit for the open. I would have to go home on my hands. First one palm flat on the nap, then a swing of the whole long body, then the other palm a little farther on. A journey of some consequence. By the time I reached the heap it would be dark underneath it. I would find my place by the end of a tube sticking out, turn round, and go in backward, drawing the tube after me. For the rest of that evening, if anything fell on the backs of my hands, I would let it lie.
