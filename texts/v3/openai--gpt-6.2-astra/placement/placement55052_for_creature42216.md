I think you would find me in one of the five courses that do not fill. There is quite a lot of afternoon left in one of those. A seam bird has stood on a stone above it, looked almost straight down it, and gone somewhere else. For a while the pebbles have the warmth of the bird’s feet, but only two of them, and not for long.

I would be one of the brown hoppers. From above there is very little to tell about me except which end is which, and even this is easier after I have gone. Down among the stones I have a certain breadth. I can put my back against the underside of a pebble and my six feet on the floor, and move along very comfortably in this attitude, combing up the green meal that grows where a little light gets in. I turn over to eat from the ceiling. There are places where this involves going some way to turn over and coming back.

I should not know much about the birds. When one walks overhead there is a noise like the turning of a stone in its socket, followed by the actual turning of the stone in its socket, which requires my attention. Now and then a narrow face looks in at one of the doors. Seen end on, with the light behind it, a bill has hardly any length. It is a small dark diamond. It takes a surprising amount of going backwards to get out of its reach.

There is something else I should be besides food. In late summer I carry a little dry field about with me. The rush seeds have a brush at one end that takes hold between the plates of my belly. I pick them up going under a crown where the seed scales have fallen in a heap. For the first dozen steps I stop repeatedly and draw my hind legs forward, first one and then the other, trying to comb the things out. After that I go on with them. If you were to lift my stone at this time of year you might see what appeared to be a small sheaf of rushes making off on its side.

On the afternoon my course does fill, there is no broken light where I am. The first thing that happens is that the dust on the floor gathers itself into little round backs. One of them touches a forefoot. By then it is rather late to take the usual way out, and I go up between two pebbles into full sun. My shadow is an astonishing black thing on the white stone. It jumps when I do. There is a bird a little way downstream attending so closely to my neighbours that I can sit for a moment in the lee of its foot.

Then the water takes me. I go along belly uppermost with my legs folded and a bright hem of air down either flank. The rush seeds come away without the least difficulty. At the first bend they go straight on into a pad of black mud, while I turn with the water and fetch up in a hoofprint. For a while I swim round the wall of it unable to find the place by which I came in. There is a birch leaf afloat there. I get out on the leaf, and from the leaf onto the toe of the print, which is shallower than the heel.

I like to think that one of those seeds takes. Not a whole new marsh, just a single rush a little out of line with the others, in a place where the water slows on a bend. Next summer a bird on a knob will have to turn its head a fraction farther to see past it. I won’t be there to see the head turn. Under a flat stone on the high side of the hoofprint there is a space with a green ceiling, and room at either end to turn over. That is where I would be.
