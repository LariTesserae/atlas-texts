From the dish I should look like a flaw in the grey hem. The other grains hang on the leaf with their broad faces uppermost, held by the hairs that lifted them. One grain hangs corner up, and below it there is a clear line like a scratch in the air. It is visible for a few minutes at noon. When the leaf takes up water the scratch shortens. One might think it had gone into the grain.

I should have to reckon myself among the mosses. I should have begun well inside the fold, on the face that looks up. There would have been a winter of growing straight, so little at a time that twice the water at my tip would have frozen against the ceiling and thawed away again before I reached it. The day I did reach it would have been a dry day. Quite a length of the tip would have lain over by evening, held to the leaf by its own wetness. From then on I could have grown in any season in which the two leaves did not freeze fast together.

There is a difficulty about saying where such a stem ends. In the course of years the leaf I rose from would have become a ladder. One end of me would still be fast to a rung of it. The other, following the underside of the sound leaf, would have come to the crimp. A crimp seen from inside is a succession of little bays, deeper on one side than the other. I should have gone round several without once coming out. It would have been the corner of the grain that took me out at last. There would have been no difference to feel: wet leaf, a little joint full of water, wet stone. By then the old upright part of me would be one of the palest things in the fold. Someone looking for me by the dish would find the half-inch that had taken the least time to grow.

I think I would like the sea best during a freeze. In summer the rung and the grain would move together. In winter the leaf with the grain on it would be fast in the fringe of ice, but the rung, well back in the dry, would still be free. I should get the whole of the movement then, a long gentle pull followed after an appreciable time by the giving back of just that much length. The jumps from the road would be quite different. A jump might leave the rung higher than it had been. There would be a slack place in me afterwards, and a bead working its way out toward the hem would stop there. One of the consequences of a hard winter might be water in an unaccustomed place well into June.

In the next good rain the cornered grain would touch bottom. For a while my outer length would be laid flat between its underside and the shale. The pool would be deep enough to take the weight off the grain; it would roll a little, very easily, without coming away from the leaf. There is a considerable difference between what a leaf can lift and what it can roll. Some of the old hairs would slip free. Others on the high side would tighten. When the pool sank there would be a narrow wedge of water under the low side and I should be in it, with a floor for nearly the whole of my length outside the crimp.

I do not think the leaf would root that time either. The bare shale would dry as before. Toward noon the hem would begin to lift, one grain at a time, until there was only the cornered one left on the ground. It would turn a little farther and slip out of the last of the hairs. For an hour or so there would be a clear stitch between the leaf and the fallen grain. Then the rung at the back, which had taken several winters of pulling, would come away at one end. Enough of it would come forward through the fold to let the hem go on rising.

That would be my place: partly in the old forest still, partly under a stone on the bare side of the dish. The stone would keep a little patch of shale dark through the long bright part of the day. On its upper face, in a groove too shallow to hold water in the open, there would be a shred of the leaf hair that had held it. Next time the hem came down it would come down on that face. It would not have quite so far to go.
