A handbreadth up, a feeler put out to find the bank finds only more water. One of the little shrimp continues for a while to make a taking-hold movement with its foreparts. It gets nothing this way until a fleck strikes it and stays. The foreparts close on the fleck and bring it in. What would have been a rather poor hold makes a considerable meal.

I would be in the water that goes the other way. There is a little animal there with a clear, pleated cup at one end and a knob the color of weak tea at the other. Between the cup and the knob, if the light comes across it properly, four threads can be seen. The cup draws shut. The threads buckle and the knob comes up after it. In this manner I am able to swim quite briskly for a distance somewhat less than my own length, after which I open again and lose a good part of the distance. It makes a difference which way I am pointing when I do this. It takes several strokes to turn round.

The knob is my stomach. It hangs inside the cup, though seen from the side it looks as if I were dragging it behind me on a string. Round the rim are eight long feelers set at unequal distances, there having lately been nine. When I stop swimming these continue to go out. They are so much finer than the threads in the cup that the only way to see where they have got to is to watch the dust. A grain travelling freely with the rest comes to a check, swings in a little arc and goes on. A softer thing does not go on. By the time it has begun to struggle there is a short hard knot in the feeler and my mouth has bent over to meet it. I can do this on one side while on the other a feeler is still going out.

One of the hatchlings comes against me, not against a feeler but against the cup itself. Its two dark eyes run up the pleats like beads up the teeth of a comb. I shut with such force that I turn partly inside out. The hatchling gets away. For some time afterward a whole quarter of my rim lags behind the rest, and each stroke takes me a little farther round a circle. Meanwhile the circle is being carried steadily west. The shrimp, which has made no more than three or four of those hitches of its own, is already a long way east of me, in company with a patch of grit that would have gone west too, had it been lifted the breadth of a shell higher.

In the trough beyond the saddle there is less grit and much more to eat. I have been there before, though there is nothing about the place I could recognize. I have no part to look out of. At the base of a feeler is a small pit containing a loose grain, and whichever wall of the pit the grain bears on is down. Even this little plain fact is not always to be had. In the turning water below the crest the grain rolls right round the pit; for the time it takes to swim a stroke there is a down in every direction. I open wide and sink. The long feelers come up past me. For a few moments I descend in the midst of eight nearly straight lines, like a weight being lowered with altogether more cord than is necessary.

It is by sinking that I get back to the bank. The edge of the cup touches first and folds under. There is a pause while the current presses the rest of me flat. To a fish passing over I would look like the wet shine on a flake, except that the knob, displaced to one side, goes on making its slow movements over a speck of food. Water works under an edge. I peel off and am whole again, and in the peeling one of the feelers slips into a slit in the shell and touches something which draws in sharply, taking the tip with it. This is more or less how I came to have eight. This time the thing in the slit lets go.

There is another way in which I belong down here. Under an overhang on the trough side of the saddle are some short stalks, whitish at their roots and clear farther up, with a few fine arms held out from the ends. The shrimp goes among them sometimes, picking with its front feet, and where it picks an arm in its way shrivels down to a white pimple. Halfway up one stalk there is a swelling. In the swelling a rim is already laid in pleats, and under the rim a knob, very pale yet, being fed through the stalk. The water rocks the swelling on a neck that grows narrower as the cup grows broad. It was from just such a place, perhaps from that very stalk, that I came loose. For the last few strokes before I did so I was swimming hard enough to bow the stalk over, and all the other arms, which had no use for swimming, went on taking food.

By the next slack water the neck has worn through. The new cup is in no hurry to leave. It opens where it is, touches the overhang, shuts, touches the overhang again. Its feelers are still short and curled at the ends. A crab walking on the ceiling above it sets one foot in the cup and pushes it off as neatly as if that were what the foot was for. Then there are two of us in the trough, the new one beating fast and all to one side, myself hanging open with a small pink catch drawn nearly to my mouth. Between us the water is clearing. For an instant each of us makes a pale round mark on the shell below, and then the marks go out together, though we are both still there.
