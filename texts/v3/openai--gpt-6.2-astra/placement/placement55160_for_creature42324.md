There is a board missing from the back of the covered stop. Looking through, you see a woman sitting at a machine with her left shoe off. That would be me. I mend the straps that people hang on to in the trams. The machine has a treadle made for somebody with a longer leg than mine; in my stocking foot I can just get my big toe into one of the holes and pull it up again. At the end of a good day I have a blue toe and forty or fifty straps hanging from the gas bracket, each with its half twist in it so a passenger's knuckles will come to rest the right way round.

The missing board is quite useful. A conductor on his way to the sheds can put a bad strap through without having to come in and explain. Sometimes there is still a glove hanging from it. I put the gloves on the back of the bench, fingers up. Seen from inside on a November afternoon it is like being asked quite a number of questions at school.

It is through this gap that I first see the dog's chin move. I can see the top of his head and the girl's knee but not, from where I sit, the toe of her shoe. She holds very still for a while. Then a shoe comes through the gap by itself.

“Can you see where it's gone?”

I take the shoe to the window. There's a little live thing going along the welt. Each stitch of white cotton is a difficulty to it. It has got over four of them since the girl took her shoe off, and there are a good many more between it and the heel. I have an eyeglass which clips to the front of the machine for picking old thread out of the needle race. We manage by turning the whole machine a quarter turn and putting the shoe where the needle ought to go. The girl stands on a box outside. The dog is obliged to form his own conclusions.

Under the glass it is possible to see that one of the two black grains on the yellow case is not a grain at all. It is the head of a very small brass tack. There used to be brass tacks in all the straps; hundreds of them must have gone through the floorboards of the cars and fallen out along the line. This one has been worn thin at the edges like a sixpence. When the creature comes to a stitch it gets the edge of the tack under first, and the rest follows with a little heave.

I have always rather wanted somebody to look through that glass with me. There's a piece of red leather that, cut across, is white inside, with a red skin on either face so thin you'd think it would come away on a wet hand. There are the old straps from the baths car, which have grown a nap from all the clean hands. I reach up to get one down, but the girl is watching the tack go under the next stitch and in a minute I am watching it too.

We put the toe of the shoe down in the break. It takes so long for the little fellow to get off that the girl has to go to work in one of my shoes. Fortunately it is the left. I see her off on the car, my shoe with its squashed back looking rather disreputable under her good black stocking, and the dog lifting his bad leg high over a step which has sunk almost level with the pavement.

For a while there is a great deal of coming and going through the back of the stop. Kit brings the yellow cases when she finds them on things that can be moved. Mr Vale brings a spectacle lens in a wire holder that will swing in front of the machine glass. The turnip man puts his tongs through to show us what has got into the hinge. He has come all the way from North Bridge with the tongs held shut on a perfectly good turnip rather than open them and risk pinching it. It proves to be a bit of paper. Under the two glasses the paper shows a fine blue down, very beautiful, and part of a printed letter rather too big to make out.

Then the line is straightened, and the covered stop, which was built with the back against my wall, is taken up in slings and set down opposite a shop that sells pails. I miss the light changing when somebody sat down. For the first week I keep putting my hand out for straps that aren't there.

What I have now is a strip of ground the width of the old bench, sheltered on three sides. By the time the men have filled in the four postholes, there is water standing in the prints of their boots. I sit at my machine and watch a white bird try each print in turn. At the last one it draws out a lace nearly a yard long. A man must have been annoyed to lose it; the brass end is still on. The bird lays it out straight and walks slowly along it, giving it every chance.

I don't think I should ever have gone out on the plain on my own account. I like a thing to come within reach of a chair. But in the slack time before the last cars come in I take my work outside, and sit with my stocking foot in the cool print where the lace was. Kit knows to find me there. She brings Pocket round after his dinner, when he is allowed to be in company again, and we have a bench of sorts made from the crate the new straps came in. It is lower than the other one. He can get not only his chin but a considerable length of neck across our knees.

The little brass heads start turning up in the postholes towards the end of summer. You can see them best with the naked eye, as it happens. Turn your own head very slightly and a point of light goes out; keep still and presently it comes on again somewhere else. Kit can pick out six or seven at once. I can do three. The dog, with his eyes shut and one ear turned towards the works where the supper belts are slowing down, occasionally gives an enormous sigh. This stipples the water in the nearest hole. For a second there are more lights than either of us can count.
