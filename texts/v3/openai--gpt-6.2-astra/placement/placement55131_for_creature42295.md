I think at first I am on the shallow steps. That is where I would sit to watch the bird. From below, the water coming over the edge of a leaf under its foot would have a bright rim, visible a moment before the water itself. I would know by that rim which leaf it had stepped on, even with the sun in my eyes. But the steps are empty. The place I keep coming back to is lower down, behind the sill, where the midge has to go round the end of a twig.

I would be the little tilt in that twig. There is a drop on the end of it, heavy enough to bring it down across a well-used piece of ground. The midge comes up against grey bark where in the morning there was air. It puts its forefeet on the bark. For a moment its fine weight is added to the weight of the drop, and I feel this a good way back from the place where it happens. That is how I come to have wood, and to be the willow nearest the sill.

I had pictured myself very neatly, a tree standing on its own feet. In fact one of my feet comes up out of the ground a little way off and has leaves of its own. The sun gets to those leaves first. While there is still a hard white line of frost along my broken branch, something is already going on at the other end of me. By noon it is the other way about. The far trunk is in the shadow of the wall and the near one stands in such good light that the small red stone in its fork is warm on top. Underneath, where bark has grown up against it, the stone is cold.

I have done quite a lot about that stone. The first winter it lay loose and worked a little deeper each time the fork opened in the sun. For several summers there was a gap behind it where a midge could get out of the weather. Now there is only a dark crescent, about as wide as the mark you could make with a thumbnail. It will take a long time to close that too. If the stone had fallen a finger's breadth to the left it would be in the street, and everything I have grown beside it would have had a different shape.

When the cloud comes I get the sound through the ground. The leaves hardly stir at first. Then the drop falls, the twig lifts, and for the rest of the afternoon there is a clear way under it. On its long walk home the midge takes the old turning all the same. It goes around a bit of bare earth and comes in under my low branches as the light leaves the sill.

There is room for it to walk upright there. There is room farther in for it to turn over and hang by its feet, with both wings clear of the ground. In the last light one wing throws a narrow shadow on a patch of exposed root. The root and the branch it hangs from belong to me, though you would have to lift the whole wood out of its yard to see how. I would rather you left it there.
