I would be the end without the mouth. On level ground I would have very little idea of our shape. I would know the long pull in the skin when the mouth got under something heavier than usual, and the easing when it let the stone down. Sometimes, after a good feed, there would be a minute sideways movement, just enough to bring a part of the ground that was still cool against one of my bladders. I would have the benefit of most of what the mouth did without ever finding out what a thread tasted like.

There would be a fly that generally worked above me. I could feel its wing strokes through the place where my skin joined the plate. It would work in short spells, with a pause to eat, and after four or five spells it would walk a little farther in. In the course of a long stay on one patch of ground I would come to take those strokes very much for granted. If they stopped I would ease the rim up. Sometimes the bead had got too big and the fly was standing out of reach of it, waiting for a little dry air.

Going uphill would be a miserable business. The mouth would be in front, following the flies, and anything we dislodged would have to pass under me. A grain would go rolling down the channel in the foot, stop against the loose skin between the bladders and stay there through six or seven efforts to go on. I think a good many of our differences of opinion would be about things of this order. From my end there would be an excellent reason to go back the breadth of a grain. From the other there would be nothing whatever the matter.

Then the ground would be wet all at once. I could take up water through the thin skin at my tip. For a little while before the rim went down I would be busy with that, taking in as much as I could without getting too big to draw in. The young climbing onto the plate would weigh hardly anything. What I would feel would be their mouths letting go: a little succession of releases, like the top skin of a water bead breaking. One might spend a long time at the very edge, where the grit was fresh and came away easily. I would keep still for that one long after the weight was gone.

I would know more about the way down. By then my bladders would be full. Stretched tight, the skin over them could distinguish the fine grit a hunter had raked aside in turning from the grit that had lain undisturbed and fitted itself together. Where the disturbed strip ran across our path I would turn and go along it for a while. To the mouth this would probably feel like another of my difficulties. But a little way along, the strip would double back and there would be the hollow in which the hunter had lain, dry still at the bottom. One could feel which way it had gone out.

Before we went down, though, there would be that interval with the rim held up and the cold getting in. A fly would come over the soft place on its belly, dragging first one wing and then the other through the narrow part. Quite soon it would begin to work above me. This one would go on without the pauses I was used to. I would lift the edge for it once or twice to no purpose. It would have found a smaller bead, farther out, no bigger than it could finish at a sitting. When we tipped forward to go downhill I would feel it lose its footing, catch again, and walk a short way in. That would be the company I kept for the next long stretch of dry weather.
