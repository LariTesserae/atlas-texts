I think I would live behind the white circuit, at the end of the lane where the takers-out bring their barrows. I would be allowed a roof there on account of the paper. There would be a window looking into the heaps at about the height of a wren, and a door wide enough to admit a hearthstone on edge. It would be a good deal too wide for the room in winter. I would have hung a blanket across all but the last foot of it, so that a caller coming in out of the light would appear one sleeve first.

My business would be to keep the shapes of things. Taking a stone out is a morning’s work, sometimes two. It is a pity to do it a second time in order to find that it will not go through the door of the person who wants it. So before the taker-out wheels it away, I go down on my knees with a sheet of paper and a stick of red chalk. I take the outline in two directions, and a third if there is anything peculiar underneath. The sheets hang on poles in my room. You can walk between the shapes of six quarters’ worth of household arrangements and come out at the far end with a little red on each shoulder.

It is work for somebody who can be interested in a difference of half a finger. I believe I could be happy at it. It is also work at which one might become a considerable nuisance. There would be a period, early on, when I put down entirely too much: whose basin it was, why they had wanted the far side raised, how long they had been unable to use the other arm. Someone would come in for a basin and have to stand there listening to me before being allowed to wash in it. I hope it would be the old woman herself who came and set me right.

“Put that it tips if you lean on the near side,” she might say. “That’s the bit I’d have liked to know.”

After that there would be less writing on the sheets. What writing there was would be rather better.

I would go about the town more than you might suppose. Paper takes an outline very well but is a poor judge of the room a knee requires underneath a table, or of the turn a woman makes with her shoulders when she lifts a sleeping boy. If somebody wanted a thing, I would generally go home with them first. On the way we might talk about it, but often enough they had told me all they knew. We would walk the rest of the way with our eyes down, each keeping the other the small company possible on a road where you can see the very house you are going to and will not reach it till dinner.

There would be failures. Once I would find a beautiful little seat for a girl whose work was picking the burrs out of dyed wool. It had a scooped place for either thigh and a lip at the back just high enough to rest against. It would fit her exactly. At the end of a week she would bring it back on a handcart. She liked to sit on one foot, it turned out, and whichever foot she chose, the seat objected. I would make tea. We would sit on the floor to drink it. For years afterwards, when I caught myself being especially pleased with a fit, I would see that girl tucking her heel up under her skirt.

I would know where to find a narrow sill that casts no shadow at noon, and the only trough in the heaps with the plughole at the shallow end. I would not know all that was in the heaps. A certain amount of stone goes quietly across the back lane after my blanket is drawn. There are things people do not care to have measured, though they have no objection to a stranger finding a use for them later. I would leave a length of rope and the low barrow under my eaves. On mornings when the barrow stood the other way round I would take particular care to go out by the garden.

In time somebody else would have to do the kneeling. I imagine a boy with very clean cuffs when he first came, and presently with the red marks up to his elbows. He would discover that a damp twist of wool takes the shape of a curved underside much better than my third drawing. I would be annoyed not to have thought of this. For a while we would do both. Then there would be a row of crooked woollen things drying over the stove, like the legs of water birds, and the third sheets would go to wrap pears.

What I should like best, towards the end, would be to go with him on a delivery without being needed for it. We would take a lintel to a house where they wanted a bench outside the door. He would set it on its two blocks and take his wool out from under it, and the people would try it and say yes, there. On the way home we would have to stop once or twice. There is no concealing a stop on that shelf. I would stand with my hand on the empty barrow and look at the grit until my breath came back. The boy, who could have wheeled me home in it and would have rather enjoyed doing so, would occupy himself with a binding.

And some market day after that I would come through the garden on my own and see the lintel in use. Not as a bench, just then. Someone coming out of the house would have set a basket on it to get a better purchase on the handles. She would lift it against her hip, shove the door to with her heel and be off. I doubt she would know where the stone had come from. I might by then have forgotten what shape it was underneath. I would sit down in the place the basket had left, in the good light, and get one of the pears undone.
