I would come up with the fitter the next time. I would have the oil and the little book in which the visits to the tank were entered. There would be a good deal in the book about the ascent: *third rung loose*, and, in another hand, *third from which end?* About the shelf itself there would be almost nothing. The float was to come to the same scratch every morning, within a pencil’s breadth, and for eleven years it had done so.

I think at first I would be rather disappointed in the snail. The fitter would have told me about the noise it made, and with the works going there would be no possibility of hearing it. Also I would be looking at the wrong thing. There is a loose flake under the plank very like the description he gave me. I would watch that for some time before he put my hand into the water a little farther along. On the side of my forefinger I would feel something like the corner of a small envelope. By the time I had got my head down to see, the snail would have drawn in its lip.

“Best leave your hand there now,” he would say. “It was going that way anyhow.”

There would be a long wait, with my sleeve slipping down. Then the clear part would come out again and try my finger in two places. It would find nothing of any use on either. There is a difference between the touch of something which has taken fright at you and the touch of something which has finished with you. I should be pleased to have felt the second.

After that I would have the morning round myself. There is a place to sit on the sill with one heel hooked under the pipe. From there I could read the float and enter the figure without getting the book wet. I would learn that a pencil’s breadth is a considerable distance: the float is nearly always going either up or down inside it. On the way up the chain comes taut one link at a time. On the way down one link rests crooked on the next for a moment before it falls straight. There are mornings when this would be the thing I had climbed all that way to see. I would shut the book and find I had not looked for the snail.

Once, late in the season, I would see a bit of white coming down the middle of the ditch much too fast. I would put the book down open and catch it in my palm. Only a chip. On the top it would have the dull white finish of the vein, but underneath, where it had lain against the next chip, there would be a narrow scallop as smooth as the inside of a teacup. I would know then where it had come from. Under the plank the roof would still be going along, a little brown showing through towards the rear.

I would keep the chip in the fold of the book. At each opening it would fall a little farther into the stitching, until the book opened of itself at that morning. No one looking up the figure afterwards would find anything remarkable there.

What I would like best, though, would come some years later, when there was somebody else to carry the oil. I would have got rather stiff by then. I would let the new boy get up over the lip ahead of me and take my time on the last few rungs. By the time I put my head up he would be lying flat with one ear against the bare part of the plank and his other ear stopped with a finger. It would never have occurred to me to do that.

I would lay the book on the sill and get down beside him. Through the wood the great running sound would be quite altered, much lower, with little spaces in it. In one of the spaces something would rub, then rub again a little farther off. It might only be the water moving a grain of stone. The boy would lift his head to ask if I had heard it. I would keep my ear down. There would be room on the plank for him to put his back.
