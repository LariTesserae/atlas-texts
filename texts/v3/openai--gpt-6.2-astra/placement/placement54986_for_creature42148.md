I think I would be on the stairs, holding the bag. It has to be held rather particularly. If you open the mouth wide to make things easy for the woman with the brush, the warm air fills it and turns it inside out. You keep it bunched in your fist and open two fingers for each pinch of sweepings. From that position you see very little of the garden. You see the backs of her hands, and whatever she gives you.

I would have come up to see the snail. I know how that drawing looks after it has been copied a few times: the blades get heavier, because nobody likes to leave a line too faint to be seen, and the thread loses its fuzz and becomes a good stout piece of cord. I would have been the one downstairs who made the cord stout. There is a hard pencil in the joint book, tied in with string, and it takes a considerable effort to make a mark with it on an old page. I would like to see what sort of a mark the thing itself makes against the sky.

But the woman gives me something with legs.

It is a shaving of fig, curled tight, with a cricket holding on inside the curl. I feel it through the cloth before I see it. For a moment I keep my fingers shut. I have been told what happens to a shaving that gets loose up here. Then I put my other hand in the bag and let the cricket walk out of the shaving onto my knuckle. It is white except for two little brown hooks on each foot. On the way up I had thought about how carefully I would have to hold my hand if I got to use the mirror. Now the difficulty is getting the hand to a place where the cricket can get off, without offering it any of the several thousand feet in between.

The gardener puts her brush down. She takes hold of my wrist and puts it against the flat underside of the branch. For a while we stay that way, two people helping something cross a distance considerably less than the thickness of either of their hands. It doesn't cross. She turns my wrist a little farther, until the back of my knuckle actually touches the wood. Then it steps over without the least hesitation. It had been waiting for a floor.

I would go down with a bag about as heavy as when I came up. There is an enamel basin on the landing where the cuttings can be spread out and gone over before they go in the stove. I don't think I would put them in straightaway after that. I would set a short piece of the branch in the basin, with one end touching the bottom and the other resting on the rim, and leave it through the change of shifts. There is room on the landing for a person to sit on the bottom stair without being in anybody's way.

That is probably where you would find me most years. I might get my look in the mirror another spring. In the meantime there is a thing I would know that isn't in the drawing or the joint book. If you sit looking into a white basin full of fig crumbs you can look for quite a long time without seeing anything alive. It's better to look at the clean part of the basin, at the little space under the branch. Sooner or later there may be two brown hooks round the edge of it, taking hold from the other side.
