I would be in the mountain at first, in a place where two thicknesses of rock have failed by a very little to lie flat on one another. There is water along the floor. Over much of it the roof is dry, but where a knob of stone hangs down the water rises to meet it. Going in from the ferns I would come to one of these places and lose the dry touch on my back. For a while I would have to go by my feet alone.

I think I would be one of the young ones. A hind foot could stay on a grain of rock until a forefoot had found another as good. The water would be coming to me out of the dark, with an occasional shred of soft brown stuff in it that would fold over my head and have to be taken off. That would be as much of the lake as there was yet: something to set my feet against, and a little food coming the wrong way.

There would be a particular difficulty about getting out. From inside, the light under the loose stone would look like a long opening of quite even width. Anywhere along it ought to do. I would go toward the nearest part and find that it admitted my head but not the first joint behind it. Back out, go sideways a little, try again. Meanwhile another of us would have come through higher up, in a place which to my eyes was entirely black. I would feel its foot come down on my back. For an instant it would put its whole weight there; then the weight would go off me toward the light. I could turn and go up by the way it had come.

What I should like best would come some days after this. I would be out on one of the dead leaves in the drowned wood, on a piece of it so far gone that my two forefeet, set down together, had gone through. The rest of me would stay where it was. There would be a vein against my breast. By drawing my feet back one at a time I could get them onto the vein, which would bend a good deal but hold. I would go on eating along it. Behind me the two holes would become one hole, with a narrow brown tongue in it moving up and down in water so still that nothing else appeared to move at all.

From the headland it would be possible, at the right hour, to see clear down to that leaf. I like to think of somebody looking, and of how much they would miss by having the better view. They could see the whole tree of veins; I could feel the difference between standing on a branch of it and standing in the fork. On the branch there would be a slight roll whenever I moved my head to feed. In the fork I could reach out as far as my mouth would go and the ground would be quite steady.

In time my back would broaden. I would work up under one of the floating leaves, where the ceiling is good to eat and has air in it, and for a long while have very little occasion to go anywhere. The leaf would do the going. One morning, reaching the end of a lane I had been making, I would put a foot past the yellow margin in the usual way and set it down on warm grit. The hillside would have arrived.

I don't know that I would take readily to flight. There is a long business, even after the back has opened, of getting the creases out. I would be one of those overtaken on the stone by the small return of water that follows a floating bed when it lifts. It would need very little water. The ground would go from under all six feet without moving visibly away, and there I would be on the dimples, with two lengths of wet wing trailing from me too fine to pull free.

Then I would want the blade of grass. Not the flight or the gulf at first, just the rough edge of a thing that goes on being there when one presses down. I would climb until it bowed. At that distance above the water I could draw a wing between my back foot and my side and have it come through dry. I would do the other one. With the wings put away again I should weigh exactly as much as before, but the blade, drying too, would have lifted a little. There would be room beneath its point for the next one to catch hold.
