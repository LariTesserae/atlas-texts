I think I would live in the row of dwellings, in a room so shallow that I could sit on the edge of my bed and open the door for someone without getting up. I would like that room in the evening. In the morning I would have to put the chair on the bed in order to sweep.

My work would be with the cupboard checks. When a cupboard was let I would put one of a pair of stamped brass discs on a nail under the hour, and give its fellow to the customer. Most customers put the check away somewhere safe and could not find it again without setting down the very thing they had come to carry their washing home in. I would know which of them to lend the chair to. At noon, when the checks began to come back as fast as they went out, I would eat my dinner standing up, with the plate on the chair.

Len's check would be one I had given him. A customer had taken its fellow up to the street in the pocket of a dressing gown, and although she had promised to look, and had in fact gone to a good deal of trouble looking, it had never come back. I would have tapped a nick in the rim of the one that was left before letting Len have it. He would have preferred it without the nick. He could hear it come round each time on the tiles, and so, once he had pointed it out to me, could I.

I don't think I would be especially good with children. When Len got down on his stomach to watch the check go round a chair leg, I would step across him with my empty plate on the way to the washhouse and step across him again with the plate clean. It would be Ada I wanted to sit by, though I would not know her much better than the man who takes the buttons. I would have seen the inside of her kitchen once, going in for salt, and would remember chiefly my surprise at finding the back of the canvas white. From the shop side it was quite grey.

One evening Len would send the check a little wide. It would roll under my door and go on ticking about for a considerable time in the dark. I would have to let him into my room.

There would be nothing there that required explaining except an ordinary saucer on the floor beside the bed. The check would be in the saucer. Len would take it out and put it back again, to make sure of something; then he would fetch another from my board, with permission, and set it on edge just inside the door. Without any turn to speak of it would find its way to the saucer too. In all the time I had occupied the room small things had fetched up beside the bed, and I had put the saucer there to save feeling about for them in the morning. It would not have occurred to me to wonder quite where the slope began.

After that, when the work was done, we would try the floor in different places. There would be a high place under the chair, and two quite distinct ways down from the sill. On the underside of one of the paper trays Len would draw the courses in school pencil, using a button of Ada's to stand for the saucer. It would take several evenings. I would find that I liked having him there, particularly when he was intent on the floor and I could go on with what I was doing without the trouble of inventing things to say to him.

Ada would come as far as the door one night and ask what we were at. Len would show her. She would watch the check go very slowly across what to anybody's eye was a level tile, incline against the saucer, and drop in. Then she would ask if she might sit on the bed a moment. From there, with the door open, she could see the wool table through the square of her own shop front. She had never seen it from quite that angle. There was a considerable hollow worn in the near edge, just where she stood.

I would give her the pillow to put behind her. Len would be trying the check from farther and farther out in the lane. For once, when it fell flat, whoever it was behind the door would call his name and he would have the satisfaction of calling back, quite truthfully, that it wasn't him. It was me. I had wanted to see if I could bring it in from outside.
