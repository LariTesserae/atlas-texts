I would come to the basin by way of the sandpiper’s footprint. From one end of the print to the other the gray shrimp has water over its back. At the end of the middle toe it has to tip up, hang for a moment beating its legs, and go off to one side. After it has gone there is still something moving in the bottom of the toe. Two bits of rush husk move apart and come together again. I think at first there is a current through the print. Then one of the husks goes uphill.

I would be the eater of the husk, a little fly in the long part of its life before it has wings. I live in a tube under the mud. There is silk in the wall, enough to hold the grains in place when I turn myself end for end, and at either opening there is a small clean lip. The sandpiper put its foot across both of them. For a while this afternoon I had only the water that was in there with me. Now I have the print as well. I can reach out into it and take in quite a length of the soft stuff that has collected against the toe.

There would be a certain pleasure in having so much body. A good deal of it can be busy farther down, making a travelling wave that draws water through the tube, while the head stays quite still at the mouth. What comes in at the other end is cooler. When a fish goes over, the light fails at one opening a little before it fails at the other. I go down like a thread pulled from below. The rush husk stays just where I let go of it; it has a pale scallop in one edge now, which is where I will begin again.

I would have supposed, from up on the shelf, that the great event for such a creature would be the joining of all the pans. In fact it might matter more that a bird set its foot here on the day the water drew back. The difference in depth between its heel and the untouched mud beside it is the thickness of a rush seed. Through the next bright morning there is water in the heel. By the time the sun gets into it I have drawn a husk across the upper mouth of the tube and fastened it there, loosely enough for water to pass. For several days the work going on under it makes no mark at all on the shore.

Later there is a skin afloat in the print, wonderfully exact, down to the two little horns through which I breathed. I have had to leave it to find out about the wind. The trouble at first is that the wind at the height of a rush top is going a good deal faster than the wind where I opened my wings. I go right past the rushes. For a few moments I am out over that interval in which the calls from two stands can be heard at once. They sound quite different up here. There is room between the knocks for a whole flight like mine.

I come down on the shelf while the stone is still warm. My feet, which have had almost nothing to do until now, hold beautifully on the smoky grains and hardly at all on the pink ones. I walk as far as the lip. Below it the toad has risen a little on its forelegs. I stay where I am, opening and closing my wings, until the warmth has gone out of the particular grain I stand on.

It is there I would look for myself at evening: on the upper edge, with the whole wet basin behind me, trying with one new foot the underside of a stone.
