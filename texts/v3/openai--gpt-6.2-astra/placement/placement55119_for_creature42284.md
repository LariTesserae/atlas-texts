I should be a little way in from the buried end of the wool. There is a turn there which is easy enough for an animal to take but rather awkward for anything the animals are passing along. A long thing gets its nose into the pile on the outside of the turn. The mouths behind it continue to push. I should have hold of the other end.

For a long while there would be no reason to suppose I was anything but another mouth. There are so many of them that the fibre, picked out and laid on a card, would have quite a good grip on the card. But if a long thing were fed in, a hair from a brush, say, with a spot on it by which its progress could be followed, at one place the spot would go *back*. It would go back rather more than the length of my body. Then the hair would come on again quite freely, with the other end first.

I am built a little like the toggle on a duffel coat. I have a waist about which one turn of the wool passes, and a mouth at either end. Either mouth will take food, but a thing must be very small indeed to get past the waist. The arrangement for dealing with anything larger is to take a good hold of it and be pulled out of the wool until the waist catches; then the free end of me comes round. There is a moment when both mouths have hold, very close together, and the length of the thing stands out behind me in a space which is usually full of water. It is necessary to wait for that space to empty. This accounts for a certain amount of the backing up of goods towards the fan.

I should know the comb quite well without ever having seen the results of its work. When it passes there is a cold round pressure on my exposed end, so evenly applied that the mouth can stay open without admitting any water. As the pressure goes off, the water draws away first from one lip and then from the other. That is the time to come round. A thing which has hung up a dozen mouths and bent a length of the fibre out of true goes on its way under a pull so slight that I sometimes keep my hold after it has gone. My lips fit exactly where the thing was. It takes a grain of something else to get them apart.

The rib of the moth scale would have been a piece of work for me. Most things that arrive have been held somewhere before and have a soft place, or a nick into which the edge of a mouth will go. The rib was as smooth at one end as at the other. It slipped each time the water came up. In the end I should have let it go by crosswise, an extravagant way of sending anything down the wool. The animals all along one side would have had to lift themselves and let it travel underneath them. I think this is how the two at the buried end came to have it. They would each have found a bit sticking out and taken hold in the ordinary course of things. It may never have been possible for either of them to give it to the other. Their turning over together would have been a considerable convenience to both.

When the plate is off, the turn in the wool opens. The weight of water which usually keeps it snug against my waist runs out along the solder and hangs in a bright drop underneath the floor. For those two minutes I can go where I like. I should go as far as the place where the eggs are. Not to attend to them; my mouth is of no use for that, and the young foot pushing against the inside of its window is quite capable of pushing me off the outside. But there is loose wool there, exceedingly fine, which has been combed out by the feet of the parents going backwards into the rooms. I should take a little of it home.

There is a visible consequence of my doing this. The fitter, if he looked before putting the plate back, would find at the turn in the blue fibre a single pale stitch, drawn quite tight, which was not in the cuff. Under a glass it would prove to be two or three filaments laid across the hollow where I turn, with the ends tucked in. When the water comes back it closes over this little lining without leaving a bubble. Things with a sharp end no longer stick there. After a time, of course, the lining wears through; something comes along with a point fine enough to find the old hole, and the mouths begin to back up again.

I should be there now with one end drawn out to its full extent and the other doubled back in the wool, waiting for the water to fall. What I have hold of is a single black eyelash, considerably larger here than the moth scale ever was. It came in root first. By the time it gets to the two under the solder it will be travelling point first, which is better. They will feel the point go by between them. There will be time to take a foot off the nickel, put it down on the other side of the lash, and let the thick end pass.
