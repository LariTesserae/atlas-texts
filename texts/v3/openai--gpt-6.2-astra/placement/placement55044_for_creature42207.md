I would live in the house with the hollow in its roof. I would be the man's sister, come up from a solitary house further along the join. My left knee would have got to the point where it would take me up a ladder quite well but wouldn't let me down one. There would be a number of quite good ladders in my old place and nothing the matter with any of them.

My brother and I would have to get used to sleeping in the same room again. He would still have the habit, last known to me when he was nine, of having a long swallow of nothing just as he went to sleep. On the other hand I would have acquired the habit of answering a noise in the house with a noise of my own. There was once a grazer that used to get in among my bowls. A click of the tongue would send it out. For the first few nights in my brother's house, just as he was comfortably off, I would send him out of the bowls.

I think I would first see the root wives in the morning. I would be sitting outside combing my hair, which is a business best done downwind of the plaster while it is wet. There would be something like a grey thumb pushing its way out of the end of the fallen limb. It would turn out to be the hindquarters of a young one. It had gone in headfirst and there wasn't room yet for it to turn. It would back out till its hind feet found the roof, walk backwards a little way up the plaster, and sit down facing its hole. After a bit it would go in again. What it was getting out of this I couldn't say.

My brother would have a job that took him away for several sleeps at a time. He went about resetting ladders. Most people could do it for themselves, but it took two, one above and one below, and there were a good many people like me along the join. It was easier for him if I stayed; he could leave bark soaking and trust me to take it out before it went sour. I would come to know the roof better than he did. There is a small trouble with a hollow made to let a limb swing. It lets the limb swing in the direction you have seen it swing. After a long combing of cloud the thick end would take up water through the break, and the thin end begin to describe a slightly different figure in the air. I would be able to tell from inside when this had happened. There would be a soft *ff* in the middle of the other noises. I could find the place by running a hand along the ceiling. Even in a cold house a place that is being rubbed is warm.

One time, feeling for it, I would feel a warmth that didn't go to and fro. It would be just over the place where I slept. I would move my hand a little and there would be another warmth, and between the two a line of little hard points showing through the weave. A root wife had got herself between the two thicknesses of the roof. She had her claws through the inner one and her back against the outer. I would fetch the good bowl, the one with the pale inside, and put it on the floor to catch the bits while I picked out a stitch. Through the opening I would see an eye. It would be very near my own eye. I had not supposed there was room in a root wife's face for so much eye. Then it would go grey across and I would understand that I was keeping her awake.

I wouldn't mend the stitch. When my brother came back he would get into bed and presently ask what the bowl was doing on the floor.

“Catching the roof,” I would say, and he would reach down in the dark and put it on his side, where I wouldn't put my foot in it getting up.

That is about where I find myself: on my back in a house I had been sorry to have to come to, listening to my brother swallow in his sleep. There is a loose end of roof stitch hanging over my face. After a considerable time it gives a jerk and is drawn up out of reach.
