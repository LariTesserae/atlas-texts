From the gallery under the root I could put my chin where the shrew has its chin and feel something that has no sound up in the air. I should like that very much. But what I should want to do then is tip my head a little, to find out which of those uneven feet it is that I can feel. That small wish would put me a long way from the shrew. It has no need to get a better place under the root. The place it has is the place from which the feet are plain.

There is a little room off the gallery, where the root has grown over a piece of slate and taken an impression of its upper edge. The slate has since slipped out. It lies flat in the bay, rusted on all but one edge. In the root there is a slot with a ceiling of wood as smooth as if it had been pared with a knife. Some of the water that comes through the wall goes in there. It might seem a good place for a listener. The water brings the sound of the seam into it; the wood brings quite distinctly the weight on the vole's two forefeet. They are not bearing quite alike. The left one is on the crown of the root and the right a little down the slope.

I think I would be in the slot, though not back in the dark part of it. The mouth has a lower lip of split wood. Most of the fibres run true in the lip, close together and black with water. One has been lifted by the edge of the slate. The fibre is still fast at both ends. It is so fine that the water runs up its sides and joins over it, leaving a dry place underneath no longer than the white mark a nail makes on the ball of a thumb. There is air in the dry place. I would have something to do with that.

For down here the *rrr* is not quite the even sound it is up in the willow. There is a grain in it. Held against that little length of wood, the air takes the grain out beautifully. It gives on its wet side and bears against the dry underside of the fibre, and what the root gets is a soft full note. A little way along the wood, where the right forefoot rests, the note is too faint to feel. It is quite lost in the mere warm pressure of the foot. It goes the other way, into the smooth ceiling of the slot. I would be that much air, with wood along my back and the cold of the running seam coming to me through a thickness of water scarcely greater than myself.

I wouldn't know the owl was there. Even the shrew, with only a paring of root between us, would be outside my acquaintance. There would be one thing I could tell very well. On the underside of the raised fibre, where no wet has reached, there are three small ridges left by the stone. Two go all the way across. The third runs obliquely into the second and ends there, leaving a little fork of unworn wood. From the bay, with an eye put to the mouth of the slot, one could make out the fibre and perhaps its two fast ends. One would take the underside for smooth. I would fit the fork as well as the long hollows on either side of it. The note would be roundest there.
