I think I would be at the counter in the parcel office. From there I could see the brass without putting my weight on any of the stones. There is room for a person on the street side of the counter, though how a person is to get round to that side now is another question. The willow has put a low branch across the little swing gate. The gate will open about as far as the thickness of a wrist.

I would have something to do with the weighing machine. It is the sort with a white face let into the front of the counter so that the customer could see the weight for himself. The glass is gone. A fine root has got in under the zero stop and the pointer rests on the root, a quarter of an ounce on the dear side of nothing. When the leaves turn silver the pointer trembles. It would be quite a good place to keep watch from. But I don't think it is the place I would keep watch *on*.

There is a plate under the counter where the shaft of the turntable used to be supported. Three of its bolts have come away with the shaft. The fourth holds the plate at a slant against the wood, and the lower corner runs into the ellipse, out of sight from above. That is the part I would be. Not the whole plate, particularly. The last inch or so of the lower corner.

For years the corner did very little. Water coming down the trunk found the metal and ran off it in a clear bead, always from the same point. The point acquired a little white chin of lime. Below it, on the black paste, there was a dimple about the size one makes in dough to see if it has risen. The lip of the animal fed to either side of the dimple. When the trunk thickened, there came a spring in which the bead did not fall. It swelled against the bark and was taken up the narrow space between bark and metal, leaving the white chin quite dry. Some way up in that space the water found the pink fold.

Since then I would have had two seasons. There is a season when the bark presses hard and I can feel the pump through it, quite a different note from the one that comes to me through the bolt. The two together make a slow waxing and failing, though the pump does not vary. And there is a season when a bead can just get past. Then, for an hour after the rest of the counter has dried, a hanging drop makes a small bright enlargement of the wood grain on my underside. The fold passes through it. For the length of one grain it is a substantial animal, with a margin like the turned seam of a glove; at either end it thins away to almost nothing. I should like to have that much of it in my keeping.

There would be no reason for the person who fitted the brass to have known about me. If they came back now, they might reach through the swing gate to try the old bolts, and find my one bolt loose enough to turn with their fingers. I hope they would leave it so. There is a little play in the hole. On a wet day the willow uses nearly all of it. I go up at one end by less than the height of the white chin, and the animal, which was pressed quite flat a moment before, fills out into the room this gives it. On the face of the weighing machine nothing registers. The pointer goes on resting on its root.

That would be where to find me: on the street side, down on one knee if there were still a street, looking in through the gap the gate allows. No need to put a hand in. Wait for the light off the leaves. There is a dull triangle of iron, a white speck at its tip and, just above the speck, a little place kept wet.
