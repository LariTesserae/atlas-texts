I would have the end berth in the tram car on blocks. There is a brass rack over it with a hollow at either end where the straps of suitcases used to hang. A wet stocking laid over the rack in the evening will be dry in the middle by morning and still quite wet at the toe. This is one way you can tell that the car is no longer running. In service there was a draught the length of it.

My work would take me out with the first tram. I would be the one who brings the lengths of condemned packing back. There is rather more to this than taking them up and throwing them in the well of the car. A length rolled with the pearly face inwards comes to pieces when you unroll it. You have to roll it on a drum, with a wet rag going on at the same time, and keep the drum turning with your knee when you stop to work a bolt-hole clear. At one time it was the youngest fitter's job. Now there is a drum nearly every morning and it pays to send somebody specially.

Along one wall of the car on blocks I would have shallow drawers, made from the old destination boards with laths nailed round three sides. On the underside of a drawer you could still read SOUTH INTAKE or BATHS. I would lay the lengths out on these, with their rag underneath them and the open ends of the drawers over a sink. There would be no need to pick the animals off. Keep the rags wet and in a day or two the slips will have gone over the edges of the packing onto the boards. It seems they like the paint. In particular they like the little ledge where a letter has been painted out. Then the packing can go to be boiled for the wire in it and the boards can go out on the next load of new strip.

For a long time I should think that was all the work amounted to. I should get very good at lifting a loaded board without spilling the film of water, and quite conceited about being able to do it on the steps. Then one morning a girl from the fifth stack would bring me a bit of old packing and ask which way a slip had gone on it.

I would show her the small patch of weave and the larger patch overlapping it, and run my finger along to the place where the animal had been taken off.

“Yes,” she would say, “but which way?”

She meant towards the stack or away from it. She had found the piece in the well of the tram. There was no telling now. We could try it either way along the edge of a drawer and make an equally good case for it. She had an idea that the slips on the underside of a joint always went towards the nearest screw. In a saucer a slip will go round a screw and get under the head, but that is a different thing.

After that I would nick the stack end of each length before I rolled it. For the opposite end I would do nothing. Two marks are a nuisance when your hands are cold, and there is no use giving a mark to an end that can be known by having none.

We would find that the little ones went either way. There was a length off the fourth hall where they had gone towards one another from opposite sides of a bolt-hole and met, and for as long as that joint had lasted neither had got past. On the big strips we began to notice something else. The tracks ran fairly straight until they came to certain places where nearly all of them turned and went along the strip instead of across it. Nothing showed on the face at those places. But hold a board up with a lamp behind it, after the slips have gone off, and there will be a dark interruption in the weave. A bit of doubled yarn. In the old days the packing was woven in such long pieces that a broken thread had to be knotted and the ends carried along for a way. The animals were following the thick place on the water side of it. You could put your cheek against the rag and feel the ridge, hardly more than the mark a seam of a pillow leaves on a face.

The girl would lose interest at this point. It had not been the screws, and there were other things she wanted to know about the screws. I would go on marking the lengths. It costs very little to leave somebody the use of both directions.

In winter there would sometimes be a passenger waiting at the car on blocks. The enamel would have taken on the same skin of ice as the tramway, and with the lamps lit inside and the boards in the windows it would look quite possible that our car was about to start. I would let the passenger in. I would move a drawer off a seat and explain about the stop up on the embankment. There would generally be time for tea. From the seat opposite, with one's eyes nearly level with the drawer, one could see the thin disturbance a fork makes on perfectly still water. The two tips do not break the surface. They raise it in two places with a little saddle between. At that angle the whole business is magnified by the yellow light from the stove.

I like to think that is where you would find me: on the edge of my berth with the dry middle of a stocking over my hand so as not to burn it on the pot, holding off pouring for another moment because the passenger has just bent down to look.
