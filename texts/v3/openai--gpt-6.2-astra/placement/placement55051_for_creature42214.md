In the maintenance man’s left breast pocket there is a dull steel rule. It goes down the stair with him and comes up again. I might be that. I like the part of its working day when the man sets the end of it against something and puts his thumb on the place he wants, so that he can carry a distance over to better light and see what it is. In the hollow there are quite a number of distances I should like to carry over to better light.

But the rule would be back in the van by four. The place I keep coming back to is at the blind end of the vole’s crack. I should be the stone there, though I would have very little notion of the shape of the crack I was keeping open. Two parts of me would be under considerable pressure. Between them there would be a dry face, rather abrupt at the top, against which a quantity of sedge had been packed. Once in a while a piece would work down far enough for its cut end to stand against me. The vole would get its front teeth on it and pull it out another half inch. That would be my acquaintance with the pencil-coloured ribbons. I should never see one made.

Before the slice came away I was wet on all sides. The change took place in a single afternoon. There was a slow wrench, a rush of grit past one cheek, then air against a part of me that had been in contact with much the same black stuff for longer than there had been heather on the island. For the remainder of that spring, rain came straight down the new face. I had a little runnel of my own, with a grain of quartz stuck halfway down it. By the second winter the lip had tipped in and the runnel was dry. That was about when the first sedge arrived.

What I would know of the machines would be rather different from what the man knows. Three machines running together set one loose grain to walking in the dry runnel. It goes a little way down, stops against the quartz, and stays there as long as the pressure is held steady. Sometimes, late at night, it falls the rest of the way. A man up in one of the houses has made a change small enough not to take his gloves off for.

I don’t think I would know about the weasel. The vole isn’t in bed when the weasel comes; there is no sudden subtraction of warmth or weight. It is a fairly ordinary afternoon at my end of the crack. Some time after the sun has gone off the moss, however, something scratches me just above the bedding. It is the stiff end of the root. The vole has brought it in doubled, and now, in a space scarcely longer than itself, it has the business of turning it round. For a while the free end describes the same short arc over my face, a little lower each time. Then it slips past my shoulder into a space the vole can reach with its nose but not its paws. There is a great deal of pushing. By morning there is a pale loop there, wedged tight, with a few shreds of sedge drawn through it. For the first time the bedding stays up against that side when the vole goes out.

I should like that particular position: not the whole stone so much as the shoulder, a blunt projection no larger than the end of the man’s thumb. When the slice next settles it may take some of the load. For the present it holds one turn of root. On a cold morning, after the heather at the upper entrance has tipped and righted itself, the hollow in the sedge keeps its shape; and if the vole comes back wet between the eyes there is still a dry side to turn its face to.
