I would look for myself at the second trying. At the first there is a perfectly good explanation for the rocking. The bowl has a grain of fired clay under its foot, and the water leaving the square has lifted the heavy side and let it down again. But the water is gone now. The heavy side comes up rather slowly. It is being raised by the edge of the next square, at considerable inconvenience to a gutter.

I should like to be the one making that wretched noise. For a time, anyhow, I think I would be.

There is a difficulty about learning a sound that carries well over water. You can hear it from nearly anywhere except the place where you are trying to make it. There the scrape of a foot against a grain of clay is enormous. From six slabs off, the same performance amounts to a pleasant high ting with no burr to it whatever. My first piece of work would be a little listening place six slabs off. I would turn up the edge of a square no higher than the lip on a saucer, enough to keep a spoonful of water there after the gutter had emptied. A good low note passing through the floor would corrugate the water. The high ting would leave it smooth.

For some afternoons I would have a bowl at one end of me and that spoonful at the other. Between them I would do very little of the ordinary work. A frame would come my way and I would let it go by with clay still stuck to one side. There would be a tract of rather poor pavement dating from this period. People interested in the history of the bowl would be able to find it long after the bowl itself had sailed away: six slabs of exceptional care surrounded by about an acre of work that ought to have been done over.

The thing that helped would be the loss of the grain under the foot. Without it the bowl would lie flat. I could lift my square as high as I liked and produce nothing but a little slide and, eventually, an overturned bowl. On its underside there would be two concentric rings, one where the foot had been pinched out and one where the potter had begun to pinch it out and changed position. Between the rings would be a curved trough, shallow at both ends. I think I would spend a whole dry morning putting a bare trickle through that trough. It would come out at the other end carrying three or four grains as fine as the one I had lost.

With the bowl right way up and resting on those grains the sound would be worse. It would begin a good half second before the ting, a little run of hard separate knocks. But out at the listening place the water would go into pleats, smooth out, then go into pleats again. I would do it several times. The pleasure would take me rather by surprise. I had meant the water for a test, and here was a thing worth going six slabs to see.

In time I would let the bowl travel. I would keep the shallow lip. In a flood there is always something going past on its foot or its side, something with a loose staple, a blister in its glaze of salt, a handle that taps against the slab beside it. Some things that make scarcely any noise set the water moving handsomely. A long flat piece of kiln wall, passing half a mile away, might raise a single round drop in the middle of the spoonful and set it down again without wetting the lip. I would get to know that piece of wall. On afternoons when the flood came from the south I would have the water ready early.

And I would make other listening places, though I should not at first know that this was what I was doing. A square of mine, turned out in the usual way, would have the slightest rise at one corner. A dozen floods later somebody far off would find a bead of water lingering there and tilt the square to get it off. The bead would run three sides of the square before it spilled. Next day there might be a second square waiting to receive it. Out there a stretch of work quite unlike me would be taking pains over the speed at which a bead went round a corner. I would have no use of those slabs, no sensation of the bead. It is important to allow the distance to be real. There is enough going on here without supposing that everything done in consequence of me is mine.

Where you could reliably find me would be just after the flood, at a place that looks dry from standing height. Get down with your cheek near the floor. There is still a little water held up by a rim hardly worth calling a rim. Beyond it the third bowl has long since fetched up against a kiln and been put to some sensible use; the stapled bowl, too, is somewhere else. Wait until the heat begins to leave the lower courses. There will be a few small knocks close together. The water will draw up in lines, badly spaced, crowding one another at the western edge. Then a square that has finished settling for the night will lift again, just enough to send the lines back the other way.
