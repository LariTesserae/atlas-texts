I would live on the dry side of the river, which is to say underneath it. I would probably have to stand on something to get at my work. The old people can reach up and tell with the backs of their fingers whether it will be a good day to wash a blanket. I would be the one going back into the house for the stool, and coming out to find that the good water had gone by.

There is work there for a person of my height. Along the bottom of the river hang the things that are almost too heavy for it. A forked stick dropped at breakfast may go some miles with the fork in the water and the handle out. A basket goes the other way up. There is often something in the basket. By the time it has passed four or five houses everybody has had a look, which is unfortunate if what is in it is a present. I would keep a length of crooked wood by the door and catch what I could. In time people would come to me when they had lost anything, even things that could not possibly have got into the river.

What I would like about the work is the interval between getting a thing down and learning whose it is. Here is a little roll of white fibre with a knot so arranged that pulling on either end makes the other end shorter. Here is a bowl with a lip on the inside. Set on the floor it gives a dissatisfied little rock and spills half a spoonful. Held up against the river it fits beautifully. One evening a boy comes for it, puts it on his head, and goes home with his ears comfortably tucked under the lip. It has been my belief for nearly a month that I know what the bowl is for.

I think I would have known the woman who sat at the last post. She would come and go past my door with a small flat cushion, yellow on one side, brown on the other. She had cut it out of the shady wall of her house, where the moss was fine and did not go to seed under a person sitting still. Once she left it behind and I took it out to her. She was sitting on her hands.

“Down there,” she said, easing one hand out. By the time I had followed the finger there was only a dent in the cloud, slowly filling from the sides. I gave her the cushion. We sat for a while longer, because she wanted me to see how big a dent it had been in relation to something else, and there was nothing else.

After that I would look over the edge rather more often. I would never see the back. I would see things which, if somebody had not spoken or I had got there a little sooner, I could have persuaded myself were the back. The woman would have very little patience with these. She had not waited all those years to be told how easily it could be done.

One morning there would be a copper leaf hanging by its stem from the underside of the river. Its grub would be gone. Inside the roll, where the grub had lain, there would be a line of fine black earth. I would get it down without disturbing the earth and carry it to the post. There are several ways a leaf can turn up in a lifted river. She would know all of them. Still, she would put out a hand for it, and roll a little of the earth against the pad of her thumb, and smell it.

I had thought I would live in one of the striped houses. The longer I stay under this one reach of water the less sure I am of that. The crooked stick is as clear to me as ever, particularly the fork of it where the wet white wood shows through. So is the woman reaching for the leaf. But I cannot make out my door in the wall behind her, or recall going in at night. I believe I might be one of those things that hang from the river. A long weed, very likely, dragged down in a loop by an old breakfast stick that nobody has bothered to retrieve. Things lodge in the fork. People have got into the habit of stopping to see what I have today. It is the hand coming up from below that does the sorting out, the wondering what a thing is for. From this side of the water all those hands have seemed to be mine.

It would be a pleasant reach to grow in. In hot weather the children lie on the moss and bat the end of the stick back and forth with their feet. Farther up, where my root is, the water is so quiet that the fine hairs keep their coats of silt. I would know the woman by the care she takes to lift the fork a little before she takes anything out of it. Her hands are stiff; she has some idea what a small tug can feel like a long way off.

She would keep the leaf. The earth she would tip back into the fork, wetting her finger to get the last of it out of the crease. For a day or two I would have a black crescent there, quite plain to anyone going past, and then the water would work it loose. A little would come to rest among the hairs. I would have no means of telling whether it had come a great way up through the weather or only from the next grove. In either case I think I would put on a little length. One morning a child lying flat on her back would be able to set the stick swinging with her bare big toe, where yesterday she had to wear a shoe.
