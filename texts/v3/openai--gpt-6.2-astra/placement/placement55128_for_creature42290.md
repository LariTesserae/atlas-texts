I would have a key to Ena’s kitchen, on the understanding that if either sister came over she could still use the stove. The top drawer would be hers, too. The first time I found her there she had come the long way round for a particular knife, and having got there and got the pears peeled she saw no sense in carrying them all the way home raw. We sat with our chairs tipped against the wall so her sister could see us both. After a while the sister put an empty plate ostentatiously on her sill.

I should like to make spectacles. It seems to me there would be enough work in the town for one person, including the mending. There would be people who could tell which of their grandchildren was on the opposite pavement by how the child stood, and who nevertheless had to wait for somebody to come home before they could pick a splinter out of a finger. Others would come to me for the sake of something far off. I would ask them to choose, across the hole, the thing they particularly wanted to see. It is no use fitting a woman to read the figures on the public clock if what she wants to know is whether the rope maker is out in his shirtsleeves.

I would be rather better at the glass than at the wires that go over the ears. For the wires I would have an arrangement with the rope maker, whose own ears are set at notably different heights and who once, in exasperation, took the pliers out of my hand. The water under the sill really is damp. I would settle that question in my first week. My rouge would go into little red cakes and I would have to keep it in the top drawer after all, in a tin beside the knife.

In time the boy from the dye yard would find his way to me with his yellow bowl. I would have been expecting him to ask for a lens. Instead he would ask for a piece of glass that did nothing at all. He had got the use of his mother’s lens on evenings when there was no blue in the vats, but by lamplight the yellow glaze threw up such a dazzle that he saw a wick swimming at the bottom of the bowl and very little else. He wanted a vessel with flat sides. He had brought a drawing. It was the first occasion on which anyone had come into the shop and asked to look at something from the side; in my trade it is generally understood that the thing wanted is in front.

We would make it out of four bits of window and a fifth for the bottom, with wax at the joins. It leaked along one corner. The potter’s son made us a trough of black clay just large enough to take the glass bottom, and we had the use of it quite a long time before he pointed out that we could dispense with the bottom altogether and bed the four upright panes directly in the clay. He had been too polite to say so while we were doing the work.

There are several little animals in that water. The one the dyer’s boy had set his heart on, a handsome speckled creature that rowed itself about on its back, turned out to have nothing whatever to do with the flies. The thing that did was a length of greyish thread with a head rather like the burnt end of a match. He made a good drawing of it, much enlarged, with the segments numbered. His mother said she hoped people would understand it was much enlarged. She had spent twenty years getting customers for her blue.

What I had not allowed for in setting up by Ena’s window was that, to everybody on the far side, I should for some time be Ena. I have seen a woman take her hat off to show me a haircut and turn very deliberately to give me the back as well. By the time she had it on again it seemed churlish to undeceive her. I made the gesture for *particularly good at the neck*, which I had to invent on the spot. Once I was actually wearing Ena’s green jersey, my own having got soaked at the cuffs. Her sister stood in the opposite kitchen watching me for a long time. Then Ena appeared beside her and the two of them looked at me together. It was a queer feeling. I took the jersey off over my head, which made them laugh so hard that one had to hold the other by the back of her belt.

I think I would grow fond of that kitchen quite apart from the view. There is a square of floor between the stove and the table where a thing dropped will stay dropped, instead of rolling into one of the wide joints between the flags. That is where I would stand to put a screw in. There is a hook for the lamp which, so long as one is sitting down, puts the flame behind one’s left ear at exactly the right height. Ena would show me these things on the days she came for her knife. She had used the hook to mend stockings by.

And there would be days when I could do nothing for the person who had climbed my stairs. A carter once sat in the chair trying one glass after another. At each change he said, with perfect good will, that yes, it was different. At last I asked him if any one of the differences had been for the better. He considered this and said he had liked the chair. It had arms, which allowed him to get his breath. After that, whenever he came up from the lower wards, he sat in it a little while, whether I was there or not. I hung the trial glasses farther along the wall so he would have somewhere to put his cap.

From the street opposite you could tell almost nothing of what went on there. You would see me standing still with my head bent, or an old man apparently sitting down to an interminable meal. Once a year or thereabouts you might see a white cloth hung across the window and three heads in a row against it. That would be the day something came out of the little glass tank. We learned to hang the cloth because the first time, with the whole bright town behind it, not one of us could see the fly.
