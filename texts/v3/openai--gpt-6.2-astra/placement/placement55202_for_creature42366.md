I would be under the lintel in the low house, though not, I think, anything that could see the animal come through. From the court my place would look like a pale interruption in the purple at the left-hand end of the lintel. A flake the size of a thumbnail has come away there. It has taken the coloured stitch with it. What remains is one of the soft parts, shut up in stone so thin that the animal’s belly, passing over it, makes the two leaves touch.

For a little while after it has passed I am the shape of its weight. The leaves part first at the edges, then in the middle, with a last small adhesion where a grain has gone through my fan. It is some time before the grain comes out. I lay down a little crescent of salt round it, and as the face of the lintel wastes, the crescent comes to stand proud. The next time the animal passes it goes a little higher up the wall.

There are four other places I would have to be. At the foot of the stairs it is early evening already; a loose end there has risen and is feeling along the underside of the first tread. In the second court there is still enough light on the wall to keep me hard. If one were to take the roof off the house, the five pieces of me would go on for a while keeping these different hours. The part between them is very narrow. A change which begins at the foot of the stairs sometimes reaches the lintel after the thing which caused it has gone away.

On the far side of the lintel there is the other end, which is not mine. It is an old acquaintance in the limited sense that there is no mineral anywhere along its length which I have ever taken up. When we were both shorter there must have been an evening on which we touched. Now the last breadth of stone between us is fine-grained and runs the wrong way for either of us. I can put a tip out over it quite easily. I cannot grow there. In all this house it is the only piece of the way down which stays where it is.

One evening a weight comes over the lintel and lifts, and then, before the leaves have had time to part, comes on again. The second pressure is well within the first. It does not reach the crescent. It remains so long that the compressed part of my fan begins to move out sideways between the leaves; a little purple shows at the edge of the flake, where there has been none for several days. By the time the lintel is free again I have acquired another exposed end. It is the sixth, and at present it has no hardness at all.

From the court one could have seen what happened. The smaller animal had got its belly across the lintel but had not found the upright. The long animal was already on the ground. For some time the little one hung with its forepart doubled back under the stone, combing the bare underside with its pale fringe. Then its own weight took it far enough round for the fringe to catch on the crescent. There was very little left to do after that. In the powder at the entrance the two outward tracks run for a yard or so one inside the other. Where they separate, the narrower track has two quite even margins.

My new end hardens in the dark. There is a small nick in the crescent now, visible from below, and the first loose reach I make from the edge of the flake lies neatly in it. By morning there is a rib there, no thicker than a husk, leading round from the top of the lintel to the upright. The opening beneath is just as wide as before. Only along one side of it, where formerly there was an edge to get over, there is now a way of getting over the edge.
