At first I would look for myself in the red grain. It has a good place, with the shell leaning against it and a little roof of moving hairs overhead. When the shell shuts, it rocks once against the grain. There would be that to feel, even in the dark.

But when the fan opens again, I go with the water. There is one hair at the short end of the fan that does not quite shut in time with the others. A thread of water slips past it, goes along the lip of the shell and out over the stone. I would be that thread, or rather the turn it takes a little farther on. The stone there is quite smooth. Nothing in the look of the place would tell you why water coming out of the fan should go to the right and come back along the left. It does so because the pink mother is in the way.

When she moves, I lengthen. When she stops to pull at something, I curl up close behind her last pair of hooks. A few scraps of grease go round in me. There is one shaped like a torn ear. It passes the young one three times, showing first its bright side and then its dull, before a hook goes through it. For those three turns it would be possible to point to me.

By afternoon the sun has moved off the best of the ice and the light coming into the pond is green. The mother works her way up onto the dry crescent again. With her weight out of the water my two sides fall apart. For a while there is only water coming out of a shell and spreading until it is still. The young one stands with the bit of grease on its hook. It has eaten the soft parts and is having trouble getting rid of the rest.

Then it does the thing its mother does. It sets the other hooks and gives a little backward jerk. The scrap comes off. So does a scale of red stone, finer than the scale that holds the shell in place. It settles on one edge in the mother's empty track. Water from the short end of the fan strikes it slantwise.

I come round again, smaller now. I do not reach the shore. My farthest turn is under the belly of the young one, which has a soft black hollow there between the roots of its hooks. The water is warm against one side of the hollow and cool against the other. The young one lifts a row of hooks, then sets them down again a little farther apart. It rests like that a long time. Very likely it is the first time it has stood still without having hold of anything to eat.

There will be intervals when the shell closes and I am nowhere in the pond. I would not mind those. What I like is how little has to stay in place for me to come back: a flake tipped up, a shell that cannot go backward, a hair a little shorter than its fellows. Toward evening the mother comes down off the stone. She enters the water sideways, one comb and then the other, without so much as shaking the flake. The fan beats. I pass between the last two hooks of the young one and the first two of its mother, carrying a red speck out into the light and bringing it back.
