I would come in with a seed. Not inside it; the part that would feed me is on the outside. I would be one of the hairs in the fork at its blunt end, until the seed stopped rolling and the hairs that belonged to me got up and walked a little way about the shell.

A mite, with a red back. In that company I should be a flag, you would think, but there is so little of me to be red with. Even a very good eye would see a brown fault in the seed coat, and then have trouble finding the fault a second time. There is food in the fork, a gray nap that grows where the two lobes of the seed rub together. I have been eating it since the seed was green and up in the wind. I have never had occasion to go to the sharp end.

For a time, after the fall, the world would still be the seed. What would tell me otherwise would be the beetle getting hold of it. The knocks come through my feet with long easy spaces between them in which to lose my hold, get it again, and advance a little farther in the wrong direction. The fork is precisely where the shell will give. When it does, I shall be on the very edge of it. Under me there will be a white cliff, damp for the first instant of its existence. Then something with a foot wide enough to cover my whole field of view will tread on the cliff, and when it lifts the foot I shall go with it.

I think I would live for some days on the cricket without troubling it. There is a place where a hind thigh folds away into the side, not at the hinge but farther along, where two ranks of hairs come together like the bristles of two brushes put face to face. Chalk can't get very far in there. Meal can; it sticks, and in the warmth a little of it goes gray. It is a good place. There is a regular change of air. When the cricket feels its way down a ledge the brushes draw apart, and for a while I can put my front legs out into a room of quite inconceivable size. Then the leg comes home and combs me gently back into the dark.

I should go right across the bright middle once. A yellow lid opening, a hind leg coming free of its groove: those would be the two ends of the journey as far as I was concerned. For the cricket it would be a bad moment, finding itself on that bare floor with the wall a long way off. I would be busy getting back a crumb that had shifted.

Where you would actually find me, though, would be at the drinking place. The cricket has set a foot down to listen, and for once leaves it down long enough for me to walk the length of it. At the tips of the little hooks by which it holds the stone there is no jump to make. One surface runs into the next. I shall go off there without much more ceremony than I came aboard with.

The brown rim of the hollow is not a rim at all when you get down to it. There are upright stalks with clear knobs on them, and undermined places, and a skin of water stretched from one grain to another with dry passage underneath. In one place a seed husk has lain long enough for the gray nap to grow right out of its fork and onto the stone. It takes me most of the night to eat my way across the join. Somewhere overhead the crickets finish what brought them here. The burr stops. A little later a single loose grain of limestone comes down among the stalks, dislodged by a departing foot, and hangs there in the clear knobs without quite reaching the ground.

In the morning you could come back and find the hollow, and probably the husk. If you wanted to find me you would have to turn it over. I would be underneath with my front legs sunk to the first joint in the gray, bright red now against the white stone. Beside me there would be another red mark, smaller and rather paler. No, not a young one. My underside reflected in a bead of water. If I took one step toward it we should meet feet first.
