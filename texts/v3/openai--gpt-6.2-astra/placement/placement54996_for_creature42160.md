For a while I would be one of the backs passing under that head. There are two little taps, so close together they might be one, and then my neighbour gets them. I have a grain of something caught under my left wing case. I have been trying since noon to get a hind foot up to it. The head of the grub is quite appreciably cooler than the shell it touches. I move on, and it is still there, trying each back as it comes.

I think I would stay with the head. The hooks have a good hold overhead. It is possible to taste quite a number of things without having to make up one's mind about letting go. One shell has salt on it; one has the bitter varnish that comes off a wing case only just hardened; then there is one with a little orange meal packed into the long groove down its back. I get a mouthful. I have to follow it for the second mouthful. One hook comes away cleanly and the other brings a bit of the skin of the egg with it.

It is a considerable journey. We go the width of two beetles toward the daylight, then rather farther back. There is no meal left, but the groove is a good place for a belly as soft as mine. When another beetle starts to come up over us I put my head into the gap under its chin. For a moment I am travelling on the lower one and holding on to the upper one. They part. I go with the chin.

What I find, eventually, is a piece of fly. It is the front part, with both eyes and the little pad of the mouth, and a hole behind where the rest was pulled off. One eye has been crushed. I go in there. Much of the inside is gone, though there is still a tough white cord that yields a little each time I shut my jaws on it. The sound of eating inside a fly's head is very good. I can hear it even with the mill going. I get my tail hooks set against the rim of the broken eye and draw on the cord till a length of it comes loose all at once and I have to spend some time getting it into my mouth.

The whole eye is on the side toward the opening. When the sun comes low there is a patch of little lights on the inside of it. A beetle passes between us and the sun and the lights go out in rows. There is room by then for me to lie with my side against them. From the trail, if anything so small could be seen from the trail, there would still appear to be a fly looking out.

I would spend much of the winter there. Once the head rolls over with me in it and for several days the lights do not come on. I get too broad in the chest for the hole I came in by. This is no great difficulty; the edge of an eye breaks very neatly, a few facets at a time. I take out enough to get my head and the first pair of feet through. The loose bits adhere to me. A beetle passing quite close in the dark stops and works its feeler over my forehead, where I have unexpectedly acquired a little of the shape of the eye of a fly. It cleans the feeler and goes on.

By then I can reach out of the eye and feed without bringing my hooks in out of the weathered little room. What comes within reach is mostly shell. But one morning there is a soft grain, and beyond it another, and when I pull on the second the whole thing lurches toward me: the leg of the grasshopper, open at the knee. I put my head in. Something farther up the leg has its hooks set hard against the wall. I can feel the faint give and tug of it eating at the other end.

For once the mill has not yet started. Between one bite and the next I hear the small scrape my own hooks make inside the fly. I loosen one and set it a little nearer the opening. The other grub goes on eating. There is plenty of leg between us.
