I think I would be on the bench at the station. It is a good bench for waiting on, being a little too high. A person of ordinary height can sit there with both heels just clear of the flags and feel, through the backs of the knees, a goods train going through on the other side of the wall. I should like to be waiting there when it rained. There would be the noise on the skylight, considerably in advance of the first change of colour along the bottom of the sheet.

But a person on the bench would presently have to get up. What I have in mind stays there in the night, and is on the other side of the sheet, in the narrow space between the shade and the glass. There is room in there for the thickness of a match at the top and rather more at the bottom. From the platform it looks green. From inside, with the sky for a background, the sheet is brown in places, and the marks of the roller are quite plain. One leaf has been put in upside down. Its thick middle vein tapers in the opposite direction to the other three.

I would be the join there, where that leaf overlaps its neighbour. Not the whole long join. Most of it is as sound as when it went through the rolls. I would be the little way it has come apart at the lower end. In fine weather you could get the corner of a ticket into me, if anybody still made tickets of that thin stiff card. In wet weather you couldn't. The two edges swell and lie together and I am then a dark stroke about the length of a needle, with water in it from end to end.

There is a peculiarity about being made of the edges of two leaves. One was cut at a slant across a great many little veins. The other was cut very nearly along one. When water comes through the putty I feel it on the first side in a dozen separate places, a row of cool pricks, and on the other as a single pull that goes a surprising distance up into the dry. Once, after three wet Sundays running, the pull went as far as the mark of a thumb. Somebody had steadied the leaf there on its way into the roller. In all the years on the skylight that was the only time the thumb came up. It must have had a little nick across the ball of it. There was a short straight line among the whorls.

The three white buttons are on my cut side. I hadn't understood what the third was doing until the first hot day after it got into the paint. Formerly the two edges curled up together as they dried. There was a minute knocking on the glass, usually at about the time they brought the evening papers off the train. Now the edge with the root on it stays down. The other lifts away, very quietly. I am getting wider. Already a few grains of the red dust from the platform have worked their way in and are past coming out.

Up in the tower there must be hundreds of places like me, except that the water there never leaves off. I should have liked one afternoon of that, long enough to soften right through and find out whether the thumb went any deeper. On the other hand there is something here that wouldn't happen up there. Late on a clear day my loose edge casts a second, very slender shade inside the shade of the whole sheet. It falls across the end of the bench. When the parcels scales were taken away, their four feet left four pale rings in the varnish, and for a few minutes the little line touches two of the rings and the bench looks as if a child had begun to draw a bicycle on it.

That would be where to look for me, if the station were locked and you couldn't get in to stand on the bench. You can see both rings quite well from the platform door. You would want a dry afternoon; after rain the line is gone. I would be up against the other leaf then, taking in water, with the dust gone a fine dark red between us and the small root holding fast under its flake of paint.
