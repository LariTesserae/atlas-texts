I would be on the dry side of one of those old trunks. There is a length of it the water reaches perhaps twice a year, and in the hollows of the bark a little silt stays after each visit. In the silt are two halves of a snail shell, a piece of yellow petal gone transparent, and something that looks like four bits of straw caught crosswise. Blow on the straw and three of the bits go. The fourth puts out a foot to keep its place.

I would be that small. I think I would have wings, though there would be rather little occasion to use them. They would fold steeply together and have the colour of whatever I had lately been eating as a grub. For the first day after coming out of the water I would be green enough for my shadow to show through me on a leaf. Later I would be the colour of the split sedge, and spend much of the day among it. The swimmers bring in a considerable number of us in making a bed. When the sedge begins to dry we walk out to the ends of the strips and hang there in the dark, all pointing the same way into the small draught from the mouth.

As a grub I would have known a much smaller piece of the mountain. One palm leaf would have done me for several weeks. There would have been a great difference between its two edges, one in quiet water where the green felt grew long and collected grit, the other continually lifted and let fall, so that to feed there I would have had to keep the hooks at my rear end fastened and eat what came within reach of my head. I would have done well along the tracks of the snails. They leave a fine loose stuff behind them which is easier to gather than anything one can tear off for oneself. The arrival of a swimmer would have been an eclipse, then a dreadful few seconds in which the leaf buckled and the whole arrangement of things went by me sideways. Afterwards there was usually a quantity to eat.

In the trunk I would feel the double knock through all six feet. There would be no reason to do anything about it. The thing that would move me would be a much feebler disturbance, hardly to be felt even with my belly against the stone: the steep little knock given by a drop falling from the roof into water instead of mud. It would mean that there was standing water quite far inside the ridge. All along the bed the scraps of straw would begin to stir. We would go on foot, up over the old grain, through a split too narrow to admit the end of a swimmer’s nose, and come out on the back of the ridge in daylight. A rail watching there would have a fairly good meal. For a few minutes after each of us came through we would be able to do nothing in the open air but sit with our wings a little apart, as though surprised to have got there.

I would like the moment when the wind turns. Not that I would have any notion of the face below, or of what a long way the cool had come to reach me. I would be sitting low down on the stem of a grass with my head toward the ridge. The grass would turn me over. For the first time that day there would be nothing touching the sensitive hairs along my back, and my wings would open of themselves. I might go twenty yards or the breadth of the bench. Where the steam lay across the sedge it would gather on my wings and bring me down, and I would have to go the rest of the way walking. There are many of us to be seen at that hour going up the sloping undersides of the palm leaves, quite bright with beads of water and seeming to push them ahead of us as we go.

I would leave my eggs in a clean oval where a snail had been. They would be hard to see except for the last one, which sometimes sticks for a while to the tip of the mother’s body and draws out on a clear thread when she walks away. I would turn and work it loose with a hind foot. By then it would be nearly dark. The crown would tip a little in the current and the edge of the leaf would come down to meet the water, making a long low room with a green ceiling, just such a place as I came from. For a while, before finding my way back into the grass, I would stand there with my front feet wet.
