I would be in the room where the small one was wiped off. I would have felt it land. There is a slack place in the floor there, about the size of an outspread forefoot, and I live under it. Usually what comes down on it is a heel. Four feet and a chin all coming down together would get me up to have a look.

The floor in that part of town is the blade over the chamber below. It is thick enough to have an inside of its own. If you look in through one of the clear green streaks you can see a row of bubbles, each with a flat bottom and a little silver air at the top. Two or three of the bubbles run together. I would be the color of the division that used to be between them. At rest I would look so much like a division still in place that the interesting thing, looking into the leaf, would be the sudden disappearance of a wall.

I would have one good foot, nearly as long as I am, with a mouth at the end you wouldn't expect. The narrow end goes ahead, trying the way; the wide end comes after and eats. In the mornings there is a soft deposit on the flat bottoms of the bubbles. I leave a clear swath up the middle with a little ridge of deposit on either side. Going back, I can take one of the ridges. There are days of work in quite a small room.

What a heel does is press two bubbles into communication for a moment. A little air goes through with a sound like the squeak you get from your teeth on a new stalk. Nothing outside the leaf hears it. The old striped animals go by at intervals that give me plenty of time to get into position, and in the length of time it takes the sleepers to leave a bedroom I can go the other way nearly the whole length of the blade. The small one would be a nuisance in this respect. I might wait a good while under one of its feet only to have it take its weight on the other three and scratch its ear.

I would have a use for that small one, though. There is a part of the blade along the high side of the bedroom where nobody lies down. The bubbles there are the first to get the light when a muzzle strips a column, and the deposit grows thick and rather sweet. Once, following the downstroke of a small foot, I got through into that part. For several days afterward I was visible from the room below. A clear place as wide as a straw went to and fro across the ceiling, making square turns at the ends. A sleeper opened one eye when the light crossed it. By the time the light came back the sleeper had moved its head the width of a straw.

I don't think I would get out to the last roof. From inside a leaf there is no particular direction in which the town gets newer. There is uphill, where the air collects, and downhill, where the good stuff lies, and there is the red at the margin, into which one can push one's narrow end quite a distance without ever finding room to bring the rest along. But there would be a time every afternoon when the animals had gone to the square and a little warmth from the sun came aslant into my run. The air in the uppermost bubble would swell. Its silver edge would creep down the wall until it touched me.

I could go downhill very easily. Instead I would stay there and let the small cool line pass slowly over my foot, all the way from the end that feels to the end that eats. Upstairs, wedged between two backs in the late sun, a small nose would be getting its whiskers parted. There would be these two occasions in the town, quite unknown to one another, when a creature that could perfectly well move out of the way did not.
