I think I should be at the inland end of the crooked path, sitting on the bench with an iron boot between my knees. From the way people stop to ask me about the wind you might take me for the person who shuts the gate. I have nothing to do with the wind. My business is to find the path when one end of it has come ashore.

So long as there is water up to the porch, anybody can do this. You can feel with one foot for the place where the going gives out while keeping the other foot perfectly safe. But the land has risen enough at our end that the first sixty yards are over meadow. The bricklayer who set the porches facing one another would have been astonished to see how crooked two brick porches can come to stand without either of them falling down. A stranger going out square from our gate comes to the water some way to the left of where he ought to be. Usually he sees the mistake in time. In snow there is rather less to go by.

I have a boot off an old ditching suit, with the leg cut short and a lump of iron sewn into the toe. There are two ear holes in the leather and a cord through them. I lower it into the ditches that cut across the meadow. On the path it stands up, rather pigeon-toed, with the grass brushing its instep. Six inches to the side it goes to the bottom and comes up streaming black. A stone would do as well. The boot was my predecessor's, though, and it is a convenient thing to draw on the back of a notice when there are children about who cannot read.

Having got both edges, I put in two sticks with the tops peeled, then go on to the next ditch. In a hard winter the job takes very little time. Once we had a June when there was no getting a measurement at all; even the bottoms of the ditches had baked solid. The farmer let me cut a slot in her hay ground with a spade. I knelt in it nearly to my waist with the boot dangling a foot above my head. The dog came out to look and all the time I was down there it stood with its back to me, keeping the cows away. When I came up it went off without looking round. I was touched by this until I saw the piece of bread and dripping I had left by the spade, quite clean of dripping.

The work I like best is the round just before dark. There need not be anything to do. You walk down between the sticks and give each a shake. One evening I met the brindled heifer coming home without the boy. She had the halter on, the loose end wet where she had trodden on it, and no bell. I caught her and stood wondering what to do. There was a little wind out of the east, nothing to close for, but enough to carry the sound of a boy calling quite away from anybody he might be calling to.

Presently the dog went past us, out toward the middle. I had never seen it take the path of its own accord. After a while I heard its nails coming back. It had the boy with it, walking quite easily, one hand down. He had lost the light on the peeled sticks. He said the worst of it was knowing he could turn the wrong way on perfectly good footing, and go two or three steps before he found out. The dog had put its head under his hand. There was a dry flat place on its head between the ears where his mitten had been.

We stood a little in the porch. Then he took his heifer on up the lane to the byre; it was too late to be thinking of going home that night. I gathered up the boot and the dog came out with me again. From the water side, with the last light behind them, the sticks were black against black alder. I had peeled them all on the side I could see as I went out.

Now I go a little way farther, even on the evenings when it is raining and there is a good fire at home. I turn round where the meadow leaves off. The dog, if it is with me, generally waits there while I walk back, shaving a curl from the other face of each stick. By the time I reach the gate and look back for it there is just a double row of pale chips hanging in the dark, and the first two blink out together where the dog is coming in.
