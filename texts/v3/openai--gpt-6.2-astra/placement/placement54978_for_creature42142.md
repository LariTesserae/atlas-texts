I would be a moth for part of the year. I would come up in the western hollow on a wet afternoon, with a little green water shut up in each of my wings. Until it ran out I could go only on foot. There would be hundreds of us in the same difficulty, walking uphill with our wings trailing, meeting the ones who had tried that way and found a bead of water too wide to get around.

The first good footing would be the bill. Even when it is quite wet there are scratches across it in which a claw will hold. I would get well along under the overhang, past the place where a drop goes on getting bigger until the weight of a moth is just enough to bring it down. Then the light would tip sideways, and a dozen of us would be going down through the leaves without having opened our wings.

An old wader folds its neck in three. Where the last bend comes against the breast there is a space about the size of a shut hand. It is warm on two sides and has a leaf for its roof. Feathers grow into it the wrong way from either end and meet in the middle, their tips worn quite smooth by the passage of the head. It is here that I would get the water out of my wings. There is hardly room to extend one, but it can be done, first on the left and then, by walking round the shaft of a feather, on the right. The tapping one hears from above is not an attempt to get out. A moth with room to spare dries its wings in silence.

I think I would lay my eggs there. The roof is the underside of quite an ordinary leaf, with a good growth of pale hairs, but the hairs above the sleeping bird have little brown knobs on them. They take up what the wader breathes out. A grub hatched there can eat the knobs off a square inch of roof and by the following night have the same square inch to do again. In a cold spell the bird keeps its head down for days together. The grubs grow nearly clear then, with two brown lines in each of them, one getting shorter and one getting longer as a mouthful goes through.

It would be easy to put my whole life in that space, and I almost would. But on a fair afternoon, when the head came up with nothing on its bill and the feathers of the breast stood a little apart, I would fly. I would be poor at it. The air under the neck has no sideways movement to speak of, and I would set off for a seam a few feet away and fetch up instead on the common water. For a moment there would be two moths there, joined at the feet. Then a flap would shut. I would be on snow, right way up, with the print of my wings beside me rather than underneath.

There is no old bird lying down in that hollow. Toward evening a yearling comes out on the hard skin, lifting its feet much too high. With every step it takes, a little company of moths goes up ahead of it and settles behind. We are difficult to see on snow until we move. Presently the yearling comes to a place where nobody gets up. It stands a long time looking down between its feet. Then its knees come forward, its breast just fails to touch the snow, and those of us still able to walk go in underneath.

By morning I would be gone, one way or another. But there would be a few eggs on a feather, laid in a hurry in the absence of a leaf. They would go about with the young bird for some weeks. When it first stood still long enough in one place, the feather would shed them. That is where I would look for myself the following summer: not in the snug western hollow but a mile or two east of the last snow, on the underside of a leaf that still slopes a little from having had a breast against it. There would be one brown knob on a hair, perhaps two. For so small a mouth it would be a considerable breakfast.
