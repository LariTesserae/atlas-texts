I would live a little way out from the last trees, in a place where the flutes run across the yellow seam at a slant. I would be one of the older animals there. My hind plate would have a shallow groove in it, a continuation of the third flute from the end. Getting that groove the right way round would be the last thing I did before going to sleep. A small turn, no more than the breadth of a hair at the rim, and I could feel the air passing evenly over my back.

There would be one foot, well down on the left, that had never quite opened. In the soft mineral it would do as well as any other. On the flats it would make a different sound. The young would know me by it. Long before I reached the common room I would feel, through the arms holding me against the sides of the passage, the slight milling about that went on below whenever anybody came home with a full fringe.

My favorite hollow would be a poor one by most reckonings. There would seldom be more than six or seven husks in it. But the bottom would be so smooth that I could put down an empty husk and send it a little way up the opposite slope, and by the time it came sliding back I would have tried the next. On certain evenings, long after my fringe was full, I would still be there doing this. There is a particular pleasure to be had from having so many arms and a separate employment for each.

I think I would be the one whose door the root came through. At first I would scarcely lift a foot to clear it. Later it would be handy to catch hold of on the way down. Its underside would fit my foremost arm rather well, having thickened there a little each day while I lay in the passage. The difficulty, when it came, would be on the other side of me. I would get the groove in my plate lined up exactly, and still there would be a thread of moving air along my belly, where no air had been before. I would push a loose husk into the chink. In the morning it would be halfway down the passage, ticking against my bad foot.

For several days I would bring home particularly good husks. It would be easy to do; there would be leaves just outside, and the roots of four little trees holding the loose grit out of the flutes. The room below would never have been better lined. Even on the evening we left I would stop to straighten a husk that had come loose, pushing its dry tip in with one arm while another kept a small, quite round passenger from slipping off my back.

I wouldn't know how far we were going. At the first exposed seam I would put my shoulder down and find it too hard. At the second there would already be a plate flush with the ground. We would pass over it in single file. From underneath, our passing would sound for a moment like a great many animals coming home.

We would find a place before the light came up, though not a room; that would take several nights. For the first day there would be a slot just deep enough to lie in, all of us in a row with the little ones underneath. Salt would begin to lodge in the joints of our plates. In the heat of the afternoon a travelling husk would come to rest in the groove on my back. I would feel it rock there, folded end heavy, while all my arms were busy holding up the roof.
