I would have a bench in the passage between the oven court and the bleaching yard. It is a poor place to sit in winter. The door at either end has a latch, but a person carrying a wash takes the first door on her hip and lets it shut when it likes. There is sickness in the court sometimes, or a birth, and the passage will be bitter for three days on account of all the linen that has to go through it. The advantage of the place is that a wet thing brought to me need not be carried into the house.

I would mend trays. I think at first that I would make them, but there is a good deal too much wood at my bench for that, and very little of the right length. It is the corners of trays that generally come to me. A corner goes dark, and for several years is the best corner to take hold of, having worn to the grip. Then one morning a woman goes down the steps with three trays on her arm and leaves a piece of the bottom tray in her hand. It is important then that she should have a place to put the other two.

There is room at my bench. I have learned to keep one end clear. It took me longer to learn this than to learn the joints.

The boy comes to me the afternoon he has been under the bridge. He has got grit in the turn-up of one leg. He gets up on the bench and pours it out, a surprising amount, enough to cover a little patch of wood from which I have just worked a splinter. He wants a tray of his own. I ask what he means to put in it. Nothing. He would like to put it where his aunt did not put hers.

We have a look among the pieces. Most were cut by people who have no connection with our court. Once, taking out a peg, I uncovered a little blue star that had been painted on the wood before the hole was bored. The wood must have been something else, a cupboard front perhaps, in a house where someone took the trouble to paint stars inside a cupboard. It was a pleasure to me for the few minutes it lay open. I bored the hole a little wider and drove the new peg in.

For the boy we find the end of a flour scoop, curved at one side. It will not take a square corner. We leave it curved and fit the sides to it, and the result looks sufficiently like the toe of a shoe that he puts his foot in. It fits. He walks up the passage with one foot scraping and one going quite normally until a woman comes in backwards with a sheet gathered to her chin. She stands on the little tray. We have to fit the sides again.

I should like to say that I am a person who has always lived here. In fact I came over for the length of a baking, to borrow a stone with a hole in it for drawing a stave through. We had broken ours. I had flour on my arms as far up as the hair grows, and for a long time afterward, in turning up a cuff or putting on my coat, I would come upon a hard white streak from that morning. I was neither particularly young nor particularly unfortunate. The water had gone down. The people at this oven wanted their stone back when I had done with it, and as I had neither the stave nor any very pressing reason to have the stone, I gave it back at once. That was my arrival in Olt, though the place I had left was Olt too.

I remember, better than the faces there, the height at which I used to expect a latch to be. For years my hand closed on air beside the passage door. One evening, going through with my arms full, I hooked the latch up with my little finger without looking. It troubled me considerably. I put the things down in the yard and came back and tried to remember where my hand had gone before. Both heights seemed quite reasonable. Neither gave me the sensation of standing outside my own door.

On the next wet morning the boy takes his tray down. I go with him, since it is understood that a child may take a thing of his own to the water but may not follow it. His aunt is there already with the grey fans. He sets his tray one breadth below hers, on the place he found with his foot. The water comes. It fills the little tray very handsomely over the curved end. The tray sits where it is.

He is deeply put out. He has understood that lower is where a tray goes away, and here it is, full to the brim, with a pair of bubbles joined together in one corner. His aunt, having looked to her seedlings, comes and squats with us. She does not tell him that he has mistaken her, for he has not. On another morning the water would have taken it. She puts one finger in and breaks the bubbles apart. They come together again.

Across from us there is a wall of yellowish plaster with a low door in it. For a little while I attend very closely to this door. It has been painted lately; there is a pale drip on the sill. It might be any height at all, seen from here. Then a man comes out with a pan to scour. He catches the door behind him with his little finger, high up, almost at his shoulder. I feel a quite unreasonable satisfaction. I should like to do something for the man. He is too far away to speak to without shouting and has his sleeves rolled very carefully above the elbow. We leave him to his pan.

The boy in the end has to lift the tray out himself. The water running from the joint we made twice cuts a pattern in the grit, one deep round hole with a collar of little pale grains. He moves the tray and makes another. By the time we get it up to the passage it holds nothing but a smear of brown in the toe. He leaves it on the clear end of the bench, where after dinner I find his aunt’s sister sitting with her big cracked heels planted on the floor and both feet resting very comfortably in it. She has been out bedding kerbs since first light. One of the heels has bled a little. The boy fetches warm water, not quite a trayful in case it should come through at the join.

That is where I would be, I think. Not on the bank watching for the yellow wall to come back, though I can imagine going down there in certain weathers. In the passage, with the door banging and a woman resting her feet. She has put her hand out beside her on the bench and found the patch the grit covered yesterday. Without interrupting what she is saying she works at the grain with her thumbnail, testing to see if there is any more to come away.
