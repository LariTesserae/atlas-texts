I think I would have the room over the bath with the chipped blue step. It is a poor room for sleeping in on account of the waste pipe, but a good one for my work. It has a drying cupboard built round the pipe, six feet high and only a hand deep. I would mend books there. A wheel book that has gone into the water open will go on smelling of struck matches for years. Shut, it is generally only the edges that suffer, unless the keeper has put it in his pocket wet and sat on it for the rest of his shift. The books have a pocket shape to them after a while. One knows better than to press them flat.

There would be a little girl who brings me the books from the western reach. Coming up all those stairs she carries one on her head, which she has seen the bath women do with folded linen. There is a red line across the bridge of her nose from looking up under the edge of the book. She likes to stay while I take the boards off. Inside a board one sometimes finds a sheet of old freight rates or half a letter: paper already written on, pasted down writing inward where no one expected it to come to light. Once we find a comb mark in the paste where the binder has used his fingers. The girl lays her fingers in it. They fit, though she has to spread them rather wide.

It would be she who notices the eight. The page has parted at the fold and I am putting a strip of thin paper along it. She asks why the keeper went over the figure twice. I say that he must have been pleased about the calf, and she says that yes, anyone can see that, but why is the page rough there? Ink gone over twice ought to be smooth. We take it to the window. There is a little damage to the lower loop, a pair of nicks with the paper lifted between them, much as a splinter lifts out of a board. He has inked it in again after the damage was done.

I could be quite happy in that room. But I find that what I want to know is how the page felt while it was still wet, and for that I would have to be down on the ledge.

I would be the calf, then, but not on the evening when it first got out. I would be it the evening after. Getting out has proved a great convenience. It means one can get *in* whenever one likes, without having to judge how soundly one's mother is sleeping or how long it will be before she wants a drink. Already there is a particular place on my belly, a little behind the ribs, that expects the touch of the sill. When that place feels stone I kick once with both feet and there I am, rather farther across the ledge than I meant to go. Yesterday the keeper had to pick up his cap.

This evening he has the book lying open on the stone, with a nut off a bearing to hold the pages down. In the crease there is a shrimp. It has come off his cuff, where the small ones often lodge in the turn of the cloth. I can feel its heft, hardly more than the heft of a drop of water, shifting from leg to leg on the paper. My lip is still much too long for accurate work on a flat surface. I get the shrimp but also a good deal of something soft that sticks to my palate. The keeper takes the book away and gives me the side of his hand instead. It is salty in the creases. He runs his thumb once under my chin where the stone has rubbed the hair off, although there was very little hair there to begin with.

For years afterward, well after I have learned the water way to Derry Steps and grown so heavy that the young ones can clamber up my back without waking me, I would like the side of a hand offered flat. There are keepers who know this about me in chambers a long way apart. One of them has a finger missing and the lip closes over the space before it will take the hand. None of them put anything about it in the books. In one room over a bath there is a page held to the light, and two people taking considerable pains not to mend the little place where I have eaten.
