At first light the cable comes up out of the cloud, carrying a load of empty milk cans. They hang by their handles from a wooden yoke, with felt sewn between them so they won't knock together. A woman at the upper station catches the yoke with a pole and swings it onto the dock. From here the cans will have to go by handcart. The grazing shelves are on the other side of the basin, a good morning's walk, though in this air you can see a kid standing on its mother's back over there, reaching for a leaf she has missed.

The basin takes up most of the crown of the mountain. Its floor is a crowd of pale stone humps, some the size of knuckles, others big enough to carry a house. They have the smooth, slightly greasy shine of things long handled. Between them run gullies so narrow that a cart requires wheels set one behind the other. Down in a gully you lose the basin altogether. There is the smell of a warm axle, the heels of the person pulling ahead of you, and a strip of very blue sky. Then the track mounts a shoulder of rock and the whole country opens again: the grazing shelves, the copper-colored beds of an old lake, three dark pump houses miles apart, and beyond the far rim the snowy tips of mountains whose feet nobody here can see.

It ought to be cold. The first people to come up on the cable often bring coats. But there is heat coming through the floor. On the lake beds, where the crust is thin, it spoils the glue in boots. Even on the sounder ground at the station, the woman keeps her lunch on a hook well clear of the tiles. By noon there are two heats, one on the crown of your head and one through your soles, and very little air for the work of hauling a cart.

Most of the water comes up the west wall in the afternoon. From below it must look like a cap of cloud settling on the summit. Up here you see it arrive in separate passages: a gully goes white to the brim while an adjoining one stays clear enough to see a lizard halfway down it. The wet air beads on the shaded faces of the humps. For an hour or so each has a dry gold cheek and a dark one down which the drops follow old green tracks. In places the water has cut little staircases. Each step holds a spoonful before it spills to the next. A child can put a finger in the top cup and stop a stream six feet long.

This is the good hour for the animals. Flat gray crickets come out along the water tracks, grazing the green film with their heads all turned uphill. Finches work up behind them in loose ranks. They are the color of the stone except when they open their wings, which are a startling shrimp pink underneath; a hunting kite discloses hundreds at a pass. In the deeper cups live small black snails. Some of those pools never dry, and the rims are thick with the empty shells of generations, cemented down by lime. Everything happens within a few inches of everything else. A finch waiting for a cricket to move may have both feet planted on living snails.

The goats keep to the outside shelves, where runoff from a great acreage of bare rock feeds perhaps an acre of browse. There are tough little trees there with horizontal tops, clipped underneath to the exact reach of a goat on its hind legs. The herders lay flat stones over patches of seedlings, raised just high enough on pebbles to let the shoots creep out sideways. It takes years to make a patch that can stand a day's feeding. Looked at from the station the shelves seem almost naked. Seen close, every scrap of leaf has a history and most of the stones have been moved.

Late in the day the pumps begin lifting what has gathered in the old lake bed into covered tanks. Their motors make less noise than you expect. You feel one through the handle of the cart well before you come abreast of the square little building, with its locked door and the blue work shirt drying almost instantly on the rail. The return cable moves just as quietly. Full cans go over the lip in pairs and are swallowed up to their lids, then to their handles. Long after the dock is empty the cable keeps bringing cold drops up from below. They fall off at the first pulley onto a single dark spot in the dust, where, by evening, all the finches nearest the station know to come.
