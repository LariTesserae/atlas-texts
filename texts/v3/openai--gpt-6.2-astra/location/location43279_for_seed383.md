At noon the drains are the brightest things in the city. They are lined with white shell, and the sun, coming down through long rents in the roof of the valley, catches in them. From the upper stair you see the street plan written out in light, even in the uninhabited quarters: a great many olive-shaped enclosures threaded on three roads, all leading gently downhill. It is possible to walk for an hour toward the lowest enclosure and find, on arriving, that your head is still about level with the step you started from. The old river did nearly all its work sideways.

There is very little river left. You can cross it on a door laid flat. It goes under the door clear and comes out milky where the current rubs against a bed of chalk. Beyond that it separates into wet marks, each a little shorter than the last, on the floor of an immense dry channel. The city occupies one bank. The other is bare except for three horn-colored rocks which have been on all the maps as far back as maps go. On a cool morning each rock wears a dark skirt of condensed water. By the middle of the day the skirts have drawn up off the ground.

The houses have hardly any backs. A family will square off a hollow in the bank, close its mouth with a wall of shell and clay, and pave the ground in front of it for a considerable distance. Most of what goes on in a house goes on on this pavement. Here are the shallow pits for soaking hide, the grinding seats with their two polished hollows for the heels, the little runnels that carry wash water to the common drain. Tools are good flint, horn, and a kind of rough file made by bedding teeth in clay. A well-made file may go through six or seven sets of teeth before the thumb groove wears through. The hands here have had a long time to come to terms with the materials.

What you notice after a while is that the paving seems to be moving. Several acres of it may change from ivory to smoke gray in the time it takes to descend the stair. Then the gray divides and goes two ways round a house, leaving ivory behind. These are soft-footed shell animals, most no larger than a knuckle. They graze on a greasy, almost invisible skin that grows where damp stone sweats out salts. Turn over a paving slab and there will be a lip of this stuff, orange as an egg yolk, along the sheltered edge. Set the slab down and in a minute the animals have found the new edge. Their shells are flat and overlap when they crowd together; a hundred moving abreast make scarcely the sound of a sleeve passing over a table.

They have their hunters. There are long white six-legged things which lie in the joints of the pavement with just the tips of their forelegs showing. There are warm little blunt-faced beasts that sleep in the abandoned ovens and come out with their young clinging in a row beneath them. They turn the shells over with an expert cuff. Up on the bank a child sits very still with a hide bag between her knees. She is waiting for the blunt-faced mother to eat her fill; the little ones are untidy feeders, and what they leave is good bait. A district that looked empty from the stair turns out, at ankle height, to be in the midst of a complicated morning.

The rents overhead admit weather from widely separated parts of the surface. One may be shining blue while another, not half a mile off as the white birds fly, lets down a compact column of rain. The rain falls a long way. Much of it becomes mist, but what survives strikes one small patch of the city very hard indeed. This accounts for the curious breadth of the drains, and for the absence of doorsills facing uphill. It accounts too for the posts at all the street crossings. They are drilled to take stone pins. A person running with a square of stiff hide can pin it across a gutter in a few seconds and turn a flood into a cistern.

When rain is seen aloft, a shiver of activity travels ahead of it. People leave off whatever they are doing and run out onto the paving. Hides go up. Children pick the shell animals out of the pinholes. From above you can watch the whole western ward rearrange its waters in absolute sunshine: one family gives up its flow to another; a woman stands to her shins in white water until her brother two streets down lifts both hands; the children race a crooked little wave all the way to the public baths. At the same time the animals are streaming for high ground, the hunters cutting across their streams, and the birds turning over and over in the shaft of cold descending air. The only loud sound is directly under the rain. A short way off you hear the click of a pin going home, and afterward the minute swallowing sounds from beneath the street.

In the evening, when the rents are still bright but no longer send down any sun, the wet district shows blue among the dry ones. A few people carry stools there to enjoy the cool. Water stands in old heel hollows outside houses whose fronts have fallen in, and in the grooves of games nobody remembers how to play. Under the lowest arch of the baths a stone has been hollowed by overflow until it is thin enough to let light through. The bath keeper puts her palm behind it to see how much longer it will hold. Her fingers show quite plainly, pink in the milk-white stone. She leaves it for another year.
