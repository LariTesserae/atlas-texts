There is a flooded town in the sill of the east sluice. It came to light when a corner of the sill broke off in a frost. From the towpath the break looks like a patch of pale aggregate in the concrete. Kneel down and the grains resolve into roofs. They have ridges, gutters, little additions done in a cheaper stone. The whole exposed ward would fit under the heel of a hand. Much more of it goes back into the sill, below the level of the canal.

Water stands flush with the broken face, even along the overhang. At the bottom of the break it bulges slightly, like the water in a glass filled by an unsteady hand. Nothing spills. A flake of weed coming down the canal will sometimes stick there, shading a dozen houses until the current lifts it off. Inside, it is daylight. A person kneeling there can distinguish the cream of new masonry from the gray of masonry a week old. Light travels along the joints in the stone, so that even a lane which turns three corners into the sill has something of the look of an ordinary street at noon.

The ground is young. In the open lots it has come up in little tilted plates, with edges so fine that a thread of silt caught on one will divide in two. Almost every plate has already been built on. Where two lean toward each other, a flight of public steps runs up one face and straight down the other. Where they have pulled apart, there are narrow, very deep houses, mostly stairwell, with a room on each landing. The great extravagance is a square of level gravel near the front. Six streets end there. Under the gravel, visible through a place where it has been swept aside, cross six more.

The town keeps the canal gate quiet. On the towpath one hears an occasional knock as the big gate takes the weight of the upper reach. Put an ear to the sill and the knock opens out into a considerable event. There is the iron report itself, then something like a goods train passing, then the hard, cheerful clatter of crockery being stacked at great speed. In the town one can see it happen. A roof lifts the breadth of its eaves. The roof next door sinks by as much. Farther down the street a row of pale paving blocks turns over, exposing dark undersides. The blow travels from ward to ward, getting longer and less violent, until at the back of the settlement it lasts most of an afternoon and is just sufficient to swing a little copper door to and fro in its frame.

Not all the water moves at the same time. At a corner near the gravel square two currents cross at right angles, occupying, so far as the eye can tell, precisely the same depth. One carries white dust from a new cutting. The other carries rust. The two ribbons pass cleanly through one another; the white goes on white and the red goes on red. But a minute or two *before* they cross, both streams turn briefly pink. This is how to tell that a gate opening is due. Pink appears in the gutters, the loose gravel begins to dance, and all over town small things get clear of the thresholds.

There are no fish, nor any of the translucent jointed creatures one expects to find in a place so full of ledges. The things that move out of the way are shutters, collecting trays, shelves that fold into walls. On the north side of the square a few strands of green grow from a chipped tile. They lie flat during the passage of a blow and rise slowly afterward in the cold water. For a long time that patch was the sole way of judging which side of the square was north. Now there is a second patch on a roof opposite. In the still interval the two lean the same way, toward a thin joint of sunlight reaching down from the towpath.

By evening there is usually a new piece of ground. Today it is a white shelf under the lowest row of houses, hardly wider than a doorstep. Dust still hangs beneath it. Already a line has appeared down the middle, and along the line, at regular distances, minute round holes are filling with blue glass. When the light goes off the canal the glass stays pale for a while. One can make out the beginnings of a street there, going in under the concrete, lit well in advance of its first turning.
