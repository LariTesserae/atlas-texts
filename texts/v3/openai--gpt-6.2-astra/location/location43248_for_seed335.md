The roof is low enough to drip on the floor without making much of a splash. It is a single plate of slate, lifted at one end by a root that grew out from the bank and has since died. From the marsh you can see the dead end of the root, red as the end of a broken carrot, and a little patch of water that keeps clearing itself of duckweed. You can't see the room.

It extends sideways into the bank rather than down. For a room with so low a ceiling it has a surprisingly generous floor: a sheet of cold water about the spread of two hands across, with one stone showing above it. The stone is black when wet and pale green in the short intervals when its tip dries. Around three sides the slate has broken recently. Its edges are still keen enough to divide a hanging drop into two drops which run, trembling, in opposite directions. Along the fourth side there is old peat, soft and fibrous, with the compressed remains of sedge stems running through it like little bones.

All the green grows at the door. There is a lip of moss so thick that the outer leaves stand in daylight while the inner ones hang down into the water in a nearly colorless fringe. Through this fringe comes what light there is. It gives the water a green edge, very fine, as if the whole pool were the cut end of a piece of bottle glass. Farther in, the only thing easily visible is the underside of the slate reflected in the water. What looks like a deep, empty space is two shallow, empty spaces meeting.

There is a considerable noise in there. The tide is coming in under the marsh, not yet high enough to be seen in the grass. It pushes air ahead of it through a seam under the peat. For half a minute there is a low, steady running sound; then a bubble swells up under the moss, lifts the whole wet fringe, and lets go with a report. Silence. A thin trickle goes ticking over the slate. Then the running sound starts up again. Close to the ground it has all the businesslike insistence of a pump in the next room. Each time the bubble breaks, a little fan of brown water goes out through the duckweed. After an hour of this the fan has cut a clean lane nearly a foot out into the marsh.

In the intervals the floor comes alive at its margins. Freshwater shrimp unhook themselves from the drowned moss and swim out on their sides, pale jointed commas with black eyes. A flatworm pours over an edge without lifting any part of itself away from the stone. There are dozens of white springtails on the water, so small that until one jumps they look like flaws in the reflection. All this company leaves the middle almost entirely alone. A shrimp that ventures across it is visible from below against the light, and there is a stickleback stationed just outside the door. From time to time its head enters, abruptly and to a disconcerting depth, like a much larger animal putting its nose into a tent. Then the water rocks and the head is gone.

The cold lasts here well into the afternoon. Even when the marsh smells of warming mud, the drops under the slate are cold enough to sting a lip. By high tide the ticking and the pumping have ceased. The water comes level with the roof at the back first, silvering over one sharp ridge after another; the last air collects in a bright lentil against the dead root. For an hour the stickleback has the run of the place. On the ebb it must leave before the moss settles. The shrimp stay. As the roof emerges, each seam in the slate retains its narrow charge of water, and long after the tide has gone back out among the sedges the little room is still having rain.
