At the lowest point of the valley the cloud is underfoot. It goes past from left to right, fast enough to show through the breaks that there is another country a long way down. The valley itself hangs between two summits. Its floor is a broad saddle of pale stone, scooped into hundreds of shallow basins. Most hold an inch or two of water. From either summit you can see the whole spread of them shining, except where the land folds over at the lower lip, and there the brightness continues for a little way out into the air.

It is a cool place, even in full sun. On the dry ridges a hand laid flat will find the stone quite warm; reach two fingers over the edge into a basin and the knuckles begin to ache. There is no soil to speak of. What looks like mud at the edges of the water is powdered stone so fine that a wet batch of it can be lifted like a piece of dough. If it dries without being disturbed, it keeps the whorls of a thumbprint for years. The ridges between pools are smooth and brownish, the color of the inside of an old crock. A person could walk their branching lengths from one end of the valley to the other without wetting a foot, provided it was done in the morning.

Toward noon the water begins to remember the floor it used to have.

The first sign is usually a runnel going the wrong way. Water creeps up the side of a basin in a narrow tongue and pours out over a rim two handbreadths higher than the surface it has left. A few minutes later there is a sheet of water hanging slantwise across the basin, running along the slope of a rock face which wore away perhaps ten thousand winters ago. Underneath it the present-day rock is dry. You can put a hand into the gap. On a clear afternoon several ages of the valley carry water at once. A little fall drums on a slab, passes through it without wetting it, and drums again, more faintly, somewhere inside.

Not much water is needed to make a great deal of noise here. The long basins ring at different pitches. A rapid pattering at the upper end gets answered by a slow, hollow *tok* from the waist of the saddle. Under the eastern wall there is a stretch where the drops fall in such close succession that the air seems full of someone roughly tearing cloth. Then a patch of cloud crosses the sun. One by one the suspended streams slacken and come down, with the flat slap of wet laundry dropped on stone. For a minute afterward it is possible to hear the grit rolling in their beds.

There are small shelters along the margin, never in the middle, though the middle would have been the easier place to build. Each consists of a hollow picked out under a leaning slab, with a low sill of fitted stones to turn the wash. One still has its door: a thin flag pivoting on a lump at either end, beautifully balanced, so that wind swings it open and a trickle gathering in a groove on the outside swings it shut. Inside, there is room to squat, a niche the size of a lamp, and a line scratched around the walls at the height of a seated person's eyes. From that height, looking out, the confusion of shining ridges resolves into a single dark path. It leads to the next shelter. Stand up and you lose it.

There is nothing grazing on the ridges, nothing swimming in the pools. Even the narrowest sheltered crack has a clean mineral smell when it is opened. Yet the water has its successions, as a meadow elsewhere might. The first spill takes the loose white grit; a later trickle sorts out the heavier pink grains and arranges them in crescents; last comes a film almost too thin to flow, which leaves a brittle clear skin over the pink. A skin may last all season. In hard light you can see older crescents sealed beneath it, each offset a little from the next. The valley is slowly making a stone peculiar to itself, striped with the positions of vanished shores.

Late in the day the glare goes off the basins from west to east. Water returns to the lowest places and stays there. Out beyond the lip, one stream continues a while longer on the support of a vanished ledge. Seen from below it would be scarcely anything, a bright stitch at the top of an immense face of rock. From the last shelter you can look along its length. Its bed is clear, its edges minutely scalloped; a single pink grain travels down it in starts, lodges for a moment against nothing visible, then rolls over the end.
