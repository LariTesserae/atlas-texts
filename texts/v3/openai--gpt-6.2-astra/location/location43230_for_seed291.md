The tram waits while a little more ground comes up between the rails. It comes up edge first, a pale blue lip with a fringe of black grit; then it tips over and lies flat. The woman in the front seat gets out with a broom. There is a slot for the broom beside the fare box, and a place on the timetable to enter the minutes spent sweeping. Beyond the windscreen, under an ordinary sky of high white cloud, the whole plain is doing much the same thing.

From the tram roof you could see seven or eight miles of it. There is nothing high except the water towers, and some of those have sunk until their tanks sit on the streets like immense, short-legged animals. The city extends in a single shallow layer, sheds and allotments and brick courts, carriage works, a greyhound track, then more courts, right out to the blue distance where the new stone is too thin to build on. No two roofs agree on a slope. Here and there a street has closed up so tightly that the facing houses share a gutter. Washing passes overhead on wheels. At ground level there is scarcely room for a person in a winter coat to turn around, but from an upstairs landing one can watch weather go by fifty parishes away.

Rain is infrequent. Most of the water arrives already inside the stone. Break a fresh plate and the fracture darkens; put your tongue to it and there is a cold, faint taste of pears. It gives up its damp in the first day or two, especially along the edges. This is why the plain is green in lines. Ropes of clubmoss six inches through mark yesterday's breaks. Between them the old surfaces are dry enough for the grit to go skating in a breeze. The great growths are in town, where every yard of buried pipe affords a cool surface to sweat on. Cellar ferns push their crooks up through the gratings. Willow roots wear the shape of the nuts and unions they have grown over. Behind the municipal baths is a stand of cabbage so dense and dark that the hens which forage under it have learned to find one another by tapping on the stalks. In winter the outer leaves freeze stiff. A hen going in lifts the bottom leaf on her back like a little green door.

The ground has come into existence too recently to have learned drainage. A shower will stand all afternoon in the shape in which it fell, a dark oval across six blocks, cut off halfway up the steps of the post office. Then some adjustment takes place below and the water goes down in a gulp. The ditches on the survey maps are proposals. So is the river. Its bridges were built years ago and are good places to sell things out of the wind. Under South Bridge a man repairs spectacles; at North Bridge there are hot turnips in paper cones. Once, water went under both bridges on the same morning, and the schools were let out.

Much of the city's industry consists of making things fit for a while. Long low factories roll fresh plates into lintels, knead the still-pliable stone into sewer collars, punch saucers out of the offcuts. You can see the enormous rollers turning through the windows. The presses are bedded on stacks of old felt, because a blow transmitted to young ground will bring up a blister somewhere else. The first carriage works, using drop hammers, raised the floor of the maternity ward by nearly a foot. Now the factories use steady pressure. Even the freight engines draw their loads on endless belts, taking up the slack so gradually that a starling riding a coil of cable need not shift its feet. The result is a queer industrial hush. On a working morning, with every window lit and four thousand people on shift, the clearest sound in the east end is the clack of the turnip seller's tongs.

The busiest creatures are little rust-coloured voles. They follow the warm cable ducts in such numbers that the inspection covers tremble. Kestrels perch above the covers; children watch the kestrels to see which streets still have current. There are also long-legged white birds that attend the survey crews. Nobody feeds them. When a surveyor sets down his tripod, the three points of pressure will sometimes squeeze a bead of water out of the grit, bringing with it a blind pink shrimp. A bird can spend half a day walking gravely beside a man for one such mouthful. Out on the unfinished margin, where there are no surveyors, the birds stand in threes.

By late afternoon the tram has gained the western terminus. The conductor reverses the seats. Here there are neat new lampposts laid flat in the moss, with their bulbs already screwed in, waiting for a street to thicken enough to take them. Boys in red mittens are playing a game that consists of choosing a patch of cream-coloured grit and betting on what shape will appear. One chooses a stair. Another chooses an ear. What comes up is a low, perfectly round rim, a little smaller than a dinner table. They brush it clean with their sleeves. In the hollow a film of moisture gathers, goes silver, and takes the reflection of a passing freight belt, the underside of a kestrel, four red mittens held out flat. It will be frozen by supper. By morning someone will have found a use for it.
