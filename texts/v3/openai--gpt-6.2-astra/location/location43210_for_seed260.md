At the notch where the salt path comes up onto the roof of the mountain, there is a stone hut with the door taken off its pins and laid on the floor. The door is woven of broad grey leaves, six or seven plies thick. Standing on it, you can hear the small dry noise your sleeve makes against your coat. Step off it onto the floorstone and the uproar comes back, loud enough to make you shut your eyes. It comes up through the heels and the hinges of the jaw. The door must have been worth taking down.

From the sill you can see most of the roof country. It would be a day's walk across, if one could walk it straight. There are three long tables of black rock, each a little higher than the last, and between them are wide apricot-coloured beds of salt. On the far table, at intervals of half a mile or more, stand what look like heaps of dark bedding. These are the groves. Beyond them everything ends in a lip so fine that the light from below runs along it like a burr on a knife. The sky overhead is almost black even at noon. Down past the lip there is still a great depth of blue daylight before the first clouds begin.

The mountain is coming up. This is easiest to see at the hut, where the lowest course of masonry, laid level and fitted with considerable care, now has a hand's breadth of new mountain between the third stone and the fourth. It is handsome stuff, grey with rose-coloured grains in it, bearing on either side a perfect impression of the tool marks on the stones it has parted. There is no rubble. There are no fans of fallen grit under the cliffs. The corners are still so sharp that on the salt path people have bound the worst of them in strips of leaf, as one might bind the corner of a chest in a room where someone sleeps.

Out on the open salt the noise is enormous. A low note is sustained for so long that what at first seems to be a beating or a wavering in it turns out, after an hour's walk, to be the effect of passing from one part of the note into another. Walk back and the same slow beat passes through you in reverse. There are higher sounds in it too, a multitude of them, very close together, like the sound of a heavy platter still turning on its rim. Yet a loose flake of salt set upright on its edge will stay there. There is scarcely a breath of wind. In all that tumult nothing is being knocked about.

A grove up close is green. The leaves grow out of a single crease in the stone, sometimes a hundred paces long, and lie over one another in such abundance that the lower ones spread flat for want of room while the upper ones stand waist-high, curled at the tips, showing a fine lavender nap underneath. Deep down, where almost no light gets in, there are little pale leaves as soft as the lining of a cheek. Nothing eats them. Nothing nests in there. Part a handful and hold still and there is only the slow return of the blades against your wrist. They are cool, utterly dry, and heavy for their size.

The roots run sideways into places where the stone has not quite arrived. If you ease a loose plate off the end of a crease, you can see them: white fibres stretched taut across an inch or two of perfectly clear country, with the underside of tomorrow's rock dimly visible through them. It is possible to look farther in. The colours become uncertain; a vein that appears to be red when seen with the left eye is much farther away when seen with the right. The roots take their substance here. No one has ever found a spring by digging under a grove. There isn't even damp on the underside of the stone, only that clear interval, diminishing a little each year, and the patient fibres drawing it into leaf.

The leaves take up the sound too. In the middle of a big grove the quiet is so complete that you can put your ear to one of the standing blades and hear a tiny remote version of the whole mountain inside it. Cut the blade and it keeps the note. This is why the woven things left about the roof country differ so much in quality. A sleeping mat plaited from one cutting is tolerable. The door at the hut is better: its maker went from table to table, choosing leaves whose notes laid across one another and came, as nearly as makes no difference, to rest. On its underside, scratched into the tough ribs with a stone point, are six small maps.

There is a second hut visible from the first, a pale square on the highest table. The path doesn't go to it. It goes to a vacant place on the salt, where there is a doorstep, a stack of unused roof slabs and a little mallet with a split handle bound in fibre. From the doorstep you can see the hut very well indeed, even the chipped corner of its lintel. Put a foot over the sill and there is just the cold pink salt underfoot. The grove beside that far-off hut is pale at its centre and dark around its edges; out here beside the doorstep there is a hairline in the ground with one grey leaf coming out of it. Apparently there is time enough. Someone has set the mallet carefully on the top slab, where it will be easy to find.

Late in the day the light from below reaches the undersides of the leaves. For a little while the three tables carry their scattered groves like low banks of violet fire. Nothing stirs in them. The hut's long shadow reaches across salt that has never known rain, up a scarp with no worn place in it, and a little way out beyond the lip of the world. There, suspended over miles of evening air, the shadow shows the notches of a flight of steps. They will be shallow steps, closely spaced. It will be a good way down.
