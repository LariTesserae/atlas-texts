At noon the shadow of the pass lies across the upper half of the town. You can swim out of it in six strokes. On the bright side every leaf shows its little pelt of silver bubbles; on the dark side the same leaves are the colour of a tench’s back. The water is clear enough that the division looks drawn on the ground, until a shoal of fry goes through it and half the fish disappear.

The whole place fits in a tarn on a high saddle. There is bare rock around the rim and snow in one crease of it well into summer. Down here the rock is almost nowhere to be seen. Willow roots come down off the banks in broad sheets. Pondweed grows between the cords, moss grows on them, and in the deeper hollows there is a short lawn of quillwort, very fine and even, as if someone came through with shears. Only at the western lip, where water goes over into the next valley, is there a shelf of naked stone. It is grey, polished, pleasing to lay a palm on, and cold enough to make the bones of the hand ache.

There are seven courts to the town. They have the spaciousness of clearings, though there is no break in the green anywhere underfoot. A court is made by leading the willow roots around its edge for two or three generations. They are pegged with forks of antler while they take the bend. When there is enough wood, the court can be hollowed out sideways: a row of round-mouthed rooms for sleeping, niches for baskets, a long common room with a sill worn smooth along its whole length by people sitting there with their legs in the water. The rooms are as full of water as the streets. The inhabitants have narrow gill pleats behind their ears, rather pink in children, nearly the colour of the rest of the skin in adults. They sleep under loose nets so as not to drift out.

The streets between courts are wide for such a little place. Two people carrying a willow hurdle flat between them can pass another pair without anybody turning edgewise. There are low handrails for coming home in winter, when snow on the ice takes away much of the light. At each branching the rail has a different grip: one swelling for the upper court, a length of close-set ribs for the basket yards, a fork like a thumb and forefinger for the public beds of edible tubers. In daylight hardly anyone takes the streets at all. A woman leaves a room, pushes once against the sill and goes gliding diagonally over three roofs to visit her sister. The streets remain below her, formal and empty, with little freshwater shrimps standing on the rails.

It is loud here. The outlet pitches its water forty feet onto a ledge, and the ledge is part of the same piece of mountain that holds the tarn. The sound comes through the floor. Shut your ears and there is still that immense rough note in the heel of your hand. Over it you can pick out the knock of the stone mallets in the basket yards, where stems are being beaten flat for hinges, and a close, irregular rasping. That last noise comes from snails. There are thousands of them, some no bigger than a bead, all working their way across old wood with their mouths. A clean pale track means a snail has passed recently. A track already furred with green may date from yesterday. For conversation at any distance people use their hands; for a private word they swim close and speak against the bone just below the ear.

For all the noise, very little seems in a hurry. Trout hang in the blue interval above the roofs. A newt fits itself into the print left by a missing peg. Caddis larvae walk about in bits of the town: a shred from a shutter, two yellow seeds, a piece of a rush mat dyed red long after the rest of the mat has gone. There is a great pike in the northernmost court. In summer it lies so still under the sill of the common room that water fleas gather in the lee of its lower jaw. The people leave it there. It keeps the tuber beds free of the thick-lipped fish that root them up.

Every autumn one court is given over to the plants. Nobody cuts there or beats down a new shoot or scrapes a doorway clean. A year later it is opened again, and another court is left alone. This is why the town has seven courts when its people could live comfortably in four. Wood that is always being asked to go around a corner grows thin on the inside of the turn. It wants a season in which to put out a ridiculous straight wand, yards long, with nothing hanging from it and no purpose in its length. When the resting court is opened, these rods are cut and sorted; the best become the handrails in the other six.

Just now it is the western court that is resting. The afternoon sun reaches it last. Beyond its far wall the mountain falls away so sharply that, looking up through the spill of the tarn, you can see sky below the level of your feet. Nobody lives in the west rooms this year. Their doors stand open; long green strands have grown across them without quite concealing their shape. In the doorway nearest the drop a young trout is holding its place in what looks like empty light. Its fins make movements too small to see from the next doorway. The willow rod beside it has put on the length of a finger since the snow drew back, and points straight out over the valley.
