The morning rain leaves one wet street. It runs from the western lip of the basin nearly to the middle, a dark streak a dozen paces wide, with a little splashing still going on under the eaves. On either side the leaves are hot and dusty. You could stand in full sun on a dry sill and reach down into a shower with one hand.

From the lip you can see why it looks like a town. There are long attached houses with a common roof, little courts set back from the main thoroughfares, and, at intervals along each street, something very like a public stair: a fan of broad leaves by which you could climb out of the shade. There are whole districts of these. The older ones, low and dun-colored, fill the western end; over east the roofs are high, pale green, still pinched together at the ridge. Sunlight gets well down among them. It shines through a block of houses and lays the shadows of all their partitions, fine as the bones of a hand, across the next street.

Up close a house is mostly joint. Wherever two great stalks lean together, each puts out a flange to bear the other’s weight. The flanges curl under at the edges, making a dry room with a narrow mouth. Another pair of stalks leaning on the first makes an upper room. Down at the base the weight is prodigious. There are doorways down there no wider than a slit for a letter, with a cool breath coming out of them that smells of split green beans. The streets are where the stalks draw apart to let their seed leaves fall. Underfoot are years of these leaves, not dead but taken back into the plant, their veins running together into a tough ribbed hide. At a fresh fork in the road the hide is pink. It has drawn apart in neat parallel cuts; their lips haven’t even had time to brown.

What the view from the lip fails to convey is the noise. The first drops on the western roofs sound quite ordinary. Then a wet flange straightens, taking the weight off a thousand dry ones, and the noise of them slipping against one another comes up through the soles of your feet. A block settles with a long tearing blat. The block beside it rises, its folded stairs popping open from bottom to top in such quick succession that the pops run together into a shriek. Far down the basin you can see the sun catch the undersides of roofs flipping over, several streets ahead of the sound. There’s no wind to speak of. A loose curl of leaf skin hangs from a sill quite still while the whole eastern quarter goes off like a rockfall. If anything lived here that called to a mate, it would have to do it by touch.

The peculiar thing about the wet street is that it keeps its length. A drop a mile up at the lip and a drop here in the middle can run together. At first this is easy to miss in all the upheaval. Then, looking into a rain pocket no bigger than a thumbnail, you see two eaves meet above it: the dun one you’re standing under and a pale one you saw far off this morning. They touch with a little hollow knock. For as long as the drops hold together there’s a short way between the two places. The pale house sheds a handful of yellow seed leaves onto your feet. Yet if you climb the stair the house is still out there in the east, identifiable by the bare patch under its ridge. All the intervening roofs are present and full-sized and tossing in the sun.

This accounts for the patches of utterly unlike growth in the old courts. In one of them there’s a stand of the tall eastern stalks, scarcely thicker than rushes yet, with roofs the size of your cupped hands. Elsewhere a whole mature stair has come down wrong side up and taken root by its tips. Its broad treads lift a little when the ground strains; in the thin bands of shade beneath them grow minute white leaves that would scorch anywhere else. The plants need surprisingly little rain. What doesn’t get taken up at once hangs in the folds of the town, moving about from eave to eave, bringing quite distant parts briefly into the most intimate contact.

By noon the joins in the wet street are drying out. Distance comes back a house or two at a time. For a moment the basin is loud enough to make your eyes water as all that weight comes to bear on the local stalks again. Then the sound subsides to a steady grinding chew. From the western lip you can pick out the course the rain took, though the shine is gone: a new, crooked line of yellow leaves across the dun roofs. At its far end, in the fresh pink fork of a street, two walls that were strangers this morning are already growing a room between them.
