By noon the rain has stopped, but a long low shelf of rock is still shedding water. It comes out along a seam halfway up the shelf. For half its length the seam is simply a dark line; farther along, under the only bush big enough to cast shade, there is a clear, steady drip. A toad sits there with its belly in the wet grit. The top of the rock is already dry and quite hot.

From up there the whole basin is visible. It has a rim only in the sense that, very far off, there is a stretch of pale grass behind which the water disappears. Between here and there are miles of shallow pans, separated by broad backs of stone. Some of the backs are high enough to carry a little soil. Others barely interrupt the surface of the water; a breeze reveals them by the change from rippled silver to smooth brown. There are no loose blocks at the foot of the shelf. What wears off it now is mostly single grains, pale pink or smoky clear, lying in small fans around the seeps.

The water has a little salt in it. Where a pan has shrunk away from its edges, the mud dries gray with a fine white nap, and very little grows there. The green is concentrated at the seeps. A stand of rushes hardly wider than the shadow of a cloud can be the only one for a mile, and full of life to the point of uproar. Little frogs call from low down on the stems in the heat of the day. There is a dry rattling note, another like two damp pebbles knocked together, and behind both the sustained rasp of insects. Out on the bare flats one can hear two such stands at once, a near one in considerable detail and a far one reduced to a single wavering pitch. Between them there may be nothing higher than a sandpiper.

In the afternoon the wind freshens from the far end of the basin. The pans are so shallow that this makes a difference. Water advances onto the gray mud on one shore and draws back on the other, uncovering a strip the color of liver. The sandpipers follow it, leaving neat little groups of punctures over the buried worms. In a deeper hollow a dozen pale fish hold almost motionless, noses turned toward a trickle of sweeter water. They have been there all through the bright hours. Only now, with the glare tipped the other way, do they come into view.

There is a season when the pans join. It takes days of rain: first the fans of grit disappear, then a little thread of current finds its way over a stone lip, and eventually fish can pass from one end of the basin to the other through water that would scarcely cover a wading bird’s feet. Even then the old outlines show through. Silt gathers in the same bowls. The rush seeds wash up at the same few places where a root can find shelter and the salt will not come back too strong. When the water falls, the great expanse comes apart again almost exactly along its seams.

Toward evening the bare stone gives off a mineral smell as the damp settles on it. Birds stand well apart in the rosy shallows, each with a long reflection that breaks at the feet whenever something stirs in the mud. The insects quiet down with the cooling of the rush stems. For a little while the frogs go on calling just as loudly, and then there are gaps wide enough to hear the water falling under the bush, dark drop after dark drop into a hollow already full.
