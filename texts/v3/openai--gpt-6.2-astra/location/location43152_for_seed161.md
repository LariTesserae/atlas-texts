From one end of the town you can see the chimney at the other, though to get there on foot you must go down two ladders, cross a threshing floor and find the passage behind the ovens. The town occupies a scoop in the summit of a mountain. Above it hangs a lip of dark stone. Below it there is so much air that the big vultures wheeling halfway down the cliff look like flecks of ash in a hearth.

Almost the whole summit has worn flat. There are pebbles up there embedded in the bedrock, the remains of some riverbed lifted to this extraordinary height; pebble and matrix have been planed so evenly that you can pass your palm across the boundary and feel nothing. The wind keeps that upper pavement clean. Anything that can come loose has long since come loose. But just under the lip, in the scoop, the wind turns back on itself and drops what it carries. Here a little flour of stone has accumulated, and seed husks, feathers, pellet bones, soot. At the deep end a woman digging a posthole once went in up to her shoulder through good black earth without striking rock.

It is warm under the overhang, even on mornings when the people coming in off the summit have to beat feeling back into their fingers. A seam in the rear wall gives off heat. The bakers have the hottest section; farther along, old people have set their sleeping platforms right against the stone. The roof is low enough in places to be propped with lengths of juniper scarcely taller than a man. Much has been made of those props. There are shelves between them, cages hung from them, upper rooms slung crosswise on poles, and, in the crook of one forked trunk, a little clay-lined bin from which three households draw their salt. Nothing here has quite enough outside wall to be a house by itself.

Standing in the passage, you would call it a green place. There are fleshy scalloped leaves growing in split pots overhead. Long ropes of prickly vine hang down through the ladders; a hen that wants to get past one has to put her head down and burrow. At the outer edge, where the light is best, stands a row of squat fruit trees. Their trunks are thick as millstones and scarcely knee-high before they divide. They have been cut back and led sideways for so many years that each one can be worked from a stool. Under their joined crowns the daylight is olive-colored, very pleasant to the eyes after the glare off the upper stone.

You can look a long time before you see anything wet. There is no spring. Once or twice a year a bank of cloud comes high enough to bury the lip, and then everybody takes the wool screens out of storage and stretches them along the parapet. The yield goes straight into jars, which are sunk to their necks in the cool earth at the cliff edge and closed with wax. For the rest of the year the gardens live largely on what the town has already used. Even the cloth with which a baker wipes the dough bowl is wrung out over a root. Between the trees the ground is tiled with flat stones; lift one and you find a mat of white root hairs pressed to its underside and a smell of earth startling in that dry, dusty air.

There is a great deal of noise for so small a place. A hand mill turns with a heavy, comfortable *rruck, rruck*, pauses while the grain is fed, and takes up again. Sparrows breed in the ends of the roof poles. Their young make an almost continuous shrilling, to which the bats in the hotter cracks occasionally add a thin objection. Lizards as long as spoons sleep on the warm jar lids. Little red beetles go up the juniper props in files and come down with their jaws full of something pale. At evening the goats are brought in from the summit, where it takes a whole day of walking to glean a bellyful of wiry grass. For a few minutes the passage is all horns and elbows and the clappering of wooden bells. The cliff throws every bell note back. A child at the ovens can tell which goat has had a kid by which bell keeps stopping on the way home.

Late in the day a bar of sun gets in beneath the lip and reaches almost to the rear wall. It shows up old door-sockets in what is now the threshing floor, and the smooth hollow where a mill stood before the present mill, and another, shallower hollow under the ladder. Nobody remarks on these things except when something needs mending and an old socket saves the work of cutting a new one. The bar travels up the warm wall. For a little while every sparrow hole has a bright lower rim. Then the summit is still in full day overhead and the people below are already feeling for their cups in the dark.
