At the foot of the bottleworks wall, where a brick has lost its face, there is a little country you can cover with your hand. The floor of it lies one finger below the level of the yard. Sun comes in until the shadow of the weighbridge reaches it, about two. Even after that it has good light: pale cinders on the opposite bank throw a soft white shine under the lip of the brick. You can see the blue joints in the legs of a woodlouse, right to the last joint where the foot goes into the dark.

The bank is new. Somebody tipped a shovel of furnace sweepings there on Monday. Where the sweepings stopped short of the wall they made cliffs with loose brows of coke, bright chips of bottle green, and whole vertical faces of compacted red grit that have never yet had rain on them. An old brass washer stands edgeways out of one face. Its hole is a considerable advantage to anything coming up from below; the rest of the climb is all backward slips and little avalanches, but through the washer you arrive on a firm lip with room to turn around.

It looks dry from the yard. Inside, a warm iron line passes through the brick and, farther in, a cold one crosses it. There is always a bead gathering on the cold one. Usually it gets no bigger than a currant before something drinks it down from underneath. On Sunday, when the warm line cools, the beads last long enough to join. One fat drop rolls out along the mortar and falls in the same place every week. Here there is a well with a black mud lining, a raft of empty mite skins, and three blades of grass so polished by passing bellies that they hardly look alive. Nothing else green grows here except a lick of algae on the shaded side of the washer. By Thursday the well is a damp thumbprint. The animals continue to come to it and stand with their mouths against the floor.

Space is let very close. Under a flake of scale as big as a thumbnail, a rove beetle has to pull in its abdomen to let a smaller beetle pass. Pale springtails graze the soft fur on a twist of packing string. Mites ride in the furrows on the backs of other mites. A spider has drawn one strand across the washer, low enough that she needn't tackle everything that passes; most of the traffic walks over it. Now and then a springtail catches it on the jump. The spider comes down, wraps the catch, hauls it clear and goes back to her crack. The strand is a little higher each time. By evening the woodlice are walking under it.

Every twelve minutes the bottleworks compressor unloads. Out in the yard it makes a cough one soon ceases to hear. Down here the loose grains rise the thickness of a hair and settle with their sharp ends pointing toward the wall. This would gradually make the bank easy going in one direction and murderous in the other, except for a peculiarity of the green glass. In the moment of the cough, whatever is touching green glass stays exactly where it is, even if what supported it has moved. A chip undermined by ants can hang for most of the morning, held up by successive coughs and the crowded bodies that keep arriving to touch it. There is one such shelf over the well. You can see daylight between its bottom edge and the earth. The young woodlice like its underside; they lie there in a tight pale mosaic with their damp gills turned out toward the rising warmth.

Very little of this makes a noise. A grain ticking into the washer is quite distinct. So is the dry little tear when the spider takes her line down for the night. The loudest thing, close to, is the grass straightening after a beetle has climbed off: three separate strokes against the brick.

On Sundays you can put your cheek to the yard and watch the shelf come down. It happens by degrees. First the works go quiet. Then the well fills and the woodlice uncurl and go off one by one to drink. For a while one last small grey animal holds up the whole green roof, without any apparent effort, cleaning a feeler between its forefeet. When it leaves, the glass drops flat across the water. A dark wet rim runs out from under it into all the streets of the bank. By the time the weighbridge shadow reaches the wall there are eggs in that rim, and feet in it, and bits of red cliff floating away; and where the roof was, in a place no sunlight has touched since Monday, a patch of pale grit is bright enough to light the hole in the brick from below.
