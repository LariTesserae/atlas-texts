At the bottom of Sump Lane there is a court just wide enough for a delivery lorry to turn in, though none has been down in years. The way in is a flight of steps now. You descend past three generations of door sill: stone at knee height, brick at the shoulder, and, level with the crown of your head, the aluminum lip of a loading bay. Water stands on every tread except the bottom one. That one is warm, and dries from beneath.

Six buildings share the court. Their backs are so close that the fire escapes have been joined up with short lengths of grating. Above them the sky is a narrow patch of dull pewter; below, there is a pleasant greenish light reflected off leaves. Ferns grow out of the mortar in shelves, all facing the middle. The old parcel office has lost its roof and contains a willow. You can still see the counter through the glass, and on the counter the pale clean ellipse where a turntable used to pass parcels in from the street. The trunk has grown up through that opening without quite touching either side.

Under the paving, an electric pump keeps the water in the ground a little lower than the cellar windows. It has been doing this for so long that the court has taken its shape. Fine material has washed out of the fill; the big setts lean together in shallow troughs. Rain collects in one trough until it reaches the chipped corner of a stone and spills into the next. Each spill is about the width of a thumb. Down at this height the sound of them is distinct even over the pump, which has a heavy, sustained note like a sheet of steel being dragged across a floor in the next room. Put a palm on a downpipe and you can feel it. Lift a loose drain cover and the sound gets big enough to be frightening.

The pump delivers the ground water to a tank on top of the dye works. The works have gone cold, but the tank is still connected to the old distribution lines. When it fills, water comes out of unexpected places: a drinking fountain in a dark lavatory, a broken gauge on a mezzanine, the perforated bar that once washed a whole width of cloth at a pass. From the last of these there falls, for perhaps twenty minutes in an afternoon, an exquisite straight curtain across a window on the second floor. A broad strip of moss directly below stays apple green even in a dry month. Next to it is a strip of perfectly bare brick where the salt comes through. There are places in the court where a difference of four inches sideways is the difference between a garden and nothing.

No wings flicker at the window when the water starts. There are no shells under the leaves, no larvae hanging from the skin of the pools. In the parcel office a year's worth of willow litter lies in wet layers, the bottommost reduced by fungus to a black paste, the topmost leaves still holding little bowls of brown water. Nothing has stepped in them. What looks from the stairs like a flock turning all at once is the silver underside of the willow foliage: the tank has begun to empty, drawing air down through a vent behind the tree.

It takes most of a visit to notice that the paving moves. One pale sett near the middle rises about the thickness of a coin as the tank fills, then settles again under the returning water. Someone long ago bridged it with a brass strip, fixed at one end only. The free end has worn a bright little arc in the neighboring stone. In the dimness this is the first thing you see from the steps, a curved glint among all the greens and browns. It is tempting to take it for a bit of rubbish and come down to pick it up. Then the water reaches the chipped corner beside your shoe, the brass lifts clear by a hair, and for a moment you can see daylight under it.
