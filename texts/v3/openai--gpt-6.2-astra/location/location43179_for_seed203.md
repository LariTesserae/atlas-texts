From below, a pond is a green coin with reeds around its rim. There are three such coins in the sky over the eastern flats. On a clear day you can see the black commas of things swimming in them. The ponds are shallow and generally frozen, held up there with their mud and reeds and little freight of daylight by a raft of intergrown leaves. Beyond them the roof goes on in all directions, pale enough to hurt the eyes. Nothing hangs from it. The leaves keep their roots on the sunward side.

The flats underfoot are leaves too, though you would take them for slabs of sawn timber. Their grain runs uninterrupted for miles. Here and there a hoop of it stands up edgewise, still moist along the break, and in the opening underneath you can see another white-lit country, apparently as extensive as this one. Drop a shaving through and it floats a long while before you lose it. There may be a dozen countries folded underfoot. Nobody has found the bottom. Stone is known here chiefly from the heads of old mallets. A piece the size of a fist is worth carrying all your life.

The cold preserves the floors. Each summer a new tongue of growth slides out from a seam, spreads, hardens, and is already silver with frost by the time its far edge has finished uncurling. The old tongues scarcely rot at all. What they do instead is come loose. For a person standing well out on one, the first sign is often a sense of being mistaken about the ponds: surely the small one was on the left? Then the horizon acquires a second, straight horizon a little way above it. Two shelves are passing. The air squeezed out between them runs across the flats in a front so sharply defined that half a field of frost will lift while the other half stays laid out, every crystal in place. A moment later there is white weather to the roof.

These upheavals are surprisingly quiet. There is a deep rubbing note, easier to feel by putting a tool down flat and resting your temple on the handle. Sometimes a vein lets go with a sound like a green apple bitten in the next room. Otherwise the enormous rearrangements take place in the ordinary small noise of your collar brushing your ear. In the distance a ridge will rise blue and clean as a knife back. By the time its shadow reaches you it may have settled again, leaving an avenue of bruised yellow tissue where, yesterday, there was a valley.

There is very little to drink. Much of the brilliance comes from the dry, silky undersides of the roof leaves, which pass daylight from one cell to the next and spill it out at their tips. The brightest places are usually the driest. For water you seek a dull place with a blurred margin. That is where two leaves overlap and a little heat has been caught. Beads collect underneath; at intervals of several days they fall together in a shower no wider than a cottage. On the floor below, the silver skin is stippled with soft green cups. Each holds a mouthful, with a thin lid of ice you can lift out whole on the end of a twig. Around some cups there are tracks like stitches, made by the little six-footed grazers that eat the soft rims. It is possible to follow a set for an afternoon and find, at the end, a pale creature no bigger than the joint of your thumb, asleep with its chin in the water.

The few shelters are sewn down. Their makers cut two parallel slits in the floor, lift the strip between, and prop it on forked pegs, so that the roof of a house remains alive at either end. Inside there might be a mallet, a bark scoop, mittens stiffened palm outward on a peg. There is seldom a door. A house travels with its shelf, and a family that leaves to gather cup water may come back to find a gap a mile wide where the threshold ought to be. It is customary to leave dry bedding for whoever arrives instead. Some houses have not been entered in generations. Their pegs have taken root and widened, and the lifted strips have gone on growing until the shelters stand out on the plain like long green knuckles, quite empty inside.

Once in a while two inhabited shelves draw abreast. For a few hours people step across a crease with bundles under their arms, exchanging stone, news, handfuls of reddish seeds that will grow on a cut edge and make it sweet. Children lie on their bellies to watch the two grains of the world slide past one another. Then a line of white appears between them. Everyone steps back. Far overhead the three ponds are still bright, and a fish going round the smallest one interrupts its light at regular intervals. For a while, across the widening gulf, people on both sides can see that same small shadow pass.
