From the lip of the basin you can see nearly all the way across it. The far side is a dark blue nick in the weather, low enough that a bank of sleet can erase it. Between here and there are miles of wet stone. It looks like a good place to cross. Only when the light catches under the nearest slab do you see that it is standing on feet.

The animal is broad as a kitchen table and scarcely a hand high. Its back is real stone, a plate of basalt worn smooth and oval, which it carries in a fold of its hide. Under it, several hundred pink feet are testing the water. Other plates on either side shuffle to let it through. For a moment a lane opens, showing clean black gravel, a rush of silver fry, the ivory hook of something much larger fishing blindly from a hole. Then the plates fit together again. A mile away there is a similar slight adjustment in the sheen of the ground.

There is very little height left here. The hills have gone down to their ankles. Where a harder band of rock once crossed a river, there is a sill no higher than a sleeping seal, and the river, which once had a valley to itself, now goes over it in a sheet three miles wide. Rain joins it without making much difference to its depth. The upper water is fresh enough to freeze; the lower water carries enough salt to remain liquid in a hard winter. On still mornings a skin of ice comes sliding down the sill at walking pace, wrinkling where the animals breathe on it from below. There is no grass along the banks, no stranded twig in the ice. The soft brown stuff that gathers in the slack water is millions of shed feet.

You hear the deep works best at the sill. First a knock, felt through the heel. Then a sustained, rising whine, curiously even, followed by a blow so enormous and so far underground that the surface water merely stands up in beads. The sound goes on east, passing from one bed of rock to the next. Birds lift in strips as it passes. By the time the last birds rise at the limit of sight the knock has begun again underfoot.

There are no works to find if you follow the noise. In a few places the bedrock has worn through and you can look down, through clear brine, into what makes it. A seam of nickel the thickness of a fingernail runs between two masses of black crystal. Pressure on one face of the crystal produces a current; current through the seam draws brine into the gap; the gap widens until the pressure falls. Then several acres of stone shut at once. Nothing in the arrangement is remarkable except its extent and the accuracy with which one closure provokes the next. The basin has been running in this fashion for so long that the nickel is polished on both faces. At dusk the seams are visible from the rim as dull red lines, briefly lit in succession under the water. Snow on the rim takes on a faint brick color. It is the warmest-looking thing for a hundred miles.

The animals make their living between strokes. Pale mats in the gravel feed on what the current frees from the metal. The fry comb the mats with their lower jaws; the bearers of stones graze both fry and gravel and let the grit fall through them, sorted fine as flour. At the first knock every bearer sits down. The blow passes over a nearly continuous pavement of basalt backs, stunning the loose, soft-bodied creatures in the upper water. Now the birds drop. They have short wings and enormous orange mouths, and for several seconds the plain seems to have opened in thousands of places to show something incandescent underneath. Their cries are coarse and merry, audible even through the whine. In hollows too deep for the birds, the blind hooks go on lifting and lowering. On their tips hang little bunches of stunned animals like drops on the teeth of a comb.

Toward the middle of the basin the strokes come too close together. There is more movement to be done there than will fit into the time available. An old channel, almost invisible at noon, takes the excess. You can watch a flock settle on the water, fold its wings, and drift away, and then see the remaining part of its flight go pouring down that channel a little later: wingbeats without birds, dimples without feet. It is not an image. The passage drives sleet sideways, strips ice from the stones. Small white eels wait at bends for the swimming motions that come loose from the fry. An eel caught in one shoots forward with its mouth open, sometimes halfway up a gravel bank. On busy days the channel runs full to the rim with the unused motions of the plain. Beside it the actual animals move with a grave economy. A bird can open its beak at daybreak and spend the whole gray morning uttering one note, while four miles downstream a patch of bare water shrieks and flaps itself to foam.

There is a quieter hour after the sleet stops. The dim sky clears from the bottom up, leaving a narrow band of pearl along the ground and darkness overhead. The backs begin to part. In each widening interval the water reflects that band so exactly that the animals appear to be carrying pieces of night across a luminous floor. One has lost its basalt. It noses among the others, naked and surprisingly small, a knot of translucent muscle with its feet tucked in close for warmth. At the sill it finds a flat stone freshly uncovered by the river. The front feet lift one edge; the body slips under; a shiver travels backward as each row takes its share of the load. By the next knock it has settled flush with its neighbors. Nothing marks the place except a little cloudy water washing away, and the new stone, rougher than the rest, holding the last of the light.
