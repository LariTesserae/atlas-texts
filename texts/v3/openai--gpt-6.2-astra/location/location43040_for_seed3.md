From the western tram you can see the underside of the east ward. It is a dark shelf about two miles long, fringed with the little squared-off ends of streets. Drainpipes project beyond the lip. A woman has put her washing out on one of them; the aprons hang quite still. Below her, in what ought to be solid mountain, there are leagues of evening air, another mountain with snow in its gullies, and, farther down, a bank of cloud lit yellow by a sun that has not reached the town all day.

Most of the town is on shelves like this. There is plenty of room on them. The school and the nearest house are separated by half a mile of level, plum-colored stone, marked out for streets in white paint. In the middle of this expanse stands a working traffic light. Crows like the red lamp for its warmth and sit in a row on its hood, looking down with interest whenever the light changes.

The mountain is still coming into existence. This is easiest to see under the viaduct, where the town keeps a flight of iron steps bolted to the last reliable pier. At the bottom you can put your hand into a gray stuff like the grit in the bottom of a pear. A foot higher it is difficult to push a nail into; at the height of your shoulder it gives a clear musical knock. The steps have to be shortened from below every few years. There are dates on the pier, going back to the days when a man could stand here and look straight through to the southern stars. Now there is just one star left, visible down a long horizontal pore early in winter. Children are let out of school to see it. Some come back disappointed: it looks very much like the light on the signal box.

The rock comes in cool. It has no dust on it, no pebbles, none of the loose change of an ordinary hillside. Rain lies in beads until a beetle walks through it or somebody draws a finger across it, joining the beads into a runnel. The oldest such runnel, behind the tram depot, is deep enough to hide a pencil. People go there to pick watercress. It grows extravagantly along those few yards of water, and then stops, with a last fat leaf resting on the dry stone.

Elsewhere the gardens follow the district heating pipes. From above they are long shaggy green lines with a square of flats or a laundry threaded on them here and there. Ferns grow high enough to brush the carriage windows. In the damp beneath them live ivory slugs, brown frogs with gold eyelids, and thousands of small blue snails whose shells turn up in the lint traps at the laundry. On a cool morning the pipes sweat; drops gather on the peeling insulation and fall a considerable distance into the leaves. All along the line you hear the frogs calling in anticipation, starting at the boiler house and proceeding east as the fitters open the valves.

Beyond the last garden is a freight district with room for six hundred wagons and usually about twelve in it. The factories here make things the mountain is about to need: long sockets for the feet of buildings, ceramic sleeves through which a growing bed of stone can pass without taking a sewer with it, sections of stairway with the lowest tread already numbered. The hammer works has broad windows of ribbed glass. At each blow they flash a dull orange and the rooks lift off the roof, settle, lift again. The blows are loud enough to feel in a coat button. Between them there is time to hear a shunter walking the length of his train, testing the couplings with a bar, and the very different, hollow note of his bar striking a wagon full of cabbages.

At this height a clear day is dark blue almost to the horizon. More often a higher range, still only partly present, stands between the town and the sun. You can see its shadow better than you can see the range itself. It has the outline of three teeth and moves across the roofs so slowly that the shaded side of a chimney is green with moss while the other side is bare. In the middle tooth it is always about four in the afternoon. The municipal clocks run on electricity and keep decent time, but a pear tree planted there sixty years ago is still carrying its first crop, hard little pears with a blush just beginning on their cheeks. Starlings come every autumn to try them.

There are animals everywhere, though it can take an hour of sitting still to understand this. What looks like a seam across the pale north face is goats making their way home nose to tail. The black grains sifting upward past the gasworks are swifts feeding on flies from the settling tanks. In the empty lots the ground gives an occasional soft thump. Hares live in chambers which were bubbles when the stone was setting; they drum with their hind feet, and a hare three streets away will put its ear to a cellar floor and listen. At dusk the tram has to slow for them. They come out in such numbers that for a few minutes the unfinished boulevard looks paved in fur, all the long ears tipped toward the same faint noise below.

Passengers at that hour often stay on past their stop. At the eastern terminus the driver turns off the current and opens both sets of doors. On one side are the ticket hall, the florist, the wet smell of cabbage soup from the rooms upstairs. On the other is the clean edge of the shelf. Out there, just below the level of the car floor, a new piece of ground has begun to show. For years it was only a lavender discoloration in the air. Last spring a magpie landed on it and stayed. Now it holds a shallow puddle, two tufts of grass, and the reflection, when the sky clears, of a mountain that you still cannot see by looking up.
