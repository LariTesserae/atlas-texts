A rise of half a metre is enough to make an island here. On the crest the ground is plum-coloured and firm, with a nap like the skin of a peach. Down either slope the nap grows longer. At the waterline it comes up to the knee, stands perfectly straight, and ends all at the same height, as if the dark water had been poured in among the hairs and were holding them stiff. There are thousands of such islands. In the near absence of light, the only way to see one entire is by its reflection: a very faint grey from the sky lies on the water all round it.

The water is warm. Under a hand the turf is warmer still. Peel back the nap and there is a rind, pale on the inside, which comes away in strips with a smell of cut pear. Below that, everything is wet and very tough. What looks from above like a chain of islands is the exposed ribbing of a single immense leaf. It has no discernible tip. Its lesser veins divide and divide again, and the smallest ones, just palpable as raised threads under the rind, enclose districts of soft tissue the size of a fingernail. Water fills the hollows in the upper surface. Other leaves, growing up through splits in this one, roof over whole reaches of it. Under a roof the pools have no reflection at all.

There is rock, though it takes some finding. In one of the splits, a lip of black stone comes up so close to the surface that the leaf has caught on it and torn. The lip has corners sharp enough to shave curls off the wet flesh. The curls are still there, white, caught between stone and rind. The land underneath is young. Long low shelves of it welled up out of a shallow sea, carrying some of the sea up with them; the leaf came over them before the water had worn the corners down. Under all the plum-coloured country the water is salt. On top it is fresh enough to drink.

This is what the country does. Brine seeps up through the stone into the underside of the leaf. There, in ranks of little muscular purses, it is squeezed against membranes so fine that the water passes and the salt stays behind. The purses draw their power from a difference in charge between the hot mineral seep and the cooler brine. Open one and it goes slack; lay the two cut edges together and they draw shut with a distinct little tug. There are belts of these organs wider than some seas. The water they deliver goes up through the veins, out along the nap and into the air. Its weight returns as dew. Far down, where no air ever reaches, long white crystals grow into the fissures of the rock.

One might expect some tremendous noise. There is a sound, best heard with the jawbone resting on a dry rib. It resembles the last instant of a gulp, repeated at long intervals, never quite in the same place. No pair of adjacent purses contracts together. A full one waits for its neighbour to empty; the tiny yielding of the intervening wall is its signal. In this way a pressure wave may travel for months and nowhere lift the surface enough to disturb a pool. Above ground the loudest thing is an occasional drop falling from a roof leaf into a stand of hairs. Even that sounds less like water than like a finger brushed over cloth.

The open country has weather of its own. Mist stands in belts many kilometres deep above the wetter seams. Rain forms in it, falls a little way, and is taken up again by a warmer layer; beneath a belt of perpetual rain the turf can be quite dry. There is just enough light there to make out the little sister plants that inhabit the nap. Some are clear beads on stalks, containing a green so deep it looks black. Some lie flat and spread five blunt fingers over the rind. Nothing eats them. They live on what leaks from the veins, and where a vein seals they dwindle, until all five fingers together are thin enough to show the plum colour through. There are no wings in that air, no water boatmen hanging under the meniscus, no small quick retreat when two wet surfaces are parted.

For all the work going on, a view here scarcely alters. From a certain crest one could look across a pool at a shelf of leaf whose edge has folded under like a lip. Dew gathers along it. At one point a drop hangs large enough to bend the lip down toward its own faint reflection. It would be natural to wait for it to fall. But there is a hair growing up from below, too fine to see, just touching the bottom of the drop and drawing water off as fast as it comes. The drop has hung there since the black shelf rose. Beyond it lie another pool and another low crest, and beyond those, in a darkness hardly thicker than the darkness near at hand, the warm and level distances of the leaf.
