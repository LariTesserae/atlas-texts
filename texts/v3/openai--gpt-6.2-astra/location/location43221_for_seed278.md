For most of the morning the only shadow is the one under a lifted stone. The sky gives off an even, pewter light. Where the plateau falls away in steps, each step is the same color as the next, and the extent of the drop is hard to make out until a piece of weather passes below. Then there is a cloud snagged halfway down what looked like a low bank, and below the cloud a further reach of country, dim as the bottom of a drawer.

The top is mostly slabs. They have had time to lose their corners. Along the prevailing wind their upper faces are smooth enough to take the print of a wet foot; across it they are ridged like the roof of a mouth. A little black sedge grows at the downwind ends. Its leaves seldom get longer than an eyelash, though a root teased free of the grit might run the length of an arm. Once in a while rain reaches this height. For an hour the roots show through the wet grit as a dark, astonishingly fine net. By afternoon the country looks bare again.

Its reliable water comes from underneath. Air from the low country enters the steps through beds of broken stone. Far inside the plateau, where the rock still keeps a winter cold, the air gives up its moisture. It takes days for the water to work out along the bedding planes. A shelf can be bone dry on top and have, along its lower lip, a row of clear drops all of exactly the same size. They do not fall. The animals get them first.

There are so many animals here that for a long time there appears to be nothing but gravel. Then the light improves a little and the gravel has a grain to it: all the little long axes lie across the slope. These are shellbacks, scarcely the size of a thumbnail, gray with a rust-colored edge where the living foot shows. On the broad shelves there are miles of them. Several shelves farther on there may be none. They feed on the faint greasy growth inside the wet seams, passing the scraped meal down a groove to the mouth. The young are almost transparent. Through the shell you can see the dark green thread of yesterday’s feeding and, in front of it, the clear interval of a dry day.

Under a crowded ledge the impression is of having put one’s head inside a great piece of working equipment. There are ranks of soft pistons reaching up into the cracks, taking in water, withdrawing; little fans beating in chambers of horn; ribbons of spent grit coming out the back. The grit is sorted very precisely. Coarse grains fall straight down. The fine stuff clings to the feet and goes with the animals when they move on. Over centuries of short journeys they have carried the white fraction of certain beds clean across the dark stone above them. From the opposite rim these deposits look like pale causeways, widening and dividing according to the supply of damp. Up close there is nothing to mark their edges but a gradual change in what sticks to a fingertip.

Very little of this work can be heard. There is a sound if a whole shelf begins to move at once, something like a sleeve drawn slowly across a table. Mostly the shellbacks stay put. Other creatures travel among them: flat furred hunters which can ease a shell up with the wedge of a chin; little flies that walk all winter on six red stilts and use their wings only in the few hours after rain. High overhead a white bird quarters the ledges. When it settles, its folded wings trail on either side of its body and conceal its feet. It can stand that way until a careless movement brings a hunter within reach. A bird on a ledge is often the only upright thing between that spot and the curve of the earth.

Toward evening a change sometimes begins far out on the plateau. A band of stone takes on a dull pink cast. It might be the sun showing through, except that the band runs on under the shadow of a cloud. The air down in the steps has warmed; the first moisture is arriving at the lips of the shelves, and the shellbacks are turning their rust-colored undersides out to follow it. For an hour or two it is possible to see the shape of the buried beds, even where they dip and vanish and come up again a hundred miles away. Then the feet draw in. The pink goes out in patches. At the edge of the nearest slab one clear bead swells past its usual size, hangs there into the frost, and is still there, hard now, in the morning.
