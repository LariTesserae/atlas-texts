At the top of the pass there is a room of red tile. The way into it is through a gap you have to turn sideways for. Even before your shoulders are through, the noise is tremendous. You expect to find men at work on the other side of the wall, dropping great slabs flat from a height. There is the curious relief of being able to see, once inside, and then the dismay of finding almost nothing that will move.

The room is perhaps sixteen paces across. There is no way to take those paces in a straight line. Piers as thick as a waist stand hardly a waist apart, and the way between them doubles back on itself. Every surface is tile, including the saddle of the pass, which can be seen through slots in the outer wall for a little distance on either hand. The saddle has been laid in broad overlapping courses, like the back of a ridge made by a roofer. Where one course has worn through there is another underneath, a slightly different red. Nobody has got down to rock.

Up here the wind blows out of the same quarter all year. The western wall is several feet thick and full of flat passages which part and come together again. Wind entering any one of them has to go around a blunt wedge of fired clay. It makes much the sound you can make blowing across the mouth of a jug, except that some of the jugs, in this case, are the length of a man, and there are hundreds. The piers are hollow too. They take the sound out of the wall and carry it down into the pavement. The loudest place is quite low down. Set a heel on one of the big square pavers and the heel goes pleasantly numb; crouch to look at the joint around it and the noise is almost unbearable. You stand up in a hurry under a ceiling built just high enough to allow it.

There is a little light, the colour of the underside of a thumb held over a lamp. It comes from narrow roof slots cut on the slant so that the sun can never strike the floor. It is always warm enough to sit here in a shirt. The mass of clay takes a long time to heat and longer to cool, and there is so little night air admitted through the crooked passages that morning finds the piers still warm on their eastern faces. In the shelter behind each one the air is perfectly still. A pinch of white grit left on a ledge keeps its little peak.

Whatever the place was built for, the work did not require accuracy to begin with. On the sheltered inner lips of the passages you can still see where somebody pinched a tile to make it fit, leaving the two hollows of finger and thumb. Elsewhere the wind has done the fitting. For ages it blew powdered tile through every bad joint; the ragged edges wore back, the smallest channels choked, and the wide ones opened until the dust went through without striking either side. All the westward mouths now have the same soft oval lip. No new powder comes in. There is a palm-sized tile by the entrance with a corner broken off, and the broken face is as smooth and dark as the face that went into the kiln.

Nothing nests in the hollows. There is no green in the dim joints, no stain below a roof slot to suggest that rain has ever found its way inside. In the central bay someone once set a shallow dish into the floor. It is tempting, after squeezing between so many piers, to think one has arrived at a well. But the bottom of the dish is scarcely a finger below its rim. It holds three beads of clay too large to blow away. They lie in three little hollows of their own.

There you can put your hand on the last pier and feel what it is the others have been doing. The thing is thrumming steadily against your palm. Lift your hand and the sound does not alter; lean your whole weight on it and you cannot damp it in the least. At first you heard a succession of terrific impacts. Now you can pick out the low note of the floor and a higher one close beside it, neither of them breaking off even for a breath. It was the effort of your own listening that divided the sound into blows. There is no interval up here in which a slab could be raised to fall again.
