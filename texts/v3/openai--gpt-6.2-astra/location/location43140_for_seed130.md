From the tram embankment you can see three miles of wet enamel. A little light comes up through the inspection ports, red in some places, the yellow of weak tea in others. By it you can make out a succession of long, low backs with water lying between them. The backs are the crowns of buried machine halls. The tram runs just high enough not to throw a wake.

At the bottom of the embankment somebody once set a flight of twelve steps. Coming down in the dark, you can feel the first four distinctly. The fifth is a dip and a rise, and after that you have to know where to plant your heel to find any evidence of a stair. All across the district it is the same. The edge of a roof runs down into the lip of a gutter in a single easy curve. A line of bolt heads has worn down to a line of silver thumbprints. In the hollows there is something very like silt, fine enough to keep the whorls of a fingerprint. Wash a pinch of it in clear water and it separates into blue grains of glass, white porcelain and a smear of iron that comes away on your skin. There is no earth under it. A sounding rod put down through a drain will strike one floor, then another if the first has rusted through.

It is coldest on the crowns. Moisture beads there even in the intervals between showers. The beads gather, hesitate, and go racing off in thin lines, each one following a scratch made by some bead before it. Nothing grows on those swept surfaces. Down along the seams, a liverwort the color of bottle glass grips the rotten packing with its whole underside. Here and there it has put out little cups in which the rain stands. A drop falling squarely into one scatters green specks onto the next length of packing. This is how a patch travels. There are lengths of seam a mile long with green at one end and nothing yet at the other.

The noise is tremendous. Most of the halls are still in service. Slow motors drive banks of bellows underneath the enamel, drawing water out of the low wards and forcing it along a main to the higher ones. When a bank discharges, its whole roof becomes a sounding board. From the embankment the strokes seem to come from a distance; at the foot of the steps you discover that the distance makes very little difference. You cannot hear the tram going away. A loose cuff beats against your wrist in the pressure waves. At long intervals two adjacent banks catch the same stroke and a ragged patch of water between them stands up like the nap on a brush. No bird ever rises from it. There is nothing here that needs to get out of the way.

What look from above like separate ponds are in fact the ends of one pond, joined below the surface by a great waste of pipe. A gate lifting in the southern ward will lower the water all along a street in the north. For an hour there may be a dry road there, complete with a tiled center stripe and sockets where lamps used to stand. Then a dark line comes advancing from either curb. The stripe is the last of the road to go. There are dozens of such crossings. The only reliable way to tell which is open is to look at the inspection lights: a clear red point means the road is bare; a long red shiver means you are looking at its reflection and must go around.

Beyond the last of the halls, eight stacks stand far apart in the dark. They lean a little, all in different directions. Each used to vent a separate works; now the partitions below have worn through, and on cold nights all eight breathe out the same pale wash of vapor. It drifts low over the roofs and comes down again as rain. Little by little the rain carries more of the crowns into the hollows. Little by little the bellows lose their stroke, a fraction too small to hear in a lifetime. For the present they are loud enough to shake water out of the liverwort cups. Even on a night without rain, the green advances.
