On the survey plans, the streets of the high works are marked in pairs. One line is the street as laid; the other is the street as it will lie when the stone beneath it finishes cooling. The space allowed between the two is generally about the width of a handcart. In the eastern wards it is the width of a house. There are people in those wards who have lived at the same address all their lives and can remember when the kitchen window looked straight down a different lane.

From below, the works appear to occupy the summit of a mountain. It is a surprise, on coming up through the last bank of cloud, to find no summit at all. There is a whole hot country up here, a long shallow basin with a low rim, like the print of a thumb in wax. Beyond the rim on three sides there is nothing nearby at the same height. In clear weather the shadow of the western lip lies across a range of much older, snowier mountains. Within the basin the distances are considerable. A little yellow crane visible above the roofs at breakfast will still be the most conspicuous thing ahead after two hours on the tram.

The stone is new. In the unbuilt lots it retains a folded skin, with hollows underneath where liquid rock once ran on and left its roof behind. Nothing has had time to make soil of it. The occasional rain stands in the folds in strings of disconnected puddles. There is one ravine, much admired, in which water has actually begun to choose a course. It is no deeper than a boot. A footbridge of quite unnecessary elegance spans it, and there are enamel dates set into the bridge abutment to record how far upstream the head of the ravine had reached in various years.

Most of the city is built on feet. A foundry may have sixty, a bathhouse eight, a signal box just three: squat screw feet with broad ceramic soles, exposed behind little waist-high gates so that the leveling crews can get at them. Above these the buildings crowd close, sharing shade. There are cooler passages where you can cross half a ward without seeing the sky, passing the backs of switchboards, laundries, winding shops, a school with its sums still chalked on the slate. Then a passage opens onto a yard and the heat takes hold of your ankles. Even in a steady wind the ground keeps a layer of hot air to itself. A dropped brass key is painful to pick up if you leave it long enough.

There is no need to burn much here. The works lower closed loops of pipe into the hot hollows and bring the heat up where it is wanted. In the long casting halls they make the cloudy white ceramic used for bearings, pipe liners, teeth, washbasins, the soles of buildings. The same cup, blown as thin as an eggshell, serves as a lampshade in nearly every room in town. From the rim, at the end of a shift, you can see these shades coming on by the hundred behind the slats of the sun shutters. The great sheds themselves scarcely change. Their machinery moves by continuous pressure rather than strokes. Stand beside a wall and there is a faint running sensation in your elbow; step away and what you hear is usually someone's shoes, or the single dry tick of a shutter turning on its pin.

Water arrives chiefly from the sides. Cloud presses against the outer cliffs without quite spilling over, and ranked curtains of fine wire hang down into it. Looking over the parapet you see their upper edges, bright and dry in ordinary sunlight, and a little farther down they disappear into white. Below sight the wires are furred with drops. Each curtain drains to a gutter, each gutter to a covered tank. By the time water reaches a public tap it is pleasantly warm and tastes a little of the tank. Children hold their wrists under it on hot afternoons, less to cool themselves than for the startling sensation of something cooler than skin.

Nothing sprouts in the gutters. Where a pipe sweats persistently there is sometimes a peach-colored film, slippery as soap, and in a few inspection chambers there lives a nearly transparent animal with many small, deliberate feet. It grazes the film in arcs. A chamber keeper can go months without seeing one and still know its size by the width of the clean tracks. Out on the roofs there is no fluttering, no nesting in the cranes. A scrap of packing cloth caught on a lightning rod has much the visual importance a bird would have elsewhere; people watch it until they understand what it is.

The favorite hour for walking is late, when the sun has gone below the rim but the undersides of the wires out on the cliffs are still lit by light reflected off the cloud. The streets are crowded then without being loud. In one square people sit on the broad sole of a former pumping station, all that remains of a building already transferred to a cooler lot. The sole will not move again. Around it, very slowly, the square does. On its eastern edge a dark seam has opened between two paving slabs, narrow enough to step across. Someone has laid a white cup on its side there. For thirty years the custom has been to try it at the end of summer, and one of these summers it will fit.
