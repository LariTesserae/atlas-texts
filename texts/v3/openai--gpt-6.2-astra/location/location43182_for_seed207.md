Under the old coast road, where one of the shoulder stones has tipped a little toward the sea, there is a hollow about the size of a cupboard laid on its back. The way in is a seam too narrow for a hand. At one end a flat stone makes a roof; at the other the roof descends in overlapping flakes until there is scarcely room between them for a leaf. There are leaves all the way to the end.

It is a cold place. In a hard winter the cold works down from the road and the cold already in the hollow meets it, and for several months there is very little distinction between the ice and the stone. In summer the distinction amounts to a fringe of wet gravel along one wall. The rest of the floor is dry. A little water goes a long way here: a single bead, travelling under the roof instead of falling from it, can wet the tips of twenty plants. Where the bead finally falls there is a dish worn in the shale. The dish is shallow and broad, the work of a drop that has seldom landed twice in quite the same place.

At noon a thread of grey light gets as far as the dish. That is enough to make out what look like folds of green cloth heaped on its far side. The folds are the outer leaves of a single plant. Those underneath are nearly white, quite firm, with a fine crimp along the edges that keeps one leaf from lying flush against the next. Between them grow forests of a transparent moss. Its stems stand straight until they touch the leaf above them, then turn and run along its underside. If the outer leaves were lifted, most of this little country would come up in one piece. There is no loose covering of dead leaves. The plant withdraws the substance of an old leaf so slowly that by the time it lets go there is only a ladder of veins, useful for a while to the moss.

Behind all this growth are three black studs set in the wall. They belonged to the road. Once there was a succession of them under the whole coastal grade, put there to ease the strain of frost heave by passing a small part of it forward to a stretch of ground where the frost had already gone. What became of the rest is anybody’s guess. These three still work. A film of ice will crack on the dish, and a little later a grain of grit will jump on the far side of the hollow; between the two nothing moves. Around the foot of each stud is a collar of rusty green growth, thickest on the side that takes the strain. The pale roots of the big plant are bedded in those collars. So little daylight ever comes under the road that for most of the year the garden lives on the road settling.

There are no flies tucked into the leaf folds, no pale quick things in the gravel. A sound one might take for a small creature stirring is the shale drying after a thaw. Its flakes come apart with a succession of soft ticks, sometimes two in an hour, sometimes one in a night. In still weather the sea can be felt through the floor, though it cannot be heard. The moss tips quiver together and come to rest together in the dark.

Once in a long while rain finds the seam at an angle that lets it in. For a day the dish is a pool, black at the near edge and silver at the far one. The outer leaves take up water and gain enough weight to bend down onto the gravel. Where a crimped edge touches, it puts out roots. When the pool is gone and the leaf lifts again, a little of its green stays behind. This is how the growth has made its way across the floor, a narrow scallop at a time. There is still a strip of bare shale between it and the light.
