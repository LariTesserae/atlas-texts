Halfway down the stair there is a dry step. People leave their shoes on it. The steps above are wet from the seep that runs down the stairwell; the ones below get washed, every few minutes, by a wave that comes up out of the white glare. The dry step is warm enough to sit on. From there you can look into the whole bath.

It was called a bath well before anyone cut stairs to it. It is a hollow in the limestone, deep under a low ridge, about the size of a covered market. The bottom is a succession of shallow stone dishes with thick, rounded lips. Some hold no more than a palmful; the largest would float a boat, if there were a way to get a boat down here. Water pours into them from slots in the floor. One dish fills, spills into six others, drains with a sound like a sail tearing, and stands empty, steaming, while a surge works its way across the far end of the room. There are minutes when you could cross the whole floor on dry lips. It would be a mistake to count on having one of those minutes to get back.

The strange light comes from three narrow bars let into the ceiling. They are quite black. From directly underneath one you can see its black reflection in a pool, and, in the same pool, clouds going by in a blue sky. The bars bring down daylight from the ridge. Whatever carries it through forty metres of stone carries the little interruptions too: on a clear afternoon the shadow of a bird goes slipping over all those white bowls. The sun up top can be low enough to light the undersides of leaves while down here it still falls straight as rain. In the glare every flaw in the limestone shows, down to the pink threads of old shells in the stair.

The noise moves. At the foot of the stairs it is all pebbles, a wholesale clatter like somebody emptying a sack of buttons. A few paces farther in, the bass note of the big dish catches you in the breastbone. Water is passing under its floor even when the dish is empty. At intervals a bubble as wide as a table lifts the surface without breaking it; you see the ceiling bend across its back, and then the whole thing falls in and the sound goes past your face like a cuff of warm air. Yet a little to one side, in the lee of a stone lip, you can hear the fine domestic tick of drops falling from a fern.

There are only four or five ferns. They grow at the height where spray reaches but the waves do not, in pockets of brown meal left by generations of their own roots. Below that line the stone feels like a wet tooth. Above it the roof is chalky and comes away on a finger. A single pale crab sometimes comes out on the landing after a wash and fits itself over a round pink fossil there, so exactly in size and colour that it can be found again only by waiting for a leg to move.

Apart from the stair and the daylight bars, the only work in the room is a bent brass peg beside the largest dish. Once it held a gauge. The peg has been there long enough for the stone to grow a sleeve around it, cream laid over green laid over cream. Nothing about the rushing water suggests such patience until a dish empties close by and you see its inside: thousands of hair-fine terraces, each keeping its little level all the way around a curve. Even the loose pebbles have their places. A jet throws them up, they rattle the length of a gutter, and the backwash rolls them home.

Late in the day the clouds in the pools turn apricot. The roar goes on without the least recognition of evening. On the stair a wave climbs, whitening from tread to tread, and lies for a moment on the one just below the shoes. A few grains of sand turn over in it. Then it draws off so fast that the wet footprints of the last person out, preserved all afternoon up on the dry step, seem suddenly to be going somewhere.
