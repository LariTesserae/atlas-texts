At low water there is still six fathoms of sea over the highest part of the bank. It is highest by very little. A skate traveling along the bottom could go for a mile up one side and down the other without tilting its broad back more than a finger’s breadth. Yet the water knows the rise is there. On the flood, long rolls of cloud peel off its crest and go tumbling inland under the clearer water, heavy with grey flour ground out of stone.

The bank lies across the mouth of a drowned valley. The valley sides survive as two darker reaches of bedrock, so worn down that in places their only relief is the pale seam of a harder mineral standing proud like a vein on the back of a hand. Between them lies everything the ice let fall when it lifted: pink granite, striped green stone, black pebbles with white rings right through them. The stones fit close. Every hollow among the big ones is chocked with smaller ones, the gaps among those with grit, the grit with clay. A handful taken up here would come away in one piece and then, at a touch, come apart into all its different ages.

It is bitterly cold. For half the year there is ice overhead, never quite fast from shore to shore. A lead opens and admits a long green blade of daylight; before it closes, the light on the bottom has already gone, blotted out by the next field of drifting fines. There is little that grows by light here. In summer a brown nap comes over the shoulders of the highest stones. On the sheltered side of the valley a few red weeds persist, small thick blades grazed blunt at the ends. They lie flat one way for hours, stand up for a minute, then lie flat the other.

That minute is the turn of the tide. It does not come everywhere at once. In the old river groove the ebb is still pouring out while above the bank the flood has begun to run in. Between the two, the sea folds and shears. Cold clear tongues thrust into opaque ones; the opaque water climbs in soft towers and falls down through itself. A bed of coarse sand lifts by successive little jumps until its whole surface appears to be running, though not a single grain goes very far. Down in the lee of a boulder it is quiet enough to see a shrimp pick something off its feeler. A few inches above it, shell chips are going by too fast to count.

There is less noise than all this motion ought to make. No great surf reaches down here. Occasionally one stone tips in its socket and strikes its neighbor with a dry, distinct *tock*. The ice gives a long low complaint, difficult to place; one hears it through the rock as much as through the water. Then a long interval in which the only thing moving within sight is a fringe of mud being combed steadily off a ledge. Under the ledge, in a patch no bigger than a palm, a white anemone opens into the stream. It is such a small expenditure of living color in all that grey that the eye keeps coming back to it.

By the end of a tide a strip of pink gravel has appeared where there was black sand. A shallow pit has filled; another has opened just beyond it. The skate, if it comes back, will have to settle somewhere else. But from one dark side of the valley to the other the great low contours hold. They have very nearly reached the shape in which there is nothing for the sea to take hold of. All that immense restless threshing goes on in the thickness of a hand above a floor that has scarcely altered in a thousand winters.
