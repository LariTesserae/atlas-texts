The first thing to show where the ground ends is a fall of seeds. They go past your knee, pale and level in the wind, and then drop out of sight all along a line. A little later the eye makes out the lip of a shelf, and another shelf far below it, green as the inside of a split pea. Below that there is cloud. The mountain goes on down through the cloud for a distance greater than the height of most mountains.

Up here it has the shape of a watercourse from which the water has long since gone. Three broad valleys come together in a basin of dark, close-grained stone. There are islands in it. There are potholes with their grinding stones still in them, as neatly fitted as a hip in its socket. What little snow falls blows about for months without settling, except in a long white seam on the lee side of each island. The sky stays dark. Once in a while there is light enough in the west to see all three valley mouths at once; they look surprisingly near in the clean air. It would take the better part of a day to cross from one to the next.

Most of the living country is in the seams. Against the scant snow grows a ruff of plum-colored moss, then a belt of little willows laid out flat, with years of fallen leaves caught under their branches. Beyond these is a heavy growth of a plant with jointed stems and soft, deeply pleated fans. Where it does well, a single fan could wrap a sleeping animal. Its edges are furry with frost. On the underside, where no wind gets in among the pleats, beads of water hang without freezing. Small black grazers move from bead to bead, drinking with their forefeet sunk ankle-deep in the leaf. There may be hundreds in a bed of fans. A few steps away on the open stone there isn't so much as a gnat.

At intervals the whole growth lies down. It happens on one island and then on the next, too regularly to be wind. Under the basin are long chambers full of air, the remains of bubbles in a lava flow so old that the rock above them has worn almost through. Far below, cloud presses against the broken ends of these chambers. With a rise in pressure, cold wet air comes up through the seams. The fans fold over at their joints, fur outwards; rime gathers on them until they are thick and white as pelts. When the pressure slackens they stand again. A strip of living tissue down the middle of each fan warms for a minute or two. Water runs down the pleats into the willows. For that minute there is a smell of wet bark and bruised leaves so rich one might be standing in a summer ditch.

A whole valley can be at work like this, taking in vapor at one end and delivering water at the other, with hardly a sound beyond a tick as each joint goes past the balance point. There are old plants whose joints have worn smooth and won't catch anymore. These stay open through the cycle. Moths shelter on them, shifting round to the dry side as the air changes. A white bird hunts the moths by walking very slowly under the fan and striking upward through the leaf. In the oldest beds all the leaves are stippled with little triangular holes. Seen from beneath, against the faint sky, they look rather fine.

Once or twice in the long dark the pressure comes from the other direction. The seams draw air down. Then loose things all over the basin begin to travel: willow down, snow grains, scraps of moth wing, the loose skins the grazers leave when they grow. They slide over acres of bare stone toward the nearest green edge. The birds settle on their bellies. In the potholes the grinding stones stir in their sockets, not enough to roll, just enough to turn the wet crescent at their base from black to silver. This is the loudest thing that happens here. Even quite close it sounds like someone in the next valley shutting a drawer.

Afterward there are hours of stillness. Out at the shelf edge the seeds have collected in the lee of a stone no bigger than a knuckle. The stone is warm on one side. In the minute pocket of air there, one seed has already put out a white hook and gripped the rock. For a long way on either side of it the ledge is bare.
