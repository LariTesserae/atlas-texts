From the middle of the shelf it looks possible to walk straight out onto the clouds. Their upper surface is level with the pale stone, and in the glare there is very little to tell one white from the other. A bird goes down through what looked like a mile of perfectly good ground. Far off to the left, where there is a break in the cloud, the shelf shows its edge: a blunt lip of limestone with nearly a mile of blue shadow under it.

The shelf is the floor of an old hanging valley. It takes most of a morning to cross. The shoulders of the valley have worn down to long gray backs, and the beds of stone between them lie exposed like the grain of a very large piece of wood. There are hardly any loose rocks. Those that remain sit in sockets, a little too heavy or a little too well fitted for the wind to trouble. On a clear stretch the only thing that stands higher than an ankle is one's own shadow, thrown up against a rise.

The noise begins well before the edge. At first it seems to come from the whole surface of the valley. Get down on hands and knees and it separates into voices. One joint in the limestone gives out a steady breathy whistle; the next has a deep, almost furry note that can be felt through the heels of the hands. Air rising against the great face below finds its way into old water passages and blows out through the floor. There are thousands of outlets. Near the lip the combined sound is immense. Two creatures could fight there, shoulder to shoulder with a sleeper, without waking it. Yet a tuft of down on the stone six inches from a sounding crack will lie quite still. Most of the wind is underground.

In the bottoms of the shallowest hollows grows a short pewter-colored turf. Seen standing up, it hardly looks alive. Seen from the side, each blade carries a clear bead at its tip. The cloud touches the underside of the shelf almost continually, and cold damp works up along the bedding. There is enough of it to keep the underside of a flake of stone black and wet, seldom enough to wet the top. Little snails graze there, so flat that several can live between two layers that seem to be in contact. Some of the shining patches mistaken for mica are old crops of their shells.

Most of the animals live one ledge down. The first few feet below the rim are densely occupied: white-bellied swifts in the dry holes, black moths packed head inward in the damp ones, little dun-colored runners with broad bare feet on the narrow sills between. The runners eat the snails. Each has a favored flake of stone that it noses up just far enough to slip a forepaw under. At the oldest feeding places the edge of the flake is smooth and the sill below is pink with shell dust. From above, one might cross half the valley and see a single runner. Lean out over its brink and there are hundreds, all holding close to the wall, their pale soles flashing as they turn. They signal by lifting their tails. A voice would be of little use here.

The swifts do not range far either. They drop into the cloud for insects and come up into a brightness so fierce that for a moment their wings cast clean black shapes on the mist below them. Then they fold and are gone over the lip. Across the valley another white shelf carries another colony, too distant for the birds themselves to be seen. Only their shadows show, flecks passing and repassing over the same patch of silver.

Very little adds up to change. The same ledges stay damp; the same few sheltered hollows hold turf. A shell falls, a bird shifts its grip, a bead runs down a blade and is replaced. Even the immense chord from underfoot scarcely varies. In the lee of the highest stone back there is one small place where the rock cuts off almost all of it. There the air is cool, the sunlight warms the skin, and what can be heard, close by and quite distinctly, is a runner lifting its old stone and letting it drop.
