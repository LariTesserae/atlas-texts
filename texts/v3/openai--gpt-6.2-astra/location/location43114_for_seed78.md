The best place to hear the high country is a little below the rim. On top, the wind goes by almost clean. Six steps down, with one hand in the rootwork, you hear what it does to the underside of the plain. There are miles of loose bark down there. The smaller pieces clatter, and the great sheets lift and strike with a noise like a boat being launched down timber ways. After a while you can pick out the much smaller, nearer sound of an animal eating beside your hand.

The plains are outgrowths of trees. A tree here grows up the last few hundred feet of a mountain and then, on reaching the level of the steady wind, grows sideways. Its limbs divide, cross, take hold of one another and thicken at the crossings. Other trees do the same. From a distance the result is a set of long, nearly level tables suspended in the dusk, each with one black foot descending out of sight. Some of the tables have joined. You can travel a season over one of those and never have to climb down. From its furthest point the next table looks like a pencil stroke. There is room for a whole storm to pass between the two without wetting either.

Even on the oldest of them you hardly ever see anything that could be called earth. The close turf is made of the little upright ends of branches, each with its tuft of plum-colored needles. Kneeling on it releases a smell of cut pear. In the hollows a silver jointed weed grows through the turf and stitches it tight enough to hold water. There will be a pond the size of a bed, with a clear lid of ice and scarlet swimmers jerking about beneath it, and then twenty miles of dry needles. Water comes mostly on the bellies of low clouds. A table will comb a cloud for half the night and by morning all its downhill edges will be dripping. The drops fall such a long way that in the colder months they arrive below as little grains of snow.

There is very little distinction here between night and day. The mountains stand so far to the winter side of the world that, for much of the growing season, midday is a greenish paling along one edge of the sky. The needles turn toward it. A person coming out after sleep can find the hour by brushing a palm over them. They are exquisitely slow to turn back; on a cloudy day the whole plain keeps the time of the last clear place in the sky.

Life is thickest at a broken edge. A few winters ago two great tables, growing toward one another, touched and buckled. One has ridden up over the other. At the lip of the upper one the wood is splintered into pale blades still sharp enough to slice a boot. Sap has set in cloudy knobs on the tips. Little grey grazers hang there by the hooks on their hind feet and work at those knobs with their front teeth. They give birth hanging up. A young one is almost all feet and fur, and for the first few days rides tucked against the bare warm patch on its mother's throat. When a hunting owl comes up from below, the whole feeding line goes still, except for the babies, whose feet continue to feel for something to hold. The owl hunts by that slight scratching. More often than not it strikes wood. The old lip is freckled with pairs of white gouges.

People live on the lower of the two tables, in among the daylight roots of the upper one. Their houses are small even for shelters, woven low and plastered on the windward side with chewed bark. There are seldom more than three together. What one sees from a long way off is the pale wood of the ladders. They have to be moved each summer or the rungs disappear into the living wall. At one crossing there is a ladder so old that only its two lowest rungs are still clear; above them, a regular series of swellings goes up the trunk, and a child climbing it puts her feet exactly where her grandmother put hers, on wood that has grown over wood.

In the quiet weather after a cloud has passed, the people go out under the overhang with bowls to catch the thaw. For an hour or so it is quite companionable there. Water ticks into the bowls. The grazers whistle through their soft noses. Somebody chips a new point on a digging wedge, turning it often to test the edge with a thumb. Then there comes a hollow stroke from very far off. Everyone looks up, though there is nothing to see yet. It is followed by another, nearer and deeper, then ten or twelve almost together. The wind is coming across the undersides again. There is time to take in the bowls and to gather a child off the ladder. At the very last moment before the noise becomes enormous, the turf lifts a fraction underfoot, the way an animal’s back lifts under a resting hand.
