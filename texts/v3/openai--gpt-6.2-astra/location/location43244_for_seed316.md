The first rain comes out of the ground. Warm drops stand up on the stone, far enough apart that for a moment you could lay a hand among them and keep it dry. Then they join. Water goes rattling away over a plain that looks level all the way to the limit of sight. You can tell where the hollows are by the speed of the rattle. The sky is still dark and quite dry. It will be an hour or so before anything falls out of it.

Most of the continent is this low. What remains of its mountains is the difference between the depth of water over your toes and the depth over your heel. The stone is beautiful where it shows through: folded pink and pitch-black bands, cut across cleanly as folded dough is cut with a knife. One band takes the better part of a day to cross. Another is a hairline that goes along beside you for miles and then opens into a lip from which brown water pours. There is hardly any soil. Whatever lodges on the stone is rolled back and forth until it is fine enough to go down one of the lips.

Below the surface the old folds are packed with water. Pressure travels through them much faster than the water itself can travel. A fall of rain in one basin lifts the lids of a million little pools in another. Hence the din. Each opening has a note according to its width, from a shriek that makes the roots of your teeth ache to a bass you become aware of only when you try to swallow. In the rare intervals when a district drains, you can hear the next district going full tilt beyond it. The noise has a definite edge, like the edge of shade under a cloud. It comes toward you across the bare shining rock; a loose flake at your feet begins to jump; then you are inside it and can no longer hear the individual blows of your own two feet.

There is a kind of rush here, the color of a ripe plum. It grows only in cracks too narrow for a grain of sand, and puts out a single long leaf, which the passing sheets of water comb flat. A bed of perhaps forty plants makes a conspicuous dark patch in a morning's journey. Little white snails live on the undersides of the leaves. They have translucent shells through which you can see a fleck of purple traveling slowly round and round. All that uproar, and something has managed to make a living by scraping one side of a blade of grass.

Toward the middle of the plain there are shelters: three slabs upright and a fourth laid over them, with a low sill across the open side. They stand on no eminence and follow no discernible road. Inside, the stone under the roof is worn into two bowls where people must have sat opposite one another, knees almost touching. The odd thing is that the sills are dry. On either side the water may be racing deep enough to cover them. At the threshold it thins to the thickness of the skin on milk and draws aside.

Sit in one for long enough and you will see what the builders chose the spot for. Out in the wet, distances keep changing. At first it seems an ordinary trick of low light. A pink rib a mile off has the clarity of something within reach; a snail on a nearby rush is dim and blue with intervening air. Then a wall of runoff comes down the rib. It takes half the afternoon to get from one end of a hand's breadth of stone to the other, and in the meantime a drift of broken rush leaves passes from the horizon to the sill in a few unhurried turns. Inside the shelter, the distance between the two seats holds good. One person could pass another a cup. It is difficult, after a few days out on the plain, to imagine a greater domestic comfort.

By afternoon the vapour raised at the warm western margin has thickened overhead. The light goes the tawny grey of the inside of a mussel shell. Now comes the falling rain, so much quieter than the rising kind that you notice it first as a slight cooling of the scalp. For a little while the two rains strike one another at knee height. A ragged, nearly horizontal sheet hangs there, punched full of holes which close and open somewhere else. Beneath it the stone goes on emptying the weight of a distant morning; above it the clouds discharge the weight of this one. When the balance breaks, the suspended water drops with a slap that can be felt clear through the floor of the shelter.

At such times it is possible to see, far out, what looks like high country: great dark shoulders with pale streams running down them. They rise where two long pressure fronts meet. For a minute the water wears the exact shape of the rock that was ground away here, down to a split summit and a notch where a river used to leave the hills. Then the fronts pass through each other. The high country goes scudding off in two directions as low, fast ripples, and the actual ancient summit shows again between them, pink, wet, scarcely enough to stub a toe on.
