There is room for about thirty people in the crown of the horn, if they stand shoulder to shoulder on its rim. The rest is air. A nut dropped from the rim falls a long way in plain sight, then passes into the shade of the throat, where it may go on falling or strike something; there is no hearing the difference. Look out instead of down and the nearest thing at eye level is the white shoulder of a mountain a hundred miles away. On a clear day it has the startling definition of something on the other side of a street.

The horn is made of cream-colored casting ceramic, in sections bound with iron hoops. It is one of the old pressure equalizers. Far below, where men still work shifts, there are compressors with cylinders big enough to have inspection stairs inside them. Their common exhaust climbs the tower and opens here, above the weather. It comes out warm. It smells very faintly of hot linen. The roar at the lip is so complete that a man once worked there for half a morning with a torn trouser leg, unaware that the loose cloth was slapping the backs of his knees. But lay a thumb on the ceramic and there is a slow knock in it, regular and quite distinct. The gardeners time things by that.

The width of the garden is the width of an iron hoop, a little less than a forearm. It goes all the way round. There is nothing growing inward of it or outward of it. Inward, the glaze has been polished to the color and texture of a used cake of soap; outward, the air is cold enough most nights to split a wet stem. On the seam itself, though, it is always a fine warm afternoon. Long ago there was a packing of black felt here to allow the sections to expand. Roots went into the felt. Now one could cut a slice out of almost any part of the seam and find, in order, green leaf, white root, a thumb’s depth of rich brown crumbs, black wool, and the neat slotted head of a machine screw.

There are quantities of things in that small belt. There are ferns whose divided fronds fit together edge to edge like the fingers of clasped hands. There are little onions, very sweet, with bulbs that grow sideways until they come out from under the hoop and hang in bunches over the height. A fig has made nearly half the circuit in eighty years. Its oldest branch is flat on the bottom and round on top, exactly the shape of the handrail it has replaced. In its shade live copper-colored crickets, blind wool mites, and a great many minute snails with shells as clear as watch glasses. Seen from the access hatch the place looks uninhabited. Sit with an eye level with the fig branch and there is scarcely a patch of bark without something negotiating it.

Water comes up in the exhaust, but usually as a vapor that passes straight on into the blue. It takes a particular wind to get the garden wet. Cold air has to come evenly over the outer cheek of the horn and cool the iron through. Then a dark line appears under the bolts. The line fattens into beads, which crawl together and spill into the felt. For an hour or two the snails can go anywhere. They leave the crowded seam and travel out over acres, to them, of empty cream glaze. By the next day they have all drawn back except for a few late ones stuck fast by their own dry silver rims. These can wait weeks for the iron to sweat again.

The gardeners scarcely garden now. Mostly they come to read the ring. If the hoop pulls tight, the fig bruises underneath; if a section starts to lean, the water runs to one side and onions flower on the other. There used to be instruments for both these things. Their little brass feet are still bolted to the lip. In one of the bolt holes a pair of swifts bring up young every summer, flying in from an unimaginable distance with their mouths full. They enter underneath the roar, wings shut, and in the last handbreadth turn neatly upside down.

Toward evening a shadow rises through the hollow. It takes an hour to fill the bowl, then another few minutes to cross the seam. For those few minutes the upper leaves of the fig are yellow in the sun while its trunk is already blue. Nothing in the sound of the place changes. The crickets begin to sing anyway. You can tell by laying a fingertip lightly on the branch: below, the knock that comes up through miles of pipe; above it, almost too fine to feel, the small persistent work of the wings.
