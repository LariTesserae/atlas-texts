From the stair you can see practically the whole town: two galleries of houses facing one another across a long court, a pump house at the blind end, and halfway down the court a solitary tree. The tree is the odd thing. It has the shape of something in a child’s drawing, a trunk and six branches and a little head of leaves. Everything else you can see is tree too, but you have to be told.

The pavement, for instance, is a single exposed root, broad enough for two carts to pass. It gives a little under a heel. Years of wheels have polished two tracks into it, dark red with a fine gold figuring, like the rails of a very old banister. The houses are hollows in the thickness of the same root where it turns up at the sides. Each has a sill, a door that can be drawn across on brass runners, and a little sloping cut to turn the rain away. There is a great deal of unoccupied pavement. In the middle of the morning a woman might cross it with a spanner in her hand, and it takes so long for her to get from one gallery to the other that you find yourself watching to see which door she will choose.

All this lies a few yards under a disused freight yard. There is still a weighbridge up there; its dial, minus the works, is the round window through which the little tree gets its light. In the afternoon the numerals fall enormous and rather fuzzy on the court. A leaf turning in the light can knock out half a ton.

It sounds, from the stair, as though a train were standing down here with every carriage full of birds. Most of that sound comes from inside the walls. Between the hard outer wood and the wet wood that carries the sap there is a layer a hand deep of little jointed animals. You see them wherever a door has recently been widened: ivory backs sliding over ivory backs, legs folded close, each animal passing a fleck of chewed fibre forward under its belly to the next. The high note is the rubbing of the back plates. Under it there is a steady frying sound made by the feet. A householder can lean an ear against a partition and hear, quite distinctly, where a knot has divided the traffic into two streams.

Much larger things live in the galleries overhead. What look like old grey washcloths hung out along the steam pipes are folded wings. The things that own them sleep muzzle to muzzle, several hundred to a pipe, shifting sideways in their sleep to follow the warmth. Now and then one drops, opens to the size of a tea tray, takes something out of the air with an audible snap, and folds itself back into the row. The air looks perfectly empty until you stand in the shaft of the weighbridge window. There are transparent flies in it in such numbers that the shadow of the dial trembles.

The town makes packing felt. At the blind end the tree puts out a soft woolly bark which has to be cropped or it will choke the pump intakes. It comes off in great warm blankets full of indignant red mites. A combing engine shakes these out into a return chute; then rollers beat and lap the fibre, and a cutter stamps out pads for the axle boxes of freight wagons. There hasn’t been a wagon on the weighbridge in living memory, but the pads go up the stair in bundles and somebody buys them. The engine is an old electric one, enamelled green, its feet sunk almost out of sight in the floor. The tree grows fastest where it is shaken. Every few years a fitter has to cut a new access hole to the oil cup.

Rain is an event here, and has very little to do with the weather in the yard. The pumps take warm water out of the low wood and send it up through cooling pipes laid against the underside of the weighbridge. For most of a shift nothing falls. Then a drop hits the little tree, another hits the pump house roof, and within a minute there is a narrow, violent shower at the blind end of the court. The air smells of cut pear. Children come to put their bare feet in the wheel tracks, which carry the water the length of the town in two shining cords. No one bothers to shut a door. The sills have been sloping the right way for two hundred years.

With the first drop the pavement opens its pores. Out come the drinking animals, which have been there all along: a stipple at first, then a nap of fine brown heads bending downstream. The grey fliers leave their pipes all at once. For a little while it is impossible to see the opposite gallery. You hear shutters banging, wings brushing wood, the engine beating on behind it all, and the surprisingly sweet whistles with which the fliers keep from striking one another in the dark.

Then the cooling valve seats with a knock. The tracks drain, the nap goes flat, and the last few fliers settle on the pipes with their fur sticking up in wet points. Across the broad red floor the woman with the spanner comes into view again. She has been standing under the little tree, in the one place in town where, for a minute after the rain is over, it is still raining.
