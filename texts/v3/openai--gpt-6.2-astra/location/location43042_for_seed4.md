At low water there is a place under the willow where a hand will go in as far as the wrist. The bank looks torn there. Two roots have grown together around a wedge of river ice, and when the wedge came out it took a plug of rotten wood with it. The hole still has corners. Reach a little way in and the whole cold floor gives under a fingertip, then slowly comes back up. It would be easy to put a finger through the market.

The market is the widest of six clearings in the root. Nothing has been dug out to make them. These are the spaces left between sheets of living wood, much as a space is left between the palms when two hands are loosely folded. The streets follow the grain. Where the grain forks, there will be a stair; where it knots, a little roundabout with so sharp a turn that the longer barrows have to be unhitched and brought around end first. In all six clearings together there are scarcely a dozen houses. A house is a considerable commitment here. Leave a post in one place long enough and it takes, and however badly it obstructs the way, no one likes to cut down a post that has come into leaf.

It is dark except on mornings when there is snow on the opposite bank. Then for perhaps half an hour a dirty silver light comes in sideways, enough to show that the floor of the market is pink. Everywhere else it is the color of the inside of a peeled twig. There are no lamps. The pavement has been scored with a bone comb while soft, so the turnings can be read underfoot. One groove goes down to the water; three go up to the dry beds. Across the mouths of blind alleys the comb has been drawn crosswise. These marks must be renewed often. The wood is always trying to close a scratch.

There is water below every street, though the river itself seldom enters. Water wells out of the walls, wonderfully clear and so cold it scarcely seems to wet anything at first. It stands up in beads on the cut ends of fibers. When the beads touch, they run together and go down all at once. A whole gutter may fill to its brim and empty in the time it takes to turn one's head. The gutters are lined with the green skins of rushes, pegged at the edges with thorns. At the low ends are little settling yards where the water spreads out and goes quiet, and where anything it has carried can be picked over before the next run. Most of what it carries is alive.

Indeed the apparent vacancy of the place is a trick of its surfaces. What looks like nap on the underside of a bridge is thousands of pale animals hanging by their hind feet. A white seam in the curb is a file of eggs, each with a black speck inside that turns independently of the rest. Once, when a piece of market pavement lifted at one corner, the space beneath it was found to be packed solid with translucent shrimp. The people working there put the pavement down again very gently. They had been standing all winter on a layer of eyes.

The animals make very little noise. They are soft, and the things they strike are soft. Now and then there is a click like a seed cracking between teeth: the jaws of a hunter closing on an empty stretch of gutter just after the water has gone by. Far overhead there is one louder sound. A duck walking on the root makes a deep, deliberate double knock, first the pad of the foot and then the settling of its weight. Work stops for this. There have been ducks that knew how to listen back.

The busiest public work is at the rear of the last clearing, farthest from the river. A new root tip is coming through the ceiling there. In the cold it advances very slowly. The workers have bent it along a frame of split reed, binding it a little farther down each week with wet thread. If it takes the curve, it will become an arch over a seventh clearing. Already there are pegged gutters on either side of the proposed street, and the comb has been laid ready on a shelf. Beyond the frame there is as yet no room at all, only two slick green faces pressed together. But a hair of water shows between them. In that hair, not quite wide enough to admit the point of a thorn, something is swimming back and forth.
