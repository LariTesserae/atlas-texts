At the east end of town the pavement gives way to wet turf, and the turf goes right up to the edge. You can stand with one shoe on a drain cover and look down through a mile of daylight. Far below, a cloud lies caught across the opening, sunlit and dirty as the snow beside a road. The noise coming up through it makes the drain cover tremble under your heel.

The town occupies the level lip of a crack in a high plateau. There are thousands of such cracks. Some are narrow enough to roof over; on the far side of others there is weather too distant to see, except in the evening when a thunderhead stands up out of an apparently unbroken plain. On a map the plateau looks badly crazed. In fact you can take the maintenance train west for six days without seeing a second crack. You see turf, a few exposures of pale stone, and the black reflection of the train keeping pace in pools. The track makes its own small horizon ahead of you, a silver bump that never gets any nearer.

Nearly everything that lives here grows within a hand’s breadth of water. This makes for an immense amount of green in a very thin layer. The turf is made up of little jointed plants with fat, glassy elbows. Kneel on it and your knees come away smelling of cucumber. There are ferns in the lee of the signal boxes, and on the vertical rock there are gardens nobody has ever reached: long pleated leaves, some purple on the underside, growing out of seams where groundwater beads into the air. Below a certain depth they stop quite abruptly. For the rest of the way down there is bare fracture, with the two walls still so exactly matched that you can pick out the socket opposite almost any spur. Not even the longest spurs have lost their tips.

The noise is mostly air. It comes up from underneath the plateau at considerable pressure, cold enough to numb the teeth, carrying water so fine that a glass held in it merely clouds over. Each opening has its own note. The town was built around a place where a shelf of rock divided the current into three, making a chord so strong that the first surveyors had to write to one another on the backs of their maps. Now there are turbine halls on that shelf. There are lifts, foundries, a works that turns out ceramic pipe, and nine levels of streets hung on brackets against the wall. All the windows face out across the gap. In the middle of a bright day the people on the lowest street have to put their lamps on, while high above them somebody is shading a shop window with a striped awning.

It is a good place for making things to an exact size. The temperature on the factory floors has not varied measurably since records began. A cutter can be stopped on Friday afternoon and started again on Monday without spoiling the cut. A less convenient consequence is that nothing dries. At the public laundry the last machine presses the sheets between warm rollers and winds them straight into waxed paper. Open a packet at home and for a minute you have that extraordinary luxury, dry linen; then the cold comes back into it, first at the corners.

The sky overhead is often perfectly ordinary, blue with high white streaks. What takes some getting used to is the permanence of local weather. There is a shower on the westbound line, eleven minutes beyond the last station, which appears in the earliest photographs of the railway. Its near margin falls across the same three sleepers. Under it the plants are nearly waist-high; step out from under it and they barely cover the toes of your boots. A child going away to school can put a hand out of the carriage at just the right moment to get it wet, and do the same coming home as an old woman. The drops are new each time. There is nothing to suggest that the shower itself will ever end.

No birds make use of the rising air. No little bodies dart for cover when a door is opened on the gardens. Once you notice this, the place seems much larger. Every speck moving against the opposite wall is either a flake of moisture close to your eye or a whole freight car a great way off. Down on the loading stages people talk with their hands, not out of any custom but because of the tremendous sustained sound. At the end of a shift they come up to the turf in the lift, bringing their lunch tins and the smell of cutting oil. They walk a short distance out along the tracks. There is a point, surprisingly close to town, where the lip of the crack cuts off the roar. Here they stop to finish the conversations they began below. Around them the green plain shines to its limits, and in the quiet you can hear the water working its way back into their footprints.
