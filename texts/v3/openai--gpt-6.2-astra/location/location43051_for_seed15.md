The little basin is under the last tread of the bath stairs. The tread is a slab of white marble, shallowly hollowed by bare feet. Just below its lip is a finger-wide slot through which warm water issues into the bath. From the stairs one sees only the water wrinkling there. From inside, the slot is a bright horizon, wavering from end to end whenever somebody goes down the steps.

There is about a handspan of water over the floor. Six cream-colored tiles would cover it, though it took seven to do the job: the last space was a little out of square and had to be filled with two cut pieces. Their cut edges still have small sharp teeth. Beyond them a plank of new mortar slopes up to the back wall. The whole cavity was lined and the joints thumbed smooth before the tread was lowered into place. In the very back, where it didn't matter how the work looked, the whorl of a thumb is still legible in the lime.

Afternoon sun reaches well in. It comes through the clear upper inches of the bath, and a footfall on the stairs will send a whole sheaf of light racing over the floor and halfway up the back wall. When the surface outside settles, the light settles too. Then the two cut tiles have the buttery color they had on the day they came out of the kiln. There is one chip of red earthenware in the middle of all that cream. It has lain there long enough to have a clean crescent downstream of it where no fine dust stays put.

The inlet is a clay pipe no bigger around than a wrist, running down from the hearth tank. It enters at one side rather than at the back. This gives the basin its weather. When the keeper tips a bucket into the tank, an extra head of water bears down on the pipe; a hot current sweeps the near wall, strikes the end, and turns along the slot. For a while everything loose goes that way. A grain of lime travels the length of a tile in little starts and finally pitches over the step into the great blue room of the bath. Then the push eases. Cooler water noses in through one end of the slot while warm water continues to leave by the other. A short green thread anchored to the sill leans first outward, then sideways. There are two or three such threads, all at the mouth where the days are longest. Further in, nothing grows yet.

It is a loud place, especially for one so small. The pipe carries the hollow crash of the buckets with remarkable fidelity. A wooden sole set down on the marble roof makes a single immense knock. Even in the long intervals when nobody comes, there is the steady coarse pouring sound from the hearth end, conducted through the clay; put an ear to another stair and one hears almost none of it. Here there is no animal to startle and very little to dislodge. The red chip rocks once in a strong flush and drops back on its flat face.

At evening the keeper closes the feed with a wooden plug. The last run of hot water passes out along the sill. Water from the bath comes gently back to level, submerging the tiles as deeply as before, and the noise in the pipe stops. For a few minutes the sun is low enough to shine straight through the slot. Every ridge of the thumbprint then casts its own fine shadow. From above, someone feeling for the last step with a foot finds a comfortable wash of warmth, just there beneath the stone.
