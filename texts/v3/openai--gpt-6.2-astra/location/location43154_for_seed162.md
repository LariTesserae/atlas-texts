The high country comes up through the cloud in strips. On some mornings a strip is narrow enough that an animal standing across it can browse in one valley and shed its shadow into another. On others there is room for a forest between the two white edges. The difference is partly the level of the cloud and partly the width of the country. It is still coming up.

Most of the stone is a dark green glass, cloudy in the mass, clear at the corners. The newest ridges have corners fine enough to split a falling leaf. Nothing has had time to make a slope out of them. A ridge ends in a wall, and at the foot of the wall the ground begins again quite level, with perhaps three green splinters lying on it, big as stranded whales. From above, what looks like a short stretch of this country will turn out to contain a storm. Its little grey cap is visible, and occasionally the pink working of lightning underneath. For days the storm goes about its business between the same two splinters.

It is warm up here. The warmth comes through the soles of the feet; the air on the face is a good deal cooler. There is seldom anything one could call a sunny day. Light comes up out of the cloud banks in a diffuse pewter wash, good for seeing the underside of a wing, poor for seeing the animal perched above it. Water in the open is scarce. It gathers instead along certain lines in the rock, lines you could follow with one finger until the stone disappeared under a mat of red roots. Along these lines grow low trees with a great many narrow trunks and a common crown of soft, cabbage-green leaves. Their fruit hangs underneath, close to the warmth. A grove in fruit smells of hot pears for miles downwind, even where the bare glass will scarcely support a lichen.

There are herds that follow that smell. The big browsers have feet like thick lips which can close on either side of an edge. On level ground they go awkwardly, rolling a little at the hip; on the knife ridges they move with the ease of beads running down a string. Small grey birds live against their bellies. When a herd passes overhead, seen from the bottom of a cleft, it looks like a solemn procession of animals each coming to pieces very slowly and gathering itself together again. There are pale mites in the birds’ throats, and a kind of glossy blue fly that drinks only from the wet footprints of the browsers. In a fruiting grove almost every available surface is somebody’s back.

What goes on below the glass is easiest to see at the lip of a fresh break. First there is a layer of empty blisters. Below that are wheels, some smaller than seeds, some so large that the exposed cliff shows only two or three teeth. They grow in opposed pairs out of the same mineral seam. A clear syrup circulates among them. Where it cools, it sets on the teeth; where it warms, it runs off and falls to a lower wheel. The whole depth of the plateau is turning at a rate just perceptible to the eye. A leaf caught in a seam at breakfast will by dusk be flattened so fine that light passes through its veins. Farther down, the seams take in the vapour of the valleys and express it, a drop at a time, along the red-root lines. There is no casing on any of this, no place where the works end and ordinary stone begins. A pebble knocked loose goes on turning inside until it cools.

One expects a din. Instead there is the occasional small *tik* of a blue fly alighting on glass. Sound here travels very readily downward. A slab the size of a mountainside can tip off into the mist in what seems perfect silence; much later, if you put your jaw against the ground, your teeth will ache briefly and the taste of iron will come into your mouth. The browsers lie flat several times a day. Sometimes a whole herd gets up afterward and leaves good fruit uneaten.

Toward evening the pace picks up. Long strips of ground feed out of the cliffs, green and wet as blades of grass drawn from a sheath. Others are taken in. For a while the groves go by one another, crown brushing crown, and the grey birds take the opportunity to change herds. This is when the extent of the place becomes difficult to judge. Across a gap a tree may go past bearing an old lightning scar and a fork full of blue flies. An hour later it comes past again, the scar still smoking. Then there are miles of unscarred trees. Distance, like water, seems to be something the deeper wheels can squeeze out of one part of the country for use in another.

The most settled-looking places are the little pools. They occupy dents no wider than a browser’s footprint, and have black mud at the bottom and a fringe of exceedingly delicate fern. In one of them there may be a dozen blunt-headed silver fish hanging quite still. Ridges rise around it, a pear grove goes sliding away to the west, a wedge of cloud is pinched between two approaching walls and streams upward in tatters. The pool keeps its level. Only when a fish turns does the reflection change, and then, for an instant, there is a clear view in the water of a bright sky and the worn, easy slopes of a much older country. The fish takes a gnat from the surface. The green corners close over its head again.
