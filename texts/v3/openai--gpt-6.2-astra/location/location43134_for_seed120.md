The summit is kept in the hollow of a clay button. Closed, it shows six thumb marks and a bit of green thread. Lift the lid and the thread turns out to be the crown of a tree, bent over by the lid into the shape of a hook. It takes its time standing up. There is a smell of hot laundry, then a much more particular smell, the smell of fern stalks nicked with a thumbnail. Down among the trees somebody is cutting stairs.

There are forty or fifty trees, according to how far the work has got. They grow in pleats of boiled felt pinned edgewise into the clay. Their roots have gone clear through the felt and stitched one pleat to the next; in some places the only way to get a foot in is to turn it sideways. The stairs are for those places. The cutter works with a flake of shell, taking a little triangular bite out of each stalk without killing it. A clear bead stands in every cut. In the dim green of the wood a finished flight looks like a slanting row of sweaty teeth.

All this is very high up. To see just how high, one must get down on one's belly beside the buttonhole. There are clouds a long way below it, with blue shadows in their folds and, farther down, the brown shine of a river making an extravagant detour around something too small to see. Drop a clipping through the hole and there is time to get up, wash the shell, wind it in its bit of cloth and come back to see the clipping catch the light. At this height the wind ought to have stripped the place clean. Instead it blows through the buttonhole as through the neck of a jug. The noise is tremendous. The workers hang wet tassels into the hole to soften the note enough to sleep.

The heat comes from the firing. Along one side of the wood there is always a narrow front of red traveling through the clay, slow enough that a patch the size of a heel stays warm all day after it passes. Behind it the ground is hard and rings under a knuckle. Ahead it is soft enough to take a print from the veins of a fallen leaf. Someone must continually press fresh clay into the lip of the button: not much, a pinch at a time, smoothed with the back of a spoon. The new lip goes on expanding inward. There is consequently room inside for quite a considerable ridge, although the button still fits its loop. Nothing here has had time to wear down. What looks like a dry watercourse is the seam between two ropes of clay. What looks like an old landslip is where the spoon stuck.

At dusk the lid goes on. This is the wettest part of the day. Steam gathers on the underside until the six thumb marks fill; then six great drops let go almost together. The trees are planted where they are to divide the fall. Even so, a drop striking a crown makes the whole hillside jump. Water runs down the stairs, overflows the felt, stands for an hour in the little yards between houses. People put their cups outside. By morning most of it has wicked back up the ridge, carrying a fringe of minute red leaves with it. There is hardly a bare surface anywhere. Even the spoon has a velvet garden on its handle, with a bald oval where the thumb goes.

Once in a while a pale mite walks out onto the ridge. Work stops for it. There are no beasts of burden here, no flocks, scarcely a gnat; the sight of so much flesh proceeding under its own power is unsettling. Its feet make eight soft dents in the unfired ground. The workers could smooth them away but generally choose to keep them. They lay slivers of bark between the dents, and when rain comes again there are eight linked ponds, warm at one end, cool at the other. Children crouch there to float the shavings from the stair cutter's shell.

What they are making, ultimately, is the mountain under the summit. The peak had to be finished first so there would be somewhere to stand. On fine mornings they lower a small basket through the hole, full of clay and rooted felt, and somebody rides it down to add a ledge to the underside. It is close, damp work, done mostly by touch. The worker comes back up with mud in both ears and cold sunlight on the soles of her feet. Above her the wood is loud with water and shell strokes and people calling for the basket. Below her, for the thickness of one more ledge, there is earth where yesterday there was only the view.
