High up in the waterworks, between the wall of the overflow shaft and the grating on which a fitter can stand to grease the gate, there is a shelf about the size of a kitchen table. You have to kneel and put your face close to the grating to see it. The light is what gets past the fitter’s boots. In it you can make out a silver slope, a strip of water black as a saw cut, and, some distance apart, three little patches of green.

The terrible noise up here has remarkably little to do with what can be seen. On the far side of the wall, surplus water from the high reservoir goes over a lip and drops nine storeys. The shelf gets the sound of the whole drop and only the water that leaks through one expansion joint. A bead comes out beside a washer, swells until it touches the shelf, and empties itself quite gently. It would be pleasant to hear what sound that makes. A man once dropped his spanner through the grating and knew where it had landed only because a sheet of water sprang up and wet his cheek.

The shelf was fitted last winter to keep seepage off a bearing. Its hills are the heads of four bolts and the stiffening creases pressed into a single sheet of galvanized steel. At the downhill end the steel is folded up to form a gutter. The corners are still square enough to catch threads from a cleaning rag. Nowhere has the water worn a channel of its own, though it has tried out several routes. Yesterday’s route shows as a white fork on the zinc. Today the trickle takes the other side of a crease and spreads out so thin that the little crystalline spangles in the metal remain perfectly legible underneath it. Every few seconds a shiver comes through the wall and the spangles vanish under rows of minute waves.

It is cold enough here that the handrail stays wet even on its underside. Beyond the louvres there is sometimes a view out over the tops of the clouds; more often the cloud comes in. Then the bolts gather water on their uphill faces as well, and for an hour or two each is an island with a bright, trembling coast. The three green patches are moss growing on scraps of felt left under the fold of the gutter. There are only a few upright blades in each, far enough apart to count. No flies come to stand on them. They lean when the gate moves, in the draught drawn through the grating, and slowly stand up again with drops caught between their tips.

The place changes most when the gate is lowered. The first sign on the shelf is a darkening along the expansion joint. For several minutes what had been a bead is a clear rope of water; it leaps the first crease altogether and fills a shallow dent left by the fallen spanner. Then the pressure eases, the rope breaks, and the dent is cut off. Long after the rest of the shelf has drained, it holds a pool no bigger than the bowl of a spoon. From the right angle there is quite a depth of light in it: the pale louvres, a bar of sky, the underside of the iron stair climbing on up the tower. All of it shakes without ceasing.
