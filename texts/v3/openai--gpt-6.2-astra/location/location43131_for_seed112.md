In the sound shadow of a curled leaf, you can hear an animal eating. It has a split lower lip and uses the two halves independently to pick the brown scales off a seed. The scales fall on the backs of your hands. A step past the edge of the leaf there is such a sustained beating noise that you feel it in the little bones on either side of your nose. The animal goes on eating. It has made a considerable heap.

The leaf that shelters it is about a mile long. It lies on its side on the upper plain, curled along one margin where the prevailing wind gets under it. From above, the plain would look almost bare: a few of these long pale shapes flung down at great intervals, the shadows sharp and blue, acres and acres of unmarked cream between. But the cream is leaf too. You can put your cheek to it. It has the cool nap of a peach, and deep in the nap, well out of reach of the light, hosts of minute red grazers are making their slow way against the grain.

The high country here consists of generations of sheets laid down over one another. In the great eastern scarp you can see the history of a whole latitude edge on. There are dark, close-set lines from lean centuries, fat cushions of yellow cork, a red seam where a species once prospered that no longer grows on top. Wind has worried the soft stuff out from between the harder layers and given the scarp its flights of rounded ledges. Animals breed there in such numbers that when they leave together the shadow goes down the cliff like a hand passing over the ribs of a sleeping dog. Far below them the air is brown. Up here it is cold enough to sting the gums, and so clear that a white gust coming across the plain can be watched for half a morning before it arrives.

There is scarcely any water. What little comes up from below is caught in the deepest tissues and passed from the dying to the new. A torn stalk bleeds a clear, bitter oil; it stays supple in the cold, and a bruise in the ground will keep its oily thumbprint for years. Much of what looks like dust blowing in the distance is wax. It wears away on the windward slopes and gathers to leeward in banks as fine and loose as flour. In the brief lull before a gust, a whole bank may be covered in tiny three-pointed tracks. Then the surface lifts, tracks and all.

It is the undersides of the sheets that make the noise. They are ribbed, with a tough loose tongue inside each rib. Air admitted at the scarp runs for immense distances between the layers, playing these tongues, which strike in their turn against pads of spongy tissue. Each stroke squeezes oil a little farther along the plain. An old sheet can pump long after its upper face has gone blind and woody. There is a steady low pounding from those depths, like heavy presses working behind a wall; nearer the surface the young green ribs chatter at quite a different speed. Nothing has been assembled here. A tongue begins as a fold in a seed leaf small enough to lodge under a claw. Given light, grit and a few hundred seasons of good wind, the fold will grow to shake a hillside.

Sometimes two immense stretches get out of time with each other. Along the line where their pulses meet, the ground bucks. Pale seams gape, close with a clap, gape again a little farther on. Grubs come boiling out of them, fat silver things normally never exposed to daylight. Long-legged runners gather from what appeared a moment ago to be an utterly empty horizon. They dash in for a grub and spring clear before the lips meet; the young ones watch the feet of the old ones rather than the ground. By evening a wandering seam may have passed a hundred miles west, taking its clamorous fringe of feeders with it. Behind it the plain lies smooth and bright except for a scattering of snapped legs, already attended by the red grazers.

For all this motion, the great shapes change very little. The same hollow catches the wax each year. The same headland parts the rising air. A sheet that tears loose at the rim exposes a younger one already bent to much the same curve, so that even a very old glider returning from the far southern breeding grounds can find the lift it learned as a fledgling. It comes up over the scarp without a wingbeat, into a dazzle of clean sunlight. For a while there is only its narrow shadow keeping pace over the cream. Then a rib opens somewhere under that enormous floor, a breath of bitter warmth rolls across the cold nap, and all at once a thousand little mouths turn into the wind.
