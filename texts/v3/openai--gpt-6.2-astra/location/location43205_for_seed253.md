The lake takes up the whole summit. A little below its western lip the clouds begin, and water spilling over there falls a long way through clear air before the clouds receive it. The lip is soft. It gives under the weight of a bird. For miles in either direction it holds the same level, a dark green welt with a comb of white water going through it.

Down in the lake there is no sense of being on a height. It is cool enough to numb the lips, the dark comes on within a few feet, and the country opens out into long, nearly level reaches. At intervals a single pale stem stands up from the bottom. Then for a quarter mile there is nothing else standing. The stems used to be the sides of steep banks. A bank has gone on wearing away on either side of a particularly tough root until the root, thick as a thigh and polished by the water, stands alone. Little silver fish keep to its lee. They could shelter there in their hundreds and leave the rest of the reach quite empty.

The lake bed looks like brown felt rubbed bald in places. The bald places are alive too. They are the backs of broad leaves laid face down, with the veins showing in low relief. Under a leaf is another leaf, and under that a mat of cords, then a space through which water moves with considerable force. Practically everything here has been laid in place by mouth. Small, blunt-faced swimmers loosen the ends of runners, carry them a short distance and tuck them under something that will hold. They do this to roof their nests. Their young roof the gaps between the nests, and the runners take root where they have been tucked. In the cut face of the western lip there are miles of this work visible, tier beneath tier, the old nest chambers pressed to slits. Somewhere far under it there is presumably a mountain.

There is a great deal of noise. It comes up through the floor. The water in all those flattened galleries is on its way to the falls, and makes a deep rushing sound overlaid by the dry, quite distinct rattle of seeds rolling along the gallery roofs. A swimmer resting a cheek on the felt can hear a seed come from far off, pass directly underneath and dwindle into the general roar. Above the floor almost the only sound is the cropping of the little shelled grazers. There are so many of them, and each takes so long over its meal, that what one hears is a sustained fine rasp, like someone dressing the edge of a board in the next room.

Overhead is another weave, much looser: floating straps the color of old olive skins, their dry upper faces silvered by the summit cold. From beneath, one sees daylight chiefly at the places where the straps cross and do not quite fit. A pin of light falls through, sometimes as far as the bed. In one such spot the blunt-faced swimmers have made a shallow court, swept quite clean, where they bring their young. The young are transparent except for the eyes and a bellyful of green. They hang in the light with their fins going. A few yards off, where it is black again, there may be ten thousand more.

Very little alters. Water wells up at the eastern end; the same depth of water goes over the west. The animals build where a thread has pulled free, and where they build too thickly the current catches underneath and strips a layer off. Even the most ancient reaches have not quite lost the width of a mouth in their pattern. If you followed one of the low seams all the way across the floor, through the dim courts and the long unoccupied stretches, you would come at last to the lip, where the weave thins and the noise grows enormous. Out there the last few leaves lie perfectly flat in the current, pointed out over empty air.
