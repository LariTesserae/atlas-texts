Rain is usually over in one part of the basin before it has begun in another. You can hear it coming from the west: the individual knocks at first, then the long rattling run, like somebody emptying a sack of teeth. For all that noise, you can stand out in it and scarcely get your collar wet. Almost all the drops fall on the other side of something.

The basin is tiled. There are broad paving tiles underfoot, barely tilted toward long, shallow gutters. There are wall tiles hung on pegs, with room for a finger to go behind them. Overhead, one course of roof tiles shelters another course set crosswise beneath it. Then comes a considerable interval of air, and then, just discernible where a little light gets in, the bellies of immense, shallow dishes. These are the collectors. On a cool night their undersides sweat. A lip fills, a dish tips on its clay axle, and the whole business of rain begins. By the time the water gets down to walking level, a mile of hollow clay has had something to say about it.

From the eastern steps the floor looks nearly flat. Walking it is a different matter. There are ridges just high enough to conceal the light of a lamp held at the waist; there are descents so gradual you only know you have been going downhill when you turn and see the little string of lamps behind you climbing into the dark. A hump near the middle, wide as a village, is the back of an old roof. Its ridge tiles still have their finials, absurd little knobs to stumble over. What was once the eave makes a good dry sleeping place, if you can put up with the knocking. Beyond it are some hundreds of kilns, most of them cold, each with a neat stack of unfired squares beside its mouth. The one or two in use give off a low orange light. It carries a surprising distance along the floor and hardly any distance upward.

There is no bedrock. A paver taking up a broken slab finds a bedding of potsherds; under the sherds is hard red plaster, and under that the impressed pattern of the mat on which the plaster was once laid. People quarry the old work for the new. They have stone mauls worn concave from crushing brick, shoulder yokes with hooks of fired clay, little copper scrapers too valuable to be put down even for a moment. A worker at dinner wears hers down the back of her shirt. Most of the day’s work is done without enough light to judge a color. A sound tile rings when lifted by one corner and struck. A good join will not catch a thumbnail drawn across it in either direction. Children learning to lay a course do it first with their eyes shut, then with their eyes open, a distinction that amuses them.

Very little here has had time to wear. The pale streak down a wall seems at a distance a deep watercourse; close up it is only a wash of lime across the maker’s palm print, every crease intact. The newest gutters do not yet know how to carry water. It beads along their floors and stops at the joints. At each rain someone goes ahead on hands and knees, drawing a wet finger along the dry clay so that the trickle will follow. In the old quarter this is no longer necessary. There the thread of water goes straight to its pool, bending round corners that were taken up and relaid level years ago.

Nothing green grows even in the pools. There is a thin gray skin on the water, and under certain ledges something like soft white sealing wax, deeply scalloped where the basin animals come to feed. You seldom see one. Once, lifting a loose tread, a mason found a creature as long as her hand flattened into the space behind it, its sides going in and out with extraordinary speed. Its eyes were shut tight against the grit. She put the tread back on two chips so it would not take the creature’s weight. That step still clicks underfoot. In a place where so much care goes into keeping things from rocking, the click is distinctive; people giving directions tell you to turn there.

The loudest hour is just before morning. The dishes have chilled through. One tips, and the discharge sets the next one swinging, and for a while the basin is all overlapping showers, some high and fine, some falling no farther than the thickness of a wall. Between them come the blows of the early shift knocking wedges out of yesterday’s work. A whole reach of new vaulting settles the width of a fingernail onto its piers. Dust pushes out of the joints in straight lines. For a moment it hangs there in the kiln glow, showing the undersides of arches nobody at floor level has ever seen all together. Then it is dark overhead again. Down in the gutter a little wave, brown at the lip with fresh clay, makes it three tiles farther east than any water went yesterday.
