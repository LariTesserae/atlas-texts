From the lip of the basin you can see almost to the sea. Three miles off, a low bar of shingle cuts off the view; now and then something pale travels along its crest and falls away on the other side. The basin itself looks full of shingle too, laid down in long gray drifts. It is only when you get down among the drifts that you see how much room there is under them.

They are shells. Most are big enough to put your head inside. They lie with the deep valve down and the lid still on, tipped a little to one side by the slow departure of the hinge. There are hundreds of thousands of them. In places they stand in tiers so closely fitted that there is hardly a way to put a foot down between two lips. Elsewhere a whole tier has subsided, leaving a lane of blue mud with shell walls a little higher than your waist. Down in a lane you have the sense of being deep inland. You can smell the sea but you cannot tell where it is.

The sea only lately withdrew from here. There is still a chalky nap on the shells that rubs away at a touch, and the broken ones have edges fine enough to take a shaving off a fingernail. Nothing grows over them. Even the oldest beds, up toward the back of the basin, have gone from white to the dull yellow of teeth without acquiring so much as a thread of green. In the dim light under the cloud bank the few upturned lids are easy to pick out. Their insides hold a soft pewter brightness long after the mud between them has gone dark.

There is water within an inch or two of the surface almost everywhere. Rain tastes fresh from the hollow in a lid and bitter from the hollow under it. Clear beads come sweating out of the mud walls; where one falls it leaves a little round window through which you can see coarser grit behind the blue. A hand pressed to the wall comes away cold and perfectly printed, down to the creases across the knuckles. It can be an hour before the water fills the print.

It takes a while to place the sound. At the lip of the basin it seems quite reasonable that the surf should be loud. Down below, the shingle bar lies out of sight and the noise is if anything louder. It comes from the lanes on both sides of you. The sea still gets in under the bar. Its pressure travels very slowly through the buried beds, raising the water in one closed pocket after another and driving out the air. A shell with a split lid makes a rough buzzing note. An intact pair may do nothing for half the day and then let out one long, quite musical breath. No one shell is especially loud. There are simply so many within earshot that, when the tide is well in, two people in a lane have to stand cheek to cheek to talk. All the while a drop of rainwater hangs quite still from the lip beside them.

Here and there someone has made a dry place. The best are built into the ends of the higher drifts: a back wall of mud, two side walls of nested valves, a roof of broad lids sloping toward the lane. They are just big enough for a person to sit in with knees drawn up. The threshold is the part that has taken thought. A single unbroken shell lies across it, convex side up, and conducts all the cold little trickles away to either side of the seat. From inside you look straight out at a band of open sky. Some of the seats have fallen in. One has a clean oval in the chalk at the back where a head has rested often.

Alive, the shellfish are hard to find. There are a few in the deepest lane, where the mud will not quite bear a person's weight. They look no different from the dead until the water rises. Then a lip opens the width of a coin and a fringe of pale flesh comes out to comb the cloudy water. A small gray shorebird visits this lane alone. In all that din it hunts by sight, walking on the lids and watching, with its head well to one side, for the slightest break in the white line of a seam.

Toward evening the sound begins to leave the upper beds. You can follow its retreat by the things that become audible again: first water ticking off a roof nearby, then the crumbling of a bank farther down the lane. At last you can hear the bird walking. Its feet make a light double tap, then nothing for a considerable time, then the same double tap a little farther out toward the sea.
