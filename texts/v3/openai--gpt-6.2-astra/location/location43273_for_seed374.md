From the ferry you can see the whole town spread low along a bar of black rock. The farther end is four miles off, a narrow band of whitewashed gables under the cloud. At the nearer end you can pick out a woman sitting on her doorstep with her feet in the water, scaling a fish into a bowl. Behind her the houses stand six or seven deep. Their roofs slope different ways to send the rain into particular yards. It is warm enough to sit in a wet shift all day, and most people do.

The bar is new land. Where a foundation ditch has been opened there is no layer of earth, just broken purple-black lava over solid gray lava, with the two fitting together as closely as the skin and flesh of a loaf. Some of the older houses have smooth thresholds, worn by bare heels and the dragging of baskets, but ten paces from a door the rock still has edges that will lay a foot open. Nothing takes root in it. The only wood in sight is the ferry itself, a big pale dugout brought from up the coast, with so many repairs along the gunwales that each patch has a patch for a neighbor.

On the seaward side a shelf nearly a mile broad takes the swell. All along it, even in weather too still to stir a hanging net, there is a standing line of breakers. You hear them over the low roofs without ever hearing a particular wave begin or end. A potter at a wheel can feel the shelf through the ball of her foot. In the clay workers’ quarter, where the walls face the sea square on, people put their mouths quite close to one another’s ears to speak. In the fish market a price is shown against the chest with the fingers and the bargain concluded by touching wrists. Children learn the signs long before anyone lets them go to market.

Most of the streets follow cracks. On either side of a crack the builders have laid flat slabs with a little inward pitch, so that two people can walk abreast with the water running between them. At a crossing there is a sound like someone steadily emptying a bucket down a well. Look through the gap between slabs and there, a hand’s breadth under the busy pavement, are the backs of little silver fish holding still against the flow. The water comes sweet out of the higher part of the bar. Farther down it tastes of salt. Under the last row of houses the cracks open out into pools full of transparent shrimp; seen from above they amount to hundreds of pairs of black eyes casting minute paired shadows on the sand.

The town belongs nearly as much to birds as to people. There are broad ledges under the eaves for the nesting terns, set there on purpose so the birds will leave the housetops free for drying fish. On a dull afternoon the ledges are so crowded that a returning bird has to hang in the air a moment while its mate makes room. The potters leave shallow dishes of spoiled clay outside their doors. Rain fills them, and the small gray shorebirds come in off the salt to bathe, up to their bellies in warm slip. Their tracks dry into the dishes. People like these for serving eggs.

Inland of the houses lies the backwater, wide, shallow and the color of unpolished tin. Here the ferry goes almost soundlessly among the tethered skin boats. The roar from beyond the town is just as loud out here; it seems odd that a boat can set down such a fine wake in the middle of it. Through the haze you can see one landing after another, each with its steps, its heap of empty shell baskets, its white mark on the bottom stone to show a boatman where the edge is in dim light. Beyond the last landing the bare bar continues for some distance at precisely the same height as the roofs, as if the houses were simply the part of the coast that had been squared off and hollowed out for habitation.

Very little here needs a date. A roof slab breaks and is lowered into the street to bridge a runnel; another goes up in its place. A daughter takes her mother’s place on the doorstep and uses the same bowl, which has a rough place on its rim just right for catching the back of the scaling knife. The tide makes a difference of two steps at the ferry, seldom more. Even when it stops raining, the undersides of the lintels stay beaded with water and the cloud stays bright enough to work by. What a traveler remembers afterward is that immense noise and, in the midst of it, the extraordinary leisure of the hands: one spreading clay on a seam, another turning a fish, two touching briefly above a counter and drawing apart.
