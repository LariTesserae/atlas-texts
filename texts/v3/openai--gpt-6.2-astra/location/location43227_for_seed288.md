The oldest part of the town is a piece of floor. It lies thirty or forty feet below the upland, under a shelf of limestone, and consists of a long pale oval on which nothing grows. The rock there has the finish of a stair tread in an old public building. Beyond the oval are streets, courts, towers with dark windows all up one side. Nearly a mile away, toward the lower end of the shelf, there is another such district. Between them there is room for a whole district more, but only a few little heaps of sorted grit, each with a lizard on top.

The shelf was once the outer bank of a river. The river took the better part of a hill away, working sideways, and then went elsewhere so long ago that even the sharp edges of its absence have worn round. From below, the lip of the overhang looks as blunt as the edge of an old cheese. There is no drip from it. Under it the air is cool, dry enough that a dead moth keeps its scales and coloring long after the body inside has gone to powder. There are moths of several former seasons caught in a single web.

Light comes a long way sideways into this country. Even at noon most of the floor is the color of the underside of a mushroom. But the shelf has split in a few places from lip to root, and down each split hangs a wood. At the top are low thorn trees growing almost level with the ground to escape the upland wind. Below are their stout, fluted roots, the old dead ones as hard and pale as antler, the living ones furred in fine brown hairs. Succulents occupy every lodgment in the walls of the split. They grow in such crowded tiers that their lower leaves never see the sky. Where the cleft widens at its foot they make a handsome grove, head-high, with blue leaves a span across and little orange flowers tucked well in among them. Seen from the bare oval, several of these groves overlap in the distance. It gives the quite mistaken impression that one could walk for miles in the green. In fact there is an afternoon's walk from the end of one grove to the beginning of the next.

It is in these strips that the insects feed. Farther in, where the rock overhead is sound and the temperature hardly varies, they build. The towers are made of grit cemented a grain at a time; their windows are the mouths of galleries, and the broad streets between blocks are broad because anything built there would cut off the draft. A fallen section shows, on its broken face, rooms smaller than lentils arranged around shafts the width of a thumb. The bottom stories have been stopped up for generations. Other things live in them now. There are soft white beetles in the walls, geckos in the cracks between walls, and in one particularly stately row, little owls that come out backward from their holes scattering crumbs of architecture.

From a distance the place seems rather empty. Up close it is difficult to put a hand down anywhere without disturbing somebody. A pebble turns out to have a fringe of legs tucked close under it. The litter under the succulents lifts in small local disturbances like cloth with mice beneath. Yet most of the time very little is to be seen moving except the black files of the builders, going out along one edge of a street and coming back along the other, each returning insect carrying a shaving of dry leaf. There is a polished track under each file. The bare oval was a meeting of such tracks when the town stood farther out under the shelf. Nothing remains of that town above ankle height.

What does not come across in a view of the place is the noise. In the groves live thick-bodied crickets with wings like two halves of a husk. They sit well apart, one male to a plant, each in the fork where the lowest living leaf joins the stem. One of them starting up close by is enough to make an ear ache. Several thousand, under a continuous ceiling of stone, produce a sound with no apparent source at all. You can stand face to face in the old market oval and see the person opposite take a great breath and shout, and hear no part of it. Out near the lip, where it is possible to get some distance from the sound, there is a perceptible wavering in it. One grove slackens a little and a grove half a mile away swells in reply. Farther out still it sounds like grain being tipped steadily into a bin.

In the late afternoon a narrow bar of sun reaches the front rank of towers. Minute shadows appear under the rims of all their windows. Lizards leave the heaps of grit one by one and stretch full length against the warm walls, their toes spread over half a dozen households apiece. For perhaps twenty minutes the front of the town is gold and apparently uninhabited; the traffic continues on the shaded side. Then the shadow of the upland rises over it again. A person returning after many years might find the same wall, the same slant of light, even a lizard lying along precisely the same ledge. The windows under its belly would all be new.
