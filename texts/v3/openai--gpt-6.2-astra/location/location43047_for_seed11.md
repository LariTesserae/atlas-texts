You can walk half a day across the crown without knowing what a height you are on. The ground rises and falls a little, like pasture. At noon there is a warm smell of crushed leaf underfoot. Then you come to a place where a long piece has fallen out of the rim. Through the gap you see the shadow of the crown lying across a brown plain, with room in that shadow for a whole river to wind back and forth several times. A white bird crosses the view below your feet. For a moment you take it for a bit of ash in your eye.

What remains up here is the hard bottom of a mountain. All the showier stone went down the river long ago. The exposed rock is gray with pink grains in it, and feels under the palm like the lip of a pitcher that generations have drunk from. Even the great notch in the rim is rounded. Across the crown run the beds of streams that no longer flow, shallow enough to step across, miles long. They wander and meet and part. From any small rise you can follow them by their green.

Down in a bed the growth is so thick that a dropped bead would be gone for good. There are ferns with dark red stems, soft clubs of moss, little gourds the color of butter. A creeping tree puts up a fresh crown every few yards; what looks from above like an orchard may all be one tree, no higher than your hip. It is pleasant to sit with your legs down among its leaves and your back against sun-warmed rock. Beyond the bed there may be half a mile of bare stone before the next green strand. There is scarcely anything standing up to interrupt the light.

The uproar begins when the lower plain warms. Air comes up the face and finds the old water holes in the rim. These go in sideways, divide in the dark, and open again all over the crown. First comes a low note you feel in the stone where your heels rest. Then, quite close by, there is a sound like a great length of linen being torn. Every hole for miles takes a part. By noon you can watch a companion's lips move at arm's length and hear nothing of the words. There is a hole no wider than a cup out of which comes a bass sound so powerful it makes the juice tremble in a cut gourd.

Nothing points the same way for long. One vent blows while its neighbor draws. Ferns flatten in a widening circle, recover, then all at once lean inward until their tips disappear into a crack. A little traveling knot of pink grit will race over the bare ground, stop dead, and go racing back along its own trail. Above all this the sky is an ordinary pale blue. You can see, quite clearly, a woman two streambeds away trying to spread a mat. Three times it folds itself neatly in half for her.

There is water in the air that comes up cold from inside the stone. It beads on the undersides of the leaves. This, more than rain, keeps the old beds green. The creeping trees send out fine white roots above ground into the draughts; near a good vent they hang in beards. People collect drinking water much the same way. Here and there a forked stick holds a fringe of combed bark over a clay pan. The pans have heavy lids with just a slit for the fringe to pass through, or a day's water would be back in the sky by supper. Beside one of these pans there is a shelter of six flat stones, five on edge and one across, with room beneath for someone to lie down. Its roof has been polished by the bellies of people climbing up to look for someone coming. From there you can see three more shelters. It would take most of the daylight to visit them all.

Toward evening the noise goes out in patches. A mile of rock falls silent while the stone under you is still bellowing. Then your own patch stops, and for a few breaths you hear small things with extraordinary distinctness: a bark thread tapping a lid, the dry feet of a lizard running up a leaf. This is when the white birds come level with the rim. There are only a few. They hang over the warm pink stone with their legs let down, waiting. Each seems to know precisely when the last breath will come out of a particular hole. It lifts the bird without a wingbeat, sometimes fifty feet straight up. From there it sets off across the long, almost level country, its shadow passing from rock to green and back to rock, until in the failing light the shadow is easier to follow than the bird.
