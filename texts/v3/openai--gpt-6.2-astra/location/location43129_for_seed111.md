The whole hollow would fit under the crown of a large beech. There is no tree within a mile of it, even straight down. It opens sideways out of a mountain ridge, a pocket left when a wedge fell out of the cliff. Such marks as there are on the ceiling continue on the wedge, which can still be seen far below, standing on end in the snow. Rain has scarcely blunted it. Up here the edges are sharp enough that even the birds draw their wings in to pass between them.

From the back of the hollow the sky is a seam of painful white. Everything else has to be made out by degrees. There is a tilt to the floor. There are shelves on the high side, a damp stripe down the low side, and in between them a quantity of something that looks like loosely piled charcoal. A piece of it gives a little shrug. Then a white eyelid closes over a black eye, and another closes a few inches away, and it becomes apparent that the floor is mostly birds.

They are small mountain birds with naked feet the color of plums. Several hundred roost here, wedged so close that a warm draft goes up from them and beads the low ceiling with water. Now and then a bird wants to turn around. Its neighbors lift themselves just enough to let it, settling again with a soft, expensive-sounding rustle, like a great length of silk being drawn through a ring. A few feed outside at any given time. They drop out of the seam without a wingbeat and go a long way down the cliff to where the air is blue with midges above the meltwater falls. On the way home they ride the rising air against the face. From the hollow all one sees of this tremendous journey is a pair of plum-colored feet coming in over the sill.

Most of the noise comes from under them. There is a pale cricket here, flattened almost to the thickness of its own legs. It eats the cast skins of the little things that live in the feathers. To call, it presses its breast against a flake of stone and works its wings. A good flake can be heard all across the hollow; a poor one merely ticks. The crickets try several in succession. In the dark this produces an extraordinarily spacious sound, as of water being poured into separate vessels in rooms much larger than this one. There may be fifty calling at once. When a bird puts its foot on a sounding flake, one whole room goes quiet.

At the low end the ceiling water gathers in a basin no wider than a palm. It is usually the only free water here. The wind moves snow clear over the opening and sends most of the rain past it in nearly horizontal silver lines. Only when a cloud lodges against the cliff does the hollow get thoroughly wet. Then the rock darkens right through to the back, and a thin olive skin on the shelves opens into thousands of minute forks. For a day there is a green margin to everything. The birds drink from the tips of the forks and leave the basin to a blind, fat-bodied swimmer that spends the dry intervals nosing around its rim, sometimes making a complete circuit with a single bubble attached to its head.

It is cool in the pocket, pleasantly so behind a bird's wing, bitterly so an inch beyond the sill. Little changes quickly. A brood grows up as a set of slight bulges in the charcoal heap. A cricket spends a season finding the stone that suits it. But above the highest shelf, well clear of any feather, a white line has lately appeared. It is the width of a hair and runs from the opening almost to the back. On cloud days it stays dry an hour longer than the rock around it. Already there is a cricket that goes up there to play: the whole ceiling answers it, on a lower note than any of the flakes below.
