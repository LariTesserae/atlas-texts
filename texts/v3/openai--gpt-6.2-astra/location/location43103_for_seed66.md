At noon the bridge casts a second bridge on the clouds below it. The shadow is distinct enough to show the gaps between the freight wagons. Water stands in the road on top, warm over the black paving blocks, cool where the parapet shades it. Along the whole uphill curb a procession of little white crabs is moving at about the speed of the train. They climb over the joints in the curb rather than go around them. The wagon wheels are taller than a house, but from the middle of the bridge it is the crabs you hear: a hard, sustained rustle, like somebody going through a drawer of buttons.

The bridge spans one of the narrow cuts in the plateau. In either direction there are wider cuts and longer bridges, and then, beyond a distance at which individual buildings can be made out, a blue-gray succession of level shoulders interrupted by light. What looks like light is sometimes water and sometimes forty miles of glazed factory roof. The country is limestone, raised high and worn down almost flat, with rounded hollows on top full of streets. Its edges are as steep as ever. Rain seldom has to travel far enough overland to find an edge. It goes down through the town.

On the upper streets the houses have pale stone fronts, dark to knee height from the splash. A doorstep may have worn through altogether; in that case there is usually an iron bar across the hollow to take the scraper that every household needs. Down three or four flights the rock walls begin to sweat. There are washhouses on these levels and public baths with changing cubicles cut into the stone. Farther down, where the seep has gathered into streams, are the wheel chambers. A person on a bath bench can feel through the seat when a wheel is being brought back under load. There is a momentary slapping of water, then the usual even tremor returns and goes on through the afternoon, through supper, into sleep.

Much of the water that comes up from deeper down is warm and smells of a freshly struck match. In the mixing pools, where it meets the clear, airy seep from the streets, there is a soft gray deposit that looks like the nap on old cloth. This is the living part of the stone. Minute mouths scrape tracks through it. The tracks fill in again. Shrimps browse there in such numbers that a dipped cup comes up ticking, and the shrimps in turn feed the crabs, the pool eels and the great flocks of blunt-winged birds that spend the bright hours standing belly-deep on the spillways. There is no grass even on the oldest roofs. What looks at a distance like a green lawn beside a reservoir is the backs of thousands of small green birds, all facing the water. When one turns, a little patch of the lawn shows silver.

The towns make their living from the pools. Each district keeps a set of shallow, tiled runs open to the deep springs, and a set it can shut off and drain for harvest. In the sheds beside them, beaten copper drums turn slowly, grading the catch by size. The smallest things fall through first and wash back into the runs. The largest crabs are picked off by hand because a claw caught crosswise can buckle the copper. Shell goes to the lime kilns, oil to the bearing shops, and bricks of pressed shrimp travel by freight to the dry precincts of the interior. These are not separate towns in any useful sense. Somebody born on the western rim can spend six days on the train visiting a sister and step out onto the same pattern of perforated platform tile, with the same little feet busy underneath it. Yet the sister will serve a yellow pool eel that cannot be had back home for any money. Its entire range is one warm cistern and the drains of the adjoining school.

Toward evening the cloud below the plateau thickens until every bridge appears to cross a level field of white. The air above stays clear. Sunlight goes a long way into the wheel chambers through the west-facing outlets, showing the wet spokes one after another and, on a ledge beyond the reach of the paddles, a row of heavy nursing animals asleep on their sides. They have hairless brown flanks and long sensitive upper lips with which they can find a single crab in cloudy water. The wheel keepers leave them alone. There are old inspection drawings with the ledge marked *occupied*, as if the same dozen animals had been asleep there for two hundred years.

In fact the wheel on that reach has been moved twice. The stream is eating toward the east, a thumb’s breadth or so in a lifetime. In the pavement overhead there are two lines of plugged holes where the former lifting tackle went through. On hot evenings people sit on the warm plugs to mend their nets. Around their ankles the pale crabs are coming back downhill now, out from under doors and over the curbs, making for water. The last freight has gone. For a while its distant grinding seems to linger in the street; then you can distinguish all the small, separate scrapings that make it up.
