A stone dropped over the edge of the tableland strikes leaves for a long time. There are whole hanging woods below the lip, rooted in the horizontal joints of the rock. Then comes a silence long enough for the person who dropped it to shift from one foot to the other. If the wind is coming up, it brings back a little slap from the water far below. Along most of the rim this is the only way to tell where one is. There is no light above or below it.

Inland, the stone has worn down to broad backs and shallow dishes. A ridge scarcely higher than a sleeping animal may divide two waters that will go their separate ways for a thousand miles. The dishes are full of warm water and the pasture that grows on it: ropes of floating pith, velvet thick as an ankle, stiff leaves curled into tubes wide enough to put a thumb in. Nothing here needs to turn a leaf toward the sky. The roots feed at the contact between two waters, one that wells up from the hot beds under the rock, tasting of iron, and one that falls out of the air almost pure. Lift a piece of turf and its underside smells like a wet handful of pennies. Little jointed things come pouring out into the hollow your fingers have made.

Rain travels slowly across this country. One storm may be old enough at its eastern end to have a dry bed full of nesting birds where it began in the west. It travels by picking up the breaths of the marshes ahead of it. First the water in the leaf tubes goes still; then, over an area larger than a city, it begins to climb the insides of the tubes in beads. The birds feel the change through their feet. They leave in such numbers that for an hour the air above the reeds sounds like someone shaking out heavy linen, fold after fold. After they have gone there is time to finish a meal, get the goats onto stone, and take the felt covers off the spillway bells. Then the rain arrives, hard and warm, flattening the pasture against the water. Fish swim straight across what was, a minute before, a bank.

There are engine houses on the low divides. They stand a great distance apart, squat porcelain buildings with roofs worn smooth by the wet bellies of roosting birds. Inside each is a wheel sunk edgewise through the floor into a running seam. A belt as wide as a bed takes power from it. Most of the power goes back out through cables laid in the old watercourses, to the lifts which haul salt and clay up the face of the tableland; a little goes to keeping the house dry. There are no lamps. The machinists know a slipping belt by the new, soft syllable it puts into the house's tremendous drumming. Each handle on the governor has a different shape, pear, fist, bent finger. On the doorframe, polished away almost to nothing, are the dents made by generations of people tapping the mud out of their boots with the same iron stick.

For a few miles around a house the marsh keeps engine time. A long-legged grazer puts its foot down on the beat, when the tremor of its step will carry least distinctly through the mat. Underneath, an animal like a broad sole follows the little traveling dent. It does not eat the grazer. It eats the white grubs pushed out of their tunnels by the weight. When a wheel stops for servicing, the change is extraordinary. Thousands of feet stand still. One hears the rain again, each drop punching its own small, hollow note out of the flooded ground, and beneath that the deep ripping noise of a piece of pasture parting from its roots. The freed islands go revolving down the channels with their birds and their pools and sometimes a bewildered goat still aboard. They fetch up against a sill of rock and grow fast there. A path learned in childhood can end in waist-deep water on the walk home.

Only the great divides can be trusted. Their tops are bare, warm to the palm, grooved in the direction that water took before there were roots here to slow it down. On one of these backs a traveler can lie with an ear against the stone and hear several weathers at once: a hard fine rustle from nearby, the blow of falling timber transmitted up from the hanging woods, and at long intervals a sustained bass shudder. That last is water going over the southern rim, so far off that no one at the engine houses has ever made the journey. It is quite distinct from the machinery. Between one shudder and the next there is just time to breathe in. The people who sleep on stone get used to breathing out with it, and find it hard to sleep anywhere else.
