The summit has a loose tooth of black glass. It rocks a little in the afternoon, when the sun is on its western face. Under it is a hollow about as long as a thumb, with a floor of hot gray grit. A city occupies the hollow. From the lip you can see nearly all the way across it, though at first the only evidence of building is a pale line on the far wall, like the mark left by the water in a cup.

That is the upper street. Its pavement is set edgewise into the wall; people walking there would have the roof on their left and the floor on their right. Farther down are two shorter streets and a flight of public steps where down changes by a quarter turn. The steps were necessary because of the original survey. Each district was granted the right to its own vertical, and by the time the districts joined, too many deep foundations had been laid to make an alteration worthwhile. A ball set on the top step would roll down three risers, come to rest against the fourth, and stay there until somebody from the other district came to fetch it.

Almost everything is built flush. There are doors under the pale paving, drain covers of hammered platinum, narrow squares where the grit has been fused and polished in place. No tower rises to proclaim a center. The center is a patch of empty air the size of a lentil, reserved in perpetuity for turning large objects around. At certain hours a warm current goes through it and lifts a single grain, which revolves with great dignity before touching down. There is no one to turn anything larger now.

It is very high up. When the tooth shifts, a blade of daylight enters under its edge, and for a moment there is a view past the last house to the curved blue back of the atmosphere. No neighboring peak cuts that line. Then the tooth settles, the light goes the color of old pewter, and the most distant thing visible is a chisel mark across the square. The mountain is young enough that the two ends of this mark still fit together. They are drawing apart. Between them is a strip of new obsidian thinner than paper, without a scratch on it.

Water comes chiefly by night. The glass above cools fast; the rock below stays hot enough to burn skin. Vapor from a thread of brine deep in the mountain climbs through the foundations and beads on the ceiling. There is usually only one drop big enough to fall. Its coming can be followed along the upper street by the round black shadow that travels ahead of it. At the boundary between districts it lets go sideways and lands in the municipal catch basin with a sound like a fingernail touching a tooth.

For a few minutes after that the city is busy. The weight of the drop tips the basin. A film runs out through the laundries and the baths, sheets across the sloped floor of the market, and goes into a row of little drinking niches, filling them in order. Hairline gates open and close behind it. In the dimness its progress is visible less as a shine than as a deepening of color: ash, slate, ink. Nothing drinks. The heat takes the water back, starting at the shallow end of each niche, and leaves a white crescent of salt just under the rim. The oldest crescents are almost thick enough to close the niches. In one there is just a pinhole left, and the water swells out of it in a clear knob that holds for a surprisingly long time.

There are no roots in the drains, no flies at the baths. What has the run of the streets is a thin succession of mineral skins. Copper blushes green for an hour beneath the basin. A black crust, dampened, comes loose in little maps; the current carries these as far as the market, where they dry flat and become part of the pavement. Out on the bare grit nothing much changes. Two chips that fell from the same fracture lie a whole square apart, both still sharp enough to score the glass they came from.

Near morning the exhaust flue gives a soft click. This is the quietest part of the works and the last still keeping accurate time. It opens onto the peak through what, from outside, looks like a flaw in the tooth, and vents a little of the accumulated heat. Sometimes the air out there is cold enough for the escaping vapor to freeze. A white whisker grows on the black summit, pointing straight out over miles of empty sky. By sunrise it is gone. Below, in the dark, the basin hangs tipped on its pivot. It takes all day for the last dampness to leave its hinge and the little bowl to swing level again.
