The first thing a surveyor learns on the Table is to put a hand down. The rock is warm, about the temperature of the inside of a coat. If there are fine parallel ribs under the palm, the surveyor is on a street. If the rock is smooth, there is no telling. Some of the oldest streets have gone smooth too.

The Table stands nearly four miles above the low country. It has much the same profile as a coin worn too long in a pocket: two broad faces and very little left of the design on either one. Its upper face is iron-grey basalt, lapped almost level by ages of wind carrying hard grains. The wind has largely spent itself. A few of the grains still lie in the hollows, bright red when you bring a lamp close. There is no sun to make them red at any useful distance. Overhead is a thick lid of soot-coloured gas, and above that, according to instruments, a perfectly ordinary day.

Across the middle sixty miles of the Table lies the city of Measures. There is a station, a foundry ward, a freight exchange, a residential district with numbered courts. Between any two of these you could walk for hours without coming near a wall. It is only when you descend a stair that the place takes on the proportions of a city. At the foot of the stair are six or eight streets, close set, with upper storeys joined by little bridges; under those there is usually another street. The builders quarried down for warmth and used what they took out to roof the cut. In the course of time the roofs wore flush with the surrounding land. A walker crossing forty acres of apparent basalt may be passing over tenements the whole way. Here and there a ceramic chimney cap projects an inch or two, like a button sewn very securely to the ground.

Nothing up here runs on water. In the machine halls, sealed engines run on the expansion of a pale oil between the hot lower rock and the merely warm rock above. They go very slowly. In Number Three Foundry a flywheel as tall as a house takes most of a minute to bring the same chalk mark round again. The bearings are so well bedded that the mark appears to travel without the wheel. You can stand beside it and hear, quite distinctly, somebody in the next hall setting down a spanner.

Measures makes flat things. Gauge faces, press beds, reference plates for distant workshops. Long before the present streets were cut, the inhabitants discovered that the old surface of the Table was truer across great distances than any plane they could manufacture. They have been working from it ever since. Certain stretches of what seems to be empty country are, in fact, the city's principal equipment. No chimney may be driven through them. No one may put down a hot load there, even for a moment. A freight line approaching one such tract describes an extravagant curve through nine miles of unremarkable darkness to spare a strip of bare stone the width of a kitchen.

Light is used at the point of work. A turner wears a tiny amber bulb clipped to one knuckle; a reader slides a luminous rule down the page. On the surface there are no lamps at all. At intervals along the main routes stand hollow posts with a bead of loose metal inside. Touch one and it answers with a note indicating the district. There is a particular pleasure, after a long crossing, in hearing the low double knock of the residential courts and finding, a little farther on, the first stair worn hollow in the middle.

A few animals inhabit the rim. They are flat, jointed things about the size of shirt buttons, with milky shells and a fringe of feet all the way round. They graze on the grey chemical skin that forms where mineral gas seeps through a fault. A colony may take a century to clean a patch as big as a door. Behind them the stone is dull; ahead of them it has a greasy lustre. Children are brought out with hand lamps to see the difference. There is very little else a child can be shown here that has plainly happened *since last time*.

At the western edge the land rounds away so gently that the city has laid a raised cord across the approaches. Beyond it the last few yards are as comfortable underfoot as any pavement in Measures. Then there is a lip, and below the lip a fall of several miles, quite invisible. Sit on the safe side of the cord for a while. The stone gives up its steady warmth through the seat of your clothes. Far down, a haze of paler gas makes the first suggestion of distance the eye has had all day. Somewhere behind you a freight bearing passes over a joint. The small click comes up through the rock, clearer through the heel than through the ear, and you can follow the load for a little way on its long journey round the empty strip.
