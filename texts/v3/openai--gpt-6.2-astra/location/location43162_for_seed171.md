You can put a hand under the moss and touch the mountain coming up. The stone is cool, faintly yielding, as close-grained as the cut face of a pear. Keep your hand there a while and you will feel a small, steady lift. Water wells between your fingers. When you take them away the moss knits over the wet patch, and in a few minutes it is difficult to find again.

This is high country, though there are scarcely any prospects. What looks from a little distance like a good bare shoulder from which to see the land turns out, when you gain it, to be covered in waist-deep silver leaves. Beyond it is a higher shoulder of the same stuff. Once in a while the weather opens along the length of a fold and you see a hundred miles of these backs, green-black on their windward sides, silver on their lee sides, running out under a low ceiling of cloud. Their ends have never been seen from here. Somewhere far off it is already afternoon; a band of leaves has turned its dark face to the light, and for a moment there is a purple meridian across the country.

Almost all the water is a finger's breadth below the surface. There are lakes you could cross without knowing it except for the long, sickening give, several steps behind you, as the weight you put on the floating turf works its way out. In the hollows, pale stems grow so close together that the water stands up between them in sheets. The upper edges of the sheets tremble in the wind. Nothing swims in them. If the light comes right you can look through a whole series of these upright panes and see one orange seed, multiplied, hanging in the green dusk.

The noise makes the distance hard to judge. What you take at first for the fall of a great river just over the ridge is the combined sound of several billion little cups of water emptying into the cups below them. Each makes quite a pretty, hollow *tock*. There is no approach to the source, no point on the slope at which it gets much louder. Under it is the bass note of air being pressed out of the turf by the rising stone. Where two folds converge, the note beats against itself so hard that drops jump on the leaves. You could stand next to someone there and see their mouth moving and hear nothing of what they said.

Yet the finer plants, the ones that would be torn to floss by all this heaving, stand perfectly still. They grow in patches of exquisite delicacy: red hairs, each tipped with a globule of clear sap, a little frost garden in the middle of a shaking bog. The thick roots beneath them have a property peculiar to the plateau. A root can take a motion out of the place where it happens and finish it somewhere else. The red hairs don't sway; a stand of jointed reeds three valleys away inclines instead. There need be no continuous root between the two. Severed ends laid side by side in the mud will go on sharing a pulse until one of them dies.

Much of the shape of the country comes of this. A heavy rain on the western slopes is borne in part by dry ground in the east, which sags before a cloud has appeared there. Water collects in the sag. Rushes grow in the water, their roots find partners farther east again, and in the course of a season a new line of ponds comes into being in country where it has scarcely rained. Seen from high enough up, each of these chains is a loose, wandering likeness of a storm that passed months ago. On the ground it is simply a succession of cold, crowded gardens, each with its own depth of shade, its own pitch of thunder.

There is very little broken stone. The ridges still have the soft seams where one upwelling met the next. No gravel has yet had time to gather at their feet. At the youngest seam the leaves draw apart for an hour or so, and there is a strip of uncovered mineral, almost painfully pale in all that dimness. Mist settles on it. Tiny green scales wash in on the mist and lie flat. By evening they have put out transparent margins; by morning the margins overlap like the skin on the back of a hand. The next rain runs off green.

In the long twilight the silver leaves fold edgewise. For the first time there is room to look between the stems, down into the lit chambers of water and the long ochre cables submerged in them. The temperature falls a few degrees. Far across the plateau a whole slope takes on the weight of the night's dew and dips, slowly, with the grace of a bough taking snow. Its partner rises here. There is a deep slopping sound underfoot; the moss comes up to meet your sole. For a little while you stand higher than the silver leaves and can see, beyond the last folds you knew about, another weather coming in.
