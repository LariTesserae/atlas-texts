There is a place on the eastern rim where you can put one hand in warm water and look down between your feet at clouds. The rock is black and thin at this point, tilted a little inward, so that water comes within a finger’s breadth of the edge and stays there. Across the gap, on the next tooth of the rim, grass grows in a crack on the dry side. A single blade of it extends over what must be half a mile of air.

Turn inland and there is no impression of height at all. There is a brown-green distance, quite level, with islands in it. It takes a while to see that the nearest island is the tip of a ridge and that the ridge goes on, visible under the water as a long bright crease, to join another island much farther off. A whole tract of young volcanic country has filled nearly to the tops of its hills. The water is clear over the heights and the color of steeped bark in the valleys. Here and there a nameable shape shows through the brown: a shelf, a saddle, the open mouth of a ravine. Then a little change in the angle of the sun puts the sky over everything again.

Most of the green is below the surface. There are meadows down there of a fleshy, jointed plant with leaves in opposed pairs, each pair turned crosswise to the pair below. Viewed from above, a single shoot looks like a small green star; a slope covered in them has the close, directionless nap of moss. On the steeper ground they grow longer. Their lower leaves die for want of light and leave pale notches, so that a swimmer passing beneath the tops would find a wood of bare, ringed stems. Between the stems hang the brown transparent remnants of last year’s growth. Nothing carries them away. Some are so delicately decayed that only the veins remain, still joined at the stalk.

The ground under this softness is exceedingly rough. Where a ledge has fallen, the break is gray and clean, and chips lie in the exact order in which they came off, biggest first, the fine splinters a little farther down the incline. In some places the plants have grown around a sharp flake from either side and met beyond it, leaving the blade enclosed in a clear green leaf. Farther out, the slopes descend beyond the reach of rooted growth. Loose leaves settle there in drifts that preserve every fold. A white fish lives over those drifts. One can stand on the same headland for days without seeing it, then see its whole leisurely passage across a patch of dark bottom, fins almost idle, shadow following at a distance.

Warm springs feed the lake through seams deep in the valleys. Above each seam the water has a faint flaw in it. A submerged ridge seen through the rising warmth seems to come apart at a joint, the two ends wandering a little before they fit again. These places grow great floating beds of round leaves, yellow underneath, with roots that descend in red tassels. The beds travel, but too slowly for the eye to follow. A bed that shades a particular shoulder of rock in the wet months will be found late in the dry months with its outer leaves resting on the shoulder, and all the fine red ends laid along the stone in the same direction. Flowers open there in ordinary full daylight: small, dun-colored, almost lost among the enormous leaves.

The lake drains through the mountain rather than over its lip. On the outer face, far below the waterline, damp bands run through the black rock. Ferns pick out these bands so exactly that from across the gulf they look like green strata in the mountain itself. Water gathers at their tips. In the cloud belt the drops scarcely have room to fall before another frond receives them. Up on the rim almost none of this can be heard. The most distinct sound on a still afternoon is a little tack, minutes apart, where two stiff floating leaves have come edge to edge in a cove.

Toward evening the shadow of the western rim crosses the drowned country. For a few minutes the glare is gone from the shallows while there is still light enough to see into them. Then the scale of the place is plain: meadow after meadow tipping away into depth, a bare pass no wider than a foot, beyond it another green hillside rising out of brown obscurity. Air and water are at nearly the same warmth. A stem comes up through the surface bearing its last submerged pair of leaves and its first pair of dry ones scarcely an inch apart. On the lower pair a bead of air shines. On the upper pair there is a drop of water.
