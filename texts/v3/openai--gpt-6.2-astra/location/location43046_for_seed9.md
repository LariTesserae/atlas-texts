At the upper door there is a boot scraper, though what comes off on it is mostly green felt, with a few white grains like coarse salt. Beyond it the whole garden slopes down to a sill of yellow tile. You can see over the sill to another ridge, miles away, where a road goes in and out of cloud. There is room on this slope for seven short terraces. The lowest is scarcely wide enough to turn a wheelbarrow on.

It is warm enough to go in shirtsleeves. Waste heat comes up from the winding house below, unevenly distributed by a floor of hollow brick. Along the track of the main steam pipe, ginger grows to shoulder height; two feet to either side it gives way to a low, very wet meadow. The meadow is laid on wool. Where a corner has come unpegged, the wool hangs out over the path, brown at the roots of the grass and still blue at the hem. It used to be a blanket. So little here has had time to wear smooth that even under water you can read the raised fist and star of the works that cast the drain covers.

The roof is a single plane of wired glass pitched towards the mountain. A narrow opening all along its uphill edge admits the cloud. Some mornings cloud comes through in a body, at about the height of the boot scraper, and all seven terraces seem to be growing out of the same shallow white pond. By noon it is usually clear. Sun falls quite ordinarily on the beans and on the square red tiles of the path; the beans have put out those long blind shoots which, having found nothing overhead, curl down and take hold of one another. Drops from the roof travel the length of the wires in the glass before falling, so the scallions below get watered in precise grey lines.

Under the sill, bolted to the outside wall, a little carriage runs on a rail. Every afternoon it makes one journey along the wall and back. It carries a hopper of crushed white brick, a spool of wire cloth and a broad brass mouth through which a thin lime slurry continually wells. It is making more sill. Each journey adds about the thickness of a fingernail to the outward edge. The wire shows through the new work like the pattern on a wafer. There is no hurry about any of this. Last summer a pencil left on the sill fell to the valley floor; this summer, dropped from the same spot, it would land on a thumb's breadth of warm pink moss.

There is always something going on, and almost nothing to hear. The carriage draws its power from the winding cable and only works when a load is coming up the mountain. You know that the skip has reached the top when the bright bead at the brass mouth swells and stays there. Water rises through wicks from one terrace to the next, against the visible slope. In the heat over the pipe, little brown seed cases twist open, a few at a time. Once in a while a bird comes in through the cloud slot. It is generally a pipit, bedraggled, astonished by the glass, but willing after a minute to walk between the rows and pick the grass seed out of the wool. There are no resident birds. The small feather caught in the bean shoots has been there since March.

The best place to sit is on the top step, before the path turns and the ginger closes over it. From there you can look down the successive greens to the yellow sill and see, beyond it, the undersides of passing clouds. A square yard of meadow near your foot rises and subsides when the winding drum starts. Everything rides on the same girders: the step, the garden, the little pool with a skin of duckweed in the bowl of an old lamp. For a moment the duckweed parts down the middle. Then the load passes, the water settles, and the two green shores come gently back together, leaving just the width of a wire full of sky.
