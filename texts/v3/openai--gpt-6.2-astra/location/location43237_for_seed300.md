The track ends at a stone lip with two iron sockets in it, where a gate would swing if there were anything here to keep in. From the lip you can see the whole hollow: an oval of wet grey ground about the size of a goods yard, a brick pump house at the narrow end, and beyond it the grassed bank that keeps the estuary from coming in overland. The bank is just high enough to hide boats. Sometimes a white cabin roof goes by above the grass, steadily inland, as if on a road.

Down in the hollow, sheets of shale stand on edge. Their ends have worn to much the same height, a little below the lip of the track. From a distance it looks possible to walk straight across. Up close there is hardly a place to put a boot. Each blade has a low saddle worn into it, and the saddles line up obliquely to make hundreds of little cross-passages. There are black stones wedged in these, slips of oyster shell, rope fibres, old brown glass gone opaque at the edges. In one passage a length of chain has been there long enough for each link to have a bed of its own. Nothing grows on the blades except a dark green nap along the north faces. There is one excellent clump of thrift on the pump-house step, round as a cushion, fed by whatever leaks out under the door.

The estuary comes in underground. On a rising tide the first evidence is a change in the light between the blades. The deep slots, which were black enough to lose things in, acquire pieces of sky. Then you notice that these pieces are travelling, some towards the bank and some away from it. Water from below finds its way up through seams in the rock under a skin of rainwater left by the night. For a while the two waters keep their separate manners. A chip of wood on the surface goes slowly towards the pump house; a ribbon of weed an inch below it streams towards the track.

Once the water reaches the saddles, the whole floor gets busy. A finger-wide run spills sideways into a long dry slot. Within a minute that slot is full and spilling *back* through the same gap, having found a stronger supply at its far end. A shoal of bubbles travels twenty feet, turns a corner together and disappears into a hole no bigger than a keyhole. Little whirlpools form in the eyes of the chain. There are dozens of changes going on within arm’s reach, yet almost no noise. The drops are short, the lips are smooth, and most of the work happens under a level shining surface. The clearest sound is a slight gritty click when a stone turns over in its lodging. On a still day you can hear that from the gate.

The pump house used to drain a cutting on the landward side. Through the wired glass you can see the green motor on its concrete plinth and, on a shelf, three spare drive belts nested one inside another. The current has been disconnected for years. Outside, a float gauge is still at work in a perforated iron tube. Its enamel hand climbs a white dial marked in feet. Where the paint has flaked, somebody has pencilled in the missing figures, taking great care with the fractions. The tube damps out the surges, so the hand moves with immense deliberation while the ground around its foot is changing its mind every few seconds.

It is cool here even in full daylight. The shale comes up wet beneath your palm however long the sun has been on it. Now and then a rock pipit lands on the lip and waits, head cocked, for one particular ledge to clear. There are pale sand hoppers stranded there when the water falls away. The bird takes two or three and is off over the bank. For the rest of the afternoon the only movement above knee height may be that slow white dial.

At the top of the tide the blades nearly vanish. For ten minutes the hollow is a plain oval pool, with a brick building standing doubled at one end. Then a single long back of stone breaks the reflection. Others emerge beside it, glossy at first, drying to stripes of pearl grey. All the intricate traffic starts up again, by other routes. By evening the chain is lying exposed in its old impressions and the passages appear to be empty. If you kneel at the end of the track and look level with the stone, there is still a wrinkle of water coming and going through the lower of the two gate sockets, polishing its square corners round.
