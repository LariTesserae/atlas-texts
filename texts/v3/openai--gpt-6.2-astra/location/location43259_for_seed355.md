The streets are the last places to go dry. Long after the water has slipped out of the houses, it stands in the streets, black and warm, with a skin on it that wrinkles from one end of a block to the other. A curb shows here and there, worn to the shape of a lower lip. There is just enough light to see the wet edge of it. Beyond that, one has to feel the slope of the ground through the soles of the feet.

The city takes up most of a shallow basin. Its districts are widely spaced. Between the bath quarter and the low rows of houses to the east there is nearly a mile of bare silt, crossed by a raised walk with turnings for neighborhoods that have long since washed away. In a few places the walk widens to make room for a bench. The benches are continuous with the walk; the walk is continuous with the chalky stone beneath the silt. There are no joints anywhere, though there are things that look very much like repairs: a second, slightly crooked flight of steps laid over a flight whose treads have been worn into a slide.

It becomes easier to see when the heat gathers overhead. The air goes a very deep brown, the color visible at the bottom of a shut eyelid. Against it a roof can be distinguished, then three roofs, then the long backs of the public halls. Their eaves shed drops almost without pause. Inside, the rooms are cooler by a degree or two, and perfectly bare. One hall has a floor pitched gently toward the middle, where there is a shallow hollow about the size of a cupped hand. A single tuft of rush grows there. Its blades are white at the tips. Once in a great while a tip touches a drop on its way down and the whole tuft leans over with the weight.

There is a tide here, although the basin has no opening to the sea. At the beginning of the rise, the water in a street remains at exactly the same height on the curb. What changes is the distance to the opposite curb. A narrow lane broadens until there is room for a square; water spreads across the additional ground without getting any shallower. The houses draw apart quite soundlessly. For a while each has a little shore of its own, with the soft ridges left by all the previous tides fitting one inside the next. It is possible at this stage to look down a familiar alley and see, very far off, a side of the city ordinarily hidden behind one's own back.

Only then does the water begin to climb. It comes up through the stone as well as over it. On the underside of every arch, beads swell in the pores and join, and for an hour the covered passages are wetter than the open ground. The city smells of hot salt and the damp insides of broken pots. Nothing swims out of the drains. Nothing takes advantage of the long undisturbed margins except a red film that advances by the width of a thread each wet season. In the oldest quarter it has colored an entire lintel a dark and handsome red.

At full tide there is a sound from below the bath quarter. Four or five low knocks, widely separated. Down there are more baths under the visible ones, smooth-walled basins with stone ledges for sitting neck-deep in darkness. The knocks travel through their common walls. At the last one the water everywhere sheds its skin. For a few minutes it is clear enough to show the paved bottom of the widest square and a little stair descending from the middle of it. The stair has nine steps when seen from the north side. From the south there is no stair, only the slow passage of the shed skin over unbroken paving.

The ebb takes most of the night, if night is the name for the longer of the two darknesses. Heat persists. Salt opens in soft fans on the walls. The far-apart houses approach one another again, not quite in their former order. A doorway that faced another doorway may come to rest facing a blank wall, marked at shoulder height by an oval patch of smoother stone. There are many such patches. The city has had time to accommodate almost every arrangement.

Last to empty is a little crescent of street at the edge of the silt. The water lies there scarcely deeper than a footprint, holding the faint brown of the air. Long after the other districts have settled, its two curbs continue to inch together. At length they meet, stone against worn stone, and squeeze the remaining water up between them in a clear bead that runs the whole length of the crescent. It stands there trembling. In all that expanse of dark ground, for a moment this is the only line one can see.
