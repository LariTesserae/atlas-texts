At the cloud line there is a shelf of new country, about as large as a village green. It joins the mountain at one end, where a pair of steps has been cut for people coming up. The other end is soft. In a wind it moves enough to spill water off either side. From the steps one can see its whole extent: three low pools, a patch of rushes no bigger than a bed, a tin-roofed shelter with a bench in it, and, well out toward the unsupported end, a white instrument on three legs. All three legs have their feet a little way above the ground.

The water arrives warm, through the floor. It darkens a spot, gathers a skin, then rolls away in a bead the size of a pea, leaving behind a little pale mineral collar. There are collars everywhere. Where they have grown together the going is quite firm, though it feels indecent to walk on them; they have the fine fluting and faint pink of a baby's fingernails. Elsewhere the rock has only a top to it. A dropped coin will go through and can be seen for several days, bright and slightly magnified, sinking toward whatever is going to be the bottom. Nothing here has had time to wear smooth. Even the pebbles in the pools are attached by stalks.

There is usually cloud against the underside of the shelf and cloud overhead, with a narrow gap between. The light that comes in sideways through this gap is good for seeing small things at a great distance and poor for seeing what is at one's feet. A bird crossing half a mile away shows every notch in its wing. The dozens of animals crouched in the nearest pool, however, are invisible until one of them coughs.

They are broad little swimmers with square muzzles. When startled they expel the water from under their chins all at once. One cough calls up another, and shortly the place is loud with an indignant, rather elderly barking. Then the flies lift. They have been lying flat on the water to drink, holding their wings upright in pairs like the pages of so many shut books. Now all those pages open. For a moment there is more silver than air above the pools. Birds come cutting through at knee height, braking hard at the rushes; the rushes are where the heavy spiders live, and even a bird that could tear its way out of their silk would have to spend a miserable afternoon cleaning itself.

Afterward there may be ten minutes in which the only sound is the shelter roof dripping onto the bench. Somebody has bored holes through the seat at intervals to let the water out. The oldest holes are no longer under the drips. The shelter is inching downhill, or the hill beneath it is inching out from under the shelter. A mug hangs inside on a nail, its handle neatly mended with green thread.

The instrument at the far end concerns itself with what counts as downhill. Its shadow falls straight into the mountain, independent of the light. At intervals of an hour or so it gives a deep, dismaying note, felt first in the hinge of the jaw. Water stops on its way to the edge. For perhaps two breaths the drops stand high on their leading rims. Then they run back, gathering flies, scraps of moulted skin and loose mineral in a bright advancing hem. The pools fill from the wrong side. The swimmers turn to face the current; the long-legged pool birds, which know the note, are already waiting where the food will arrive.

A person on the bench feels no change of slope. A person standing on the unfinished ground does: a distinct small shift of weight onto the heels, as if someone had taken hold of the landscape by one corner and straightened it like a rug. In that moment the soft end sets. What flexed in the last wind will ring under a knuckle in the next. Beyond it a new lip sweats out, translucent, tender, with room in it for another foot or so of world.

There is very little green. A seed arriving here has a fair chance of water and almost no chance of finding a crack older than itself. Most wash into one shallow hollow behind the steps, where generations of failed seedlings have at last made a spoonful of dark soil. The rushes grow there, leaning far out over the bare pink stone. In the evenings the swimmers climb up among their roots to lay eggs. The eggs are amber and show beautifully in that low slit of light. Under each one, by morning, there is a minute white cup where the mineral has risen around it. Long after the young have hatched and barked and learned to turn into the backward-running water, the cups remain. This is the roughest patch on the whole shelf: the first bit of ground here to have a past.
