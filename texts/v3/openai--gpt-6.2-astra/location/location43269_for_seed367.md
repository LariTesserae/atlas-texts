On the summit of the mesa there is a stone that sounds hollow when a goat steps on it. It lies level with the surrounding pavement, its upper face worn to the same dove grey, with a few grains of red sand lodged in a shallow print where a shell once was. The goat goes on. Under its hoof, for the time it took to take that step, a whole town fell quiet.

The stone is the roof of a cleft about as broad as a spread hand. One end opens on the great drop down the south face of the mesa. A lip of rock hides the opening from above, and a fringe of grass hides it from below. The grass grows upside down out of a seam. Its blades fold lengthwise in the heat, and the light that comes through them falls in green and straw-coloured stripes across the floor. Farther in, there is just enough light to see a moving thing but not to be sure, once it stops, where it has gone.

It is very old ground. The soft beds between the hard beds have long since blown away; all the ledges that were going to fall easily have fallen. Even inside the cleft, where a sharp edge might have lasted, the sills have the easy curves of bones in a palm. A little red dust comes in each afternoon and a little goes out. There is a bank of it against the back wall with the flared foot of a grass root showing through. It looks freshly laid until one notices the pale, empty snail shells in it, each exposed on one side and worn on that side almost to the spiral within.

The town occupies the middle sill. Its builders are small brown ants with honey-coloured legs. From the mesa top one sees scarcely an ant all morning; down here it is difficult to find a patch of stone across which one could draw a line without touching one. Their streets have roofs of grass chaff, shingled narrow end to broad end and held down with grit. The grain stores are in the dry upper tier. A seed too long to turn the corner there has to be taken out into the street, stripped of its awn, and brought in again. Below are warm shelves crowded with pale young, and below those a sloping refuse tip, rich in husks, shed skins, dead workers and small, extraordinarily lively beetles. Everything is carried. A red pebble over the main entrance has the smooth underside and rough upper side of a lintel, but it is an ordinary pebble that took most of a day to get uphill.

There is no spring here, no dark stain on the rock. A loose husk stays crisp enough to crackle under six small feet. Yet at the mouth the growth is so thick that a long-legged spider crosses from one side to the other without setting foot on stone. Silver rosettes fill the pockets between grass clumps. A creeper with fleshy paired leaves has come round from the outer face and put out short white roots wherever a joint touches dust. Much of what looks like low copper brush behind it is the exposed root beard of a single plant growing somewhere in the glare above. In the brief season when clouds drag across the summit, the outer leaves comb a little moisture out of them. For the rest of the year the cleft lives on what the plants have kept. Scale insects stud the creeper stems; the ants visit them at the tender joints. A dropped leaf is eaten from the inside out until its two skins lie together like a little empty purse.

By afternoon the roof holds a pleasant, steady heat. This is the busiest hour. Mites the colour of sealing wax pick their way among the hairs of the roots. A blind cricket shoulders out of its slot, feels an ant road with the tips of its two long feelers, and steps clear across it. There are flat spiders under the flakes of mica and round spiders in the grass and one dust-coloured lizard that comes only as far in as its head will fit. Its arrival sends a running break through the traffic. Workers nearest the mouth bolt under the chaff roofs; farther back, where nothing has been seen, a seed continues uphill. For a moment the lizard holds a struggling cricket sideways in its jaws. Then the head withdraws and the long feelers, trailing behind, draw two fine lines in the dust.

The place has a bigger voice than one would expect. Wind passing the lip makes a low bottle note. Seed wings caught in the grass chatter against each other. When the cricket chorus starts up in earnest, the thin roof takes the vibration and gives it back, so that a singer tucked alone behind a root seems to occupy the whole warm dark. Every so often the light at the mouth flashes white. A gust has lifted all the grass blades together. In that instant, from the outermost doorstep of the town, there is a view across eighty miles of sunstruck air to the blue foot of another mesa. Then the blades settle, the stripes return to the floor, and two workers come out to fetch the awn somebody left in the street.
