The street is dry down the middle. A little to either side of the crown the paving darkens, and at the foot of the houses there is an inch of clear water. Lime has come out of the water and made a pale lip along each gutter. In places the lip has grown higher than the doorstep it was meant to keep dry. A threshold goes down into blue water; four feet above it, in the face of the wall, is the sill of what was once a perfectly ordinary window. You could sit on it now and dabble your heels inside the house.

The city occupies the low side of a high plain. It has no single outlook from which you can see its extent. From the roof of a bathhouse you can see the roofs below, the silver bend of a channel, the beginning of a long stone embankment. On the next rise there are houses again. Beyond those, a bright seam which looks like the edge of the plain turns out to be sunlight on a second embankment. It takes most of a morning on foot to come to the end of the bathhouse quarter. By then a bank of cloud which was lying level with the roofs ahead has risen, dropped its rain, and gone on without ever shading the street you walked.

Very little grows high enough to interrupt the view. There is a russet grass that puts down roots in wet mortar. Its blades all have a crook at the same height from spending their first weeks bent over under snow. A few willows stand in the lee of the embankments, broad and scarcely taller than a person, with the dead twigs still caught in the living ones. Out on the plain are whole square miles of exposed limestone, gray in the dry and the color of a mussel shell in the wet. Sunlight on it is quite strong. Even in summer you want something warm across your shoulders when you step into shade.

Water arrives here by two routes, and seldom at the same time. Rain runs quickly off the bare upper plain. It fills the open channels with a thin brown wash that leaves a fringe of grit on every inside bend. Then come several fine days. People take their wool out to air on the parapets. The gutters clear. It is often on the finest of those days that the springs begin to rise. Far back in the hills, rain has been making its slower way down through the stone. In the bathhouses you can watch a dark sixpence of sand tremble on the bottom of a pool, open in the middle, settle, then open again. By supper a clear current is pouring over the rim. Children float last autumn’s willow leaves in it and go down three flights of steps to see whose comes out first.

Much of what looks like abandoned housing at street level is occupied a story up. There are back ways between the houses, little landings shared by four or five kitchens, stairs worn into a lean by generations taking the inside turn. No one has tried to drain the whole place. When a lower room becomes reliably wet it is useful for keeping crocks of milk cool; when it gets too deep for that, a flat stone is laid across the doorway and the stairs come down to a new landing. In the oldest quarters a person carrying a basket can cross a dozen streets without once coming down to the original paving. Underfoot there is a mild smell of cold lime. From an opening by your ankle comes the perfectly distinct sound of somebody rinsing a bowl in a room whose roof you are standing on.

For so much masonry, so much moving water, it is quiet. The channels were given only just enough fall. At their junctions two smooth skins of water tuck one under the other with hardly a ripple. Sound travels best through the walls: a footfall at one end of a terrace, the knock of a crock being set down. Once in a while a water rail walks out of a drain. It has surprisingly big red legs. For a moment its reflection doubles the bird, then it turns sideways into a crack that you would have sworn could not admit a hand.

Toward the lower end the plain widens. The houses thin out but the embankments go on, low enough now that a child can scramble up either side. Between them lie shallow sheets of water, some receiving rain, some quite still under a clear sky. From here at last it is possible to look back and understand a little of the city’s shape. The high, closely packed quarters follow not the old riverbeds but the strips of ground *between* them. The broad streets with their handsome curves were streams before anybody paved them. In wet years they remember the bends exactly.

By late afternoon there are showers standing all along the horizon, each dark at the bottom and sunlit on one side. Their light moves over the roofs in great slow sections. Near at hand the smallest changes are already under way: grit sliding a finger’s breadth along a gutter, the wet mark climbing the bathhouse stair, a woman on a landing drawing in the last length of wool. She folds it against her chest. The parapet stays dry for another minute where it lay.
