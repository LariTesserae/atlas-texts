At the top of the stair there is one dry step. The next is under an inch of warm water, and after that the stair goes down by such shallow degrees that a crab can walk the whole way to the bottom without ever having to climb down a riser. The stone has worn to a long buckle, a little higher on either side than in the middle. Where the crab walks, its feet make six dark marks. The silt settles back into them before it has gone very far.

This is the summit cistern. Its outer rim is a little above the usual height of the clouds. For much of the year there is cloud on the stair outside and clear water on the stair within, with the dry step, polished the colour of a tooth, between them. Nothing of the summit itself shows here. The floor is laid with close-set slabs, the walls are built in diminishing rings, and even the ceiling, where one might expect to find the underside of a great boulder, is a vault of narrow tiles. Each tile was made by folding wet clay over a thigh. At the crown of the vault they are small enough to have been made over a child's.

Almost the whole vault is full. A thumb's breadth of air lies along its highest seam. Light comes in low through the doorway, travels down the stair, and ends on a single pale slab. In that light one can see shellfish opening by fractions. Beyond it there is room for perhaps a dozen people to stand abreast; there is no way to see from one end to the other. The most serviceable map of the place would be one made by hand in the dark: smooth floor, then a row of blunt knobs, then an interval of remarkably soft mud, then the lip of a trough. What feel like knobs are the remains of a partition. At one time there were separate basins here. Now a fish can pass over the walls between them without so much as tipping its head.

The water enters from below. The builders brought a warm seep up the last few feet of the mountain in a square pipe of four long stones. It still issues among the floor slabs with just enough force to keep a patch the size of a saucer swept clean. There is a grey skin around this patch. Tiny pale animals keep their mouths against it, grazing the living film that feeds on the minerals in the seep. Their backs shiver continuously in the current; a finger's length away the water is quite still. Nothing puts out a leaf here. The long soft cords that hang from the joints of the vault withdraw up to a yard of themselves when touched. If one waits, the ends come down again, each with its little open fan of fingers.

There are more animals than the first look down the stair would suggest. Much of what seems to be the texture of the masonry is shell laid against shell. In the grooves between tiles live translucent shrimps, packed head to tail, making room for one another by turning on edge. A broad flat fish rests in the trough with a dusting of white grit on its upper side. Only the two clean crescents in front of its gills give it away. There are blind crabs small enough to live in the sockets left by missing pegs, and one great old crab that carries, on the plate between its shoulders, three generations of little hinged shells. It goes the circuit of the room so slowly that the fans overhead open again before its hind legs have passed.

The place has a sound, though it takes a while to separate it from the pulse in one's own ears. It is the rubbing of shell edges against the clay. At long intervals there is a clearer note. An air bead from the seep has travelled the length of a sloping tile and arrived in the air at the crown. The tile gives a tiny *tik*. There are a great many beads that never make this journey: they lodge in hollows, shining there in absolute darkness, until they go back into solution.

Near the doorway the old masons left a gauge, a vertical strip of red pottery incised with lines. The bottom lines have been worn away. So have the middle ones. Only the highest mark can still be felt, just above the water, where a thumbnail drawn down the strip will catch. Once in a few seasons, when the clouds stand deep over the summit for weeks, the water rises past it. For that time there is no dry step. A thin sheet goes over the sill and down the outer stair, dividing at every worn tread and joining again below. A few shrimps go with it. Then the sheet narrows to a thread, the tooth-coloured stone dries from the edges inward, and the great crab, somewhere in the dark on the other side, comes round again to the foot of the stair.
