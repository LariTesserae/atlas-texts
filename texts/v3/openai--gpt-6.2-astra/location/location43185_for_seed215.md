By noon the rain has passed, and the high plain is full of little lakes too new to have muddy bottoms. You can see every chip of stone under the water. A flock of dunlin is feeding in one of them, moving steadily east, the birds at the rear flying a few yards to take their turn at the front. There is nothing for them to dig for yet. They pick drowned flies off the surface. Behind them the water is clean enough to show the shadow of each bird that passes over it.

The plain stands high enough for snow to lie through summer on the southern faces of buildings. It has been rising for nearly nine hundred years. From the rim you look down a mile of successive gray ledges to a belt of cloud, and through holes in the cloud to old farmland, brown rivers, a sea. Up here there is scarcely a river worth naming. The rain starts out across the rock in sheets, gathers itself into a promising channel, then comes to a fresh lip of stone two fingers high and has to begin again in another direction. A watershed can change in a night. People who go out to count the birds take dry socks even when the forecast is good.

The city occupies most of the plain, although from any one place it looks like a town of perhaps six streets with another town a long way off. Its builders left room for the ground to move. Each district stands on its own raft: apartment courts, school, shops and all, on a slab with a clean dark underside that clears the highest point of the rock by the width of a hand. When the rock rises the slab gives way before it, without a jolt. At the edge of a court you can lie on your belly and look under an entire neighborhood. There are lost balls under there in impeccable condition, far beyond the reach of any stick.

Between districts the passenger cars run a few inches above the wet stone. Seen from a distance, a car is a yellow rectangle crossing twenty miles of slate, vanishing now and then into a shower. The lines meet at intervals in roofed exchanges where there are flower stalls and lunch counters and warm rooms for people waiting out the weather. One exchange has stood empty for six years because a lake formed around it. The cars still stop there on request. In April the old departures hall is a good place to watch diving ducks through the floor-length glass; they come up with small black shellfish and lie high in the water while they swallow.

Most of the green is down in the seams. There is a rush that grows wherever grit has collected to the depth of a thumbnail, and a close silver turf on the older ground, cropped so short by geese that a dropped key stays in sight. The geese arrive from below in relays. On a clear morning you can watch one flock circling over the farmlands far beneath you, another laboring up through the cloud, and a third already level with the bakery windows, feet extended for the landing. Much of what feeds the plain comes up this way, in bellies and feathers, as seeds stuck to a wet leg. Foxes make the journey in passenger cars. They board at the lower terminus before dawn and get off where they smell birds.

For so populous a place it is remarkably quiet. There is no loose dry stuff to rustle. The houses keep their heat in, their windows shut, and the cars make less noise than a bicycle on pavement. Close by, you hear the little ripping sound a goose makes pulling grass, or water ticking down the enamel back of a street sign. Once or twice a day a report travels through the soles of your shoes. Somewhere a bed of stone has parted. At the survey office someone puts down a cup and checks which way the water is going now.

By late afternoon the western districts are in sunlight while another storm is building out beyond the eastern exchanges. Its shadow takes an hour to cross the flats. The dunlin lift before it arrives, turning all at once from brown to white, and settle farther on. In the lake they have left, a narrow tongue of rock has broken the surface. Water beads on its back. Before the light goes, a gull steps onto it, folds one foot up into its feathers and stands there as comfortably as if the place had always been an island.
