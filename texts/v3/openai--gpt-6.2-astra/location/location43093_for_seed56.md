The first drop takes seven years to fall. It hangs from the underside of a willow root, fat as a currant, with a little beard of ice on its shaded side. By the time its weight draws it free, the root has grown down far enough that the drop has scarcely any distance to go. It lands on a leaf. The noise is astonishing.

This close beneath the meadow, one expects to be able to smell earth. Instead there is a sharp smell of split stems, and something fishy from the pools. The hollow is the size of a small orchard. At its highest point there is room to stand; at the edges the roof comes down in folds against a floor of pale winter moss, and a shrew can go where a hare cannot. Everything solid enough to put weight on is alive. Even the grit under the moss consists of hard little seeds. Some have germinated in place, splitting neatly about their middles, so that a patch of what appears to be gravel is lifting on a thousand white elbows.

The cold is severe and very even. Hoarfrost on a blade keeps the same crisp branching shape from one winter into the next. Still, there is water everywhere: in the cups made by opposed leaves, in thin dark seams behind the bark, in the footprints left by animals in the moss. The animals drink by licking the rims. A tongue thrust into the middle of a pool comes out wearing a mitten of ice. Along the rim, where the water touches living tissue, it stays fluid and tastes faintly of pear.

Most of the light comes through the willow roots. Up in the meadow their fine tips gather daylight; down here it leaks out at the forks, a greenish gleam just sufficient to distinguish the red legs of a swimming mite from its shadow. One fork shines blue at midnight. The branch belonging to it is still receiving the light of a summer afternoon many years ago. In that little wedge of blue the mites are always courting, wheeling on the surface with their front legs held out, while a finger’s breadth away the others lie torpid, glued belly-up under leaves.

There is a great deal to hear. A vole chewing somewhere overhead makes a grating like a whole slope of scree giving way. Under that runs the steady bass note of water entering the roots. Each root is hollow for part of its length, and as water displaces air the pitch descends by minute intervals. The large white moths roosting in the folds of the ceiling keep their wings pressed flat to it. Occasionally an entire fold goes bare at once: the pitch has passed out of their liking, and they go tumbling off through the dimness like bits of a sloughed skin. They settle again a yard away. Their abandoned patch is a fresher green than the rest.

What the plants are growing here, very slowly, is depth. At the narrow end of the hollow a patch of moss no larger than a hare’s bed has already grown more distance on its lower side than on its upper. A beetle pushes its head through and its abdomen follows at once, but seen from below it takes the better part of an hour to descend the length of its own antenna. It has time to browse. There are whole pastures on the down of that moss, cropped close by beetles whose hind feet are still visible above. Nothing has been cut out or carried away to make the hollow. There is simply more underneath the meadow than the meadow has top to cover.

At the advancing edge the leaves are almost transparent. Through them one can see the first grains of actual stone forming, cloudy pinpricks suspended in sap. In time there may be a bank here, then a bed for running water, perhaps even a stream with a nameable direction. For now the grains sway when a vole puts its foot down. The water gathers where the leaves incline. In the pool beneath the willow, the ring from the fallen drop travels outward until it reaches a stem, runs a little way up it, and hangs there, a bright wet collar, while the next drop begins.
