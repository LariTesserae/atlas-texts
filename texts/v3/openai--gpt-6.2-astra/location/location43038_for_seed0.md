The black ground came up through the forest in strips. Some are no wider than the trunk of a palm. Others run half a mile across, broad backs of stone with the old forest standing in the lanes between them. On one side of the basin there is a place where the stone flowed around a single fig tree and rejoined on the other side. The fig died standing. Its white crown shows above the living trees long after the rest of the valley has gone dark.

It is a warm night. From the high end of a strip you can see the stars clear down to the opposite rim of the basin, a low, uneven line as of something torn off. Down among the trees you can't see your own feet. You put a foot down and discover by the give of it that what you took for ground is a mat of stems spanning a crack. There is a considerable drop. The stone at the lip is still sharp enough to lay a finger open. A few yards on there are waist-deep drifts of leaves so thoroughly worked by beetles that a hand thrust down comes up smelling of good black soil. It is quite possible to pass from one of these kinds of country to the other in a single stride.

There is water, but very little of it lies out to be seen. A shower that would turn an ordinary path to mud goes straight through the black strips, making a diminishing noise like dry peas poured from a height. It gathers below, in the hollows where the stone set hard over running stone and the running part went on without it. Roots hang into those galleries by the thousand. The ends of the roots are white and furry and so tender that a blind shrimp can shear one off and sit turning it in its forelegs to eat. Up top, in the forks of the trees, there is another whole system of water: cups of brown leaf, curled bark, the hollow stumps of broken canes. There are frogs that are born in a spoonful of water sixty feet up and never once come down.

Tonight it is those frogs you hear first. Each makes a sound very like a thumbnail drawn across the teeth of a comb. There are too many for the ear to keep separate. Beneath their level rasp is the honking of fruit bats in the figs, and beneath that, at intervals, a profound hollow *tok* that you feel through the soles. Little rust-colored pigs are out on the stone turning over slabs for the pale crickets underneath. A pig noses an edge up, its young go in almost to the shoulder, and when they have taken what they can get, the mother lets the slab fall. All along a mile of ridge other families are at it. The fallen slabs rock a while on their beds. It sounds as if someone is trying every door in an enormous house.

Much of the forest travels by night. The great snails come out of the cane brakes and go up the smooth trunks, grazing a clean green track through the velvet on the bark. Crabs the color of spoiled plums leave the damp cracks to collect fallen fruit; they carry it high, at arm's length, to keep it from the ants. Among the crabs walk small long-legged birds. You glimpse their ankles in the weak light of the fungi and then, much higher up than you'd expect, the quick ivory flash of a bill. Nearly everything here knows how to stand so still that the moving things flow around it. A toad can sit in a stream of ants with its toes tucked under and come away without a bite. A young toad has a miserable time learning how.

Toward midnight there is a change in the noise at the far end of the basin. It comes nearer quite fast. The frogs go quiet tree by tree; the bats climb out above the crowns. For a few seconds the only sound close by is the minute, deliberate tearing of a snail's mouth on bark. Then the wind gets here. It runs easily down the open stone and strikes the walls of growth from the side. Cane whips flat. Two trees that have grown leaning against each other part for the first time in years, and a wedge of old leaves, bones, nutshells, a whole warm nest of mice, falls out between them. The mice hit the leaf mold running. Far above, in the dead white fig, a limb comes away with a report like a cliff splitting. It hangs up in the creepers before it can strike the ground.

There are a few minutes of rain, enormous separated drops that smack the leaves hard enough to turn them over. The ground hardly changes color. But the cups in the branches fill to their lips. One spills into another below it, which spills in turn; for a little while there are slender, fitful streams running down through the air among the trunks. The birds stand under them with their bills open. In the galleries below the stone the water rises by the breadth of a shrimp's back, and the shrimp move up the roots after it.

By the time the frogs start again the stars are out. The fallen limb is still settling, letting itself down a handspan at a time as the vines stretch. It will be morning before it reaches bottom. Already there is a line of ants going out along it in both directions, crossing easily in the dark from a tree they know to one they have never had a way to reach.
