The opening is under the lip of the summit, a little way below the last survey stud. It is just large enough to put your hand through sideways. There is no shelf to stand on to look in. You would have to hang there, with the soles of your boots pressed against a face of slate that continues almost vertically for a mile. From that position you would feel a surprisingly loud note in your teeth. You would see, at first, what looked like a piece of green velvet caught in a crack.

Inside is a whole basin, about the size and shape of a deep soup plate. Its floor descends in narrow benches to a shaded bottom. These are old surfaces. The slate has lost its cutting edges; even the little flights of steps where one bed ends against another have the comfortable bluntness of much-used stairs. In the age before the green stuff took hold, dust driven in through the opening went round and round in here. There are still three white pebbles lodged together at the bottom of a beautifully turned hollow. There has never been water in it.

What looks like the nap of the velvet is the uppermost of several gardens. The leaves up there are round, scarcely bigger than pinheads, dark green with a pewter rim. They fit edge to edge. Farther down, where none of the dim silver light from the opening can reach, there are great cream-colored fans, great in the sense that one fan roofs a dozen stems. Under the fans is a low growth the color of split peas. It goes into the seams of the slate and comes out on the undersides of the benches, where it hangs in tightly crimped skirts. There is no good place in the basin to put the point of a pencil without bruising something.

The plants are dry all the way through. A broken leaf would show thin bright laminae, like the leaves of a book with all the print on the inner faces. Their roots take up metal from the stone a grain at a time. It is vibration they live on. At this height there is a standing note in the mountain, made by the passage of air across two deep slots on opposite faces of the peak. Down on the accessible slopes it is too low to hear. Here, where the stone thins to a lip, it sets the basin sounding like the inside of a bell. The leaves divide the note among themselves. The little round ones have the high, hard sound of a spoon ringing against china. The cream fans carry a much lower register. The buried skirts barely sound at all, but they are the reason you can feel it in your jaw.

There is one piece of machinery on the second bench. A porcelain block the length of a match supports a copper winding and a sprung iron tongue. Two insulated wires go from the winding into a drilled hole in the roof. There used to be an instrument house farther along the ridge; all that remains of it outside is a level rectangle of slightly paler rock. No current has gone down the wires for a long time, but the tongue still hums on its spring. The plants have made use of it. A stand of narrow purple blades grows to precisely the height at which their tips touch the iron. Nowhere else in the basin is there any purple.

Nothing visits. There are no shells under the leaves, no paths eaten through the close-fitting canopy. It is cool enough for a bare fingertip to take a long time warming the porcelain. The light comes off the pale face of the next peak by way of a bend in the entrance, so that even at noon there are no sharp shadows. Nor is there much distinction here between one year and another. The plants long ago filled the places where their particular pitches would sustain them. A blade that added so much as a rim to itself would fall out of tune; it gives back, through its roots, just what it takes. The three white pebbles stay where they are. The purple tips rest against the tongue. For all the noise in this pocket of the mountain, the smallest leaf, seen against the rim of the one beside it, appears perfectly still.
