From the west rim you can see the whole of the hanging valley, even the white wicker chair outside the rain house four miles away. There is nothing much at eye level to interrupt the view. Most of what lives here lives in the first six inches above or below the ground.

The valley floor is a flight of broad limestone steps. Once a river came down them, cutting a narrow waist in each sill and a round basin underneath. Now the river runs three days in a good year. The rest of the time you can walk up its bed, through warm scoops of stone the color of the inside of an almond. In some the old current has left a single hard blue pebble, too heavy to lift, with room enough around it to turn. The sky up here is a deep, almost evening blue at noon. Out past the lip of the valley, several thousand feet below, the tops of clouds go by.

Every so often, miles apart, a little rain falls upward out of those clouds. It comes up as a loose, wavering column, the drops drawing closer together as they climb. Three of the columns end in midair over the valley. A fourth comes up just inside the rain house, through a hole in the kitchen floor. There are brass screws around the hole where somebody once tried fitting a collar to it. When the column is running, you can put your hand in at ankle height and feel a cold pull as far up as your shoulder. At the height of the windowsill the drops lose their direction and make ordinary kitchen puddles. The door has been cut short at the bottom to let the water out.

Nobody has had much success moving the places where the water comes up. The stones responsible are out of reach, hanging somewhere in solid rock; every instrument lowered toward one comes back with a slightly different length of cable. What people have done instead is lay shallow gutters. These go a long way on very little fall. One skirts half a mile of perfectly bare, dazzling pavement to feed a patch of rushes no bigger than a cart. Another ends at an old trough where little tawny horses come to drink. They arrive in single file, as many as sixty, then spread out over the slope to crop plants so low and so nearly the color of the limestone that the horses seem to be licking a bare plate.

Sit down anywhere and other lives resolve themselves. The black dashes in the gutter are fish, braced on their breast fins, taking transparent grubs off the upstream sides of sand grains. What looks like a brown stocking caught on a thorn is a dozen long-eared bats asleep one on top of another. Under the slight overhang of each step is a strip of green felt with thousands of little doors in it. When a cloud passes below the sun the doors open. Bees come out, fly the few yards to the yellow penny flowers, and return before the pavement gets hot again. A lizard waits belly-up against the roof of the overhang and takes one bee in perhaps fifty. Its throat works; a pair of wings comes loose and skates a long way downhill.

Very little of this carries sound. You hear the bees if you put your head under the ledge. You hear a horse bite off a tuft, but not the rest of the herd moving. In the hot hours the clearest sound is an occasional hollow *tock* from somewhere down the staircase. For a long time it seems to come from the empty sky. Then, looking into one of the old river basins, you find a tortoise in the narrow crescent of shade beside the blue pebble. It is eating apricots fallen from a stunted tree that grows horizontally out of a crack. Each time it finishes one it lets the clean stone drop from its mouth.

Late in the day the white chair at the rain house disappears. The woman who lives there has taken it round to the east wall where there is shade. She keeps a towel over the chair back and sits with her feet in the thin wash that comes out under the door. Far down the valley the horses lift their wet muzzles from the trough. Their shadows already reach across two or three of the old river steps. There is still an hour of bright sun on their backs; under their long shadows, all the little doors are opening.
