From Bell Street you look down into what seems to be a square of green roofs. Then a fox steps out from under one of them onto white stone, and the scale comes right: the roofs are leaves, some as broad as a dustbin lid, and the square is the floor of the old cutting yard. The street goes round it on three sides. On the fourth there is a row of workers’ houses with their back doors level with the yard, so that to get from the pavement to a friend’s kitchen you can either walk the length of two streets or go down eighteen iron steps and cross in the sun.

The yard is scarcely bigger than a bowling green. Its floor is the top of a bed of pale limestone, sawn out in strips when the houses went up. The saw stopped halfway through its last pass. There is still a white lip there, straight enough to lay a ruler against, and a little fan of broken stone as sharp and clean inside as smashed crockery. Elsewhere a skin is just beginning to form: grey on the sunny flats, black where condensate falls from an overhead main. Only under that pipe, and in the deepest of the saw cuts, is there anything one could call standing water. A wagtail bathing in it has to turn lengthwise.

Under the pavement is a working refrigeration plant. Most of the district’s fish passes through the cold rooms, though you would never know it from down here; the yard smells of chalk dust and crushed green stalks. Two long banks of condenser fans face out through the retaining wall. The near bank makes a hard, continuous blowing sound. The far one runs a shade slower. Where the sounds meet, a deep note swells up, holds, and dies away, like a lorry labouring up Bell Street that never quite reaches the top. At the back doors people talk into one another’s ears. A dropped key makes no sound that its owner can hear. Everyone looks down when they take a key out.

Most of the stone is bare. The green grows in five or six extravagant islands, wherever the masons left a pit full of chips and barrow dirt. Butterbur stands shoulder high in the oldest pit. Behind it are nettles, purple willowherb, even a currant bush that fruits on the kitchen side of the wall, where the brick gives back a little warmth. Each island ends abruptly. You can put one hand into cool tangled abundance and lay the other flat on naked rock. At noon the sky lights the bottom almost as well as the street above; it is only when you kneel to pick a currant that you feel the draught along the floor and remember how far down you are.

There are whole populations here that a person making the short crossing misses. At the lip of a cut, solitary bees queue in the air for little tunnels in a pocket of dusty mortar. Young blackbirds run in the narrow lanes under the butterbur, keeping the leaves between themselves and the windows. The fox has a day bed behind a disconnected switch cabinet. On the stone beside it are cherry pits, the blue half of a clothes peg, and a dry heel of bread so old and so nearly the colour of the yard that somebody has drawn round it in chalk rather than pick it up. When all six fans are running the animals scarcely mind the people. It is the unusual quiet of a Sunday shutdown that sends everything into cover. A spoon stirred in a cup at a back door can empty the open ground.

For years the department that owns the yard has meant to fill it level with Bell Street. A red survey mark on the brick shows how much of the place would disappear, right up to the lintels of the kitchen doors. Meanwhile the changes are small enough for the people living there to keep account of them. One more finger of black stain under the pipe. A willow taking hold where no one thought there was soil. Each spring the woman at number eight cuts one butterbur leaf away from the foot of the steps so that a stranger coming down can see where to put a shoe. By August another leaf has grown across, and the last step appears to lead straight out onto that unbroken roof of green.
