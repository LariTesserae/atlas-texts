From the rim you can see the whole town except for the river. There are six quarters of white stone, set well apart on a shelf of the mountain. Between them lie great level stretches of grey grit where a person on the road is visible for the better part of a morning. The river is down a cut so narrow that a bridge over it looks, at that distance, like a door laid flat on the ground.

It is high country. The light is good enough to sew by almost anywhere outdoors, and the air is cool enough that the stone takes the warmth out of your seat right through a folded coat. The mountain appears to have split quite recently. Along the river cut, little shelves on one wall answer to little hollows on the other; even the pale intrusion of quartz in the grey rock can be matched up across the gap. There is hardly a pebble at the bottom. In the clear water lie slabs with corners on which you could still cut a thumb.

The remarkable thing about this place is that nothing wears away. A mason can part a slab with wedges, but the lip of a public step, after generations of feet, is as square as the day it was laid. The oldest stairs are rather uncomfortable on this account. In the newer quarters the masons take the trouble to round the nosing with a chisel. It is the surest way of telling which part was built first.

Much of the town is given over to water, though there is very little water to see. Long walls of loosely stacked stone stand across the shelf, their windward faces quite dry. Feel in among the stones and your hand comes out wet. At the foot of each wall a covered channel carries the seepage away to one of the quarters. There are inspection holes you can put your ear to. Even when the wind is up and the grit is ticking past your ankles, down there it sounds like someone quietly pouring a second cup.

Where a channel enters a quarter it feeds a public garden. The change at the gate is astonishing. Outside, there is room to turn a cart on either side of the road without touching so much as a blade of grass. Inside, the currant branches have to be held back with loops of cord to let people pass. Ferns fill the joints of the cistern; small dark pears hang over its parapet, each with a pale oval on the underside from the light reflected off the water. The houses have their doors on the garden and their blank backs to the open shelf. Their roofs are low, laid with split poles and sod. From the rim you see one green roofscape inside each white circuit of wall, with thin curls of cooking smoke spaced through it.

Animals use the town rather more freely than people do. Voles go the length of a quarter inside the covered channels, using the dry ledge left for the water inspector’s fingers. Little blue pigeons lodge under the eaves of the dye house. Out on the grit, where at first there seems to be nothing at all, there are scores of hares. You pick them out by their shadows. They lie facing into the wind with their ears laid along their backs, each in the lee of one of the low square stones that mark the buried conduits. The markets sell felt soles, wooden bowls, combs cut from horn; the carts run on hide-bound wheels. A hare asleep by a conduit stone will often sleep through the passage of a whole market’s worth of people.

There is a well in the lowest quarter that nobody draws from. A child dropped a red cup in it long ago. You can still see the cup about a yard down, lying on its side on a shelf in the shaft. Its handle broke in the fall. The two pieces have stayed beside each other, bright as fresh slip, and not even the fine green fur that coats a water jar has grown on either one. People will take a visitor to look at it when they cannot otherwise explain what it is like to live here. Things happen: a cup falls, a stair is cut, somebody sets a pear tree against a warm wall. What does not happen is the long, soft work that usually follows. The mountain keeps its edges. The town has had to make, by hand, every comfortable place it has.
