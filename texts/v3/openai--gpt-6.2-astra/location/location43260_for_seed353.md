The first thing to go over the western parapet is a station roof, six acres of ribbed black leaf. It goes slowly. Its inboard edge is still taking parcels of cable from the winding sheds while its outermost ribs curl down into the gulf. By the time the last cable has passed, the roof hangs vertically against the face of the plateau, with all its little loading doors shut. There is a station of precisely the same dimensions rising two streets inland. In the dark it is easy to think the station has moved. A hand laid on the new wall would find it hot, furry and scarcely stiff enough to stand.

The plateau has been the same height for a very long time. It is built on the folded-down edges of previous plateaus. Down the western face lie miles of roofs, pavements, nursery beds, switchyards, all pressed into a fine-grained substance rather like ebony, except that a cut across any portion of it will slowly purse shut. There was once considerable relief up here. The stump of a freight incline, now almost flush with the surrounding fields, has teeth on its underside for climbing a grade that has not existed in forty thousand years. The teeth are worn round. They still engage, once per circuit, with a little ripple in the ground.

There is no water in the works. What rises through the stems is a clear oil that thickens when cool, and there is consequently great care taken that very little of the plateau should ever cool. Leaves overlap like the skirts of furnaces. The spaces beneath them are packed with tow, a pale floss grown in place and never cut, through which the oil creeps sideways from one district to the next. A cold spot the size of a thumb brings an immediate response: the tow knots, current is diverted through it, the spot warms. Seen in the sparse red light of the inspection pits, the surface of a field appears to have gooseflesh racing across it. Each little rising is a repair.

The inspection pits afford the longest views. Stand on the lip of one while its shutter is up and you can see, for perhaps three seconds, a town laid out in low relief: the blunt spindles of the winding sheds, a belt of espaliers carrying live current, the great polished oval of the interchange, beyond it another oval smaller with distance. There are no streets in the sense of unoccupied strips. A street is a wide belt of muscle-root that conveys things along its own length. Cross traffic passes through it at the grafts. Even the apparent sky between two distant towers is usually a membrane, thin enough to take the colour of whatever darkness lies behind it. When the pit shutter closes the town persists in the palm of the hand as a sequence of tremors, far more detailed than the view.

What all the factories make is fittings. A knuckle that will let a heavy canopy turn. A plug of insulating cork. A reel of tough conductive bast with a growing tip at either end. The fittings ripen on low bushes in standard gauges; when needed, they are pinched off by the belts and delivered warm. Nothing here has ever had to be made to the size of a hand. The smallest fitting will pass through the eye of a needle and the largest hinge takes a season to unfold. Yet there is something almost domestic in the finish of the work, in the neat selvage on a mile of belting, in the soft pad provided wherever two hard pieces might otherwise knock. Noise costs heat. In a well-kept district the only sound is the dry, spaced *tik* of seed gauges testing themselves against their stops.

All this quiet gives a false impression of leisure. Under the uppermost skin the belts snatch one another sideways; whole wards of machinery turn inside out to clear a jam. A difference in temperature between the east and west ends of the interchange can send a fold running out across the winding fields faster than their shutters can close. For an instant thousands of spindles stand naked in the air. Then the floss billows up and takes their exact impressions, and somewhere else, sometimes a mile off, a long low mound gives a violent shrug. No distress call follows. There is no animal here to be alarmed, no bird to lift from the stricken field. The next parcel of cable arrives a little flattened on one side.

Only at the parapet does any of this activity accumulate into an event one could watch from beginning to end. The old station is nearly down now. For a while its ridge makes a narrow balcony, hot underfoot, looking out into a darkness so extensive that the busy surface behind seems no thicker than a table top. Then a fringe of fine roots comes feeling up from the buried stations below. They find the sockets made for them under the eaves. One by one, without a jar, they take the load.
