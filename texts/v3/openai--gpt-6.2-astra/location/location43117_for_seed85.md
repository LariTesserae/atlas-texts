The summit has a harbor on it. From the valley, on the few days when the cloud lies low enough, you can see the underside of the harbor water: a dark oval extending a little beyond the lip of the rock. It is about the size of a town square. Snow blows up against it from below and sticks. There are afternoons when a line of white powder is all that distinguishes the end of the mountain from the beginning of the sky.

Up here the mountain is new. A slab of basalt has come up edgewise through the older gray rock and split along its length, leaving a cleft just wide enough for a street. Both halves still bear the same markings. A seam of pale stone like a bent elbow shows on the left wall, and across the street, a little higher, there is its other half. The corners are sharp enough to cut a glove. On the bottom lie three or four chips which could still be fitted into the places they came from.

The town occupies the split. There is a freight office, a row of six dwellings, a public bath, and, on a landing halfway up one wall, a court with room for perhaps twelve people to hear a case. At the far end of the street a covered stair goes up to the quay. The whole place is under water, to a depth of nine meters over the highest chimney. Nothing was drowned here. The stair treads have grips on their undersides for the convenience of anyone coming down headfirst with a parcel. In the court the benches have foot loops. A person waiting to speak could let their knees straighten and hang quite comfortably upside down.

Very little of this can be seen at once. The roof of the harbor is a skin of dull silver that turns nearly all the daylight back. Below it the water is cool and dark and almost painfully clear. A lamp at the freight office lights the number on the opposite house without lighting the space between. There is no silt to catch the beam, no green on the glass, no fringe of weed along the eaves. The houses are faced with big flat pieces of the stone taken out to make their rooms. Someone took the trouble to match the bent elbow seam across two closed shutters.

What holds the water up is most easily felt at a washing place beside the bath. Put a hand into the stone basin and the water there will bear the weight of your arm. A handspan farther on it has no purchase at all. The builders could assign a load to a volume of water as precisely as a mason assigns it to a pier. Much of the town's structure is consequently invisible. The court landing rests on a square column of water. There is a water lintel above every door. Out beyond the quay, where there is nothing underneath for the better part of a mile, a narrow lane of water will take the weight of a loaded cart. The little notches at its entrance are wheel marks in the last inch of stone.

There is one animal you might see. It is white, the length of a thumb, with four soft feet set well apart and a head that seems too heavy for it. It lives where the quay meets the open sky. Water borne on water makes no difference to the eye, but at the edge of a bearing surface, dissolved salts gather in a fine dust, and in that dust live things smaller than the grains themselves. The white animal grazes there. Once in a great while it gets far enough out along the cart lane that a person looking up from the mountain below might see its four little soles against the silver.

The bath is worth entering, though it has long since been shut to customers. It is a room of black tile with a deep tiled pool set in the floor of it, water inside water. At first this looks like a joke at the expense of the visitor. Then you lower yourself over the rim. Outside the pool the whole weight of the harbor is taken up by its innumerable appointed bearings; inside, the water is allowed to weigh what water weighs. You feel it close gently on your ribs. If you move your hand, a current travels away from it, touches tile, comes back. This was what people came down the street for, with their towels and their small change: an hour somewhere that yielded and went on yielding after you had passed.

Outside there is hardly a sound. The shutters do not knock. The outer water does not lap. From the foot of the stair you can hear, very faintly, a white tooth scraping the quay. Then even that stops. Down in the valley a whole bank of cloud can travel the length of the mountain while up here the last ripple in the public bath is still finding its way into the corners.
