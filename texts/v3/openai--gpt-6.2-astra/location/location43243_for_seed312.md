On the high plain the houses keep to the cracks. The ground between is hard white limestone, tipped a little to the south, with parallel grooves down which a cupful of meltwater will run a surprising distance. Then it comes to a crack and is gone. Some of the cracks are narrow enough to step across. Others are wider than a market square, and down in them, out of the wind, there are market squares.

From above, the first sign of a town is often a patch of paving of a slightly different white. It sounds hollow underfoot. These are the capstones over the uppermost rooms. A little farther on there will be a stone trough, with loops polished into it by the lead ropes of pack goats, and an opening with stairs. Go down five steps and the wind passes over your head. Go down another fifteen and you come into a street in full sunshine, lined with narrow houses four stories high. Their backs are the living rock. People sit on the upper landings to plait wool in the good light. Below them a man is levering up a flagstone to get at a drain. A child has been given the important job of keeping the chickens out until the stone goes back.

The plain was under ice until recently enough that most of its loose stones still have corners. Where a ridge has split, the halves fit by eye even across a gulf: a nose of stone on one side and its exact blue notch on the other. The builders take advantage of this. They follow a level bed clear around a wall, tapping wedges of antler into the seam, and lift out slabs already faced on two sides. A slab that takes eight people to move will roof a pantry. What they cannot move they build around. In one of the oldest streets a blade of limestone comes up through three floors of a house and out through the roof, where its tip serves the neighbors as a sundial.

There is very little wood. Door pivots, loom bars, the worn handles of chisels: these come up from the low valleys on goatback, and a sound straight pole has a history that people know. Walls are stone, bridges are stone, and the little public benches have been left standing when the stone around them was cut away. A door is more often a double felt curtain than a door. For heat there are small covered hearths burning dung. At night the family draws its stools close to one; in the morning someone carries the pot, still warm, across the landing to the common oven. A whole tier of houses smells for an hour of barley cakes.

From the market you can take the south stair, cross a bridge no wider than its parapets are thick, and enter another inhabited cleft without once coming up to the plain. So it goes for much of a day's walk. Then the paving ends at a sheepfold. Beyond it there may be fifty miles of bare channels before the next town, made in much the same fashion but with round oven mouths instead of square ones, and a name for the pack goat that no one here would recognize. On a clear morning banks of cloud are visible in three directions, each belonging to a different reach of the plain. Snow can be falling on a city whose messengers will not arrive with news of it until the season of snow is over.

Seen from the heights, it is hard to account for all the animals. There is a little stiff grass along the seams, and islands of cushion plant, scarcely higher than the grit that collects in them. Yet whole strings of wild sheep pass along the skyline. The grazing grounds extend far beyond sight; the sheep bring what those distances have fed down into a few sheltered folds to sleep. Small dun birds go with them, taking seeds and insects from the disturbed turf. Foxes use the drove roads. In the towns there are swifts in nearly every unpointed joint that faces south. They drink on the wing at the public cistern, coming in single file through a gap left for them under the cover. People watch that gap in spring before they bother to look at the sky.

Water is a matter of choosing the right wall. On a warm afternoon the naked shelves shed their last snow in half an hour. In a north-facing cleft a plug of old snow can last the summer, wasting from underneath, giving a steady cold drip after everything in sight has gone dry. There are gutters cut a finger deep to catch it. Each ward keeps its own lengths of gutter clear, and once a year an elder walks the run with a jug of water tinted red with earth. Children wait below to see whose ceiling stains. The usual fault is a shift too small to feel, two stones that stood flush in a grandmother's time and now have the thickness of a fingernail between them. The repairs take an afternoon. The red mark stays.

For so much life the place makes little general noise. A call will carry across a cleft from one kitchen to another with every syllable intact, but two turns of the stair are enough to lose it. Up on the plain you hear the scuff of your own soles and sometimes, from apparently solid ground, the muffled knock of a mason dressing the underside of a lintel. Toward evening the wild sheep gather on the white rim. Their shadows reach across the streets long before the sheep themselves can be seen from below. One after another the wool workers put their plaiting away and go in. For a while the high bare stone is still in daylight, and the only things moving in it are the swifts, feeding high above the roofs, where the day's last warmth has carried a thin living dust of flies.
