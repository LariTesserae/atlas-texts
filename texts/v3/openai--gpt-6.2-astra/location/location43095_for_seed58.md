At street level the entrance looks like a coal chute, with two iron leaves folded back against the paving. There are stairs under one leaf and a little freight lift under the other. Ten steps down, the heat meets you at the collar. By the twentieth step you can see all of Copper Yard: its six shop fronts, the washhouse, the backs of three dwellings, and the sand-colored wall where the excavation stopped. It takes less time to walk the length of the yard than it does to come down the stairs.

The wall is the one thing here with room around it. Where the masons lifted out a staircase of blocks, they uncovered the cross section of an old gravel bed. There is a white pebble still seated in its own exact hollow, cut cleanly in two. Children coming home from school put a thumb on the flat of it as they pass. The rock lower down is too hot to touch. It was the heat the diggers were after, and the yard was originally no more than a place to stack the stone they took out while sinking the heat wells. Then somebody put a drying cupboard against a wellhead. There are now forty drying cupboards, rented by the hour, and a business that presses wet paper into trays, and another that recovers the wool from old mattresses. Most of the shop names have had to be painted on the lintels because a sign projecting into the lane would catch on the lift baskets.

The place is busiest in the middle of the day and even then you can hear a pair of scissors being set down. The electric drives are bolted to bedrock in a chamber underneath the washhouse. What comes up is a vibration too low to hear except when a loose buckle on a basket happens to sound it. Belts move overhead, turning corners on wheels of pale wood. A mattress goes in through a square opening; a loose white fleece comes folding out onto a table in the next shop. Someone reaches through the partition to pass back the buttons. In the narrowest part of the yard the awnings overlap, and the only daylight is reflected off the moving belts, which have been polished by use to the color of a brown shoe.

Water has its appointed places. The drinking tap shuts itself off. Wash water goes from the rinse tank to the scrub tank and finally down a grated hole, carrying a few blue threads that hang on the grate until they dry and lift in the draft. But there is also a cold main coming down from the upper city. For about an hour after the washhouse opens, enough cold water runs through it that a sweat breaks out along its underside. Beneath one elbow in the pipe, in a chipped basin full of mortar crumbs, grows a fig tree no taller than a stool. It has seven dusty leaves and one new one the size and color of a peeled pistachio. Someone has moved the basin several times to keep it under the drip; the pale circles its foot has left on the tiles record twenty years of small repairs to the plumbing.

There are no flies on the washhouse panes, no sparrows waiting where the workers eat. A crust dropped at breakfast will still be there at closing, gone hard enough to skate ahead of a broom. Toward evening the lift brings down the last bales and goes up with the dry trays nested a hundred deep. The belts stop one by one. For a few minutes, with the awnings rolled away, light from the chute lies across the cut gravel bed, and every pebble casts a tiny level shelf of shadow. Then the iron leaves are lowered over the street entrance. People turn on the lamps above their doors, take chairs out into the lane, and wait for the first faint movement of cooler air down the idle lift shaft.
