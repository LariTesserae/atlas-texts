From the road below, the town looks like a single white line drawn along the shoulder of the mountain. It is the parapet of the watercourse. Almost everything else is built of the same gray stone as the shoulder itself, and you have to climb until you can look across the roofs to see how large a place it is. There are houses as far apart as the two ends of a lake, yet a person could walk from either end to the other without once leaving a paved street.

The mountain has a broad top. Long shelves of lava lie one above another, with squared-off noses and deep, narrow clefts, some no wider than a hand. The grains of the broken stone still catch at cloth. There are no round hills up here, and the streams have scarcely begun to cut valleys. Water comes out along the seams between shelves. In some places there is a whole wet acre with no stream in it at all: just an inch of perfectly clear water traveling over stone, the joints visible beneath it, the occasional black corner sticking up dry. At the outer edge of the mountain one can look down through a mile of warm, bright air to country where a river has had time to make loops.

The streets follow the dry ribs between these wet places. They are much older in design than any particular house. A row of dressed blocks carries the street across a seep; then there may be a quarter mile of natural pavement with only a few notches pecked into a steep pitch for the sake of a wet sandal. Where two streets meet there is generally a court, a drinking basin and a group of houses sharing a kitchen wall. Beyond the last house the street goes on at its full width. It serves the reed beds as well as the houses. Reeds supply the roofs, the sleeping mats, the flat baskets in which people carry lime. A good bed of straight reeds is worth a long detour with a heavy stone, and several of the town’s exacting right angles have this simple explanation.

There is a great deal of green, though from any one doorway you see it mostly at a distance. The bare slabs are too hard for roots. Along their lower lips, where a finger’s depth of dark earth has collected, ferns grow so thick that the old brown fronds remain caught upright among the new. Groves of small trees stand in the deeper pockets, their crowns spreading sideways into the light. One grove fills a fissure so neatly that you can step from the street and find yourself level with the treetops, with the damp trunks going down into shade below your toes. The trees shed fine yellow pollen onto the water. In the basins it collects in a rim that shows, more legibly than a mason’s mark, how little the water level varies.

Most of the town’s work with water consists of keeping it slow. At the head of each court a slotted stone divides the flow, a little for drinking, the rest for washing and the lower gardens. At a drop they have laid a slanting slab rather than let the water fall. This keeps it from digging under the footings, and it makes the whole long watercourse surprisingly quiet. Beside it you hear a faint dragging sound, much like someone drawing a length of cloth across a table. Farther off even that is lost. A wooden bowl set down on the rim of a basin makes a sound by which you can locate a neighbor without seeing them. No dog answers it. There are no hoofprints in the soft ground and nothing rises from the reeds when a cutter parts them. The paths out to the gardens are narrow because everything taken there has always been carried by hand.

In the afternoon the stone is warm enough to sit on even where a little water has spread over it. People take their mending out to the parapet for the light. From there the roofs are visible in groups of six or eight, pale new thatch beside thatch gone almost black, and between the groups lie immense unoccupied surfaces, silver where they face the sky and gray where the water is too shallow to hide the rock. A child walking home on the far side of the town is visible for a very long time. Now and then a grove interrupts the view; then the small figure comes out again on precisely the course one expected. By the time the bowl and the mending have been taken indoors, the child has reached the next court, the sunlight has left the lower river, and up here there is still quite enough light to see the yellow rim around the water.
