From the upper footbridge you can see six miles down the valley, as far as the yellow end wall of the paper mill. Its paint shows up in weather that takes away everything else. On a clear afternoon you might pick out the windows in that wall. Today there is just a thumb of yellow under a low pewter sky, and between here and there a whole hillside of wet green steps.

They are filter beds. The town built the first ones to take the rust out of the water coming off the old workings. Since then the beds have spread along both sides of the valley, joined across it, and climbed almost to the pass. The upper ones have had eighty winters to settle. Their concrete lips sag in the middle, so each one spills a little silver in just the same place every day. Below them are square miles of newer work: brick races, expanded-metal walks, long islands of planted gravel held in steel baskets. What looks from the bridge like a low wooded ridge is the roof of the main settling house. The willows up there all lean downhill. Their roots have made a difficulty for the men who have to get the inspection hatches open.

There is no good place to put a foot except where somebody has provided one. The beds are packed with sedge, rush, purple loosestrife, alder stools cut back to the knuckle. In one narrow strip a different generation of workers planted yellow iris, and it has kept the exact width of their trench for nearly a mile. Even the gravel margins have knitted over with moss. A few inches below this abundance lies a perforated pipe or a course of numbered tiles. Water comes through cold enough that if you kneel on the walkway to put a hand in it, you feel the ache in your wrist before you feel much else.

All morning the operators have been shifting the load downhill. It rained hard beyond the pass last night. From up here you can follow the incoming water by what happens to the plants. A dark line runs up the rush stems in one bed; a minute later the next bed bows flat at the inlet. An alder branch caught under a footbridge begins to turn, slowly at first, then too fast to follow. Whole rafts of last year's cuttings pull apart and go streaming off down separate races. At the same time, across a wall no thicker than a hand, a compartment is draining so quickly that each pebble as it emerges has a little bright cap of water on it. Stand still for ten minutes and the islands about you change shape a dozen times.

Very little of this carries as sound. The drops between levels are shallow, the gates rise on oiled screws, and most of the fast water travels in covered culverts. The big pump motors are three floors down in the settling house. You know when one starts because the handrail trembles against your palm. Now and then there is the small hard clap of a non-return flap somewhere under the greenery. In the interval you can hear water ticking off the hem of your coat.

A grey wagtail works the length of the draining bed. It has learned how long the pebbles will be bare. When the inlet opens again it is already over the wall, the only quick warm thing in sight.

Toward evening fog comes level with the footbridge. It takes the mill first, then the far slope, then a row of red indicator lamps one after another. Close things grow distinct: the blunt ends of reeds sliced by a mowing bar, a greasy thumbprint on a switch box, the soft orange fur of iron on a spillway lip. There is a bench at the top landing where the early shift waits for the works bus. Years of wet trousers have polished its slats. Sitting there in the dimness, you can watch one last sheet of water creep over the crown of a bowed concrete wall. For a moment it holds together so smoothly that you can see the underside of the bench in it. Then a load comes through from somewhere high in the fog, the image wrinkles, and the whole long wall begins to overflow.
