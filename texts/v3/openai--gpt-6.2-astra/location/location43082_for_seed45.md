The mountain is still making its top. Seen from the next height, the summit looks broad enough to hold a town: a long black common, stepped down a little in the middle, with a faint copper light showing through the step. Only at its near edge can you see how far the ground falls. The flank goes down through a belt of brown haze and comes out underneath it, still going down, so much diminished by distance that its great folds look like the wrinkles at the joint of a thumb.

Up here it is warm. The warmth comes through the soles of the feet; a hand held above the stone feels very little, a palm laid flat feels plenty. There is scarcely any air to take the heat away. The sky is dark even in the direction of the low sun, which rides all day behind a rank of higher peaks and puts a dull shine on their teeth. Most of the common lies in their shadow. Small things are visible chiefly by their edges. A flake of glass with its edge turned to the sun makes one short hair of astonishing brightness in half a mile of black.

What seems from a distance to be level ground is a loose pavement over a rising body of stone. There are plates the size of doorsteps and plates that carry whole hills on their backs. The middles are quite safe for a while. At the margins there is a continual, largely noiseless commotion. A lip catches under another lip and lifts it; a long avenue opens between them, with two sheer walls exactly fitting one another, down to a knob on one side and its socket on the other. For a little while a warm red line runs along the floor of the avenue. Then the end walls meet, the sides buckle inward, and what was a passage has become a ridge. In the course of an afternoon this may happen in forty places, most of them too far apart to be seen together. Stay by a single stone and the impression is of a very quiet country in which, for no particular reason, the horizon keeps changing.

Here and there stand what look like little roofless enclosures. Each has three or four walls of about the same height and a floor heaped with black scales. They are the remains of blisters. Gas once held up a skin of cooling rock; the crown fell in and left the tougher sides standing. One of them is just high enough to lie down in out of sight. Its inner walls bear a succession of fine horizontal ledges where the molten stuff sank by degrees. In the deep shade under one such ledge is a thumbprint of gray powder, softer than the surrounding grit. It is the nearest thing here to a colony. The grains darken when a sulfurous breath passes over them, and after many such breaths the patch is a little wider. Nothing feeds on it. When its wall overturns it will lie face down in the heat and end there.

There is no soil between these places, nor even much dust. What breaks off a plate tends to remain beside the break. On a slope overlooking the central step lies a single fallen slab in several hundred pieces, all still arranged in the shape of the slab, with lustrous needles of fresh fracture pointing across the gaps. No rain has ever sorted the small pieces from the large. No foot has pressed the needles down. Even the steepest gullies are blind clefts, beginning abruptly and ending against a wall; there is no agreement among them about which way is downhill.

Once in a long while the stone immediately underfoot gives a low double knock. It is felt in the ankle a moment before it is heard. Far out across the common a narrow rim catches the hidden sun, brightens along its length, and goes dark as the lifting plate tips past the angle of reflection. Beyond it another rim lights up. For several minutes it is possible to follow the passage of a deep heave all the way to the far escarpment by these separate, fleeting marks. Then there is only the copper seam in the middle distance. It is a little higher now.
