From the upper goods platform you can see the whole valley, including the part that has not been lowered into place. It hangs over the eastern end: six acres of pale decking with a school, two rows of houses, and a yard full of washing already on it. From underneath, the school and the houses look alike. Each has a copper belly, bulging slightly between the straps. A woman comes out into the yard to pull a twisted sheet straight. She has the sun a good hour later than they do below.

The valley is made of terraces, broad enough in the middle for a town and narrowing at either end to a flight of industrial stairs. The steep sides are white enamel on ribbed iron. You can pick out the newest sections by the blue paper still caught under the bolt heads. Nothing has had time to round off. At a turn in a footpath there will be a little cup on a post, full of scraps of felt for people to fold over the handrail while they take the corner. The old people use them; the young people have little ladders of nicks across their palms.

It is high, cold country. Clouds pass below the outer galleries, and when they brush the underside of the town the flagstones sweat. For an hour or so the streets are dark grey instead of white. Damp gathers especially along the brass numerals set into the pavement, so that the numbers of demolished houses sometimes show through the floors of the houses built over them. Otherwise it is dry enough to hang flour in cloth sacks under an awning. What looks from the platform like a winding silver river is a continuous strip of tin, laid down to keep a route open in case there is ever enough water. There are bridges over it. Under one of them, in the dip made by a dropped girder, there is nearly always an actual pool, about the size of a kitchen table.

A great deal lives at that pool. Small red-legged birds stand shoulder to shoulder on the curled tin edge. One will lower its head to drink and the next will raise its head to make room, a courtesy that goes clockwise around the pool for most of the afternoon. Pale crabs live under the overlap in the metal. There are grey moths with the oval windows of the bridge punched out in miniature along their wings; settled upside down on a beam, they let the daylight through. Up on the dry streets you see relatively few creatures until you sit down. Then you notice that every drain hole has a whiskered face in it, that the dark seals on the cable joints are beetles, that the laundry lines are sagging less under the weight of the washing than under the weight of swallows clinging to the hems. There is hardly anything green. A few clumps of rushes have taken hold in the wet packing of the expansion joints. In winter the maintenance crews loosen the joints half a turn so as not to crush the bulbs.

For a place so full of works it is remarkably quiet. The main engines are down in the piers, turning at the rate of the minute hand on a clock. You know when one has engaged because the tea in a glass leans toward the east and stays that way until it is drunk. Even in the rolling mill, where they press out the great white lengths of hillside, the men can talk from one end of the floor to the other without raising their voices. It is the small noises that carry: a crab letting a mussel fall; the pop of the blue paper tearing free on a hot bolt; the woman overhead snapping out her sheet.

Distances here depend a little on what has been fastened to what. Two balconies joined by a clothesline are close enough for their occupants to pass a spool of thread from hand to hand, though from the goods platform you can see a whole district intervening, with streets and a milk depot. When a line breaks the balconies go back to their ordinary separation. For this reason no one cuts an old line without first walking its length and seeing who has come to depend on it. On sunny days, when the lines are all in use, the valley is wonderfully open to the eye and almost impossibly dense to cross. You may step out for bread and come home through the bedrooms of half your relations.

The piece of town to the east has been descending for eleven years. It is near enough now that children on the highest existing roof can reach up with brooms and brush the copper belly of the school. They do this on bright afternoons. From below you see a little patch of warm metal appear, then another, until the light coming off the school is the colour of a loaf. Eventually the straps will go slack, the streets will meet, and there will be one short step down where the surveyors got the levels wrong. Already somebody has made the step. It stands at the end of the last finished street, its edge bound in felt, leading up into the clear cold air.
