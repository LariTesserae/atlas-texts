There is a town in the space left by a slab of mountain falling out. The slab itself lies half a mile below, white side up. In the right light you can see where two stripes of dark stone on its face would fit the stripes on the cliff. Nothing much has happened to the edges yet. They are sharp enough to cut a rope. The town occupies the narrow sill at the bottom of the break, under an overhang that keeps off all but the lowest evening sun.

It takes a minute to walk from gate to gate. There are six public ovens, a bathhouse, a council bench long enough for thirty, and a paved square with little sockets for the feet of market stalls. Most days the square is empty. The people live one flight down, in rooms hung from the lip of the sill on forked timbers. A floor down there is a beautiful piece of work: willow woven so tightly that a lentil dropped at supper stays in the house. Between houses there may be forty feet of cold air. To visit a neighbor you go up, cross a few yards of town, and go down again. From the council bench you might see a hand come up out of the paving, set down a bowl of peelings, and disappear.

The bowl begins to travel. From a distance this looks like some property of the slope, but close to, you can see the little grey backs all around its foot. The animals have fur as short and dense as the nap on a cut stem, four digging feet each, and pink, quite human-looking ears. They want the salt on the underside of the bowl. Working against one another, they carry it a considerable distance without ever getting at what they want.

There are animals on practically every surface. It is possible to sit for a while without realizing this. The cliff is the color of a pigeon’s breast, as are the birds clinging to it and the flat moths covering the birds. An empty stretch of paving, looked at edgewise, stands a finger’s breadth higher than it ought to: the whole stretch is occupied by a ray-shaped creature with a skin of fitted scales. Its eye opens at one end of the street. People step over the eye. Elsewhere it doesn’t mind being walked on. The warmth of a pair of bare soles leaves two pale marks that fade by the time the walker reaches the gate.

Green grows in separate, extravagant places. A single crack behind the bathhouse supports a thicket of translucent currant canes, tongue ferns, hairy little palms and a kind of cabbage big enough to sit inside. Ten paces either way there is nothing but stone. The crack is cold; when cloud comes up from the valley, water beads along it while the rest of the wall stays dry. There are perhaps a dozen such seams. The town was laid out to leave each one an open run from cliff to brink. Even the council bench is built in two unequal parts so that a green lane the width of a wrist can pass between. In the depths of these lanes things are continually hatching, eating their shells, unfolding appendages, being snatched up. Sometimes a fern lifts as a whole, like the lid of a pot, and a procession of blind white beetles goes under it carrying bits of blue wing.

Very little of this carries to the ear. The streets are bedded on felted root, and what sound goes out over the edge has a great way to fall. One hears intimate things: a tongue detaching from the rim of a cup; the minute gritty adjustment of a moth settling its weight on a sleeping bird. When the cloud rises high enough to enter the town the people stop work and hang their good cloths outside. For a little while everything with hair or thread collects a silver fringe. Then the cloths are wrung into jars. A child can learn to do this without waking whatever has gone to sleep in the folds.

Once in a long while rain comes sideways under the overhang. It finds the sockets in the market square and fills them neatly to the top. This is what the paving animal has been waiting for. It gets up on its many frail legs, folding as it rises, until what looked sufficient to pave a street is a tall, narrow beast that could pass through a doorway. It goes from socket to socket drinking. Beneath it the stones are almost luminous, unworn since the day they were laid. The people come up with stools and sit around the edge to see them. There are games cut in those stones, and names, and a shallow, lopsided picture of the mountain before it broke. For an hour or two, while the animal drinks and rain hangs in drops from its lifted skirts, the oldest people in town show the youngest where everything used to be.
