From below, the tableland has the blue, blunt edge of a mountain. Up here it takes most of a morning to notice that the ground is sloping. A round brown stone you see at breakfast, apparently on the horizon, is still in sight at noon, a little to your left now, with a bird asleep on it. There are miles of warm gray rock in every direction. The sun brings out a faint pink grain in it. Wherever a piece has split off in recent memory the broken face looks startlingly white, like a chipped tooth.

A few pools lie along the summit. They are broad enough to show a whole cloud and scarcely deep enough to cover a foot. Between them run bands of darker stone, the beds of rivers worn down almost exactly level with their banks. Rain still finds these courses. For an hour or two a river will occupy its old bed from side to side, moving so slowly that the dust on the water keeps the pattern it had on the ground. By afternoon there will be separate puddles, and by the next afternoon a little mud under a crust you could lift with a fingernail. There is grass at the downhill ends of the pools. It grows in single wiry tufts with a clear space around each one, as if each had been given all the ground its roots would ever need.

At a distance the dry riverbeds look empty. The impression lasts until you stand in one and wait for your shadow to cool the ground. Small lids begin to lift. What looked like the overlapping scales of the rock are the backs of animals, about the breadth of a hand, with a soft gray fringe that fits into the groove around the next animal. Under each lid is a little amber eye. Thousands of eyes open in the patch of shade; beyond it, the stone remains stone. If you step to one side, the first eyes close again, not quite together. A last one stays open for some time, looking straight up into the light.

They live on the film that grows over the riverbeds after rain. It must take a great deal of grazing to keep the rock so clean. There are places where a body has moved on and left a green crescent no thicker than a brushstroke. Other creatures live in the seams between the grazers: translucent shrimp in water too scant to see, blind red mites, a flat white thing that folds itself lengthwise to get past an eye. Even on the driest afternoons the fringes feel damp. A bird can feed all day by walking the seams and never having to unfold its wings. There are a great many birds. Seen end-on they are narrow and inconspicuous; seen all at once from the side, when they turn toward a breeze, they make a mile of country turn pale.

Here and there, well back from the water, stands what looks like a little round house. Some are only knee-high ruins. The largest has a roof about level with your shoulder and three low entrances, their sills worn into saddles. They are made of the grazers’ old backs. At the end of each dry season the animals creep uphill to shed, working their way under the rims of earlier shells for shelter. The new shell is soft; for a few days the animal takes the shape of the hollow it has found. In this way a low arch tends to produce more arches. Lime from the dew fastens them together. You can follow one crooked passage with your arm until your fingers reach a chamber of astonishing coolness. Often there is a bird in there in the heat of the day. It lets you feel the warm, quick beat in its throat before it troubles to move away.

The little houses provide almost the only sound on the heights. As the sun goes down, a roof will settle with a small china tick. A long way off another does the same. The birds sleep. The wind runs too low over the smooth ground to set much of anything stirring. Only at the edge of the tableland is there a steady noise, so low you feel it in the soles first: air rising out of the hot country far below and passing over the lip.

In the evening that air brings a bank of cloud up to the edge. Often it comes no farther. Its bright, heaped surface stays level with the old riverbeds, and the birds on the outermost stones cast their shadows out onto it. But on some nights a tongue of mist slides inland. In the morning you can tell exactly how far it reached. Every grass tip bears a drop, the roofs are pink and wet, and across the bare gray distances, pool after pool has gained just enough water to touch its neighbor. For a while a single long reflection lies across the summit. Under it the animals feed, all their lids a little way open, the old river passing through their fringes without a sound.
