The lowest room of the laundry is hot enough that people leave their coats on the stairs, even in January. There are twelve hooks on the landing and only the first two are shiny. Two people still come down here. They come to change the lint basket and to see whether the old floor has come up any farther.

You can cross the room in thirty paces with a lamp, though it takes longer than that suggests. There are tanks to go round. Some have been out of service for so long that smaller tanks have been installed inside them, with a little stair cut through the outer shell to reach the inner one. Between the tanks are brick piers, and between the piers run the glazed earthenware throats through which the wash water used to come down. There was a time when it took this whole room to receive one afternoon's washing. Now it takes one insulated pipe about the thickness of a thigh. It goes diagonally across everything, keeping the room hot. On its canvas jacket the arrows pointing the way of flow have faded to the color of tea.

Most of what the lamp shows is dry. The moisture is very particular about where it goes. It beads on the incoming cold main; it runs down one bolt of each iron collar and leaves the other bolts dusty. Under the third collar it falls into a shallow white saucer the size of a washbasin. Nobody put the saucer there. Lime grew it, first as a ring round the splash and then as a rim high enough to hold a little water. In the near darkness its contents are perfectly still for minutes at a time. Then a drop lands and the light of the lamp travels out to the rim and back.

There is no earth underfoot. There are blue pavers, a layer of cinder concrete, an older pavement of yellow brick, and under that the arched roof of a closed settling tank. At one corner the blue pavers have risen in a long low ridge. Water getting in between the floors has laid down mineral after mineral, lifting them without quite breaking their joints. The ridge is broad enough to walk along. At its summit is a brass floor drain, raised now above nearly everything it was meant to drain. The two workers lay a straightedge across it once a year and enter the clearance in a little ruled book. Their predecessor made the first entry in inches, in a hand so small there is still room on the page.

Almost the only green is at the foot of the delivery chute. High up, the outside flap doesn't shut flush, and for part of the afternoon a blade of daylight reaches down as far as a cracked tile. Something like moss grows in that crack. It has collected just enough lint to make a gray nap over itself; you see the green only where a drip has parted the fibers. Once, when the basket was lifted, there was a small brown toad sitting in the square its base had kept damp. Neither worker had seen one here before. They set the basket down a little to the left.

Upstairs there are extractors big enough to shake buttons off a shirt. Down here the engines are felt principally through the palm, if you take hold of a rail. What you hear instead is the little suck of the air valve admitting air as a line cools, and, much later, the click with which it seats itself again. After the last shift the hot main contracts along its hangers. One hanger after another gives a faint tick, taking the sound diagonally across the room until it reaches the wall. For a long time afterward nothing seems to happen. In the white saucer a skin forms on the water. By morning the next drop will have broken it, and the pieces, too slight to pick up, will be part of the rim.
