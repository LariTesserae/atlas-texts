From below, the summit looks too narrow to walk on. Once you get up over the lip there is room for a little town. Three streets cross a market square; beyond it are long roofless yards, a flight of shallow steps and a terrace with a view straight out into daylight. Everything is low. You can stand in the street and put your hand over a wall into somebody's garden. The neighboring peaks, though miles away, look very tall from here.

It is difficult to say who did the laying out. The streets follow seams in a single bed of grey stone. There are red grains in the stone the size of currants, and on the corners where two streets meet you can see halves of the same red grain facing each other across the gap. Yet there are distinctions here that a town would make. The square has excellent drainage. The yards hold water. Every garden has a little raised sill that keeps its soil from washing into the street. Whatever made the place had time enough to try all the arrangements and keep these.

On a clear morning most of it is dry. Sunlight reaches the floors of the yards and brings out their pale green color; the cold persists even in the light. There is a skin of ice on the drinking pool at the head of the steps. Under it, pressed flat by the ice, are little round leaves with air caught on them like beads of mercury. All the green is down at that level. In one yard grow shoulder-to-shoulder willow trees, complete with grey furred buds and twisted trunks, none taller than a spoon. You could miss an entire wood by looking for a tree.

About midday a cloud comes up the face of the mountain. First its shadow runs over the square; then wisps begin to come *out* of the streets. The seams go right through the summit in places. Air squeezed up through them makes a tremendous vowel sound, deep enough to be felt in the hinge of the jaw. For a few minutes each street has a different note. They waver together, break into a racket like a hall full of people pushing back their chairs, and stop. By then everything has a wet face on the side toward the drop. Drops gather in the willow fur. The thinnest ice in the pool goes soft, and those submerged leaves rise half an inch and lie cupped on the surface, each with a sip of meltwater in it.

There may be a bird on the terrace through all of this, a small dun bird almost indistinguishable from a worn end of stone. When the cloud clears it goes from cup to cup, picking out the black midges that have begun to stir there. Its feet leave two fine tears in the film of the pool. It is quite something, after hearing the whole summit give voice, to see the only inhabitant take off without a sound.

By late afternoon the street floors have dried again. The yards stay dark and succulent behind their sills. At the far edge of the terrace a last trickle passes over the lip, darkens a hand's breadth of cliff and freezes there. Far below, rivers are carrying away whole hillsides of loose earth. Up here there is hardly anything left that can go. The steps have been worn down until they are scarcely an inconvenience to water; the corners of the square have the easy curve of heels. Tomorrow's cloud will find very little to alter. It will come up through the same three streets, sounding them to see.
