From the path the place looks dry enough to lay a coat down on. It is a broad shelf of pale brown rock with six or seven trees standing well apart on it, each with a neat dark patch of shade. Then a fish crosses one of the patches and comes out into the sun. There is water over almost the whole shelf. In most places it would scarcely cover the laces of a shoe.

The shelf ends on the seaward side in a lip about the height of a seated child. Water laps over it into the reeds. At either end you could walk round it in a minute and be back in the ordinary low country of drains and vegetable plots. A woman working in one of the plots sometimes leaves a green enamel mug on the lip, where the water running past keeps it cool. From inside the grove it is just possible to see the rim of the mug above the reeds. It gives the place a scale that the trees, all of different sizes, do not.

The rock underfoot has been planed down through the roots of an old folded range. Its bands come to the surface in hairpin turns, rose inside brown inside a gray almost the color of tinfoil. A mountain must once have stood over each turn. Now the hardest band stands a finger's breadth above the softest, and along that much of a hillside a whole population of minute limpets sorts itself by depth. The very small ones live high up, in water so thin that each shell wears its own lens of it. Lower down their elders graze shoulder to shoulder. What first looks like the grain of the stone is the paths of their mouths through a brown skin of algae.

There are animals enough here to furnish a much noisier place. Hundreds of clear shrimp hang head down in the shade of a single leaf. When the leaf stirs, they go with the shadow rather than with the water. Little dun birds walk among them on toes like bent lengths of wire, picking one out now and then with the absorbed air of people undoing bad stitches. Under the ledge is a nursery of rays. Sometimes in the middle of a perfectly blank stretch of floor two eyes open, and a piece of the floor the size of a dinner plate gets up and goes elsewhere. For all this business the usual sound is one drop following another off the lowermost leaf of a tree. The air is warm enough that the wet cuff of a trouser leg is no hardship. Out of the shade the light coming up off the shelf is hard on the eyes.

The trees grow where old solution holes have filled with soil. They spend very little wood on height; most of each tree goes sideways, carrying a heavy thatch of glossy leaves, creepers and dangling red bunches of fruit. Between one green roof and the next there may be twenty paces of naked brightness. Fallen fruit collects in the holes. There is a rich smell there, half cider and half wet pennies, and a standing dance of blue flies that part around a bird's bill and close again one fly fewer.

Only three things on the shelf have clearly been made. They are black ceramic staples, big enough to put a hand through, sunk into the rock in a crooked line. At the turn of the tide a surface sometimes stretches across the gap in one. It shows the underside of the shelf: the pink fingers of worms down in the silt, a tree root feeling its way along a seam, beads of fresh water squeezed from the stone by the weight of the incoming sea. It is tempting to take this for a picture until a shrimp swims out of it. For those few minutes the distance through a staple and the distance down through thirty feet of rock are the same distance. The staples have no lights or lettering. Their edges have worn round. Whatever else has altered since they were set here, the tide still fits them.

Toward noon, when the water on the open rock gets warm, the rays begin to gather at the middle staple. The little ones pass through. The bigger ones cannot, and settle close to it instead, in a patch of cool upwelling water about the size of the woman's vegetable plot. Birds come for the shrimp it brings up. Their feet dislodge the limpets; fish nose in after the scraps of weed. For a quarter of an hour there is hardly room in that patch for another living thing, while the rest of the grove stands bright and seemingly vacant. Then the surface in the staple goes slack and a bird puts its head clean through the gap to peck at something on the other side. By the time the woman comes down for her mug there are only small muddy tracks leading away in the clear water. She rinses the mug twice, watching whatever is in it swim out.
