The bay is only a little wider than a house. Underwater it seems wider, because there is nothing in the middle to give the distance away. The bottom is pale granite. At either side the same stone rises in long, rounded shoulders, then turns inward a little at the mouth, so that drift from the lake goes past rather than in. One can see out between the shoulders. For a few yards there is still a bottom, distinctly lit; then it slopes out of sight.

Ice holds the surface level from shore to shore. There has been no snow worth speaking of. Daylight comes through the ice much as it comes through the high, frosted window of a stairwell: enough to see the grain in things, not enough to tell where the sun is. The feldspar is faintly pink. A narrow seam of darker rock crosses the floor and goes up one side, keeping its width all the way round the bend. It stands proud just enough to have caught a line of sand.

Ice of a different thickness once passed through here, bearing down on the granite with the weight of a hillside. There are scores left by it. They run under the sand line and come out the other side. In a hollow near the mouth lie three cobbles of foreign stone, all too big for this water to shift, and none so big that it looks particularly rooted in place. There is a patch of stonewort behind one of them. Its stems are stiff with lime; the little whorls have space between them, and cast a more delicate shadow than their rather coarse substance would suggest. Apart from that patch and a green stain along the better-lit shoulder, the rock is bare. Nothing grazes it. Nothing takes cover when a shadow falls.

There is a generating station farther down the lake. None of its works extend into the bay, but its running note carries through the bedrock. Out in the middle one might miss it altogether. Close to the granite it is a deep, even hum, the sort of sound that only becomes distinct when one thinks, for a moment, that it has stopped. It hasn't. The machinery is doing a considerable amount of work somewhere else. Here its entire effect is that the silence has a pitch.

Very little silt settles. What does settle lasts, caught in the shallow scores, so that the old direction of the ice is written out in soft grey as well as cut into stone. Even in summer the shoulders keep the wash out; in winter there is hardly a movement to lay a stem over. The water is cold and perfectly ordinary and so clear that, looking across the bay, one sees the far slope in every detail and scarcely sees the intervening water at all. Only the light gives it depth. The nearest grains of feldspar are pink; the ones across the room of water have lost their pink, though one can still make out each grain.
