There are seven steps down into the hollow, though from above you can make out only the first two. The rest go under an overhang. On the fifth the temperature changes so abruptly that the iron handrail feels heated by a current. On the seventh there is room to stand upright, and enough brown light to see a little country laid out below: three terraces, a bed of pale gravel, and at the far end a brick works no bigger than a washhouse. A fig grows out of its chimney. The tip of the fig, reaching the level of the fields overhead, is in full sun; its white trunk goes down through forty feet of shade.

The hollow used to be a cavern. A strip of its roof fell in so long ago that the fallen blocks have lost their angular faces. They lie in the gravel like a row of great shoulders. The remaining roof is low at the sides, and it is here, in the wedge of space between ceiling and slope, that most things grow. There are broad leaves with one edge fitted almost exactly to the rock. There are fans of fine green blades, thick as sheaves in a loft, all leaning out toward the opening. Seen from below, the undersides of the leaves are the color of apricot skins. It takes the eyes some time to find the green.

Almost all the water in the hollow is on these undersides. At dawn a bright seam forms along each rib. By noon the seams have worked their way down to the stems and the leaf faces are dusty again. Only under the northernmost overhang is there standing water, a black pocket about the size of a sink, with a chipped enamel dipper sunk handle-first in the middle. The gravel bed, wide enough to suggest a considerable river, has been dry for years. A few roots have ventured across it. Each has made a little bridge of its own shed bark to keep the living wood off the hot stones.

What fills the place is the noise. You can feel it through the soles of your shoes before you hear it clearly: a deep industrial *orr*, with a much higher scraping note riding on it. Inside the works are two stationary flywheels, a belt stiff enough to stand in a loop by itself, and a tiled wall on which the days of a long-vanished shift are ruled in blue pencil. Nothing there could make the sound. It comes from the bores beneath the floor. Hot air from deep in the hill passes up through pipes once used to carry brine, and the pipes open into a common stone chamber under the gravel. Cover either ear and the noise seems to come from the other side of the hollow. Set the dipper against the floor and its enamel sings painfully loud.

For half the year the hollow sounds one note; for the other half it sounds two. The change occupies about a week. It begins with a wavering like the sound of someone running a wet thumb around the rim of a glass. Salt has been growing in one of the pipe mouths, narrowing it grain by grain. When the first real rain reaches the chamber, some of the salt dissolves and the second note returns. The old shift chart has a narrow column for this event. Whoever kept it used a tuning fork: beside each date, in very neat writing, is the number of beats they heard in a minute.

There are no wings in the light overhead and no small feet stirring the litter. A fallen fig stays where it lands. Its skin slowly wrinkles down around the seeds; then a white bloom takes it, and eventually all that marks the spot is a patch of unusually dark gravel. In the absence of anything to carry them, the plants travel by other means. The blade grass advances on cords as tough as bootlaces. The fig has lowered a root through the chimney into the furnace and out through the ash door. Already, on the little iron lip where a stoker would have rested his shovel, it has put up a second stem.

Late in the day a bar of sun gets all the way down to the dry bed. For perhaps ten minutes the pebbles cast long, distinct shadows and you see that the bed is not level. Each pebble sits at the head of a tiny furrow. They have been moving, very slowly, away from the works, nudged along by the vibration under the stone. Near the building the finest grit has gone first. Farther off lie grains the size of wheat, then the pale pebbles, then the great roof blocks, which may have moved only the width of a finger since the pipes were sunk. From the foot of the stairs it looks remarkably like running water. Then the sun slips up the opposite wall, the furrows disappear, and the river goes on by sound alone.
