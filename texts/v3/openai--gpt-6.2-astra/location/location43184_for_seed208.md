You can cross the basin with a cup of water on your head and never need to steady it. From the west rim to the east is most of a morning’s walk; the descent to the middle is the height of a door. The rock is grey, fine-grained, worn to the finish of an old doorstep. Rain lies on it in sheets so thin that a little warmth from the palm will clear a handprint. The print fills in from all five fingertips when you lift your hand.

In the bad light it is difficult to tell where the sheets deepen into pools. You go by the pale stones that have been set out at intervals, each just big enough to sit on. Some have a little crescent chipped out of one side. The crescent faces deep water. Where a stone has split or gone over on its face, the people who cross here kneel and put an ear to the ground. There is usually time to do this early in the morning. Later on you couldn't hear a cart go by.

The sea is on the other side of a ridge scarcely any higher than the basin rim. Underneath that ridge the two are nearly one body of water. There are miles of drowned passages in the rock, most of them low enough that a seal going through has to turn on her side. As the tide comes in, the first thing that rises in the basin is air. It comes out of the pools in big single bubbles with a sound like someone testing a drum. Little rings travel a surprising distance over the flooded stone. Then the long cracks begin to blow. One produces a clean note, another a wet tearing noise; a row of holes not much bigger than thumbprints whistles so high it hurts the roots of the teeth. In half an hour the whole floor is going. The rain jumps on it. To speak to someone you put your mouth against the bone behind their ear.

This is when the animals come up. What looked like a streak of discoloured stone separates into hundreds of slate-coloured crabs, each carrying on its back a patch of grit that fits the patch on its neighbour. They run sideways to the vents. The incoming water is full of little white swimmers, soft as the inside of an egg, and for a few minutes there are more of them than the crabs can eat. Gulls come down out of a ceiling of cloud so low that their feet appear first. They swallow the swimmers, overturn the crabs, fight, lose their footing in the current. Further out, in a hollow as wide as a village square, a seal's head and then another stand perfectly still amid the commotion. They're waiting for the long silver fish that follow the swimmers in. There isn't a blade of grass anywhere for a gull to carry to its nest. On the ridge they lay their eggs in hollows in the rock, on little mats of hair pulled from the seals’ rubbing places.

There are four shelters on that ridge. You can see daylight between the slabs of their walls. Down in the basin, their builders have made something more delicate: low fences of split stone, hardly ankle-high, with gaps that open into bell-shaped baskets. At full tide you might step over a fence without seeing it. On the ebb, a basin's worth of water has to find its way downhill by the weight of a few fingers’ depth, and the fences are enough to persuade it. The fish go with it into the baskets. A child can lift a basket at the narrow end, let the small fish out through the weave, and keep a supper's worth flapping in the bell. Children working out there in the din wear bits of greased wool in their ears and keep looking back at the ridge for the signal to come in: a white hide lifted and dropped against a dark wall.

As the noise dies, you begin to hear what it has been covering. The clatter of a basket set down. A seal coughing in one of the passages directly underfoot. Rain again, fine and cold. Here and there on the emptying floor are round pale places where there used to be hills; the grain of the rock runs in rings around the clean cut through their roots. Across one such circle goes a groove rubbed by generations of people dragging the heavy baskets home. It holds water a few minutes after the rest of the circle has drained. The last gull of the evening walks the length of it, taking the stranded swimmers one by one, its feet making no sound at all.
