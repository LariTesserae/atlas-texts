The rim of the valley is level with the surrounding plain. From a long way off there is nothing to see but grass, and what looks like another strip of grass, a little bluer, behind it. The noise comes up out of the ground. A few paces farther on, the near grass ends at a lip of limestone. The blue strip is the far lip, nearly a mile away. Everything between is below your feet.

It is a long subsidence trough, a whole run of old caverns whose roofs have come down. Great pieces of the roofs still lie on the floor. Some are tilted up on edge and show their undersides, brown and fluted by water that ran there when there was still a ceiling. Others have landed right side up. On top of one of these there is a patch of turf the size of a small meadow, with the same wiry grass as the plain above; all around it is a drop of forty feet. Between the fallen pieces run broad lanes of pale grit. There is room to walk for hours down here, although in places a lane ends clean against a wall and the only way on is to go a long way back.

Most of the rock is dry. The break faces are still sharp enough to snag wool, and even in the fine stuff underfoot there are flakes with cutting edges. But at the foot of the eastern wall a single dark bedding seam gives water along almost its whole length. For fifty yards there may be only a stain, then a lip of green moss with its fringe turned down under the weight of drops, then a pool deep enough to hide the bottom of a boulder. The water leaves through the grit. There is no stream to follow. A little fern grows in the wet joint itself, each plant flattened into the crack as if the two beds of stone had been closed on it.

The noise is birds. The ledges carry a colony of small grey petrels, so like the stone in this light that at first one sees only the ones in flight. Then a whole shelf that seemed to be pebbled resolves into heads and folded wings. Farther along there is another shelf like it, and another. The sea is just beyond the plain; the birds come inland to these fissures to breed. Under a projecting slab the calls are quite distinct: a churring intake, three or four hollow notes, a last little cackle. Out in the open they merge into a tremendous rolling purr. It can be felt very faintly through the soles on the thinner plates of rock. Now and then one bird calls from a hole almost at ankle height, astonishingly loud and close, and a bird far down inside the rubble answers it.

For all these numbers the lanes are nearly empty. A feather will lie by itself on a hundred yards of white grit. The life is tucked into edges: birds in the dry slots, beetles under the feathers and cast eggshells, minute pale crustaceans working over the drowned moss in the pools. In one deep pool their movement makes a spoonful of bottom mud look as though it were simmering. The water above it is perfectly still.

Little direct light reaches the trough. The plain has a low maritime sky, and what comes down between the walls is soft enough that a bird passing overhead scarcely throws a shadow. It is cool even out in the middle. The air smells of scratched stone, with pockets of a thick oily, feathery smell that mark an occupied cleft before the cleft is visible. There is no path, though the long bare lanes have the inviting look of paths. Nothing has been laid flat for a foot or piled to mark a turning.

After a while the impression of some immense recent disturbance wears off. The blocks fit too deeply into their beds of grit to seem about to shift. The wet seam keeps its narrow black margin; the dry faces keep their white scars. A petrel leaves a ledge and the gap it makes is barely the breadth of a hand. Stand far enough back and even that much change is lost. There is only the long pale floor, the dark stitching of birds along the rock, and the sound rising without a break to the grass above.
