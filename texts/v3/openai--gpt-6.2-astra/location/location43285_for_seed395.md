At the edge of the plain you can step down onto it. The bank is no higher than that. A few inches of pale earth show under the lip, with grass roots hanging out of it, their tips as white as the earth. Beyond it, all the way to the limit of sight, are little domes of grass. In the morning light each has a bright side and a dark side. It looks like a closely settled country seen from a great height, though you are standing at the level of the roofs.

Some of the roofs come to pieces when a tortoise walks over them. These are old tussocks, nothing much left inside but chaff. Others would bear your weight. The grass has grown over termite houses, and on the sunny side, where the stems stand farther apart, you can see the fine finish of the clay. There is no standard size. There are thumb-sized outposts and long buildings with a dozen blunt chimneys, and in between them runs a network of covered ways, just high enough to admit the workers carrying bits of dead grass on edge. At any one time a great deal of the plain is being taken apart under cover and carried somewhere else.

It is difficult to find a bare patch large enough to sit on. Even the spaces between grass roots are full of purslane, its little pads fitted together like the green glass screens at the bottoms of old bottles. There are yellow flowers down there. You would miss them standing up. The grass itself looks dry until you part a tussock and find the thick stand of green blades inside the ring of last year's straw. By noon the outer blades of this year's growth have rolled shut too. They show their pale undersides to the sun. From the bank you can watch the change of color moving west, a breadth of country too large to have noon all at once.

There is nowhere to get a drink. The last rain lies under a lid of fine dust, a little dampness held in the clay well below the reach of a spade. Nearly everything that can go down there does. Roots go down; termites follow the roots; the small burrowing lizards spend the hot hours with their bellies against the cool walls of the galleries. Up above, a hawk quarters the plain so low that its feet occasionally brush the grass. Where its shadow goes, the neighborhood closes. Lizards slip indoors, seed mice freeze with their cheeks full, grass heads cease to dip. A few yards behind it the business starts up again.

The quiet is mostly a matter of what the place is made of. There is no rock to strike, no timber to creak, no loose leaf broad enough to clap against another. The grass takes the sound out of a footfall. A tortoise cropping purslane makes a noise like someone clipping a single thread. On still days the most persistent sound is a dry sifting close to the ground: at a thousand little breaches in the roofs, workers are fitting grains of earth into wet seams which will be hard before the shadow of a stem has moved its own width.

The plain is still acquiring its topsoil. Far off to the south there are flats of loose silt, and when the warm air begins to rise off them it carries a little of the country up with it. By afternoon the southern sky is the color of unbleached linen. No great storm arrives. The light stays good enough to see the narrow blue line around a hawk's eye. But look again at one of the low yellow flowers and its petals are freckled; the windward faces of the domes have taken on a pale nap. The grass catches what the wind brings. Next season the new shoots will start a grain or two higher.

Toward evening the tightly rolled blades open. Beetles come up the chimneys and stand on the warm rims. In a lane between two long mounds a tortoise has settled so neatly into the dust that only the growth rings of its shell distinguish it from another roof. A mouse comes out, runs the length of the lane, goes over the tortoise rather than around it. For a minute, with the sun low, every stem and back and chimney throws a separate shadow. Then the small shadows join up, first under the grass, then across the lanes, until there is one continuous shade at ground level and the tips of the taller grasses are still in daylight, moving.
