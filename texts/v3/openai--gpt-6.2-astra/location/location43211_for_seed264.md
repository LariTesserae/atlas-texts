The floor of the basin is pale enough that the wet places show from miles away. They look, from the low rim, like a handful of dark coins scattered over a kitchen floor. Down among them the distances are considerable. You can leave one pool in sight behind you and walk until your shirt is dry before you can make out the little shelf of stone around the next.

What you hear all the way across is the sound of heavy machinery under load. There is a low beat you feel in your heels, with a much faster clatter riding on it. Now and then the two fall into step and the ground gives what feels like one good solid kick. In the clear warm light you can see from one end of the basin to the other. Nothing sticks up higher than a shoulder. There is nowhere even a small engine could be standing out of sight.

The stone is limestone, laid down in beds about the thickness of a mattress. Where a bed ends there is a low step, so thoroughly worn that you can slide a bare foot over its lip without catching the skin. In places the topmost bed is gone except for a few long white bolsters lying well apart on the level below. Their undersides are dark with condensation. A drop gathers there, hangs until it is quite round, then runs back along the underside into a seam. Very little of the water here ever has to fall through air.

Underfoot there are two rivers. One comes down cool from country beyond the rim. The other comes up hot, carrying gas and so much dissolved lime that anything dipped into it would in time acquire a white jacket. Where they meet, the water has worked out an arrangement of its own. A narrow passage in the stone fills with gas until the gas can lift the water standing over it. The whole column goes over a lip into the next chamber; cool water rushes in behind it, and the business starts again. There are miles of these passages. Some have a loose flat pebble seated over the inlet, lifted and dropped at every stroke. That is the clatter. The bass note comes from much farther down.

At the surface the result of all this work is modest: a clear pool, perhaps six feet across, with a thumb's width of black wet stone around its edge. Its level lifts a little and subsides. A bead of gas comes up always in much the same place. The pool has no outlet you can see. If you put your hand in at the shallow end, the water is pleasantly warm across your knuckles and cold in the clefts between your fingers. Two currents pass there side by side, as distinct to the touch as two lengths of cloth.

There is a little animal that lives on the underside of the shelves. You might see one in a day's walk: a broad translucent oval with most of its legs gathered at the front, where they do the delicate work of combing a grey bacterial skin off the rock. Its back is white with lime. When it comes to a dry patch it tests the margin with one leg, then walks sideways along it, keeping to the damp as faithfully as if there were a fence. In the great stretches between pools there is nothing to graze and nothing to cast seed. Even the old cracks are clean to the bottom.

A curious thing about the basin is that it cannot wear itself much farther away. Where a throat underground grows narrow, the cool current runs faster and eats the lime from its walls. Where it widens, hot water lingers and lays the lime back down. A pebble beating itself thin on one face grows a fresh milk-white crust on the other. The same balance holds at the edges of the pools. Each has a polished notch where water has lapped for ages; below it, stone is being deposited almost grain for grain as fast as it is rubbed off. There is an immense throughput here, enough heat and water passing under this empty acreage to keep a mill town busy, and almost nothing to show for it.

At the far end the floor rises by three shallow steps to the rim. The climb takes less than a minute. Halfway up, the beating in your heels stops, though the sound is still loud enough that you would have to raise your voice to speak. From there you can pick out the pool you have just left. A small tremor goes across its bright face, too slight to see from any farther off. Beyond it lie the other pools, set deep in their old notches, each receiving exactly as much water as it can lose.
