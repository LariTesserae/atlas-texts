It takes forty paces to cross the meadow, and on the fortieth the ground curls over like the lip of a jug. A little water is always going over there. In still weather it falls clear of the rock. More often it comes back up as rain, slanting in from a stretch of blue sky so clean that you can see the moon in it.

The meadow occupies a scoop in the side of a mountain, well above the last place a tree can grow. From its upper end you can see the river on the valley floor, with a white stitch halfway along it where there must be considerable falls. None of that water makes a sound up here. For much of the day a bank of cloud intervenes, and then the scoop of grassy earth and its short back wall of rock seem to be all the ground there is. A bird leaving the grass goes a remarkably long way before it passes anything else.

Underfoot there is hardly a finger’s breadth of bare soil. The turf is made of little tough plants fitted close together, each with the habit of a much larger plant: willows that have to be parted with a thumb to see the wood in them, rushes that stand knee-high to a pipit, cushions of pink flowers whose roots go far down into old cracks. Their leaves are warm at noon. The water among their roots is cold enough to ache in the joints of a hand. It comes out along a dark seam at the foot of the wall, runs in half a dozen bright threads, then disappears under a level green patch that shivers all of a piece when stepped on. Eventually it finds the lip. There are three notches there, worn smooth and deep; which of them carries the stream depends on what the voles have been doing inland.

One thing people made remains at the upper end. Two slabs stand on edge and a third leans across them, making a shelter just big enough for a seated person to pull their heels in out of the wet. The seat has been raised with flat stones. Somebody went to the trouble of cutting a little gutter around the uphill slab, and every spring somebody must still come and clear it, for there is pale grit in the bottom and nowhere else. In the dry dust under the seat a bumblebee goes in and out of a hole. On a sunny afternoon the stone is full of a low burr you can feel through your trousers.

Most of the noise comes from below. The cliff under the meadow is split into long blades with dark intervals between them, and in those intervals hundreds of choughs have their young. A parent arriving with a full crop sets off first one recess and then the two on either side. The racket comes up through the turf. At times there is nothing in sight but a fly washing its face on a willow leaf, while just out of sight an entire cliff is screaming. Then a fox steps down from the back wall, placing each muddy foot neatly in front of the last, and black birds come pouring up past the rim. They hang there on the updraft with their red feet lowered, scolding. The fox turns over a pad of moss for beetle grubs. It need never look up to know exactly where the edge is: the grass for the last yard all lies combed uphill.

Late in the day the cloud bank rises. For a minute its upper surface is level with the meadow, and a vole run, a bent flowering stem, the little channels full of shining gravel all end against what looks like miles of level white land. Then the light goes pearly and the farthest pink flowers disappear. The choughs fold away into their cracks. Drops collect on the shelter roof and run down its underside to the lowest corner, which has grown a green beard. Even in thick cloud it is easy to find the way across. Walk toward the smell of wet stone for shelter. Walk toward the patch where the rain strikes the underside of your chin, and you have gone quite far enough.
