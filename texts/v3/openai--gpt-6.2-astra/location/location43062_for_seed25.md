The wind comes off the gulf with the smell of an overturned stone. It has a hundred miles of water behind it in which to pick up rain, but the rain mostly stays out there. Here at the head of the gulf it leaves beads on the west sides of things. A pebble can be black on one side and dust-white on the other. The air is cool enough that the beads last well into the day, though there is little else to tell day by. Day is when a faint leaden seam shows where the banks of cloud over the gulf don't quite meet the water.

The gulf used to come farther in. For six miles inland there are level runs of shingle between low humps of soft gray rock. The humps have been undercut on their seaward sides, some almost through; the pebbles that did the cutting still lie in the hollows, smooth as eggs. Water backs up through the shingle on the highest tides. It comes up without a ripple in a hundred separate basins, lifts the skins of dust off them, and leaves again, taking the finer dust with it. Between those tides a basin may have a thumb's depth of brine or a crust of salt with something pushing at its underside. Nothing puts down roots. Even on the shoulders of the humps, where a little sweet water collects, there is only the slick buff stain of things too small to see.

From a distance the shingle runs look empty. Up close it is hard to find room on one of them to lay a hand. The commonest animal is about the size of a fingernail, with a domed back the color of wet clay and a soft foot that fits the curve of a pebble. There are so many that they have polished the stones well above the last reach of the sea. In the dry hours they rest foot to foot in the gaps, soft parts turned inward, shells making a second, much less tidy sort of shingle over the first. When the wind wets the pebbles they come apart and travel west, grazing the buff stain with a pair of hard black lips. One stone stripped clean in this way has a pale track round its middle, wide enough for two lips and no more. After a wet night whole gravel bars are striped.

The noise that fills the valley is largely the work of the animals that eat these grazers. They are long, low things, scarcely thicker from back to belly than the shells they swallow. A row of little gripping pads goes down each flank. One end pries a pair of sleepers apart; the other holds fast to the rock and beats on it with a loose horny plate. Six or seven blows, a pause to swallow, six or seven more. The blows drive the sleepers on the underside of the rock out where a second hunter can reach them. A good stone may have forty hunters on it, and there are acres of good stones. The clatter has a bodily force. Down in a run it catches in the hollow under the breastbone. The ear keeps trying to pick out one set of blows and follow it; within a moment it has lost that set in another going just a little faster. Up on the gray humps most of the sharp sound falls away. What is left is a deep thrumming, with the high, surprisingly sweet whistles of the hunters' young carrying over it. The young have no plates yet. They whistle to get a mouthful passed to them.

There are animals here that never touch the ground. In the lee of each hump hangs a loose fleece of little pale fliers, each keeping its place by flying very slowly into the wind. They catch the smaller fliers coming up from the brine. Now and then the whole fleece sags to one side as a black, wide-mouthed thing passes through it, and the gap closes so gradually that for several seconds the shape of its wings is still there. On windless nights all these multitudes have to settle. Then the rock shoulders turn soft. They smell of warm fat, an extraordinary smell in all that cool salt air, and shake gently wherever a sleeper in a lower layer works its way to the surface.

Out toward the mouth there is a basin too deep to dry. Its rim can be found in the dark by the change in the shingle: the stones grow smaller and more even, then give way altogether to a bed of shed plates. They lie overlapping in the shallows, translucent, worn thin at the striking edge. This is where the hunters come to molt. Before a high tide they arrive from well inland, dragging the old plates behind them, and for a few hours the valley loses its clatter one run at a time. In that lull the lesser sounds come through with astonishing clarity. Lips rasp on stone. Wings rub in their thousands. Under the bed of old plates there is a little continuous fizz as the rising brine displaces air. Then from somewhere far back in the valley, where one hunter has stayed behind or one new plate has hardened early, come six distinct knocks, close enough together to count.
