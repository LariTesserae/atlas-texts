It rains under the river. The drops hang from the iron plates of the riverbed until a tug goes over, or a train crosses the bridge upstream. Then they come down in a long traveling shower. A person standing on the lower tramway can see the passage of a boat by the wet patch moving across the pale floor, sometimes with a second, smaller patch trailing it on the towline.

Down here there is room for a boat to take quite a while going over. The floor extends for six miles, gently dished, with a ridge along one side where the return mains run. From the ridge you can see three pump houses at considerable distances from one another, each with a little porch and a lit clock over the door. There is a sense of cold early morning at all hours. Nowhere is the ground earth. The broad gray flats are cast in numbered panels; the ridge is hollow; the dark shingle along its foot consists of half-inch porcelain saddles washed out of the filter beds. Even the hollows where water stands have been specified on a drawing, though not, in most cases, for the purpose they currently serve.

They built the place to give spring floods somewhere to go. Its first flood was only four winters ago. Above the tram wire there is a yellow stripe marking the height the water reached, an improbable height to look at from beside a set of tracks. Most of the time now there are just inches of water, continually changing address. A sluice lifts under the far clock. Water slips off one flat and spreads over the next in a sheet so thin that the painted panel numbers remain legible through it. At the edge of the sheet, ice forms. On the next change of sluices the ice stays where it is, held up by the roughness of the floor, and for a few minutes there are two surfaces, the dull white one and the dark one dwindling underneath. Then a whole acre settles with a noise like somebody treading on a basket.

It is difficult to shout to anybody here. The return mains carry the low, quite musical complaint of the pumps; the air-handling fans beat across it; a loose cover somewhere in the middle distance goes *dang, dang, dang* in a maddeningly leisurely fashion. You might see a fitter on the ridge turn and cup a hand to her ear, and give up and point instead. At a change of shift the clocks flash blue. The porches have double doors, mats for knocking ice off boots, pots of red geraniums in the space between the doors. Seen across all that concrete, a geranium makes an astonishing quantity of red.

There is some green outside as well. Beside the eastern pump house a seam leaks warm condensate, and the maintenance trams used to stop there in the days when men were still bringing mud in on their wheels. A strip of fine grass follows the seam downhill. It is hardly wider than a shoelace, but nearly two hundred yards long. In its upper reaches it flowers. Little pale flies crawl on the grass heads, too cold to take wing until the pump has been running for an hour or so. Once in a while a wagtail comes down the vehicle ramp and works its way along the entire strip, stepping rather high over the grass as though this were one furrow of an immense ploughed field and the rest might come up any day.

The newest feature is a ravine about a hand deep, below the overflow lip. Grit carried in the flood has cut clean through the gray wearing coat there and exposed a layer of blue tile which the present crew didn't know was there. It makes a pretty little blue bed for the runoff, branching at the bottom around an obdurate brass bolt. People from the other stations come over on their break to look. There are plenty of larger cuts and channels in the basin, some big enough to drive a tram through, but this is the first one the water chose for itself.
