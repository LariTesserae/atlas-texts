From the service road all you see is the top of a porcelain insulator, brown as a glazed bun, standing up out of the salt grass. The pole it belonged to is long gone. Take three steps down the bank to look at it and you find a whole little hollow. The insulator sits on a shelf of black stone about level with your knee. Below it the ground falls away again, in a series of steps too shallow to be stairs, to a pool the size and shape of an open newspaper.

It is hot down here. The bank cuts off the wind from the flats, and the afternoon sun gets in almost squarely. Even the few blades of grass that hang over the edge cast good black shadows. There is no grass below that edge. Most of what looks like grass from above is the reflection of it, very fine and green, in water spread so thin on the steps that you would otherwise take them for polished stone.

The black material is furnace slag. This end of the road was filled in with it years ago, and only this summer a section of the bank eased out of place, as neatly as a drawer pulling forward in its runners. The hollow is the space it left behind. Nothing in it has had time to go blunt. There are little caves the size of thimbles, bubbles cut in half; there are ridges you could crease paper over. A rusty piece of angle iron sticks out of one face. Its other half sticks out of the opposite face, a little lower down. Between them, in undisturbed air, hangs the smell of hot pennies.

Water comes through the joint under the road. It does not drip. It takes hold of the underside of the top step and works its way around the lip, broadening into a fan as it goes down. A pinkish crust has begun at the sides of the fan. Near the bottom there is one patch of living green, a soft nap no larger than a thumbprint. The rest is bare. Nothing skates on the pool. No wings tick against the bank.

What you hear instead is a tremendous steady rasp. There is a pumping house on the far side of the road, out of sight and scarcely audible up on the flats. Down here you have your head beside the old iron main. It passes through the bank without showing so much as a bolt, but a whole wall of the hollow gives out its noise. If you put your palm flat there you can feel, inside the rasp, the quite leisurely turn of the machinery, one heavy beat and two light ones. The porcelain on the shelf chatters against a grain of slag. Move it the width of a finger and that small, insistent part of the din is gone.

For all the racket there is almost nothing to watch. A shaving of white salt at the pool’s margin has one end underwater and the other end dry. The wet end is going clear. By evening the shelf will be in shade, and a little more water will get past it without drying; by morning the shaving may have floated loose. For now the light reaches all the way to the bottom. You can see the square sandy impression of a bolt head down there, every corner intact, slowly filling with pink silt.
