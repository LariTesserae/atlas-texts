Half a block below the tram terminus there is a square of water about the size of a schoolyard. It occupies what was to have been the lower ticket hall. The stairs go down to it in two broad flights, with a landing at the turn and sockets along the wall for a handrail that was never fitted. On the last three steps the concrete is dark with water. On the fourth there is usually a clean line of little brown frogs, all facing out into the hall.

The hall was sunk into gravel, and no amount of pumping ever quite dried it out. Now it serves as a sump for the whole underpass. Most of its machinery is gathered at one end, behind a locked blue gate. The rest is open space: a few square piers, a galvanized inspection walk, and the reflections of these things extending a surprisingly long way through an inch or two of water. Only at the pump end is the water deep. There the unfinished floor slopes away and the reflections disappear into the black comb of an intake screen.

It is hotter down here than in the street. The steam main serving the tram sheds runs the length of one wall, bundled in stained insulation and ticking at its brackets. After a summer shower the water coming down from the asphalt is warm enough in its own right. You can tell where the deep groundwater gets in because the frogs crowd those places, sometimes piled two deep in a little fan of cool grit below a joint. At the foot of the stairs the air smells of wet pennies. Farther in, the smell is mostly that of a hot engine room, oil and old paint, with an occasional sweet breath from a bruised plane leaf brought down through a drain.

There is daylight under just one grating. At noon it makes a pale grid on the water, in which a clump of grass grows out of a lost street-sweeper brush. Its roots have taken a good hold between the bristles. Apart from that, the green in the hall is a slippery nap on the submerged steps. There seems too little here to feed anything. Then a tram passes overhead, shaking the grating, and the whole bright grid turns gray with midges lifting off it. What looked like mortar stippled along the wall is mostly the empty skins of midges. Mosquito larvae hang in the still bays between the piers; water boatmen work the open stretches in quick silver strokes. High on the cable trays, well clear of the water, crickets stand so close together that their feelers keep touching. Their scraping fills the concrete box. The frogs eat whatever falls.

The floor is acquiring a geography the survey drawings do not show. Every rain delivers a spoonful of the street through each downpipe: sand from paving joints, brick dust, seeds, little crescents of safety glass. Around the downpipe mouths low, clean-edged fans are spreading. Two have nearly met behind the middle pier. In the narrow water left between them the current has begun to sort the grains, laying down a dark thread of heavy stuff in the bottom of a channel no wider than a thumb. A fitter penciled the outline of one fan on the pier last winter, with the date. Its tip is now a handspan beyond the mark.

For hours the only mechanical sound is the slight knock of the steam pipe. The water climbs the lowest riser so slowly that a stranded leaf makes a better gauge than the staff bolted to the wall. Eventually the float in the deep end tips its switch. A contactor claps shut behind the blue gate. There is a heavy, gathering hum, and the leaf begins to move sideways, at first so gently that the frog sitting on it stays put.

By the time the pump cuts out, the fans are islands again. Their wet flanks shine for a minute in the amber light over the gate. In the new quiet you can hear grit still traveling inside the downpipes, one grain striking an elbow, then another. The frogs come off the steps to hunt along the edges the water has left.
