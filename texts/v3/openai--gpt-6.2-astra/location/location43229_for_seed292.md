From the doorway of the pump house the far shore looks like a low bank of cloud. It is only when a patch of sun travels over it that you see grass, then the white sides of sheep, then grass again. Between here and there are eleven miles of water. This morning the depth at the pump house step is four inches. Yesterday it was dry. Out toward the sheep there are drowned valleys where a sounding line will run off its reel.

The whole wet country is the top of a mountain. At its eastern edge a creek goes rattling through the sedges and leaves the plateau in a single fall; on a clear afternoon you can look down beside it and see another weather altogether, a brown river in sunshine, roads fine as scratches, smoke standing upright. Up here the air is cool enough to hurt a wet hand. There are no summits to speak of. The mountain was old and worn nearly level before the ice came over it, and the ice polished what was left. Wherever the peat rolls back there is the same gray, close-grained rock, with little garnet knobs standing proud of it. The ancient divisions between watersheds are so low that in a strong west wind the water crosses them the wrong way.

Much of what looks from a distance like solid green ground is alive and afloat. The sedges knit their roots into a hide thick enough for geese to walk on. Along its ragged edges the water is clear as window glass for a foot or two; you can see the red root tips, shrimp working upside down among them, and the long leaves of pondweed laid all one way by the draw of the creek. Farther under, the light turns the color of steeped tea. In sheltered bays there are whole submerged meadows of quillwort. A duck can go down in one and come up yards away chewing, with a silver bead on every feather of its head. Even the boulders carry gardens, crowberry and dwarf willow on top, moss at the wash line, a dark green nap below.

Very little of this ever holds still. Squalls run in lanes over the plateau. One bay will be flattened white with rain while the next still reflects a perfectly ordinary blue sky. The waves are short, hard, and very close together. They clap under the edges of the root mats and lift them in sequence, so that a field of flowering sedge appears to take several quick breaths. Sometimes a piece tears loose. There is an island with three rusty fence posts in it that has been all the way to the far shore and back; it fetches up for weeks at a time, becomes popular with nesting gulls, then goes blundering off in the night with all the gulls aboard.

The pump house is one of five brick sheds built when men cut peat here on an industrial scale. They laid a narrow railway out onto the beds. Two lengths of rail still show in shallow water, bright on top where the drifting grit scours them, and minnows hang in the slack behind the sleepers. Only this pump still runs. Its business now is to keep the road culvert clear, a modest purpose for such an enormous iron wheel. When it starts, the windowpanes chatter in their putty. You hear it best during the small lulls when the geese have stopped complaining and the hail has stopped leaping on the roof; then there is that slow paired thump, and, between the two beats, the astonishing multitude of lesser sounds that were there all along. Water sucking through grass. A snipe calling. The tick of gravel being rolled up and let go.

Toward evening the wind drops on the near shore but keeps blowing out in the middle. For half an hour the step stands dry. Little black beetles come out of a crack in the mortar and spread their wing cases to the sun. Then the water the wind has heaped against the far bank begins its long return. There is no breaking wave to announce it. A puddle beside the rails fills from the bottom; the minnows turn; the gray stone of the step goes dark from one end to the other. By the time the sheep across the water disappear into shade, the beetles are back in the wall and a tuft of willow is sailing quietly past the door.
