The first thing you hear on coming over the lip is a river. It sounds full enough to carry trees. Below you, in the poor grey light, you can make out the whole valley floor: four miles of shingle, three long shelves of bedrock, a pond about the size of a house. There is no river. The shingle is dry enough that dust comes off it on your cuffs.

It takes a while to find out where the noise is. Down on the first shelf it is overhead; halfway up either wall it is level with your ear. The valley is sounding at its old height. Water wore it down by inches through the dark limestone, and in most respects the water went with it. There are still pebbles up there turning over in the current. You can hear the hollow knock of a big one that has worked loose; if you stay a long time, you get to know that pebble very well. It never quite rolls free.

The peaks beyond the lip are nearly black at this height, striped with little seams of snow too deep in the rock to see the sky. Far below, a white bank of weather fills the country where trees can grow. Only the finest part of a cloud ever comes up here. It collects like breath on the undersides of ledges. Along one wall the ledges are wide and tilt back a little, and each holds a narrow bed of mud, green at the uphill edge and frozen hard at the other. Small round plants grow in these beds, each with a tight skin of overlapping leaves. What looks from a distance like a continuous green stripe resolves, close up, into twelve or fifteen plants a good walk apart. Between them the wet limestone is green on its own account.

There are a few stones set on end on the middle shelf, with broad slabs laid across them. The slab roofs come scarcely to the knee. They might have been shelters when more of the floor was there; now there is only headroom for birds. One small dun bird does live under the last of them. It comes out to feed on the pale flies at the rim of the pond, running rather than flying, stopping with one foot raised. When it opens its bill the call comes from well above it, out in the wash of the river. The bird waits for the brief lull behind the big stone to call again. There may be an answer. From the floor it is hard to tell.

At the pond you can put a hand into water cold enough to hurt between the fingers. Its bottom is brown silt, perfectly smooth except for the two starry tracks where the bird goes in to drink. Over the middle there is a tremendous downpour, the sustained, battering sound of rain hitting deep water. The pond takes no notice. From its rim you can look straight through the rain to the far wall and see a white thread hanging there, the one actual fall of water in all that space. It descends perhaps the length of an arm before it soaks back into the stone.

After a time the ear ceases to insist on a flood. The separate sounds begin to serve as landmarks: the ford with its gravelly hiss, the high boulder with water tearing on either side, the long reach of almost quiet water toward the head. It would be possible to walk the valley in the dark by these alone. Perhaps that is what the low roofs are for. Under one of them, lying flat with a cheek against the cold shelf, you find a pocket of plain silence. You can hear the dun bird's feet coming home across the roof, a few light ticks, and the small dry rustle as it settles in beside you. Outside, immensely steady, the missing depth of the world goes on passing.
