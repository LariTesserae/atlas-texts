Under the tram interchange there is a second square, a little smaller than the one where people wait in daylight. You can see from end to end of it by the light that comes down the stair. There are four street names in the tilework, a kerb with a shallow notch for wheels, even a strip of rough paving where a person ought to stop before crossing. Beyond the kerb there is twenty metres of clear warm water. The lines of white tile continue across the bottom.

It was a street until this spring. The city lowered the ground beneath it to make room for runoff. The paving went down in one piece; the walls, which are cut straight into the hill, show what a fresh break looks like here. The stone is dark plum with little flat cream-coloured inclusions that stand out far enough to catch a sleeve. Water comes out all over that surface. At first it hangs in separate drops. Then one drop gets heavy, takes the others with it, and leaves a black line fine as a pencilled hair from the ceiling to the pool. In the months since the cut, the longest of these lines has worn a channel about as deep as the lettering on a coin.

Most of the green is at the stair end. Turf from the old square went down with the stone, and the grass has grown long and lain over, its pale undersides showing where the tips trail in the water. Among it grow rushes and a low fleshy plant with paired leaves that hold a drop between them even after the stair has dried. A plane tree still stands in its iron grate on the last dry corner. Its crown was cut off to fit the space beneath the interchange. Now it has sent a single branch sideways towards the stair, with leaves as big as hands, all held in the same plane. For half an hour near midday their shadows reach the opposite wall. There are no bites out of the leaves. Nothing skates on the pool, either. When a leaf falls you can watch its rings travel all the way to the submerged shopfronts without meeting another disturbance.

That is in the interval between fillings. A shower up on the interchange begins here with a clack underfoot. Then every gutter around the square delivers water at once. The sound is astonishing in so small a place: a coarse tearing noise at the outlets, a hollow drumming from behind the shut metal shutters, and over both the clear, spaced notes of water falling down the stair, step to step, until the separate streams join. The air smells warmly of pavement. It gets difficult to make out the far end. A loose mat of grass lifts off the kerb like wet hair being combed out, and the tree grate disappears.

There is no pump. Beneath each of the four street names is a little enamel arrow, and when the water reaches an arrow the tiles below it begin to lie farther apart. The joints stay the width of joints. It is the distance *across* a tile that increases, though a tile prised up and taken upstairs is an ordinary handspan of fired clay. For as long as the shower lasts there is room here for another street's worth of rain, then another. From the stair the only sign is that the white crossing on the bottom takes longer and longer for a fallen leaf's shadow to traverse. When the sewers uphill are clear again, the city takes the distance back. The water goes out through a single brass mouth in the old tram stop, no bigger than a letter slot.

Long after the gutters fall quiet that mouth is still talking, a low full sound like someone pouring from a jug in the next room. The grate appears again, then the rough paving. The grass settles in a slightly different direction. On the wall above the pool there is a band of damp red stone, and at one end of it, already, a row of little green points where the next strip of moss will be. Up at the interchange the shower is over; a narrow patch of sun has got as far as the third step. Down here the tree holds out its branch.
