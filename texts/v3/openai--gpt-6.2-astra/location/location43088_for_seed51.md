Rain has stopped on the near side of the mere. Across it, a good hour's walk away, rain is still whitening the alders. Between the two shores the water lies in long dark strips, divided by ridges of peat scarcely higher than a sleeping person's back. Each ridge carries a little different company of plants. On one there is nothing but rust-red rush; on the next, willow scrub so thick that last year's leaves are still caught upright between the stems. The highest support a few broad alders, all leaning, though no two lean the same way. The ground has gone soft under them at different times.

It is cold enough for breath to show. The light comes mostly off the water and strikes the undersides of things: the silver nap on a willow leaf, the pale gills of a fungus out on a drowned bough. Inland the ridges run together into low bluffs of blue clay. Water sweats out of them in beads. Now and then a piece the size of a plum comes away, exposing a blue so deep and clean it looks for a moment like a hole clear through the bank. At the foot, all these little falls of clay have been kneaded into the same easy slope. The place has had a long time to arrive at its present untidiness.

There is a great deal of noise for somewhere with hardly an animal in it. A continuous ripping sound comes from the rush beds. Under that is a bass note which varies enough to keep drawing the ear: two pitches almost in tune, then closer, then one. Look down through a clear reach of water and the mud is trembling in little squares. The squares keep their edges even where the bed slopes away out of sight. A shed alder leaf settling through them turns a quarter turn, descends the breadth of a finger, turns again.

The whole lowland manages water in a fashion peculiar to it. When a pool has more than its banks will hold, the excess can enter any other pool whose bed is trembling at exactly the same pitch. There need be no channel. Miles of clay may lie between them. For the duration of the note the two bottoms are one bottom, and the water goes the shorter way. There is no apparatus to uncover. Dig down and there is clay stacked in fine greasy leaves, with mica between them and the black threads of roots, the same all the way down. Whatever determines the pitch is distributed through that substance as thoroughly as the blue is.

It is possible to see nearly the whole exchange from one of the bluffs. Out in the rain the low willows begin to emerge, wet to their forks. Close by, in a hollow where the rain has been over for an hour, water wells up clear, lifting the flat weed off the mud. A thousand stems take the strain together. That is the ripping sound. It travels along the shore faster than wind, stops for the length of a breath, then starts again much farther off. Sometimes a patch of rain can work its way around three sides of the mere in an afternoon without once falling on the mere itself.

The plants have made considerable use of this arrangement. A sedge drops its heavy seed straight down beside the parent stem; by dusk the seed may be on a shelf of silt beyond the farthest alders. There are whole crescents of yellow iris in hollows separated by acres of unsuitable ground. But on the occasional hump of gravel that never quite takes up the note, the vegetation is close and private. Each has its low juniper, its sour little cranberries, its particular patch of lichen the color of flour. In one of these dry places a snipe stands with mud halfway up its bill. When the ground sounds it tilts its head. It does not fly.

Toward evening the rain on the far shore clears. For a while the mere grows louder. Old deliveries are still arriving; the roots underfoot creak, and the clay bluffs shed a last few blue pieces into their aprons. Then the pitches drift apart. Water left halfway up a willow proceeds down the bark in ordinary drops. The snipe walks out onto a tongue of newly bared mud and begins to feed, probing among small round holes while their rims are still sharp. Across the open lowland there is just enough light to see where else the mud has risen: pale, narrow beaches, each with a dark fringe of weed, appearing one after another all the way into the rainless distance.
