The top of the mountain is wide enough to have weather at one end that the other end never gets. From its western lip you can see snow falling on the eastern third, a gray fringe that stops well short of the middle. In between lies a country of long, almost level hollows, the green gone silver in the cold. There are perhaps six things tall enough to cast a shadow. At this distance they look like trees. One of them folds up its legs and disappears into the ground.

Almost everything exposed to the sky is a leaf, though very little of it looks like foliage. There is a close brown nap on the rises and a smoother, bottle-green skin in the hollows. A drop of rain landing on the nap stays a bead until another drop touches it; then both are gone. On the smooth skin it spreads for yards, so thin that the first sign of a shower is a widening reflection of the clouds. Underneath, the leaves are thick as shoulders, fitted loosely over one another, with clear cold water moving in the fit. The lowest layers have been pressed to black felt. Once, where an entire flank slipped in a thaw, stone came to light: a few acres of pale granite with the corners worn off its crystals. By the following summer the leaves had drawn together again. The mountain has very little left to lose. Even its precipices come to an end in long easy slopes, far down under the cloud.

The noise up here takes some getting used to. A steady *ka-lung, ka-lung* comes from no discernible quarter. There are long runs of it, then an interval in which one hears the little intimate sounds of a wet place—a beak probing, a bubble breaking—and then it starts up again, nearer by a mile. Down in the felt are chambers lined with muscle-red vegetable tissue. Water accumulates in one, stretches the lining taut, and trips a flap. The discharge opens the next chamber. A whole drainage can go off in sequence. From above, on a clear day, the progress of the work is visible as a dark band traveling uphill against the streams. It leaves millions of small round holes breathing vapor. In the cold the vapor freezes around each hole in a white cuff; for an hour the western half of the mountain is white with these little chimneys. Then the flow reverses and takes them in like teeth.

It is a remarkably populous place to look so vacant. Most of the animals live in the thickness of the leaves. Flat fish swim there on their sides. Little six-legged grazers shear the pale hairs from the undersurfaces and carry their young in the swathes they have cut. Birds with narrow heads walk about below the skin, pushing up a traveling crease, until they find a seam through which to put a bill into the air. The tall animals are waders. They stand with each foot in a different chamber and seem to know, from the discrepancy in pressure, where the fish will be sent next. When a flap opens a wader simply lets its body drop between its feet. Often enough it comes up swallowing. There might be half a day's walk between one wader and the next.

Toward evening the pumps begin to disagree about the shape of the country. A discharge starts in the east and finishes in a hollow in the west without passing through the intervening ground. For a few minutes the two hollows have a common surface. A fish slipping under at the snowy end leaves a ring at the clear end; snow itself comes drifting up through green water into air where no snow is falling. This is when the birds emerge. They come out in such numbers that the skin goes slack behind them. They stand a bill's length apart all around the joined water, dipping for things carried across, keeping their feet well back from the edge. Sometimes the hollows part gently. Sometimes they part with a report that rolls loose ice off the granite miles below.

Afterward there is a smell like a peeled stalk, sweet and a little dirty. The split places exude a transparent gum. Moths crowd onto it, their wings held tight over their backs against the sleet, and the birds which missed the fish take moths. By nightfall the long views are empty again. The snow in the east has stopped. In the west a single patch of it rests on the brown nap, still bearing the delicate forked tracks of a bird that spent the whole afternoon at the other end of the mountain.
