There is a room under the white hill where, at midday, a shadow falls upward. A moth clinging to the underside of a ledge has a little gray moth on the stone above it. The light comes down a slit at the high end of the room, strikes a bank of powdered limestone, and goes everywhere from there. By noon the floor is painful to look at. The ceiling, which is low enough in places to touch, is lit like the underside of a hand held over a lamp.

The room is about thirty paces long. You could walk in through the slit if you turned sideways and didn’t mind a short slide at the end. For the length of that slide there are grass roots in the soil beside your face, then the roots stop, the air turns warm and chalky, and you are standing quite alone in all that light. Nothing occupies the middle of the room. Its floor slopes almost imperceptibly from the bright bank toward a dark line at the far wall. Long ago water went that way in some quantity. It took the corners off the outcrops and left a succession of shallow lips, like the marks on sand where little waves have come to rest. The lips are stone all the way through. A lizard lying against one fits very neatly into the shape of a vanished wave.

Up close, there are lizards everywhere. Most are the color of the dust, except for their eyelids, which are yellow; once you notice the first one blinking, the edges of the room begin to wink. They keep to the edges for good reason. Out in the open there are no insects and no cover and the glare comes straight into their eyes. All the business of the place goes on in a strip a handspan wide where the floor meets the wall. Here are polished seed husks, small heaps of shed wings, beetles with white flour in the joints of their legs. A few wiry plants grow sideways from cracks and lift their leaves just clear of the stone. Their lower surfaces are green, their upper surfaces almost colorless. In this room it pays to turn a leaf over.

The dark line at the far end is damp. On the other side of the wall a seam of clay holds rain from the hilltop for weeks, giving it up a bead at a time. A drop swells there very slowly. Small flies can stand on the dry stone beside it and drink without breaking its skin. Occasionally one grows too heavy and rolls down into a hollow that would just hold an egg. There is a whole chain of these hollows, each with its own level and its own narrow rim of brown life. Only after a hard rain does water pass from one to the next. Then for an afternoon there is a stream not much longer than a man, running on a course older than the hill’s present skin of soil.

There are snails that spend the dry weeks sealed to the ceiling. When the seam begins to sweat they loosen and make their way down, though the stone between them and the water still feels perfectly dry. They leave tracks that are invisible from above. Toward evening, when the light comes level through the slit, the wall shows what has happened there: hundreds of silver lines, crossing and recrossing, little purposeless loops, a long straight descent interrupted by the exact outline of a sleeping lizard. By morning the wall looks blank again.

Almost the only sound is a faint, irregular ticking from the seed beetles. Each works a fallen seed into a notch in the limestone, braces its legs, and knocks the husk against the hard edge until it splits. The good notches have been used for so long that their bottoms are glossy. Sometimes a beetle loses its purchase and a seed rolls out across the floor. The noise it makes is surprisingly large. For a moment every yellow lid along that side of the room is open. Then nothing follows. The seed has come to rest in the bright, unvisited middle, where it will stay until the next great wash of water, or until the hill wears low enough for rain to fall directly on it.
