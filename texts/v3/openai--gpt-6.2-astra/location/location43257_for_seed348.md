The street is wide enough for two wagons to pass, except at the corners, where the walls have bellied out. Halfway along it stands a weighhouse with a broad iron platform flush with the paving. Beyond that there is a row of six houses, a machine hall, and a flight of steps leading up over the sea wall. From the top step you can see the whole borough. There is a tin mug on the sill of the third house. It is about the right distance away for you to see the handle but not the chip in the rim.

At this height the sea looks like a continuation of the roofs, grey and ridged and lit by the same mild daylight. Below the wall the air is quite cool. Damp gathers on the undersides of things. Put your hand under the weighhouse rail and it comes away wet, though you could sit on the rail all afternoon in dry trousers. At the corners of the platform there are four little wells with brass lips, each holding about a finger of clear water. Nothing grows in them. The oldest masonry here is a pale brown brick full of bits of shell, and it wears away to a pale brown sand without passing through any green stage at all.

There is a terrific noise. You feel it especially in the hollow of the jaw. From the steps it sounds like one long freight train crossing an iron bridge; down at street level you can distinguish the individual knocks, the grinding undertone, and, traveling the length of the block at regular intervals, a sound like a thousand people tearing cloth. The machine hall accounts for surprisingly little of it. Inside are two electric motors, a reduction gear, and a man in a blue jersey eating a pear out of a tin. He has laid the lid carefully under the tin to keep the juice off the bench. He can hear you quite well in here. The walls are lined with felt.

What the motors do is draw sea water through the foundations of the borough. Every house has two cellars, a dry one for household use and a wet one belonging to the municipality. The wet cellars communicate through arches. A surveyor’s lamp lowered through any of the four wells outside the weighhouse would show, some distance below, a square of white tile almost entirely covered with animals.

They are about the size of shirt buttons, six-footed, with a single hard plate on the back and a soft grey fringe that works continuously at the water. They stand so close that their plates touch. In the narrow seams between them live much smaller, transparent animals which go sideways, slipping past one another with the facility of coins going into a purse. There are long white eels under the sill of each arch. There are blind red crabs in the gratings. Even the eels, if you could hold one still under the lamp, would prove to be densely inhabited along the gills. The pumps deliver a steady ration of fine animal matter from the sea. Very little of it gets as far as the outlet.

Periodically the button animals lift one edge of their plates to clear away grit. One lifts, obliging the next to lift, and a ripple goes across the tiles, up the cellar wall and over the crown of the arch. That is the tearing sound. The knocking is the crabs. Each has a particular bar of a grating against which it breaks shells; some of the bars have been struck so often that they are waisted in the middle. As for the deep train noise, no one has succeeded in sorting out what proportion belongs to the water, what to the animals, and what to the fabric of the town. A loose cup set down anywhere outdoors proceeds by small jumps to the nearest joint in the paving and stays there rattling.

Very little happens aboveground. There is a good deal of room between one thing and the next: the mug on the sill, a coil of cable against a whitewashed wall, the one person crossing the square with a loaf tucked under his arm. The houses have no front gardens. Each has instead a shallow stone tray beside the door, where a caller can put down a parcel without its walking away. The trays have been hollowed from years of use. Nobody remembers having them made.

You might expect so much life underneath to break through somewhere, to lift the platform in the night or come pouring out of a sink. But the borough has kept its level to within a quarter of an inch since the weighhouse was built. The same volume of water passes under it each hour. Where a tile comes loose, small shellfish occupy the gap and furnish, in time, a surface of almost exactly the same thickness. Down in the loaded dark there is scarcely room to insert a knife blade; up here a scrap of paper can blow the length of the street without encountering anything. Both conditions seem permanent. On the third sill the mug trembles in its old ring, its handle turned outwards towards the light.
