Under the copper market is a square the sun has never reached. It is about the size of the market above it, though the two lie a little askew: the north stair comes down beneath a fish stall and the south stair comes up in somebody's kitchen. People use the north stair. For its last nine steps there is a handrail on either side, and then, where a newcomer might particularly want something to hold on to, there is thirty paces of warm dark air.

The floor slopes in from all four sides. It is made of old municipal stone, the dull red composite used for curbs and public baths, with flecks of crockery visible wherever it has worn smooth. A hairdresser's blue tile has survived down here as a blue comma in the lip of a gutter. The gutters have changed course since they were laid. Water coming off the east wall, for instance, has preferred to take a long diagonal across the square, and the stone has yielded to its preference. The diagonal is only a finger deep, but it goes through two generations of neatly squared-off repairs. No one repairs it now.

There is a city block's worth of machinery here and almost none of it stands out from the walls. Behind a single flush panel on the west side are the freight lifts, the heat exchange for the bathhouse, and the switch that keeps the upper tramway level when a car takes the market bend. The city used to put brass plates over such things. The plates have been taken away, leaving pale rectangles which, once your eyes have adjusted, are among the brightest objects in the square. Otherwise there are just the stairs, a low bench, and a porcelain cup on a chain at the mouth of a drinking pipe. Even the chain goes slack into a little hole when the cup is set down.

What fills the space is the noise of the tramway switch. It is extraordinarily loud. There is a wooden *tok* as a car enters the bend, followed by a full descending chord that seems to come from the floor, your ribs, and the hand cupped over your ear in equal measure. A visitor listening at the west panel hears very little there. The switch works by letting an oncoming weight bear for an instant on a much larger area than the patch of rail beneath its wheels. For that instant the whole square is under the tram. The bench sings on after it has gone. A regular can distinguish a passenger car from a delivery flat by setting a heel on the bench, but nobody bothers to do this except at the end of the afternoon, when the flat with the empty bread tins means the market is closing.

Down here afternoon is otherwise a matter of water. All morning heat seeps through the ceiling from the little businesses overhead. By midday the air is warm enough to smell of damp brick dust, with a sweeter smell, like cut melon, close to the drinking pipe. Then the fish sellers wash their counters, the bathhouse draws its cold supply, and a strip of ceiling as wide as a street turns cool. Beads gather on it. For ten minutes or so it rains indoors, over one half of the bench and nowhere near either stair. The first drops strike hollowly; the last ones make hardly any sound, falling into the water that the diagonal has gathered. An hour later there is nothing left of it but a thread of wet red stone.

A small stand of maidenhair grows at the foot of the north stair, where a little daylight comes down reflected from the fish seller's steel cupboard. The fronds all face the cupboard. When its doors are opened to take stock, the patch of reflected sky travels several feet up the wall, exposing the pale undersides of the leaves and, occasionally, the transparent shell of a snail no larger than a shirt button. Farther in, nothing has leaves. There is a soft dark skin along the diagonal which survives between rains by drying to the thickness of a pencil mark. A solitary cave cricket walks the length of it after the water passes, feeling ahead with antennae that spread nearly the width of the gutter. During the chord of a turning tram it stands still with one foreleg raised. In the silence it sets the foot down again.

People from the market come below to sit through the worst of the summer heat. They carry no lamps; too much light makes the climb back up surprisingly hard, since it shows you the bare height of the ceiling and the long unsupported span beneath all those shops. They know where the bench is. They sit on the dry half, pass the porcelain cup, wait out a tram before finishing a sentence. By closing time the pipe has drawn a cold circle on the wall around its mouth. The last person up the stairs usually puts a palm there for a moment. At supper, long after the square has emptied and the rails are quiet, somebody cutting onions or counting change will still have one cool hand.
