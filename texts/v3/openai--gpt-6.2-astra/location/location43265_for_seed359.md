The garden is laid in a shallow tray, broad enough for nine households and their paths, nowhere deep enough to hide a standing person. Its rim is a curb of fired clay. On the inside of the curb you can still see the heel of the hand that pressed each section into the next. On the outside there is no view. Lean across, shading your eyes against the quite ordinary afternoon light, and you see the inside of the curb again, with nine small sets of footprints coming up to it through the dust.

It is warm down there, especially at ankle height. The paving holds heat. It was laid in narrow strips, pale and dark by turns, and the dark strips give on the sole like leather. Under them are the floors of earlier gardens. Nobody has struck earth, though a family sinking a post at the east end once went down through sixty floors, the last made of yellow thumbnail tiles, each with four little holes for sewing it to its neighbors. They put the tiles back in order. The post now rests on a broad foot and the gate it carries sags a little.

From the curb the growth looks like one continuous cabbage, immense and intricate. Go down the three steps, though, and it separates into little holdings. There are fans with dusty lilac ribs and a nap that changes color when brushed. There are apricot-colored tassels that trail from one low roof to the next, so heavily inhabited by white climbing animals that some tassels scarcely show their color at all. In a court no bigger than a bed, a woman tends a stand of plain green blades taller than herself. She is proud of their plainness. She made the first blade from a strip of beaten fiber, creased it lengthwise over her knee, and tucked it into a warm joint in the floor. All the others came of it. They have even inherited the slight jog in the crease where her kneecap had once been broken.

Nothing here needs watering. There is no word in use for watering, though there is an old implement with a perforated snout hung over one doorway, and the household finds it excellent for sifting flour. The roots of the garden feed on the difference between things that have been made alike. Lay two bowls side by side: one will be a grain heavier, the other a hair wider at the lip. In the night a root as fine as a loose thread will find its way between them. By the end of a season there will be a thicket there, and no one, not even the potter, will be able to tell the bowls apart. This is why so much of the floor is paved in pairs. It is also why a good gardener does not strive for perfection at the bench.

For all that growth the garden is almost silent. Most of the animals have pads, or move by taking hold and letting go. The white ones on the tassels are little more than two soft hands joined at the wrists, with a russet eye in the hollow of each palm. They graze facing inward and travel blind. Beneath them, flat brown grazers ease their way between the leaves, carrying on their backs what look like scraps from a basket maker’s floor. At dusk the scraps uncurl and go off on their own. A child sitting very still on a doorstep can sometimes feel a whole litter pass under the arch of one foot. The loudest sound most days is the click of a bowl set down beside its fellow.

Only the western quarter is bare. A new floor was fitted there last winter, slightly above the old one; its edges are sharp enough to snag a hem. The houses on it are new too, made of pressed dust faced with thin scales of horn. As yet each has only its one door, its one back room, its one square of shade. In the older quarter such simplicity is long gone. There a cupboard opened from a kitchen may give on the underside of a bench three houses away. A path used daily by two sisters going to visit each other has shortened over the years until they can exchange a dish of meal without either taking her hand off her own latch. Nobody arranged this. It happens where a pair of things have nearly finished feeding their roots. First they look alike, then they are alike, and finally there is very little point in having the distance.

That is the worry at the curb. Its maker was a patient person with an unvarying grip. Along almost its whole extent the prints of her hand have grown smooth and identical. There is a place by the third step where the inside has already come to meet the inside, and a white climber can be seen moving slowly away in both directions, opening and shutting its small fists. Every few years someone suggests cutting a notch there to distinguish one side from the other. Every few years they get as far as bringing out the chisel. But the growth along that stretch is lovely: close dark fans, the new fronds still folded, a fine gold meal caught in the nap of each, warm to the wrist when you reach in. And there are nests. They generally agree to leave it for another season.
