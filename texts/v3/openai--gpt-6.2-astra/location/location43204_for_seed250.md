At noon the upper sea casts a blue shadow over the lower country. From below you can see the undersides of waves going over, pale and immensely broad, as the people of a wetter world might see cloud shadows going over wheat. There are no people here. The lower country is a plain of lilac-grey rock, still warm in the deep cracks, and the sea hangs some four miles above it in plain daylight.

It is easiest to judge the distance where a mountain goes up into the water. The mountain is dry most of the way up. Near the top there is a belt of rain, then a belt of bare wet stone shining in the sun, and then the peak enters the underside of the sea and is lost to view. Now and again, in very clear weather, the submerged part shows through: a continuation of the same black shoulder, apparently broken off and displaced a little to the east by refraction. Up there a whole province of cool green water is sliding over the peak. A torn scrap of weed takes three days to make the crossing.

Most of the peaks have only just arrived. They rise by fits, with several quiet seasons between one heave and the next. Their flanks have the coarse grain and the clean corners of rock exposed by a single hammer blow. There is very little gravel. At the foot of a cliff one finds three or four fallen pieces so large that they amount to cliffs in their own right, and between them no slope of smaller debris, no soil, nothing to soften the meeting of the vertical face with the level plain. The rain has begun to draw lines down the stone. One could still count the lines.

The sea does not stay up by any ordinary buoyancy. There is a mineral in the mountains, a dull white inclusion which looks unpromising even when split and polished by ice. In a newly risen range the inclusions settle into agreement with one another. A change in one grain is answered far off in another, with no detectable interval between. Between them, at a height that depends on the shape of the range, water acquires a local down of its own. Rain falling through that height from above slows, stops, and falls back up. Water below it climbs in cold, crooked ropes. The effect extends well out beyond the mountains, so that out over the plain there are thousands of miles of sea with nothing visible holding it and no shore in any usual sense. At its far edge the upper and lower surfaces meet in a long, rounded green lip. Mist streams off the lip in both directions.

There is an extraordinary amount of room in it. For days a current may carry nothing at all that the eye could pick out: no sand, no leaf, not so much as a drifting shell. Daylight comes in from both sides. Looking up, one sees the corrugated silver of a surface under sky; looking down, the distant plain in its blue shadow, with occasional patches of sun that wander fast enough to climb a mountain in a minute. In the middle depths these two lights mingle to a mild, serviceable green. The water is cold enough to keep a flake of ice for a long time without quite being cold enough to make one.

What grows here grows on the heights. There are a few olive-coloured straps of weed in the seams of the submerged peaks, flattened so close to the stone by the current that at first they seem to be stains in it. On the lee side, where the water briefly goes slack, the ends lift and stand clear. Underneath they are a startling yellow. Once each long summer they let go a fine golden dust; for a few hours the wake of each mountain is visible for miles. Nothing comes to feed there. The dust spreads, thins, encounters perhaps one other summit in its travels. Most of it simply goes on.

For such an empty place it is very loud. Wind bears against the upper face of the sea while a different wind, often travelling across it at right angles, works on the lower face. The waves they raise pass through each other in deep water. In the shallows over a summit the two sets sometimes meet. There is a blow like the slam of an enormous wet door, followed by a sustained grating note as water is forced through the cracks in the rock. The note carries much farther than the blow. A peak a hundred miles away will answer in a slightly lower register, and after a long pause a third, so faint that it seems less a sound than a loosening in the stone underfoot.

Every so often a range rises sufficiently to alter the level of the sea. The first sign down on the plain is a cessation of rain. All the drops overhead have turned at once; for a moment they hang like bright seeds, each with a little quiver in it. Then the whole underside goes white. Water begins moving toward the new height along a front too broad to take in, roaring at close quarters but almost noiseless from a distance, where it has the leisurely look of a bedcover being drawn up over a sleeper. Dry pinnacles enter the water for the first time. Elsewhere a summit that has been submerged for centuries comes out streaming into the wind. Its weed lies bright against the black rock for part of an afternoon and darkens by evening.

After one of these shifts the weather takes a while to find its feet. A hollow in the sea may enclose a lake of air as large as an inland country, complete with fog, crosswinds and a brief fall of snow. There is nowhere in that hollow for the snow to settle except on water. Seen from beneath, the flakes make a faint white floor; then the floor tilts, the air escapes in a single rolling disturbance, and the snow is gone. Long afterwards the swell from its departure is still abroad. It runs out toward the green lip under an ordinary sun, lifting first the upper surface, then the lower one, passing over reaches of clear water in which there is not even a speck of dust to mark its passage.
