At the bottom of the town there is a room the tide comes into. It is as wide as three houses and no higher than the underside of the street. A cart going over sounds very close. The driver would have to go a long way round, down the salt stair and back through a low square door, to stand directly under his own wheels.

The floor is made of great shallow dishes. They were laid side by side in wet mortar, and the men who laid them pinched the rims together with their thumbs. You can still see the prints in the sheltered parts. In the middle, where the water does its work, the rims have worn through in a sequence of little saddles. Water pouring from dish to dish has made a winding brook entirely in the glaze. There is a place where this brook forks, goes either side of a patch of yellow the size of a hand, and comes together again. The yellow is no higher than a few coats of paint. It has divided the water longer than anyone in the town has been alive.

Except for the floor there is very little to see. The roof is a single low barrel vault. Its bricks have been rubbed with lime so many times that the joints have filled up flush; it looks like the inside of a ladle. A strip of daylight comes in at the seaward end, gray even at noon, and floats on whichever dish happens to be full. On cold mornings the ceiling sweats. A drop hangs over its image in the glaze for a long time before the two go together. The salt stair is green on the lowest tread that stays dry at high water. All the growth that has a leaf to it is on that one tread. It is a handsome little pasture, perhaps long enough for a man to lie down in, and two fingers wide.

Nothing much crosses the open floor until the tide turns. Yet there are animals enough here to feed a street. The bricks were made hollow to save clay and help them fire evenly. Little openings left by the brickmakers’ sticks face into the room, hundreds of them, each with its own dark wet interior. A shrimp can spend its whole life in the walls and never be in darkness or daylight for more than half a day at a time. There are soft white crabs that fit sideways through the openings, and black hard ones that have grown too broad to get out and must take what passes their door. In one brick near the stair a little fish backs out tail first every evening, turns, and looks back into its hole. Usually several smaller fish come out then. If they do not, it goes back in.

The water arrives from underneath. First the mortar darkens; then a bead comes up in the deepest dish and rolls about like a loose eye. For a while you can hear all the small separate noises: claws sorting grit, the suck of a limpet shifting its foot, something fine and hard being dragged across clay. Then the sea gets into the wall. Air comes out of the stick holes in a great uneven chord. Some holes whistle and some burble and one, clogged with shells, makes a noise exactly like a woman calling a boy in to supper. It goes on calling long after any boy would have come. Up in the street the cart horses lay their ears back. Down here there is so much sound that the water itself seems oddly quiet, slipping over one worn lip after another until every division on the floor has vanished.

For an hour the room is a single sheet of pewter, ankle deep. Shrimps flick across it by the score. The white crabs climb down to graze the threads of red slime in the saddles; the fish that lives by the stair makes short, profitable runs among them. From above, through the seams in the paving, comes the smell of whatever rain has lately washed off the street: horse, ashes, cut fennel on market days. The sea is cold enough to ache in the bones of a bare foot. People once came down here to rinse wool. There are still two smooth hollows in the stair where a washer knelt, one deeper than the other.

When the tide leaves, the separate dishes come back, not in the order in which they disappeared. One drains through a crack beneath its neighbor. One keeps its water until a crab dislodges the bit of shell in its saddle. The yellow island comes up steaming faintly in the cold. For a few minutes there are hundreds of animals abroad and not enough water left to hide a single one. Their haste makes a dry pattering noise like peas poured out on a table. Then the floor is bare again.

Only at low water can you make out what the room was built for. The last dish, close to the square door, has a spout shaped to fit the lip of a bucket. Fresh water has been coming down through the street all along, one cold drop at a time, keeping to a narrow raised runnel the tide cannot quite overtop. Someone has set a clean bucket there this morning. In all that racket it has filled without a sound anyone could pick out.
