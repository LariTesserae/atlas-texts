Turn up one plate of ice at the edge of the inlet and the whole district would come away with it, hanging from the underside. It occupies a hollow about as wide as a mitten and nowhere much deeper than a thumb. Seen from above, through the cloudy ice, it looks like a greasy thumbprint. Seen from below it has streets.

They are upside down, if one takes the bay for the right way up. Their paving is the ice itself, a faintly corrugated blue, and from either side of each street hang rows of dwellings made of grit. The smallest grains are black and the larger ones amber. There is very little round sand here. A cliff split in the frost last autumn, and this is the flour from the fresh break, still full of little faces that catch what light comes through. Each dwelling is a tube, slightly too narrow for its tenant to turn around in. To look out the other end the tenant has to go out into the street, bend double, and back in again.

The tenants are pale, many-legged things with a streak of brown down the gut. They do most of the building. A grain is tried against the lip of a tube, turned several times, sometimes let go even after a long examination. The grain that falls seldom makes it out of the hollow. Someone farther down catches it. In the older quarter the tubes touch along their length and share walls; there are passages through five or six houses where the intervening grit has come loose and been carried off to build somewhere else. A worm the color of watered milk lives in these passages. You can see where its head is by the row of tenants backing out into the water ahead of it.

There is no particular scarcity of room to either side. There is the whole underside of the ice. But only here does a seam in the ceiling give out a fine, steady fall of food. A little brown algae grows inside the seam, no more than a stain until the sun comes round the cliff. Then the stain takes on depth. It is a grove of separate gold-brown blades, each with a bead of gas somewhere along it. In that weak afternoon light the blades loosen their grip on the ice, one here, three there, and come down broadside like shutters falling off a house. For a while every open doorway has a pair of arms in it.

What keeps the streets clear is the salt. As the ice thickens overhead it squeezes its salt into narrow runs; the heavy brine slips out through the floor of the street and goes straight down into the bay. No one builds in its path. A new run opening under a row of houses can empty them in a minute. The occupants feel it first with the delicate ends of their legs. They leave with whatever they happen to be holding, sometimes a good building stone, sometimes a shred of somebody's skin. By morning there is a clear lane where the houses were, with a scatter of handsome empty porches overhanging it. Out at its mouth the lane is still being made. There are needles of new ice there so fine that a passing swimmer bends them apart with its forehead, and they go back to growing a little crooked.

All this goes on almost without sound. Once in a long while a pressure change far out in the inlet makes the plate give a low note. It is felt throughout the district. The little red swimmers stop rowing and coast; the milk worm draws its head level with the nearest door and stays there. In that pause the hollow is briefly easy to see into. There are eggs tucked up against the blue paving, each with two dark dots inside. There is a dead tenant quite cleaned out, its transparent legs still hooked around a pillar. At the very back, beyond the tenanted rows, a solitary youngster is holding a chip of amber grit against bare ice. The note dies away. The youngster takes the chip down, shifts it half its width toward the food seam, and tries it there.
