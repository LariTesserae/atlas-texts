From below, the weather mark looks like a white dash painted on the black face of the summit. It is actually a porcelain hood, about as long as a hand, fitted over a chip in the rock. The town lies under its lower lip. To see much of it you would have to put your cheek against the mountain and look sideways.

The floor of the chip slopes inward. This matters more to the town than the drop of two thousand metres immediately outside. A bead of water catching on the lip will travel back under cover, picking up black grit as it goes, and arrive at the lowest point, which happens to be the old exchange. The exchange is still in use. Its ground floor has been given over to water, and its fine south entrance, with three steps and a pair of square pillars, is now the mouth of a drain. If you look in on a bright day you can see the grit lying in little fans on the blue tile.

There are about forty buildings. They take up very little of the available ledge. They were laid out well apart so that any one of them could be lifted clear of its foundations without disturbing its neighbours. Between them are stretches of raw fracture surface, silver-grey where the daylight reaches in, almost violet farther back. The chip came out only eighty winters ago. There are still splinters standing on the floor that fit their negative shapes exactly in the ceiling. In one street the two halves of a broken garnet face each other across the tram wire.

The town tends the summit instruments. Its inhabitants are made, not born here; they come up inside the insulated core of the wire, and take their bodies out of numbered drawers at the terminus. On most shifts only six or seven drawers are empty. There is nevertheless a hospital, a calibration hall, a bathhouse, a freight court and a long row of dwellings, each with its own door opening on the bare rock. Winter relief used to come all at once. Somebody who remembered those changeover days had the good sense to keep the bathhouse full size. Even with a whole shift in the water there is room at one end to swim.

Out at the lip it is cold enough for ice to persist well into the afternoon. Farther in, waste heat from the wire keeps the floor just above freezing. The summit spends much of the day passing in and out of cloud. Fog comes level with the windows, collects on the cold porcelain, and rains under the hood under a perfectly clear sky ten minutes later. The light is ordinary mountain light, strong enough to cast the shadow of each falling drop on the rock behind it. Nothing green grows toward it. The green at the corners of the freight court is copper showing through worn enamel.

Much of the town's work consists in getting through that brief indoor shower. The tram waits beneath an awning. Roofs ease sideways on their bearings until their edges overlap above the footpaths. At the back of the exchange a worker watches the blue tiles disappear, one row after another, and puts a hand against a plate in the wall. For a few seconds the low end of the ledge and the high end trade places as far as loose water is concerned. Water runs briskly uphill to the lip. Nothing else moves. The grit stays on the tiles for someone to sweep up later. From the valley, on a clear afternoon, you might just catch the wink of the discharged drop.

There is almost no sound in the streets. The tram takes its current without touching the wire; the roofs move too slowly to scrape. What carries is the small domestic noise of a door falling shut, and occasionally a hard tick from deep in the mountain, at which conversation stops until the next reading comes in. Once or twice a summer a grey wingless midge walks in out of the glaring light and stands drinking at the drain. It is large enough to interrupt traffic. People coming off shift take the upper path rather than disturb it.

After the shower the roofs part again. The rock dries in patches, with dampness lingering in the fine lettering on the public steps. Over at the bathhouse somebody has propped a door open to let the steam out. For a little while, if the cloud stays down in the valley, a swimmer floating at the quiet end of the bath can look straight out along the surface of the water and see the tops of the other mountains.
