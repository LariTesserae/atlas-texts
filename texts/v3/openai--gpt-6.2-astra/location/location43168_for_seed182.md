From the street there seems to be nothing in the block but a low, crooked hedge. Look over it and there are the backs of clocks, six flights of washing, the green awnings of a provision shop, and, at a depth at which one expects to see water, a woman sitting quite still having her hair cut.

The block went down in one piece. It went down forty years ago in a bed of hot blue clay, and took, according to the municipal record, eleven afternoons to get where it is. The street on three sides stayed up. On the fourth it bent, and this is now the delivery ramp, steep enough that the grocer takes his eggs off the cart at the top. There is scarcely any rubble. The old paving still fits together down there. What is new is the wall of earth all around, a clean, high cut with white shells sticking half out of it. A brass gas main emerges from this face, crosses eight feet of air, and enters a kitchen at the proper height. Someone has hung an apron on it.

It is much hotter below the hedge. The heat comes up through the soles; one can tell the hour at which the bun shop took its trays out but not the hour at which the sun went down. In the lowest lane people wear wooden clogs with a good inch of clearance under the foot. Almost every door stands open. There is a great deal to see through those doors in the weak green daylight: satin on a dressmaker’s dummy, brown bottles in their pigeonholes, an enormous flywheel going about as fast as a man will turn a page. A shaft runs from it under the lane. It drives the hoists, the dough kneader, the barber’s fan. Wherever it crosses a threshold there is a little hump of fitted tin, polished bright in the middle by feet. The machinery has a low note which one mostly notices on Wednesday, when they stop it to oil the bearings and the place seems suddenly much deeper.

There is no well. Cut the clay and it comes away hot and dry on the blade. The water is overhead. Iron beams laid across the cut to brace the standing streets are cool for an hour or two before dawn; moisture gathers underneath them, travels as far as a rivet, and falls. The same spots have received this scant rain for forty years. Beneath one rivet there is a fig tree with a trunk the breadth of a waist, its roots accommodated in three adjoining cellars by agreement with the owners. Beneath another there is a green column of tiny round leaves growing down a bell rope. It has grown so thick that the bell can no longer be rung. Between these wet places a newspaper can lie for weeks on a sill without losing its crispness. People shift their window boxes an inch this way and that, studying the pale marks left by last night’s drops in the dust.

Most of the foliage is high up. From below it resembles a second, less reliable pavement. Squash vines have found their way from balcony to balcony; runners pass through the handles of jugs nobody can now take indoors until the fruit comes off. There are very few insects. Once in a while a grey moth as big as a folded hand gets in under the leaves and rests on the lit dial of the barber’s clock. For several evenings people have to ask him the time.

The curious thing about the block is that the middle is still at street level for certain purposes. A glass of water carried down the ramp remains level until it passes the old tobacconist’s red doorstep; beyond that it leans a little toward the wall the block broke away from. The hoist ropes hang at the same slant. It is very slight. A stranger notices it first in the haircut: the barber sets his chair on two shims, but loose hair drifts sideways and collects in a fine dark seam all along the left-hand skirting board. Nobody agrees whether the slant is increasing. The surveyor has put a white tile on the opposite wall and drawn a pencil line on it, against which the point of a suspended needle may be read. He comes down each spring, wipes the tile, and goes away with his book.

Late in the day the strip of sky above the hedge turns the colour of the clay with its fresh face exposed. Then for a few minutes the whole depth of the cut is legible. You can see the old nail holes where families first put up ladders, and the higher, more hopeful holes above those. You can see where the fig has pressed one shell completely back into the earth. Down in the lane a boy puts a chair on the barber’s step to save the place for his father. There is room to pass it, but only just. Everyone coming home turns a shoulder at the same spot, without looking, while overhead the first bead of the evening slowly fattens on the iron.
