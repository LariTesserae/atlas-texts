The trees up here have no trunks. Each is a sheaf of black ribbons growing from a socket in the rock. In slack water they stand a little taller than a house, the ribbons just far enough apart for a person to pass between them. When the water begins to run west, whole groves lie down. A ridge which a moment ago looked wooded shows its bare crest for half a mile, with every leaf streaming off the lee side like hair being rinsed.

There are three such ridges in the upper lake. All are under water, as are the passes between them and the old road that comes up in switchbacks from the drowned country below. The lake is held in the lap of a mountain range. Its roof is ice, dark with successive winters of dust; its outlet is somewhere inside the mountain. For most of the year the only daylight comes through one long fault in the roof. Seen from the road, it is about the width and the color of a knife cut in green wood.

The water is cold enough to preserve the tool marks in a wooden peg. One still stands at the outside of a bend, driven into a hole laboriously pecked in the granite. There must once have been a rail. Farther up is a shelter built for travelers to sit in out of the wind: three walls, a slab roof, a shelf the right height for a tired pair of legs. There are six miles of open country between it and the next shelter. Both now face into the current. Fine sand goes in at the door, settles on the shelf, and spills off the front in a little continuous fall. The grit has cut a shallow trough in the floor exactly where a seated traveler would have set down a load.

Nothing swims here. A fleck crossing the open water can be followed for a long way in the light from the fault; it never swerves or pauses to investigate another fleck. It is a shred of leaf, or a scale of mica, or one of the small white seeds the ribbon groves shed in such quantities that for a week the lake seems full of distant snow. The seeds have three soft feet. Most settle on silt and are buried. Those that catch on the raw edge of a stone dissolve a socket for themselves, a grain at a time. When a grove finally dies back, the hillside beneath it is left full of thumbholes, very regular and quite deep. Some slopes that look bare have carried forest after forest.

There is less growth down in the passes, but it is richer. Warmth seeps up there along seams in the slate, scarcely enough to warm a hand, enough to keep the mineral salts in motion. Around each seam grows a bed of folded leaves, dark red at the edges and cream deep in the folds. They feed on what comes up through the stone. The beds are separated by acres of plain gray gravel. Now and then the warm seep shifts a few yards and a bed begins its slow move after it: new folds opening at one end, the abandoned end coming apart in damp red sheets. One has been moving across the road for so long that its dead leaves have filled a switchback level from end to end. Above it the road seems to stop. Below, it begins again in perfectly good condition.

For so empty a place the lake makes an immense noise. Snow accumulating on the roof presses water into the mountain. Somewhere in those buried passages a pocket fills, tips over its lip, and drains, pulling a broad current across the ridges. First comes a low knocking, distinct blows a few seconds apart. Then comes the rush. Loose pebbles go hopping up the road. The shelter roofs hum on their walls. For several minutes the fault overhead shines brighter as its edges grind and part, and in that brief light the whole upper basin is visible at once: the three combed ridges, the gravel wastes between, the red gardens far down, the little square of a doorway with sand pouring out of it against the prevailing flow.

Then the pocket empties. The stones drop where they are; the fault closes. Long after the sound has gone out of the water it persists in the granite, and the peg on the bend shivers in its hole. In the dark the ribbons rise separately, a few caught under stones until the next running of the lake, the rest lifting by slow degrees until the high woods stand again.
