The part of the mountain that breaks the water is a white lip, about the width of a footpath. Stand on it facing out and there are three ranges below you, blue with distance, and a little weather going on in the lowest of the valleys. Face in and there is warm water as far as the next headland. A few brown fingers of reed show above it. You can walk half a morning along that lip without suspecting there is a town on the inside slope.

The mountain is still making its top. Water comes up warm through the old gray rock, bringing with it the white stone of which the lip is made. Wherever it spills over, it leaves a little stone behind. The lip rises; the water follows. On the outside face there are whole aprons of wet, pleated white, smooth as the inside of a cheek. Nothing has had time to cut a gully in them. On the inside, two arm lengths down, one can press a thumb into what will one day be rock and leave a good clear whorl.

It is at this depth that the streets begin. They run level around the slope in long easy curves, with steep lanes of knotted cord between them. At first sight the buildings look very much alike: low clay bells, each with a dark mouth underneath. Swim under one and put your head up through the mouth and you are in a room. There is warm air, a dry shelf wide enough to sleep on, perhaps a woman sitting cross-legged with a bone awl, putting a new sole on a climbing slipper. Through the floor, which is simply the level surface of the water, she can see who is passing in the street. A child coming home gives the underside of that surface a slap before rising through it. It is considered rude to come straight up.

Air is the principal public work. Little bubbles issue all day from certain seams in the slope. The earliest streets followed those seams; the first roofs were set over them like cups. Now there are wide collection hoods of waxed cloth, and runs of hollow cane tucked under the street rails. At the far end of town the air comes through a cane with so little force that its arrival in a kitchen is a single silver bead, swelling for a long while at the lip before it breaks free. The air keepers go their rounds feeling the joins with the backs of their hands. In the quiet you can hear a loose join from several houses off: the small persistent sound of somebody sucking a seed from a tooth.

Between the streets the slope is deep in plants. There are water meadows of a curling green strap, tough enough to plait while still alive. A family will braid a handhold all the way from its door to the bean beds, where the straps grow over frames and put out rows of soft red nodules. Farther down, broad leaves overlap like the shingles of an enormous roof. What little daylight gets through is the color of bottle glass. The water there is so clear and so nearly still that last year's cuttings hang in the forks of the plants, with pale threads of new root reaching out on all sides. Once in a while a long, almost transparent grazer passes beneath the leaves. People stop work to watch the bare track of its back travel through the green. A single one will crop the lower meadow for a season. Then for years there may be none.

Out beyond the meadows the bottom drops away. From the last rail one can see the other neighborhoods along the curve of the mountain, not their houses exactly but the lighter bands where feet and hands have kept the streets clean. One band ends at a dark comb of stakes. That is the new quarter. The stakes are willow, cut in the valleys below and carried up on shoulders; their tips are already fat with white deposit. Reed workers weave loose walls between them and leave gaps for windows. The rest is done by the mountain. A young couple will go out after work to feel how their house is coming on. For a year or two it is a basket one could put a fist through. Then one evening there is a sill.

No street stays at quite the right depth forever. In the oldest quarter, too far down now for a comfortable trip to the surface, the cane pipes have been pulled out and the door cords taken home. The roofs are still sound. They hold little pockets of yesterday's world: an air space no bigger than an apple, a dry patch on a ceiling where the soot of a wick lamp has never washed away. Above them, at the present waterline, the reed tips stir in a wind that does not reach the town. It brings the smell of cold rain from far below. The rain itself will never get up here.
