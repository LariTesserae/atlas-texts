The roof of the old sea intake is easy to miss at high tide. A line of pale bubbles stands over one corner of it, and a gull that knows the place will wait there, riding up and down. At low tide the sea slips off six long concrete slabs. There are brass lifting eyes in them, worn open on one side like the ears of very old cups. Under the slabs, at either tide, there is a room full of water.

It is about the size of a village hall. At the seaward end a grille admits the sea; at the landward end two black mouths take it away. One mouth usually has a current and the other hasn't. Between them and the grille there is very little: three square piers, a low sill across the floor, the remains of a ladder. A fish out in the middle of the room has a long way to go to get behind anything. Most of the time the middle looks vacant.

The floor was laid in brown engineering brick. You can still find the maker's stamp on a brick under the bottom rung, where nothing much has scoured it. Out in the runs between the piers the brick has worn down to the colour and smoothness of a wet thumbnail. Even the sand there is mostly the building: grains of brick, green bottle glass, the white aggregate washed out of the roof. It has taken a remarkably long time to make the shallow dip behind the sill. With every inrush a little of this grit goes round in it, and with every slackening the grit settles back almost exactly where it was. A flatfish lies there that is mottled red and cream. Its two eyes are about the only things in the dip that don't roll with the wash.

It is cold enough inside that the seasons show first in the visitors. In spring, transparent young fish come through the bars and keep in a sheet against the ceiling. Later there are prawns under the ladder, so many that its shadow appears to have whiskers. A few straps of brown weed grow at the grille and nowhere else. What looks like deep-piled yellow moss on the seaward faces of the piers is animals, each with a little crown spread to the food coming in. The backs of the same piers are almost bare. A crab can sit there with one claw extended round the corner into a quite different climate.

The pumps are in a building ashore. Their racket down here is prodigious. It comes along the iron pipes and out through the walls, so that putting an ear near a brick would bring you no nearer its source. Over the steady running note there is a separate, irregular *tack* from a loose bar in the grille. On rough days it hardly stops. Light comes in through those bars in green strips and reaches to the first pier; farther back there is just enough to pick out the white lips of open mussels. When a shoal turns there, a patch the size of a tablecloth shines once and goes out.

Once a day the duty pump changes. For a few seconds the note runs down and the whole weight of the sea seems to rest quietly in the room. You can hear the grit pattering onto the floor. Then the idle mouth begins to draw. The yellow crowns bend the other way, prawns lift clear of the ladder, and fish come pouring out from behind what was, a moment ago, the sheltered pier. A few go back through the grille. That is the moment the gull has been waiting for.
