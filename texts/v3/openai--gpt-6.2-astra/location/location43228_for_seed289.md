The hollow begins just under the sill of a gate that is no longer there. A little daylight shows through one of the hinge holes. If you were to put your eye to it, you would see, quite close, the pale underside of a snail’s chin, and beyond the chin a country of dazzling white terraces. The whole of it would fit in the space left by a missing brick.

The brightness comes through the roof. The sill is a single slab of milky spar. It lets the sky through without letting through the shapes of anything that stands against the sky. Down here the light has a body to it, like the white of a peeled apple. Beneath the projecting lip of each terrace there is a fine blue shadow. Nowhere else is it dark.

The terraces are still in the first crude stages of becoming terraces. On one side of the hollow there is scarcely more than a row of wet beads stuck to a wall. A little farther round, each bead has a chalky skirt. Farther round again, the skirts meet, and a shrew could stand on the resulting ledge, though it would have to set its feet with care. The stone has never been worn. Every edge carries its original fringe of needles. There are no broken needles lying about below. At the bottom is a patch of grey clay, about as big as a thumbnail, on which the first drop from the roof has raised a perfect crown.

It is cool enough that the moisture stays mostly where it is, in pearls on the roof and in the tiny cups made by the meeting of two skirts. Between cups the stone is dry and rasps like the back of a saucer. The only green is in the hinge hole. Six little tongues of moss reach inward there, and their reflections in the spar give the impression of a much larger planting, set out in rows somewhere overhead. A single root from the field above lies in a loose white loop on the clay. Inside its loop are the eggs of something very small, clear as grains of boiled rice, each with two black eyes.

For all its bare stone the place is crowded. Woodlice fit under the lips in graduated sizes, big ones at the deep end, little ones where the shelf dwindles to a seam. The damp cups have their own pale, many-legged swimmers. A mite the colour of sealing wax occupies the summit of almost every dry bead. What looks like a piece of curled bark in the middle is the ear of a sleeping mouse; the rest of the mouse fills a bay so exactly that only the ear and three whiskers show. There is even an old bit of building down here, besides the sill: a crumb of red mortar in which a mason’s thumb left two ridges. Between them, in that serviceable little trough, lies a copper-coloured centipede with all its feet touching both banks.

It ought to smell close. Instead it has the keen smell of a stone split open in cold weather. The noise is astonishing. The cup on the left rings high and thin; the larger one under the mouse makes a low gulping note. There is the hard patter of feet on spar, the tear of a snail’s mouth against moss, the almost metallic rattling of the swimmers. From the patch of clay comes a tremendous, full-bodied *plock*. The sill keeps the sounds in. With your ear at the hinge hole you could take the place for a stairwell full of people coming in out of the rain.

Yet you could keep your eye there all afternoon and never see a foot come down. There is no next arrangement of this place. The clay has the drop in it up to the waist; the snail has one green toothful just lifting free. The mouse is at the easy full stretch of a breath. Sound belongs here the way colour does elsewhere: the lifted crown of clay is loud, the mite is red, the shadow under the ledge is blue. Move your ear a hair’s breadth along the sill and the gulp gives way to the ringing, which is how you can tell the two cups apart.

Out in the field the gateposts have rotted to round stains. Rain flattens the grass and it stands up again. All that has come of the gate, down here, is the neat round view that its hinge once bored through the stone. At the far edge of the view there is a white bead with no skirt at all. You can see straight through it to the ridges on the mason’s thumbprint, enormously enlarged, two red hills with the bright feet of the centipede set all along their crests.
