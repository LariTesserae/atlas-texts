On a clear morning you can see the old river forks, although there is very little water in them now. They make a pattern like the creases of a palm across forty miles of sunlit turf. The river ran here when this country was low. It has been lifted so far since then that the weather moving in from the east generally strikes the cliffs halfway up. From the rim you look down on rain falling onto rain. Up here a shower is an event; people remember which fork it fell in.

There is scarcely anything on the skyline. A shed with one end fallen in; beyond it, much farther away than it looks, a line of five posts. Yet the ground is so vegetable that a child here may be quite old before she handles a stone. A low, jointed sedge grows out sideways, rooting at the joints, its wiry runners plaited through those of a hundred former generations. Its dead parts are slow to decay. In the old channels they make a brown felt several fathoms deep, with a tender green nap that lies all one way in the wind. In bright weather you can walk for hours over it in your bare feet. There are patches that feel like the back of a sun-warmed dog and patches that give a little and leak cold water between your toes.

Almost all the animals are in there.

Put your hand down flat and keep it still. Presently something will go ticking under the heel of it, stop, turn, and tick out under your thumb. Those are the little amber crabs that spend their whole lives in the wet felt and hang their empty shells on the roots. There are white mice with green-stained chins; there are long grubs as thick as the mice, which bend double and flick themselves down the root holes when a mouse comes by. Above them, just under the youngest leaves, are the singers. They have thin blue backs, two hard pegs on their bellies, and wings almost wholly given over to making sound. A silver coin would cover three of them. In a piece of turf the size of a door there may be ten thousand.

They begin on the warm slopes a little after sunrise. For perhaps a minute you can distinguish the first few strokes, a sound like a fingernail drawn along a comb. Then the whole slope comes on, and the one behind it, until speech is something you feel yourself doing in your throat without hearing a word of it. The tremendous noise belongs to no visible object. Nothing sways or opens or rises to account for it. A woman on her way out to mend the posts, seen from a mile away, appears to be walking quite alone through absolute peace.

The posts mark a crossing. Below them the old river took a long time to wear its way round a knob of harder rock, leaving a deep elbow where every shower still drains. The sedge has grown straight across. Most of this bridge is good going. One end, where the rock sweats in the night, is rotten underneath. To find the safe line the woman takes a stripped wand of willow and pushes it ahead of her, feeling for the little succession of catches as the tip goes through one sound layer after another. Where it goes in easily to the wrist she draws it out and moves the nearest post. The posts have forked tops. A cord laid in the forks lets people cross after mist comes up from below. There is no use calling directions.

At noon the light is plain and strong, and everything casts its proper short shadow. This is when the birds arrive. At first there is apparently a low ridge on the western horizon that was not there in the morning. It detaches, breaks into long strands, and comes skimming over the turf: dun birds with broad mouths and short, rather inadequate legs. The first rank lands and runs. The rest overtake it on the wing, land a little farther on, and run in their turn. Under this rolling weight the blue singers boil up out of the sedge. For an hour a whole river fork glitters. There are birds enough to darken it from bank to bank, yet from the shed you can look north or south of them into leagues of unoccupied air, pale blue right down to the grass. By midafternoon they have gone over the eastern edge, dropping into the enormous height below as casually as ducks going down a weir.

The quiet they leave is patchy. In the strip they fed over, you can hear mice chewing the dropped wings. On either side the singing is still so loud that to step across the strip is like going through a wall. People put their sheds along the birds’ usual courses for the sake of that hour. They sit on the sills to do small jobs that need an ear: testing a fired cup for a crack, shaving a reed pipe, listening to a little boy say a new word he has been practicing all day.

Toward evening it cools just enough for drops to collect in the joints of the sedge. The crabs climb up to drink and sometimes, in a good slant of light, the entire plain seems to have acquired a fine amber grain. Far under it lies the old stone surface, rounded and finished, with its potholes and gravel bars and the last scratches made by gravel turning in a current. At one place near the rim a square of turf has slipped away. You can lie down there and reach into a pothole. The stone is smooth, the water barely a finger deep and warm from the day. In the bottom is a single round pebble, much too big now for anything in that little cup of water to move.
