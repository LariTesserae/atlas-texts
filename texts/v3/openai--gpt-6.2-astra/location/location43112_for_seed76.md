The shade under the trees is red. You can see a grove a long way off by that stain on the white ground, long before you can make out what casts it. Between one grove and the next lie sixty or seventy miles of bare, pale stone. There is generally one in sight and sometimes two, one ahead in a shallow saddle and one behind, almost lost in the brightness. Where the land bulges gently upward there may be nothing in sight at all.

This is the high surface of a continent. What look from above like a few scratches across it are openings miles deep, with cold blue air in them and, far down, shelves of the same white stone. The plateau ends at these openings without much drama. Its edges have been worn round; the ground begins to slope away like the shoulder of a sleeping animal, and keeps on sloping, steeper and steeper, until it is quite vertical. A little grit goes over the edge most afternoons. Sometimes it takes until evening to reach a shelf.

There is almost no water here. Break a slab in two and even the newly exposed face is dry. The stone is mostly salt and feldspar, with seams of a third, buttery-looking mineral that softens in strong light. The roots of the trees follow those seams. A root brought out into daylight by the wind looks less like wood than like a length of peeled orange rind, white and pithy on one side, intricately pored on the other. It presses its pored side to the seam. What circulates in the trees is a clear oil, thin even in the chill before dawn. A broken twig will hold a bead of it at its tip for days. Dust sticks to the bead until it looks like a tiny fruit.

At noon in a grove there is a sense of having come in out of the weather, though all that has happened is that the light has changed color. The trees grow low and wide, their trunks dividing close to the ground to carry tier upon tier of translucent, wine-colored leaves. Deep in the crown the leaves are almost black. Out at the tips they are the clear pink of an eyelid held shut against the sun. Smaller plants live in the forks: upright cones of curled green scales, grey cushions soft enough to take an impression, and a fine hanging growth that appears to be all flower, hundreds of minute yellow mouths on a single branching thread. In an old grove the crowns overlap for miles. There is hardly a patch of bare stone underneath, hardly a leaf without some smaller leaf enjoying its shade.

In the morning the leaves hang edge-on to the light. By degrees they turn broadside, the eastern trees first, so that a band of deepening red travels west through the grove. The shaded ground stays cold. Warm air rising off the white flats draws the cold air out from under the trees, and by midday there is a steady outward breath at ankle height, carrying fallen scales and papery husks. These go skimming for astonishing distances. Out on the bare plateau they collect in rings around shallow hollows. Most are worn to powder there. A few come to rest against an exposed yellow seam. The first sign of a new grove is a line of pink leaves no bigger than fingernails, all facing the same way across a hundred yards of stone.

The flats have their weather too. One can stand in clear, still air and watch a low bank of glitter moving along the horizon. This is salt lifted from the lips of the chasms, where the air coming up from below spreads suddenly and scours the ground. It travels in sheets too thin to obscure anything. Through a passing sheet every edge in the landscape acquires a second, trembling edge. There is a slight taste of minerals, a faint tick against the larger leaves. Then the bank has gone on. Over immense stretches of time this gentle weather has cut yard-long flutes in the rock, all of them smooth enough inside to shine. In the slant light of evening each flute holds a stroke of gold. Whole districts that looked featureless at noon turn out to be covered in fine handwriting.

It is possible to cross several groves without seeing an animal. In some of the oldest trees there lives a small pale browser with six feet and a broad upper lip. It feeds on the hanging yellow flowers, drawing a thread through its mouth without breaking it, and sleeps pressed flat in the fork of a branch. Its feet leave little clean ovals in the dust. These are much more often seen than the animal. On the open ground there is a different creature, light enough to travel on the outward breath of the groves. It folds its limbs to its belly and lets a loose rim of skin carry it over the salt, touching down now and then with one heel to change direction. A crossing takes days. Seen from the rim of a chasm it is a moving speck with a much sharper, darker speck beside it: its shadow keeping pace across the bright floor.

Towards evening the light withdraws first from the openings in the land. For a while the plateau seems to float in pieces on an enormous depth of blue. Then the leaves turn edge-on again. The ground beneath them loses its red, the air under the branches comes to rest, and the last travelling husks settle, often balanced upright in the flutes. It gets cold enough for the exposed mineral seams to harden. Here and there one contracts with a small, distinct click. There is so little other sound that it is easy to hear the next seam do the same, farther off, and the next after that, as the shadow of the world goes slowly over the high ground.
