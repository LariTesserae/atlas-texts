From the road the hollow is almost invisible. You can see clear across it to a stand of larches on the far rim, and on a grey afternoon the larches seem quite close. Then a maintenance man comes up the stair at the rim. For a surprisingly long time you see nothing of him but his orange cap. There are forty steps down to the floor of the hollow, and the larches are four miles away.

It is a worked-out peat field. The cutting stopped thirty years ago. The last crew left a flight of broad terraces at the west end and, for no evident reason, a square island of uncut peat big enough for a house. The island still wears the heather of the upper moor. Down on the floor nearly everything is the height of a mitten. Moss covers the cutter tracks without quite filling them in. At this low angle of light each track casts a finger’s breadth of shadow; you can follow the turns the driver made on a morning before half the men who service the field were born.

There is very little to interrupt the view and nowhere to put a foot that is not on a plant. Where the moss gives way there are close mats of bearberry, with hard leaves turned burgundy by the cold. Little terraces which from a distance look like exposures of sandstone prove to be the buff sheaths of last year’s sedge, laid one on another and frozen stiff. Even where a boot breaks through the surface, what shows in the heel mark is a mash of older stems. Here and there a birch has got a start. The tallest stands by itself on the square island, well below the skyline, its few twigs all brushed over to one side.

The noise comes from three compressor houses set into the north bank. A gas line crosses the moor here, underground except for a few lengths of elbow pipe in fenced yards. The houses are low, windowless and the same brown as the cut. From above you hardly hear them. Below the rim it is difficult to believe they are the only things making a sound. There is a coarse tearing note from the air intakes and under it a sustained pounding that seems to come up through both legs. The stair rail buzzes in the hand. Two men standing on either side of a yard gate wait until they have caught each other’s eye before attempting to say anything; what they have to say generally takes one gesture. Out toward the middle the separate beats run together. You can watch a loose strip of roofing slap against a shed a hundred yards away and never hear the slap.

In winter the sun clears the southern lip for less than an hour. It picks out the tops of the moss cushions, thousands of little dull gold patches in the blue of the floor. There is seldom snow enough to bury them. Most of the water is locked up in the peat, though in the lee of each engine house a drain discharges a little warmth and wet. For a few feet there is black water with green weed waving in it. Then the weed is caught leaning downstream inside clear ice. A hare sometimes comes down to one of these places. Its prints follow the old cutter track with great fidelity, even around a turn that takes it out of its way: there is shelter to the depth of its ears between the ridges.

The edge of the workings is still sharp enough to measure things by. Each spring a thin slice falls from the face of the square island. It comes off with the heather attached and stands for a season like a partly open door, then settles, and the moss grows across the join. On the oldest slices there are already berries. On the newest the pale fine roots still show. Nothing much else moves. All afternoon the exhaust blows flat across the north terraces, and the pressure in the line is held steady, and down in the enormous noise of the hollow a bead of water gathers at the end of a sedge blade in the one small place where it can thaw.
