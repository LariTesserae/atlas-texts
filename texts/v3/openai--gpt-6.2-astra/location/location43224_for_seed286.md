At the lip of the high sea, a fish can look down through the water and see clouds two miles below. The lip is clear green, about as thick as a glacier snout, and rounded under like a wave about to break. It never breaks. Fine roots run out into the curve, so fine that their shadows are more substantial than they are. Water drawn into them at the very point of falling travels back inland. There is a cold sucked-dry strip all along the underside where a little white moth lives, with the sea for a roof and nothing at all for a floor.

Inland the lip gives way to shallows. What looks from above like a bottom of long pale gravel is a bed of seeds, each worn smooth in its socket. Farther in, the sockets grow deeper and coalesce into grooves broad enough to take a river. Water runs in both directions in these grooves, outward along the bottom and inward along the sides, parting the soft nap there so that one bank shines silver and the other bottle green. The sea occupies most of the top of a mountain range. In its middle the water is deep enough to take all the red out of daylight. The high passes show as bars of yellow weed far out in the blue.

There is no finding the place where the weed attaches to the mountain. Pull the small yellow blades apart and there is the green hide they grow from; under the hide, knotwood, and under that, older knotwood, dark and dense, with little blind shrimp in the wet seams. The long strain of holding the water up has given the grain a magnificent sweep. On a rubbed-bare spur it is possible to trace one black line with the eye from the foreground clear to the next headland. The bights and narrows have the ease of forms arrived at after many poor attempts. Nothing cuts a fresh bank here. Even the shrimp holes have smooth, flared mouths.

For so much life there is an astonishing amount of room. A swimmer passing between two stands of bladder trees might lose sight of the first an hour before the second came clear. In the interval there would be water, good cool light, perhaps a single shed scale turning on its way down. The herds keep well apart. Long-nosed grazers work the silver sides of the channels, cropping with their upper lips, bracing their chins against the pull. A flat green ray follows each herd for the little snails their lips dislodge. Seen end on, the ray disappears, and for some distance the snails seem to be vanishing into a crease in the water.

Sound reaches across the gaps. There is a tremendous working noise from below, a knock with a long rolling tail to it, overlaid by the continuous hiss of water being forced through hair. The great sacs buried in the knotwood are seldom all shut at once. One presses flat under the weight of the sea; the pale muscular rim of the next opens to receive its load. Miles of them work abreast. They separate salt, press oil from the seed beds, force air down to the deepest grain. At the exhaust pores the grazers stand with their mouths open, letting the current clean between their teeth. There are places where the beat comes up through the jaw so hard that an animal lifts its head to chew. All this power is expended to keep the water at precisely the height at which it already stands.

Above the middle of the sea hangs a broad low cloud, fed from underneath, raining out at its eastern end. Its shadow has lain on the same reach of water long enough for a dark-leaved meadow to have fitted itself exactly inside it. At the edge of the shadow one half of a leaf can be black and the other half a clear, ordinary green. There is movement everywhere in the meadow: stalks bow, fry start out of their cover, beads of air go skidding up the undersides of leaves. Yet the shape of the rain is as settled a feature as the shape of a pass. From far off you could steer by it.

The brightest place is still the outer lip. Daylight enters there from the side and below as well as from above. Out in that cold brilliance, the fish that have come to feed on the roots cast three faint shadows apiece. The moth on the underside can feel their mouths tugging through the thickness of its footing. It holds on with six small gray hooks, its wings pressed flat in the draught of the pumps, facing out over the sunlit backs of the lower clouds.
