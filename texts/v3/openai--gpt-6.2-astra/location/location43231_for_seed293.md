At the lower end of Dye Street a wooden spoon is going round and round in a puddle. It goes bowl first, quite fast. A boy sits on a doorstep waiting for the handle to come within reach. Each time it does he lets it go by. The sun is out; his shirt is spread on the step beside him, drying on the warm green nap that covers the step. From here you can see most of the town. It occupies a shallow oval in the plain, about as much ground as a traveling fair would need. There are six long streets, a bathhouse, a freight court, and, at considerable intervals, houses with open fronts. Everything is one plant.

You notice this last fact less in the houses than in the puddle. Its bottom is a tight pink skin through which a pulse passes every few seconds. The pulse tips the spoon. All over town water stands in hollows of this skin, sometimes to the depth of a fingernail, sometimes deep enough for the bathhouse divers to enter feet first and go out of sight. Between hollows the ground is dry and faintly rough, excellent for walking barefoot. There is very little shade. The plant puts its great effort into growing sideways, and a person standing up in the freight court casts something like a tower shadow across all that low, close velvet. Beyond the court the velvet goes on for miles, getting yellower, until it ends at a line of salt.

There used to be relief here. A shelf of stone comes up through the plant by the bathhouse, the last exposed bit of a range that once divided the waters of half a continent. It's about knee high. Children slide down it on their bottoms, a pleasure which takes a couple of seconds and leaves two wet stripes on the seat of the trousers. Even under the rock the plant has got a purchase. Fine white root hairs come through its grain and stand beaded on its upper face. At noon, when the air is warm enough to bring out the smell of the town, the stone smells of cut pear.

The houses are cultivated to a pattern: a rib at either end to carry the roof, a deep recess for sleeping, a runnel at the back for washing things away. Most of the intervening space is left alone. A heron can open its wings and fly the length of a street without rising more than a yard. In one bedroom a woman is sewing; two houses down, a man saws the lip off a window that has begun to curl inward. The cut seals over behind the blade, smooth and dark as the end of a cucumber. It will keep that shape now. The saw has taught it where its edge is.

Much municipal work is done this way. What the town learns at one point in its body it can do at another, though the two points need not be quite contemporary. In the freight court there is a patch the size and color of a kitchen table where the skin can be made to take a crease. The freight clerk lays his hand on it and feels, among other things, the weight that will come onto the streets in the next little while. If it's heavy he puts a crease across the patch with a horn roller. A supporting rib swells under the appropriate street. This morning three ribs have come up for a load that hasn't arrived, and the carters sit on them eating little sour fruits and trying to guess who has ordered something big. Nobody speculates very far ahead. Beyond about an hour the clerk says everything feels like rain.

The heron has been watching a seam in the ground. Now it jabs at it, misses, and a sheet of water peels back from the whole width of the freight court. Underneath are hundreds of blunt little silver fish, all traveling on their sides in less than an inch of water. They make a noise like a broom being shaken out. Frogs spill from the ends of the supporting ribs. The carters get their feet up; one drops his fruit and it goes bounding along on the backs of the fish. For a minute the place is prodigiously busy. Insects lift off the velvet in separate brown columns, join in midair, split, and go down again. Water comes up the bathhouse steps. The open houses ring with the slapping of shutters as people shut the lower halves of their doors. Above the waterline everything stays sunlit and sociably open. You could still pass a neighbor a cup.

The disturbance moves on into Dye Street. It is a tide, although the sea withdrew from here so long ago that no shell remains intact in the local stone. The plant still keeps the sea's hours. Water that has spent the ebb belowground comes up warm, carrying fish and shed skins and sometimes small domestic articles lost clear across town. A hard flood can rearrange the streets in an afternoon, rubbing out soft partitions, throwing up a new bank of spongy green that takes a season to wear flat. Yet the oval in the plain stays the same oval. Nothing carried to its rim goes much farther. The town has been rinsing itself in this one place for an age.

At the foot of Dye Street the puddles join. The boy puts on his shirt and stands up. His spoon sails away without a turn, down the long bright opening between the houses, past a beetle riding high and dry on a pearly blister of air. Farther up, a woman reaches over her half door and takes the spoon out of the flood. She gives it a considering look, taps it twice against the jamb to let whoever lost it know where it is, and goes back to her sewing.
