Late in the morning, when the cloud lifts off the rim, the shadow of the west viaduct appears on the cloud a second time, half a mile below the viaduct itself. Little white flakes cross that shadow. They are birds coming up. It takes them so long to arrive that from the parapet you can watch a whole flock turn from flakes to strokes to heavy, pink-footed birds with water running out of their feathers. They settle facing the same way along the hot rail. The cloud stays down in the gorge. Up here the light is fierce enough to shine through a pigeon's foot.

The town occupies what looks, from above, like an empty saucer set on a high shelf. There are eight or nine blocks of houses on one side, some broad sheds on the other, and a mile of dimpled white ground between them. But most of the town is one story down. In the white ground are round openings like the finger holes of a great wind instrument. Look through one and there is the tiled floor of the exchange; through another, three clean shirts turning very slowly on a line; through a third, six people in the cool light of a barber's shop, looking up at you. Where a street crosses a warm seam in the rock, its paving ends at a landing and begins again on the far side. Between is a few yards of clear water, with steps down into it. There are places where it is quicker to swim to the post office.

The water comes up hot, in no hurry. It wells through a bed of soft limestone under a cap of much harder gray stuff, and for perhaps a hundred thousand years it has been lifting scales of that cap and letting them settle again, a little out of true. Hence the extraordinary shallowness of things: flights of natural stairs across the plain with treads thirty yards wide and risers no higher than a lip; waterfalls you only recognize by the line of tiny bubbles crossing a road. There are no trees to give these distances away. What looks like a loaf-sized stone in the middle of a pan of water proves on approach to be a bathhouse with a domed roof and a bench all the way around it.

Along the downhill edge of each tread grows a slippery gray felt. It lives on the difference between what is dissolved in the deep water and what is dissolved in the air. This thin margin of the plain supports nearly everything that moves on it. Thumb-sized shellfish shovel the felt into their mouths with a pair of fringed scoops. Little flat fish lie on top of the shellfish and pick the scoops clean. In the deeper runs there are long, ivory-colored waders with a black mark halfway up each leg, so that in a row they look remarkably like a surveyor's equipment. They hunt by standing on one foot and trailing the other in the current. A fish comes to clean the offered toes. The bird folds almost in half to take it. All afternoon these deliberate transactions go on, without a splash you could hear from the bank.

The town makes ceramic bearings, insulators, drain sleeves, and a particular kind of blue tile, curved on one edge, that is shipped down the mountain in crates. Its oldest works are cut straight into the shelf. Water on its way to the gorge falls inside the rock through six successive wheel rooms; each neighborhood has rights to the shaft of one wheel, and can draw on it by slipping a broad woven belt onto a pulley. On the surface there is very little indication that a factory is at work. A belt travels out of a slot in a shed, crosses forty feet of sunshine and goes in through a slot in another shed. A wet patch on the belt comes by again and again, getting smaller. Down at the loading end a crate emerges, pauses while a boy lays a square of felt on it for the next crate to sit on, and proceeds. The works have been there long enough that the lint from the belts has built soft blue-gray nests in the joints of the viaduct. The pink-footed birds line them with their own white down.

Toward evening the gorge fills up with cloud again. The first sign is that the birds' feet disappear from the rail. Then a cool draft comes across at ankle height, delicious after the long heat, and every door in town is opened to admit it. Condensation collects on the bare ceilings underground. In the barber's shop the chairs are set, by long experience, between the places where drops will fall. The white plain goes pearly and loses its edges. For an hour the many separate pans of water seem to join, and the houses and sheds stand apart on what looks like a single pale lake.

This is when the shellfish travel. They let go of the stone in such numbers that a faint nap rises on the water, all running one way. At the drain mouths women kneel with flat baskets, skim off enough for supper, and tip the smallest back. There is a minute sound, like a handful of shirt buttons being sorted on a blanket. From farther off comes the click of the street lamps coming on, one circuit after another under the ground. In a little while the cloud closes over everything above the waist. From the west viaduct you can see nothing at all; from a swimmer's height there are still long views, under the luminous lid of it, of doorsteps, shins, the undersides of bridges, and here and there a black mark on a white leg, holding perfectly still.
