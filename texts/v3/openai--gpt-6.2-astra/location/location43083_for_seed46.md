On the lee side of the summit, just under its lip, there is a hollow about as long as a thumb. It opens sideways onto a hundred miles of air. The peaks below are far enough away to have lost their shadows in the blue; inside the hollow the shadow of a single leaf is sharp enough to show the notch at its tip. For an hour in the afternoon the sun looks straight in. Then the upper lip cuts it off, and the little place goes on by reflected light, the color of a butter bean.

The floor is mostly bare, a pale mineral with a greasy shine. At its rear edge stand four clumps of vegetation. There is room for several times as much growth between any two clumps as there is in the clumps themselves, but where the stuff grows it grows extravagantly. Leaves turn back beneath other leaves and come out through slots in them. The youngest are plum-colored and almost transparent, with grains of yellow meal showing through; the older ones are a deep bottle green. They are full and cool-looking. In fact one of those leaves laid against a wrist would raise a white welt. No rain reaches this height, and there is no moisture even in the pale sweet-smelling pulp at the heart of a shoot. The pulp runs, when it runs at all, like hot wax.

Heat comes up unevenly through the mountain. The roots lie across the seams where a hot band meets a hotter one. Across that small difference they maintain a current. It draws metal out of the vapor in the hollow, grain by grain. On the underside of each leaf is a row of dimples, and in each dimple a foil takes shape, a perfect six-sided flake with a little burr along one edge. A season's production would hardly gild a fingernail. Nevertheless the uniformity of the work, the ranks of dimples on the folded leaves still waiting to open, give the impression of a great deal of plant standing idle between shifts. There is no machinery to find by looking closer. The burr is simply where the leaf lets go.

One hears that release occasionally: a tick, quite dry, followed much later by the softer tick of the flake landing. Between the two there is time for the shadow at the mouth of the hollow to move perceptibly. This has little to do with the weight of the flake. The floor beneath it is not entirely there yet.

From outside, the summit looks old and finished. Inside the lip it is still finding out what shape the last thickness of mountain will have. A flake falling toward the floor may find a slant there on which it will one day slide, or a small depression in which it will come to rest. Until these agree on an edge, the flake hangs at a slight tilt in the air. There are five hanging now. Beneath the lowest, a minute shoulder of pink stone is coming clear of the pale ground. It has no chips around its foot, no sign of having pushed anything aside. Its upper face is in afternoon sun even in the morning. When the rest of the shoulder arrives, its shadow will keep time with the others.

Nothing comes to browse the leaves. Nothing walks across the metal dust. The only track on the floor is a green curve left by the outermost clump, which has been turning very slowly toward a better seam of heat. The old root hairs are still visible in the curve, fine as the pile on a peach. At the far end of it they stand upright in empty air: the plant grew there a little before there was ground. Here at the youngest edge of the mountain, that is generally how a patch of earth begins. Something needs a place to stand, and for a long while stands without one.
