The lowest step of the old weighhouse stair sounds hollow under a heel. The others give a good flat smack. Nobody in the square thinks much of it; the step has never shifted, even under the men who bring in the millstones. On its shaded end, almost at pavement level, there is a notch just big enough to lose a lentil in. Look in there on a bright day and you can see, quite distinctly, a flight of stairs going down.

They go down seven steps to a landing and divide. The left flight serves the oil gardens, the right the town. Both are cut out of the same pale stone as the great stair overhead, except that here a grain of mica makes a whole landing glitter, and a fossil threaded through the rock is an inconvenience that the road builders have dealt with by putting in two rather awkward bends.

Most of the interior is air. The roof is a shallow dome, the floor a shallow saucer, and all the works of the town lie along one lip of the saucer. Across from them the bare floor rises again, softly ridged, like the inside of a worn spoon. There isn't even a track across it. A bright flake fallen from the vault has lain out there through six reckonings of the gardens. Everybody knows the flake; nobody has had business over there.

The gardens grow where a black, buttery mineral works its way through a seam. In the cool hours it is hard enough to take a thumbprint and keep it. By noon it softens. The roots of the garden plants are flat and close-fitting, like the tongues of people trying to get the last grease off a plate. Above these coarse, rather ugly parts the growth is magnificent. There are fans of pleated amber, crimson fiddleheads, long green leaves so finely divided that the light puts a green fur on the wall behind them. A single joint of one of the silver grasses can send up thirty translucent blades. Nowhere is there so much as a bead of water. A cut stalk bleeds yellow wax and seals itself almost before the knife is out.

In a photograph taken very close, the gardens would look like unbroken forest. From the opposite lip they make a green stitch beside a white stitch, the white one being the town. The houses have been hollowed one above the next with punches of harder stone. Doorways wear out well before rooms do. When a threshold has worn too deep to step over comfortably, the owners put in a lintel at knee height and make a new door underneath the old one. Thus the upper streets are mostly windows now. A person coming home late can look up and see, four storeys above her, the notch where her grandmother used to scrape the garden wax off her clogs.

The noise is extraordinary. The square above is paved with loose-edged slabs. A cart rolling across them makes hardly more than a pleasant clatter to the carter, but down here the whole vault takes up each separate blow. Long after the mule is unhitched the sound is going round and round the saucer, running together into a steady bellow. Dust stands up in stripes on the bare floor. In the gardens the fans shut with a snap to save their pleats, then cautiously begin to open; often they are only halfway open when the next wheel goes by. At market the prices are shown on fingers. An angry customer has to put her basket down to do justice to her feelings.

Light comes chiefly through a finger of clear spar that runs from the sunlit tread to the underside of the dome. By a piece of good fortune the spar ends above the bare side. All that empty white stone a little way off gives the town a broad, steady daylight, much better for close work than the dazzle directly under the spar. There are people here who can take a hooked flint and split a grass blade lengthwise into five even ribbons. The ribbons are plaited into hinges. A rigid pin works loose in a week of market traffic overhead, whereas some of the garden gates still swing on the same soft green hinges they had when the lower stairs were new.

Very occasionally something from the square gets in. An ant once came down backwards dragging a crumb, lost its footing at the divided stair, and went tumbling out onto the plain. For the little while it took to right itself, the inhabitants saw the funny pale undersides of its feet. Children still draw an ant that way, on its back with the six soles showing. Mostly the only movement out there is the striped dust shifting its position as a handcart turns a corner far above.

Late in the day the sun leaves the tread. The bare slope goes lilac; the gardens, which have been storing light in their wax all afternoon, stay yellow for a while. As the black seam stiffens, the gardeners go down it with blunt paddles, pressing the youngest roots snug to the grease so they won't curl away from it in the night. This is the hour when someone who has spent all day in the town may hear a sound she has made herself. A clog scuffs. A gate brushes its sill. From a kitchen in the row above comes the little double knock of a knife being laid down on a board, first the handle, then the blade.
