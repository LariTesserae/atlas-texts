The stair goes down twenty steps, turns on a landing, and stops. There used to be a further flight. Its outline is still on the wall in clean gray zigzags, and the bottom three steps are standing by themselves on the clay floor, very useful for sitting on. From the landing you can see the whole extent of the hollow: an oval a little larger than a covered market, with a roof of tilted paving slabs. Along one side the slabs have ridden up over a bed of shale. Turn a light on the shale and it shines like the edges of a new pack of cards. Nothing has had time to make a slope of rubble underneath it.

What you see without a light is a stand of leaves in the middle distance, each with a fine green rim. There are five such stands, widely spaced, and as your eyes adjust they turn out to be hanging. In each place where the paving has opened, the soil of the nursery overhead has sagged through in a great stocking held together by roots. Ferns grow out of the stocking in all directions, even straight down, though their tips curl up again. The narrowest stocking is hardly thicker than a wrist and has a magnificent skirt of foliage. The broadest has come to rest on the floor. Its weight has flattened its lowest fronds into a dark green ruff on the clay. Between one stand and the next there is mostly warm black space. The green rims come from daylight traveling down the cracks; at noon a person standing directly under one of the openings might have enough light to see the lines on their own palm.

A district steam main crosses the hollow at about shoulder height. Where the ground used to support it, its insulating jacket is stained brown; here and there a little wedge of the original earth still adheres to the underside. Even at the far wall there is heat enough from it to make clothing uncomfortable. Above it the fern tips are dry and intricately folded. Below it, in the shelter of the root bundles, are small soft leaves the color of the inside of a cucumber. They get their water by way of the roots and scarcely any other way. Rain up top makes no sound here. A day or so later a stocking will darken, from the neck down, and for a few hours the air around it smells of potting soil. Sometimes enough comes through to fill the three little triangular hollows left by the feet of a machine. Each holds about a cupful. There is a lime mark around each, white on red, as precise as the rim of a saucer.

At the far end is a brick valve house, small enough that the door takes up most of its front. It has settled at an angle. A blue enamel sign on the door still says GRADE LEVEL, which was once as reasonable a thing to say here as anywhere. The machinery inside is in service. Once in a long while there is a low *tock* and a few grains of brick dust fall from the lintel. You can feel the steam moving if you sit on the detached steps with both feet flat on the ground. There are no wings beating up in the greenery, no little disturbances in the cups of water. When a dead leaf works loose it often comes down entire, all its leaflets still in place.

Most of the old nursery is still up there. This is apparent less from looking through the cracks, which show almost nothing, than from the peculiar way spring arrives below. One root bundle puts out a dozen pale crooks; three weeks later another does the same. The heat below is constant, but up above, the shadow of the nursery wall withdraws across the beds a few feet at a time. The last bundle to wake is the one beside the steps. By then the first has unrolled leaves as long as a forearm. For a while there is an almost continuous green ceiling at that end of the hollow, and even the thin noon light no longer reaches the ground. The three white water marks remain there in the dark, each a little wider than it was the year before.
