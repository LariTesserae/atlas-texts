At the top of the water tower there is a landing where the stair turns back for no apparent reason. On the wall above the handrail is a row of white enamel plates: SEED ROOM, PRESS ROOM, UPPER WALK. The stair used to go to all three. Now, if you put your forehead to the little round window in the fire door, you can see straight across one warm green room to a bank of high windows, and through those windows to the weather going by below.

The room is full to the crown of its riveted roof. It takes a moment to see the water. Nothing swims in it; there is no surface to catch the light. What gives it away is a long yellow leaf, broken at the stem, standing on its point above the checker plate. In the course of a morning it will turn over once and come down flat. From the landing you could mistake the whole place for a conservatory into which, some years ago, the gardener had neglected to put a door.

The floor climbs in three steep steps from the fire door to the windows. The steps were poured last winter in a white ceramic that sets under water. Their corners are still sharp enough to split a leaf resting across them. Each tread carries a row of presses, dark green machines with their flywheels up against the roof. Between them grow stands of something like celery, though the stalks are clear and the leaves as large as aprons. You can see a fine green thread running up the inside of each stalk. They grow from cups in the press beds. Every cup is full. Where an old stalk has gone soft and folded, half a dozen small ones have come up inside its elbow.

This was a factory for making shade. Down below the tower, the roofs of the town were once covered in the broad pressed leaves; even dry they kept their green, and a sheet laid over a skylight gave a cool and quite convincing impression of sitting under a tree. There are still a few sheets hanging on the upper walk. The little brass clips have held. Under water the sheets have put out roots all along their cut edges, so that each square of shade now hangs in a pale, knee-length fringe. Where two fringes touch they have knitted together. There isn't much left of the walk except the yellow rail, which goes into one thicket of roots at the height of a person's waist and comes out of another a little farther on, still conscientiously indicating the way.

It is bright up here. At noon the squares of the east windows lie on the white steps with the blue pencil lines of their wired glass enlarged inside them. By two o'clock the light has gone round to the side and gets in under the leaves. Then you can see how densely the place is planted, all the smaller lives that make no impression from above: red bladders no bigger than currants on the bolts, a green nap on the insulated cable, fans of fine brown weed inside the open throat of an oilcan. There is no mud. What looks like a slope of rich black earth at the back is the cast-iron housing of a motor, warm enough to keep a patch of exceedingly tender white grass through the winter. The grass grows nowhere else in the room. Its entire season of bad weather is the ten days in April when the motor is off for inspection.

For most of the year the works still turn. A shaft comes up through the middle of the tower, driven by the pumps far below, and every nineteen days the nearest press closes. The movement is too slow to make a current. A leaf caught between the platens darkens from the middle outward; the leaves on either side scarcely stir. On the dry side of the door there is a tick as the pressure needle passes a nick in its dial. That is about all one hears. No wing strikes the high panes. Nothing noses through the cups in search of the soft new growth. A seed that lodges on a ledge has only the width of that ledge and the direction of the light to reckon with.

Sometimes a low cloud goes across the town and hides everything below the windowsills. For an hour the room seems to be the only thing at this height: the presses in their rows, the yellow handrail, the hundreds of leaves in warm clear water held up in the sky by four legs of bolted steel. Then a gap opens in the cloud, and far down, through a press wheel and two thicknesses of green, you can make out the flat silver roof of the station. One of its skylights is still a slightly different colour from the rest.
