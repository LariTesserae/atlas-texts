The plateau has a weather that goes on under stones. Turn over one of the flat brown flags and the underside is black with damp. There are beads of water on the roots there, each with a white mite standing head down to drink. The upper face of the flag is warm and dusty. A wind has been going over it for the better part of a year without bringing enough rain to lay the dust.

Most of the plateau lies in the dark. It is the broad back of a continent on a world that keeps one face to its sun. Along the western rim one can climb high enough to see an edge of sun, crimson and flattened, tangled in the faraway bad weather of the lowlands. Farther in, even that goes. What light there is comes from stars, from a little chalky shine in the mouths of fissures, and at long intervals from lightning below the horizon, by which one sees a hundred miles of country perfectly clearly and then has to remember it.

In such a flash the country looks like a sea that has drawn breath. There are long swells, blunt at the crest, with scarcely a loose rock to interrupt the slope from one crest down to the next. These are the worn stumps of lava flows. New stone still comes up, but mostly deep below; its heat is spread and tempered by all that weight of old stone above it. In the long troughs the ground feels like the flank of a sleeping animal. A little way upslope it is cool enough to sit on. At the crests the wind comes over with a fine mineral grit in it and nothing grows taller than a thumbnail.

The troughs breathe. Water that fell on the bright side long ago travels along the tilted beds, gathers heat, rises as vapor, and finds a way out. A mile of narrow cracks will begin giving off a faint smell of eggs. Then the warm breath comes through, steadily enough to lift the loose fringes of the black felt that grows on either lip. Deep in the felt, bacteria live on what the vapor brings up; farther out there are clear grazing worms, and beyond the reach of the worms a sparse growth of gray fans as thin as the wings of moths. The fans catch the damp on their combs and conduct it down their stems. Each makes a wet place about the size of a palm. There may be fifty paces of sterile dust between one thicket of fans and another.

Nothing here was built. Nevertheless there are districts with the character of great works running on a night shift: heat delivered from far away, gases separated out by successive beds of mineral, whole populations making their living from the waste of the population farther up the line. The dry ground beside an old vent is strewn with yellow grains sorted by weight into exquisite little drifts. Occasionally something heavy knocks far underground. For a while afterward all the vents along one side of a trough draw air *in*. The fans fold flat. The tiny grazers have time to get below before the flow reverses and a breath hot enough to cook them goes washing over the stone.

There are larger animals, too, surprisingly many of them. Most are low and soft-footed. There is a beast like a broad leather bag carried a finger’s breadth off the ground on twelve legs; it feeds by running its tender lower lip along the margin of the felt, leaving a clean brown track. Its young cling underneath it, dropping off one by one wherever the parent finds a patch too small to bother with. There are lean hunters with pits for sensing heat along the whole length of the jaw. On the warm floor of a trough they hunt badly. They wait instead on the cool slopes where anything that goes by is briefly distinct. Sometimes one hears a single scramble in the grit. More often the only evidence of a hunt is a fan slowly standing up again after something has brushed it flat.

Once or twice in a long season fog spills over the western rim. It has come an immense way up from the sunlit rivers. For several nights, or what would be nights elsewhere, the stars go out in order from west to east. The high dry crests take water first. Eggs that might be mistaken for grains of sand split open in the hollows; long-legged things step out already moving, already searching for one another. The leather grazers climb in such numbers that for an hour a slope seems to be sliding uphill. They leave the fans alone. What they want is a blue skin that swells on the bare stone only when wetted from above, and tastes of salt.

When the fog passes, a little of its water stays behind under the flags. The rest goes down through the crevices to join the slow traffic in the rock. The crests empty. A hunter lingering up there can feel, through the pads of its feet, the faint continuous tremor of all the animals descending out of sight. Overhead the stars are sharp again. From one rim of the dark to the other there is hardly a sound.
