From the top of the pass the city looks like a ruled sheet of water. There are six miles of it, tipped very slightly toward the west, where the sheet ends in a lip of red stone. Cloud rubs along the underside of that lip. Above it there is room to see a long way in the poor copper light: a black line running straight for half a mile and turning a corner, a square of paler water, a little white thread traveling across the square. What looks from the pass like the white wake of a boat is the crest of a wave about an inch high. The boat would have nowhere to put its keel.

Up close there are roofs under almost all that water. The city was built down into a shelf of hot basalt, first just to get out of the wind, then because the cut stone could be sold in the low country. A district paid for itself as it was dug. Its last expense was a lid. Consequently one can walk from the eastern baths to the freight yards without descending a step or passing anything taller than a hip. There is an occasional vent with a stone cap broad enough to sit on, though too hot for it. The black lines are slots left open above the streets. Look down one and there are lintels, galleries, ceramic power insulators, the backs of lamps, all the familiar accommodations of a city stacked along two walls with scarcely twelve feet of murk between them. Look up again and there is nothing for a mile but a wet plane and one more vent.

Water wells through the floor of the lowest galleries. There are still engines lifting it. You can feel one working if you set a heel on the right roof: three strokes close together, a long pause to let a bucket fill, three strokes again. It comes up hot enough to hurt, spills out through bronze mouths, and travels west over the roofs. By the time it goes over the lip it is merely warm. The pall above the shelf is largely the city's own water on its way back. Under that low ceiling even noon has the light of a room with the blinds tilted shut. Every few hours large lukewarm drops fall, widely spaced. Each hits the shallow water with the distinct smack of a hand against a table. Then for a while the whole plateau smells of wet brick.

Roof and street no longer quite agree. Centuries of flow have taken the sharpness off the ridges between wards. There are channels as smooth and deep as the hollow of a collarbone, crossing the old property lines at a slant. Here and there a roof has worn through. Lean over one of these holes and the stream curls down through the middle of a room, carrying a long wavering patch of daylight to the tiles below. In one room the patch falls on a white enamel sign giving the night fare to the western terminus. It is the easiest thing to read for miles.

Nothing grows in the channels. The soft orange nap along their sides is iron; rub it and it comes off gritty on the thumb. In the settling tanks below, a colorless skin lives on the meeting of two sorts of water, one carrying sulfur out of the rock, the other bringing down air from the roofs. Once in a great while a flat, six-legged creature climbs a tank wall to scrape that skin from the waterline. It is almost transparent except for a brown seam of food moving through it. When a pump starts nearby, the creature folds its legs into grooves in its belly and lets go. For years the little oval things found in the strainers were entered in the maintenance books as faulty washers. Someone eventually crossed out *faulty*.

The strangest hour comes when the western outlets shut. They do this by weight, without a keeper: the collecting trough below the lip fills, sinks on its pivots, and draws a row of gates up out of their beds. The water stops going over. One by one the slots above the streets take the overflow instead. Far below, cups of trapped air are squeezed out of stairwells and ticket halls, up the dry conduits and through the old public-address horns. A horn will give a low, almost tactful note. Another will answer, four blocks off. For perhaps a minute the districts can be told apart with the eyes shut: the thin whistle of the exchange, the double bass note of the baths with their two great empty bathing vaults. Then the trough empties through a hole in its bottom, the gates settle, and the city goes back to lifting precisely the water it has just let in.

Out at the western lip there is a broad dry place under an overhang. All the stone there has been polished to a dull shine at sitting height. Whoever sat there had a view away from the works, over the cloud that fills the lower valley. They could have stayed through the gate change without getting wet. When the outlets open again, the first water falls in separate heavy ropes; then the gaps fill in, and for another few hours there is a curtain between the seat and the sky. From behind it the light is better. Every drop that goes past has seen the open air.
