Under the saddle of the mountain, where water might be expected to run away in either of two directions, there is a room in which it stays. Three people could lie down in it if it had a floor. What it has instead is a bed of shallow, warm pans, their rims fitted together like the cells of a comb made by something with no particular gift for geometry. Some are big enough to wash a shirt in. Some would scarcely take a tooth.

There is no light. The way in turns twice, and at the second turn there is a hanging lip of rock under which you must put your face almost flat against the ground. It is cold there. You smell the wool of your own sleeves and the damp dust you have pushed up with your chin. Then your forehead comes out into warmth, a warmth so complete and local that for a moment it feels like somebody's palm.

The room is easiest to understand with a hand laid flat. Under the water there is a fine meal of stone. Press through it and you meet a skin, a little yielding, with hard beads set so close in it that you cannot put a fingertip between them. The rims of the pans are made of these same beads grown together. Their upper edges are thin and blunt and have never been worn smooth; they come smooth. Even the big folded mass against the back wall, which feels like a heap of heavy blankets abandoned in the wash, has that tender finish. The whole place is rock caught at the point of setting.

Warm water comes up through the meal without disturbing it. Over most of the ceiling the damp has gathered into drops that do not fall. A hand raised there comes down wet to the wrist. In one place a drop does fall, with long intervals, onto a strip of bark laid across a pan. Somebody has weighted the bark at either end with a pebble. The sound is a little hollow *tok*. Without the bark it would make almost no sound at all. Perhaps it was put there by someone who disliked having no notion how far away the other wall was.

There are a few other marks of visits: three notches at knee height in the entrance turn; a plaited rush loop, gone stiff with lime, where a shoulder needs help through the pinch. Nothing has been planted or cut back. The only root that has found its way down hangs beside the loop, a long bare cord with a fan of fine ends feeling the warm air. Sometimes a small soft-bodied animal climbs it. Its presence is known mostly by the clean track, the width of a sewing thread, that it leaves across the paste on the wall.

Up on the saddle, water freezes in the grass and rips stones out when it goes. Here it has arrived at an accommodation. The spring gives up a little stone where it enters a pan and takes a little back where it seeps out underneath. A rim that rises the thickness of a nail loses its supply; one that sinks is fed again. The pans go on being newly made, without getting any newer or any older in shape. Even the bark has acquired its exact weight of stone and goes on sounding like bark.

To leave, you find the cold place with your cheek. There is a brief difficulty getting your elbows back under you; then the air thins and the smell of bruised mountain grass returns. A person standing on the saddle could put one heel above the deep pan and one above the bark and never know there was room between them for that small sound to travel.
