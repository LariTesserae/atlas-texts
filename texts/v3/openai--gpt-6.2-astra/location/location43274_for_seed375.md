From the lip of the hollow you can see the whole town, including the backs of the houses. It would fit comfortably inside a cathedral close. The hollow has no great depth. At one end the uppermost street runs level with the surrounding plain for a few yards, so that a little of the plain comes indoors there: a sill of grey powder, with the tracks of one animal in it, going in and coming out.

There is hardly any substance to the town. A house is four uprights of pale salt joined by a roof about as thick as the cover of a book. On the shaded side there may be a second storey. Houses share walls, and at intervals open passages have been left through an entire block, the lintels diminishing one behind another. The streets have that particular look of having been made for somebody: at a difficult corner the wall curves back to give a little more room. Yet nothing here has hands.

The builders grow in the joints. Seen close to, one resembles a short length of plum-coloured stitching, pulled too tight, with a minute pucker in the stone at every stitch. The exposed part is quite hard. Underneath it divides into soft fans, thin enough to live between one leaf of salt and the next. There might be twenty such plants to a street. For most of the day, if this interval of slightly less dimness can be called day, they do very little. The east faces of the houses lose a little mineral to the air. The west faces acquire it. A doorway shifts by less than its own bevel. In a fortnight it will have crossed the room.

Down here the air is cool enough to keep that mineral suspended as a fine lilac dusk. Higher up it clears. This gives the odd impression, looking along a street, that the upper windows are open on a much better day than the lower ones. Nothing drips from a sill. There are no stains beneath the eaves, no softened corners anywhere. Even the oldest house, the low one with three courts near the middle, has edges sharp enough to part a fallen husk under its own weight.

Towards evening the stitching loosens. All over the hollow, without a breath of wind, the loose ends rise. They are much longer than one would think. Some reach across the street and lie on an opposite sill. Where two touch they draw apart again, leaving a clean gap; where one finds bare stone it fixes there, and by morning has drawn a white rib of salt up after it. This is how a bridge begins. It is also why there are streets. No growth will close the space in which it has once encountered another living tip. Even after both plants die, their successors follow the slight difference in the grain. That courteous bend at the corner preserves the reach of something long since gone.

There is plenty happening to watch and almost nothing to hear. A skin comes free of a roof and revolves on its long axis all the way down to the pavement, where it lands on an edge and stays there. A purple fan slowly turns a shutter of stone inside out. In one of the courts a six-foot shadow contracts to a thread as the arch that cast it thins to transparency. Once, from somewhere too far inside the block to locate, comes the small sound of a seed hull giving way.

The animal that made the tracks spends these hours flat against the underside of an arch. Its belly is white and its back is the colour of the powder outside. Only when it moves can you see the narrow dark fringe with which it combs ripe seeds from the lifted threads. It takes three or four. Then it crawls out to the plain again by a different opening, its fringe dusted plum, and for a while there is no moving creature in sight.

By the time the light returns, a new cross-passage has opened near the low end. Through it you can see clear across the hollow to a patch of unoccupied ground, dim and smooth. At its centre stands a single white angle scarcely higher than a fingernail. On one side of it the powder is still level. On the other there is already a little shade.
