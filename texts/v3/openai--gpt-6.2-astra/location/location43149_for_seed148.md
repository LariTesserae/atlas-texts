The first thing you see of the town is a handrail, pale enough to pick out in the dark. It comes over the lip of the meadow and runs downhill for six steps. The seventh is still a bud. You can get past it by putting one foot in the fork and swinging round, as the postwoman does, but you will have green on the seat of your coat.

Below is a hollow about the size of a covered market. All the town is in its skin. There are dwellings in the rind, a pumping station in the floor, high windows that open out among the grass roots. If there were a lamp strong enough to light it all at once, you could stand on the seventh step and see the numbered doors of three streets, the freight gallery, the blind at the midwife’s window, and an immense quantity of absolutely unoccupied air. Even the laundry is drawn flat against the walls to dry. It is an old municipal habit to keep the middle clear.

The floor gives a little. A fresh part of it has come in since yesterday, a low pale shelf the shape of the milk cart that stands beside it. There is no grit here. What comes off the soles of boots is a fine curl like the paring of an apple. Under the first tough layer the tissue is veined and quite wet; on top it is cool and dry except for hollows where condensation collects. These have each acquired a black floating leaf and, under the leaf, a shrimp no bigger than a comma. In the deepest hollow the shrimp have had time to become transparent. Their meals show through them as minute lengths of green thread.

There is iron in the pumping station, though you have to knock on things to find it. The flywheel has furred over with root hairs. A brake shoe has produced a hard little pear. Every few hours the motor starts and a shutter slides back from a slit of amber light. Through it one can see a belt running up into the dark and the oilman holding his thumb to a bearing to judge its heat. The pumps take the night’s dew out of the roof and send it along hollow runners to the houses. At the end of the run, for perhaps twenty seconds, it rains on one particular doorstep. The woman who lives there sets out her kettle.

The sound hardly carries. The walls are full of small warm bodies that take it up. There are mice asleep three deep behind the station clock; there are moths folded into the pleats of every blind. On the freight gallery a long strip of bark appears to be traveling slowly uphill. Look close and it resolves into hundreds of slate-colored beetles walking under a loose flake, carrying their patch of darkness with them. A shrew runs a finger’s length out of a drain, takes the last beetle in the file, and runs back. The flake tips, steadies, goes on. One can spend an afternoon here without seeing anything cross the open floor.

Toward evening the reason for that becomes clear. A moth shaken out of a blind flutters a little too far from the wall. For a moment it hangs quite still, beating its wings; then there are two moths, one where it is and one where it is going to be. Between them stretches a fine white fiber. The first moth falls away like a husk. The second gets back to the blind. The fiber remains, thickening very slowly, putting out pairs of sticky leaves.

This is how the town gets its joists. In the works office there are drawings for a second freight gallery, with timings for the flight of a trained pipistrelle written along the spans. It is delicate work. Once a surveyor tossed his cap across the hollow and by spring there was a solid rib of timber right across the midwife’s view. She hangs her towels on it now. The old rule about the middle is largely a matter of daylight: there is little enough as it is, just what seeps through the meadow in a hundred tiny punctures where larks have pulled up worms.

After rain, each puncture shines for a while. You can see then that the hollow has no one color. The newer streets are ivory, with the faint pink of a peeled twig; the older ones are bottle green. The oldest door of all has gone the deep red of a plum. A child stands there in stockings, holding a saucer out to catch a drop that has been swelling overhead since noon. Behind the child, in a kitchen cut from a single knot, someone is frying onions. The smell reaches farther than any sound. For a little while noses emerge all around the hollow, at every height, from holes you would have sworn were nail marks.

By the time the drop falls, the meadow overhead is black. The station slit closes. Somewhere up the stairs a sheath splits with a soft, domestic click, and in the dark there are seven steps home.
