The first thing to learn on the high common is that a puddle with a black bottom may be only a finger deep, while a dry patch the color of straw may have nothing under it for half a mile. The black is the skin of the water leaf, which grows in dishes. The straw is a loose fringe along the edge of the land. In good light you can lie down short of the fringe and look between the fibers. Far below there is another fringe, green with distance, and beyond it a bright piece of sky.

Most of the common is quite safe to walk on. It gives a little, like the thick part of the palm below the thumb. There are miles of this going almost level in the sun, smooth except where two fields have joined and the stitches stand up in a low double ridge. It is easier to see the plan of the country from one of these ridges. There are broad receiving fields, tipped very slightly inward to keep the rain; long narrow fields that carry it; and here and there, set so far apart that at most three are visible together, the great white knees. Each knee is the place where a field folds down and becomes its own support. They look white because the fold pulls the green skin tight and parts its minute scales, showing the milk-colored stuff between.

Nothing here had to be cleared to make room for anything else. The room was made first. A pair of runners, going out over the warm empty air, curled back until their tips met; smaller runners crossed the enclosure and drew it taut. Only then did the skin grow across. This still goes on at the eastern edge. There are openings there the size of a village, neatly bounded, with one or two green cords stretched across them, shining wet. They suggest an early stage of construction. Yet the oldest knee has, on its sunny side, a little hollow shaped exactly like the largest opening, down to the inward hook at one end. The common has been building that particular piece of itself for as long as it has existed. The cords thicken on top and come apart underneath at precisely the same rate. Stand at the edge all summer and not one additional foot of country will be yours to stand on.

It is warmest down in the seams. A seam will take a person out of sight without bringing their head below the general level of the land by more than a yard. The sides lean together overhead. Water beads along each line of stitching and hangs until the beads touch, when the whole bright line runs off at once. There is a smell of cut cucumber, though nothing is cut. In the bottom grow short red hairs, inclined all one way by the passage of water; part them and there is another skin, perfectly dry, a dull buff color, with the close nap of a peach. You can spend a long time in there examining an inch of ground. Climb out and it is startling to find how little there is to look at. A white knee in the middle distance. Its reflection in a shallow dish. A second knee much farther off, no bigger than a tooth.

The noise belongs to the space below. A steady wind comes up through the hollow supports and has to go somewhere. Most of it escapes through slits along the ridges, each slit furnished with a pair of soft lips that beat against one another. A single pair makes an undistinguished flapping sound. Heard across several miles the result is a tremendous low vowel, fuller in the chest than in the ears. It does not stop at night. Rain adds a fine hard rattle to it: the drops that miss the upper fields strike the dry undersides of lower ones. In a heavy shower you hear the rain descending a long time after the light has come back. On top, the ordinary afternoon sun shows every drop distinctly, and draws from the wet green surface a thin haze hardly high enough to hide your ankles.

There are a few animals. One is a gray, six-legged thing about as long as a fingernail, which spends its life at the lip of a dish. When the dish is full it leans down to graze the black skin under water. When the dish empties it leans farther. Its legs leave six little pale pits, slow to rise, and sometimes you come upon the pits alone and feel the disproportionate pleasure of finding that somebody has lately been there. There are no trails between the dishes. The next one might be an hour's walk away.

Toward evening the undersides take the light. What seemed at noon to be a single plain proves to have terraces, aprons, deep hanging walls, whole lower commons fitted into the intervals between the knees. For perhaps twenty minutes it is possible to see the workmanship almost to the bottom: how a brace divides to go around a watercourse, how the parted lengths flatten and broaden and meet again as a basin for the water they have just let through. Then the sun goes behind the upper fields. The depths fill with blue. The near ground stays warm and plainly visible, its last inch still wet, its unfinished lip still putting out into the air the same tender, tightly rolled green point.
