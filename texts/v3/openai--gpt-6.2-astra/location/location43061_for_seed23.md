The first thing visible from the pass is a red sled going sideways across a roof. A child lies flat on it, pulling herself along a rope from chimney to chimney. In the glare it is hard to see the seams between the roofs. Only when she comes to a gap, and a pair of hands reaches up out of it to take a basket from her, does the whole slope resolve into a town.

It occupies a notch in the mountain no bigger than the shadow of a cloud. The rock here is pale green and folds in close pleats. Ice has worn the crests of the pleats round and scoured the hollows between them deep; one can lay a mitten on the crest of a ridge and look straight down either side into different households. The inhabitants have spanned the hollows with short slabs, bored smoke holes through them, roofed the gaps with hide. There are eighty hearths in the notch, a public bath the length of a man, a hall where judgments are heard, and hardly a level place outdoors to set down a cup. The few willows grow flat against the stone. Their upright parts are the catkins, which at this height look extravagantly tall.

Water wells up through the green rock just above freezing. In the open it skins over almost at once, but water arriving behind it keeps the skin lifted. All through the town are these clear, moving blisters. They range from the size of a thumbnail to sheets that cover a whole court. Under them the current runs every which way, according to which outlets have frozen and which have come free. A court goes dry in an instant. Its ice settles onto the paving, taking a perfect impression of a lost button. Two doors away, water swells up through the cracks of a stair and lifts all seven of its loose treads together. The woman who lives at the head of the stair has a forked pole for putting them back. Her mother cut the notches in it. They still fit.

What keeps the town quiet is the depth of snow in all the unroofed clefts, and the fact that most of what goes on here goes on under a lid. In six inches of water under the bathhouse window, hundreds of little amber shrimp swim against the light. A black waterbird, scarcely bigger than a fist, hunts them from below. It comes up for air in the same hole through which the bathers let the steam escape; sometimes there is a wet black head among the pink ones. The birds nest in the warm joints of the masonry. White mice live higher up, in the dry thickness of the roofs. On a fine day their shadows can be seen from indoors, running across the translucent hide patches like hasty stitches. Outside, a fox steps from patch to patch and listens. The mice stop. The shadows of the shrimp go on flickering over the bathhouse ceiling.

By noon the light is coming from so many surfaces that a nose or a knuckle scarcely casts a shadow. Women working bone at their doorways wear strips of dark hide over their eyes, with a slit fine enough to draw a hair through. They make needles, toggles, shrimp combs, little cupped spoons. The spoons are for visitors. People born here drink by laying the side of a finger on the rock and letting the seep run along it into their mouths. Every ridge has a slightly different taste. At the judgment hall, where a broad vein of salt crosses the back wall, generations of children waiting for their parents have licked a smooth oval hollow at the height of a child's face.

Toward evening the shadow of the pass reaches the uppermost spring. This is when the town changes fastest. Outlets close in succession down the slope. Men lift baskets of fish out of one lane and lower them into the next; someone rolls up a hide bridge with three mice still clinging to the underside; the red sled shoots home with nobody on it. For a little while there are silver domes everywhere, trembling against one another. The last daylight passes through them sideways and throws an immense green wavering up the cliff. From across the notch one can see the whole commotion and hear almost nothing. Then comes a single homely sound, a slab knocking back into its old bed. A door opens. In the covered lane beneath it the water is running steadily again, and somebody puts down a bowl.
