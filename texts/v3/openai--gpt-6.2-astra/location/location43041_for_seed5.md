There is a little ward under the steps of the lime market. To see it you have to go down to the salt flats and look back. The bottom step rests on two cheeks of stone, and what looks from above like a crack to put a stick in is, from down here, a broad, low opening full of daylight. The flats throw the light in. Even at the back of the ward, under three fathoms of masonry, a woman can sit in her doorway and see to thread a bone needle.

The whole place would fit under a fishmonger's awning. It has four streets, if you count the stair that goes up seven treads and ends at a currant bush. The people here are small enough to carry a currant between two on a yoke. Their houses are made of flakes shaved off the backs of oyster shells. They lay them dull side out, so the streets have a mild, substantial grey look; through an open door you get the surprise of a pink ceiling. The oldest houses have been patched so often that the original shell survives only as a curved sill, worn into two hollows where generations have sat with their feet outside.

It is cold enough to keep milk sweet for days. There is very little water. A dark seam crosses the underside of the step, and in certain weather beads form on it, hang for a long time, and fall. Below each falling place stands a public jar with a square of wool tied over its mouth to keep the drop from splashing out. Wool tails lead from the jars into the gardens. Most of the gardens are overhead. The soil is held up in nets, the roots felt through the nets, and the leaves come down so thickly that at two crossings the paving has had to be lowered to let a loaded cart pass under them. There are spoon-shaped leaves with a taste of mustard, and a pale vetch, and little striped turnips that grow with their shoulders pointing at the ground. In the hard white light off the flats one can see the dark curl of earth through the skin of a young turnip.

Nothing much is kept off the streets. Mites as glossy as chestnuts browse the fur on the water jars. A white moth lives folded into nearly every roof joint; sometimes what appears to be the chipped lower edge of a gable is six moths side by side. Along the stair live blunt-faced grey lizards. They lie across the treads, and people step over them, except at the seventh tread, where it is customary to wait. A lizard warmed by reflected light can take most of a morning to decide to move its tail. There is a seat there for this purpose.

What seems odd, after a while looking in, is that one can see a busy street all the way to its end and hear almost none of it. The barrows have rims of plaited rush. A shell cutter scores her work with flint and breaks it in her lap, inside her apron. Neighbours talking lean quite close together. The market overhead moved inland a long time ago; no one comes thundering down the steps. Once in a while there is the small, unmistakable sound of somebody biting into something crisp.

At the front of the ward, where a rail is needed against the drop to the flats, the currant bush has been trained along a row of forked pegs. Its wood has thickened around them until the pegs are wholly inside the branch. Every few years a child is shown where to set a new fork under the growing tip. Beyond that tip there is room for another house, and beyond that the blank bright reach of salt. They have never put a house there. It is where you go with a piece of sewing when you want a little more light, or with nothing in your hands when you want to see the sky.
