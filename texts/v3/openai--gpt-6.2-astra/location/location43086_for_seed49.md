At low water, the street under the fish market is nine steps down. You can descend them in ordinary shoes. The stone at the bottom is warm, almost dry, worn into two shallow troughs by carts whose wheels were a different width from those in use now. There are brass numbers on the doors. A woman sits in the third doorway repairing a string of little white floats. When the first water comes back, it comes up between the stones, not down the street. She finishes the float in her hand before she moves her stool.

The whole city is built in the bottom of a sea that never quite retired. The oldest plans show a chain of separate ports around its rim. Between them lay broad reaches of warm, shallow water over folded beds of salt and limestone. It was cheap to build out across the shallows; the water supplied heat, carried freight, took the weight of a foundation while its feet were being set. The ports went on building until their outer districts met. Today a train can run east for six days without passing the last house. There are people on that line who have never seen a slope that wasn't made to a specification.

It looks quite modest from a ferry. Six or seven stories of pale ceramic, brown galleries, sheets hanging out to dry. Sunlight falls straight down the narrow gaps and makes a brilliant moving script on the undersides of balconies. The extraordinary density is mostly below the ferry's keel. There are the drowned streets, then service floors, spawning floors, reservoirs, goods exchanges, the great horizontal pump rooms. Under those are rooms nobody has needed to enter for centuries. A probe sent down a disused laundry pipe once emerged in a tiled public bath ninety kilometers away. The bath was full of transparent shrimp. Its hot tap still worked.

Distance down there is a municipal service. A pair of gates can be made neighbors for an hour, provided the pressure on both sides is the same. This is how a basin wider than a continent keeps its appointments. At a quarter to noon, a district smelling of pepper and hot oil opens directly onto a district where the breakfast shutters are just going up. Freight goes first. Then come passengers, swimming animals, lost parcels, sometimes an entire afternoon's weather: cool rain driven through a gate on a sea wind and falling for three minutes inside a roofed station. The gates are very reliable. It is the water on either side that keeps changing its mind.

Most of the noise is the work of keeping it level. The pump rooms give out a note too low to hear until a cup or a window takes it up. Sluices bang open in long runs. Overhead trams brake with a descending howl; bargemen sound horns pitched to carry through the concrete as well as through the air. Add the millions of hard-shelled grazers rasping the wet walls. In a side alley, put your ear near a drain cover and you can distinguish each small scrape. Out in a square they make a continuous tearing sound, like someone ripping a sheet of heavy cloth that has no end. People talking face to face rest two fingers on each other's wrists. Much of the local accent is in the fingers.

There isn't a tree. What looks from a distance like green moss along a waterline is an accumulation of shed scales, each with a little oil sealed under it. Rub a thumb across the patch and it turns silver. The grazers live on a cream-colored skin that grows wherever mineral water sweats through the mortar, fed by iron coming up from the deep beds. The fish follow the grazers, the long-necked diving animals follow the fish, and the market follows all three. Several districts have declared their oldest walls protected feeding grounds. There the mortar is allowed to wear away until a block stands out on a narrow stem of its own stone. At night the cavities are packed with sleeping fish, noses outward, eyes reflecting the lights of the last ferry home.

There is scarcely a stretch of open ground, but there is weather. By midafternoon the western roofs have warmed enough to lift a column of wet air. Shutters begin slamming a hundred streets ahead of the rain. Then for a while every cornice pours. Water races along the gallery gutters, jumps the missing sections, bursts apart on awnings already bowed under the weight of small animals taking shelter. The gates close as the levels diverge. A journey of five stops becomes a journey of four thousand kilometers. Nobody attempts it. People buy something hot in a paper twist and stand under the departure board, watching the names of impossibly remote places give way to the names of places next door.

By evening the water beneath the market has risen to the seventh step. The woman has tied her floats from one brass door number to the next; they mark the safe passage for little boats above the old cart road. A boy sculls through with a load of clean dishes, ducking where the ceiling lowers. Far under him a gate opens. For a moment the floats all lean the wrong way, toward a morning tide on the other side of the world. Then the string eases. The boy ships his oar, catches the sill of the third doorway with one hand, and passes in the dishes, still warm from washing.
