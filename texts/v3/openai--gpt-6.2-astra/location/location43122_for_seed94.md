From below, the shelf looks too thin to have a country on top of it. There is a dark lip of rock, a line of grass bright enough to be seen through miles of air, and water falling off. Much of the water blows back against the cliff. There are gardens of yellow moss on its underside that get rain from no other direction.

Up on the shelf you lose the sense of an edge almost at once. The ground rolls away in long low swells. Birch scrub lies down the backs of them, all leaning one way, with the dwarf trees so closely fitted together that in autumn a fallen leaf can stay up there for weeks. Between swells there are sedge marshes and cold, clear pools with rust-red bottoms. Water stands in the hollows of old hoofprints. Even the scattered knobs of bare stone are wet on one side, although it may be a fine morning with quite ordinary shadows and little white butterflies going about in the sun.

Most of the shelf is a stack of ancient lava flows. Water gets in between the layers and travels astonishing distances on very little fall. You can see where a layer comes to the surface: a wet green seam goes round the nose of a hill and continues, at precisely the same height, on the flank of the next. Along these seams grows a kind of rush with a grey bladder at the foot of each stem. A bladder takes water in slowly through its skin. When full it contracts, feeding the water up a stiff central tube to the crown, where it leaks out among the seed scales. Put a hand around a stem and there is a definite little kick, followed by a long slack interval. No one stem delivers much. A hillside of them can keep a marsh on the *upper* side of the seam.

It is on a cold, sunny afternoon that the extent of this work becomes visible. Each crown wears a bead. The beads run together and drop; a shallow current starts up among the roots, carrying birch pollen, shed insect skins, the occasional minute black newt curled for the ride. Here and there a runnel finds an old course lined with flat pebbles and fills it from bank to bank. In the space of an hour, whole watersheds come into operation. There is something of a manufacturing district about the place then, in the sheer acreage given over to one repetitive task, the intake below and the delivery above. Yet the loudest thing for a considerable distance may be a snipe pulling one foot free of the mud.

The larger grazing animals travel along the rock swells. They have broad feet and a dark parting down the spine where their coats shed water. Often the only evidence of a herd is a patch of birch tops turning their pale undersides to the light, then another patch farther on. The young go nearly the colour of the red pool bottoms. When alarmed they wade out up to their chins and stand among the rushes. This serves them well against the long-bodied cats of the scrub, less well against a cold night; toward dusk the mothers come back along the bank, stopping at each pool, making a small nose sound like someone testing a berry for ripeness between finger and thumb.

There is weather here that never goes over the cliff. Vapour rises off the afternoon marshes, drifts inland, and thickens against ridges so far away that their snow is usually mistaken for cloud. A day or two later a low grey ceiling moves back out across the shelf. Rain darkens each swell in turn. In a clearing you might see a hundred miles of that slow change and still be standing in sunlight, with the shadows of butterflies crossing the backs of your hands. By the time the rain reaches you the rushes have gone slack in the cold. The pools rise quietly over their rims. Down at the lip, water begins to fall from places that were dry in the morning, and underneath, in the permanent green shade, the yellow moss takes its share.
