From the survey hut you can see all the way across the pass to the red roof of the valve house. It looks half an hour’s walk away. It takes most of a morning. The ground between is a succession of broad, low swells, each with a wet hollow behind it, and there is scarcely anything standing upright by which to judge the distance. A cotton-grass head on the nearest swell and the red roof on the last one are about the same size in the eye.

It is an old high pass with a very young floor. There used to be a tongue of ice here. Its last hummocks are still settling out under the gravel at the upper end, where once in a while a perfectly circular pool appears in what was, the summer before, a sound piece of meadow. The stones the ice laid down still have their corners. Water has not committed itself to a channel yet. It goes a little this way, a little that, dividing around a cushion of moss too low to cast a shadow, coming together again a hundred yards on. In clear weather the whole pass seems to have been ruled across with a diamond point. There are thousands of those fine bright lines.

Nearly everything you can put your hand on is growing. What looks from the hut like a slope of loose grey shale is a willow bed. The trunks are at ankle height, pressed along the ground by snow, with little upright twigs carrying leaves grey on the underside. A wind crossing it turns an acre silver and then turns it back. Even the brown bars between the pools are alive: last year’s sedge folded flat over this year’s pale green needles. Along the coldest runs there is a thick, dark, close-clipped moss. Water wells up through it if you set your palm down, so cold it hurts at the root of the thumb.

The great noise comes from below the lip of the pass. All the threads of water, and a much bigger stream from under the remaining ice, go over a ledge there. You cannot see the fall from anywhere on the meadow. You hear it as soon as you get out of the hut. In the open doorway it is possible to say a few words to the person inside; two steps away you have to turn back and show them with your hands. It is a steady tearing sound, so nearly unvarying that after a while the startling thing is to put your hood up and hear, at close quarters, the little dry scrape of cloth against your ear.

Part of the ice stream is taken off through a screen above the fall and sent down a steel pipe to the generating house in the valley. Up here there is only the valve house, the hut, and a line of numbered stakes. The stakes mark a maintenance track that never quite became a road. On the wet stretches the old sleepers are still in place. Sedge has come up between them in straight green seams, preserving the spacing of the timber even where the timber itself has gone soft. Once a week a man comes across with a grease gun and a long rake for the screen. There is a dry place for his coat behind the motor cabinet. Through the soles of his boots he can feel the screen rake’s drive engage; he couldn't possibly hear it.

By noon the mist has drawn back to the edge and hangs there in full sun, white enough to make the eyes run. Light gets under the willow leaves from the water as well as falling on them from above. In all that brightness there may be one snipe standing on one foot in the lee of a stake. It can stand there a long time unobserved. Its reflection, wavering in a water thread no wider than the bird, is easier to pick out than the bird itself.

The survey hut has photographs of the pass pinned to its inner wall, each taken from the same doorstep. At first they seem to be so many prints of the same exposure. Then you notice that a pool has closed at one end, that two silver streaks which used to meet now pass on either side of a patch of brown, that a stone once conspicuous beside the track is showing only its tip. The oldest photograph has a white date written on it in a young man’s careful hand. Outside, in the immensely loud, cold afternoon, the water is just getting over the rim of another cushion of moss. On the downhill side three or four sedge blades are trembling where it has begun to come through.
