At the front of the town there are six steps down to the water. Only the first two have been dressed with a chisel. The next is the old landing place, with two round holes where the mooring posts stood; the last three are cuts in blue clay, made this spring by a woman who got tired of coming home from her boat on hands and knees. A little water runs down all six steps even when the tide is coming in.

The whole town would fit in the crook of a river bend. It stands on a bank that came up out of the inlet within living memory. One winter the bottom heaved, the boats tipped over in their moorings, and by morning there was a long back of clay in the middle of the water, carrying shells still shut tight in their beds. It has risen a little at a time ever since. The end toward the sea is quite low and raw. Small falls of clay there leave faces as clean as the cut end of cheese. At the other end the ground is high enough to bury a jar without its filling up overnight.

This was a useful place to stop a boat, and there was never very much of it. Houses occupy both sides of the ridge and meet at the top. Walking along the crest, you pass through a succession of shared porches, each with a different floor: split poles, shell grit, a handsome square of flat stone, then poles again, worn pale where people put their feet. Below the porches, every wedge of space has a use. There are racks for drying line and a shrine scarcely bigger than the lamp inside it. A potter keeps her unfired bowls on a shelf over her neighbor's bed, the driest place on the bank. Timber has to come by boat. When a house gets a new door, the old door can generally be seen a day later somewhere farther down the slope, laid across a runnel for a bridge.

For all this company there is very little din. Most of the work is tying, scraping, basketwork, the pressing of wet things into shapes in which they will dry. From inside one wall comes the soft, dogged rub of a stone being worked flat on another stone. When it stops, you hear drops falling into a crock. The air is cool enough that a hand taken out of a basin begins to ache as it dries. In the middle of the day there is good plain daylight right down to the lowest lane, reflected up under the eaves by the inlet. You can see the grain in the undersides of the boats.

Fresh water comes out of the bank in unexpected places. It gathers behind the blue skin of the clay until a spot darkens, bulges, and opens a hole the width of a straw. One such spring lasted nine years in the back of the bakehouse. Another ran for half a morning through the eye of a carved face beside a door. There is an understanding here that a spring belongs to everybody, wherever it breaks. A householder sets a spout of folded bark against the hole and joins on lengths of gutter until the water reaches the lane. Some gutters cross two rooms to get there. In one kitchen the family lift a section out at supper so they can sit around the table; for that hour the spring drips into the soup pot.

Very little grows. There is a patch of rushes below the steps, and on the north walls a black-green fur that comes away on a sleeve. An old willow cutting has rooted beside the potter's window. Children are sent out to pick clay off its buds after a hard rain. Out on the bare tip of the bank a single red-legged bird sometimes walks the tide line, stopping to look down into each new crack. Its feet leave such distinct tracks that you can tell where it stood on one leg to scratch its head.

Toward evening the tide covers the woman's three steps. The boats lift clear of their beds and begin, almost imperceptibly, to turn. Up in the lane somebody is on a stool easing a gutter sideways. The spring has shifted the breadth of a finger since breakfast; most of the water is still going into the common jar, but a thin bright thread is running down the wall and into the shells beneath the porch. The person on the stool calls down to ask if the thread has stopped. For a little while everyone passing through that porch steps over it, though it is hardly enough to wet a shoe.
