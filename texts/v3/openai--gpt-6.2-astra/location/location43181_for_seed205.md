On a clear morning the distances are deceptive. A black fork sticks up on the skyline, with three white birds attending it. It looks like a twig within throwing distance. By noon you have walked far enough for the nearer swells to hide the fork altogether, and when it comes in sight again there are still three white birds there. What looked like birds are the peeled places on a storm-broken limb. The birds themselves, thousands of them, are too small to see.

This is high country, a hot shelf extending along nearly a third of the equator. The air has a thin, clean taste. Sun reaches the ground almost everywhere. Very little grows *up* here. What would be separate plants in a kinder climate grow sideways, welding where they meet, sharing the expense of a single weatherproof skin. There are leagues of tawny rind with hardly a stalk above ankle height. Underfoot the rind is finely checked, like the glaze on an old bowl; in the checks you can see a delicate celadon green. A dropped pebble bounces. A walking person leaves no print, though an hour after they pass there may be a darkening where each heel came down.

The contours have been worked over for so long that scarcely an angle remains. Wind shaves the windward slopes; dust made of cork and pollen settles on the lees, where little feeding roots take it in again. Old watercourses survive as wide, shallow troughs. They turn in ample bends around obstacles that wore away ages ago. Along the steep western edge of the shelf these troughs hang open to the sky. Looking into one from below, you can see layer after layer of former walking surfaces, each with its cracks healed over, and long pale cords descending through them all into the blue shade. Somewhere under that weight there must be rock. The only loose stones anyone finds on top are the worked ones, passed from hand to hand until even the hollows for the thumb are polished.

For all the apparent vacancy there is hardly a quiet minute. The rind is hollow underneath. Most of the animal life of the shelf occupies the warm fingerbreadth between the tough upper skin and the wet growing layer. Flat-bodied grazers shear the green with paired fans of teeth. Pin-legged things run upside down over their backs, picking lice out of the joints; the lice in turn carry bright beads of eggs belonging to something smaller. You can lie on your stomach and hear a particular mouth working just beneath your ear. Stand up, and what you hear is all the mouths for miles around, conducted through one continuous sounding board. On a hot afternoon the pitched whine of the males courting below the crust makes the bones beside your nose itch. People walking together speak at sunrise. Later they use their hands.

Water comes up before it comes down. Toward evening the deep cords lift moisture into the exhausted growing tips. The rind swells a little. At the bottoms of old troughs, seams pinch shut and squeeze out lines of perfectly clear drops. Each drop has a crowd awaiting it: velvet mites, long-jawed flies, thumbnail-sized birds that alight with both feet braced and drink their fill without folding their wings. There is a blunt green lizard that drinks only by licking the wet breasts of these birds. It stands among them quite peaceably until the water is gone. Within half an hour the seam is dry enough to strike a spark on, and the lizard has slipped sideways through a slit too narrow to admit its head.

Where several troughs converge there may be a pool, no wider than a kitchen table, shading from amber at the rim to black in the middle. A pool that size changes the country around it. Out of the rind come tall, widely spaced limbs, each carrying at its tip a single tilted pad of foliage. From a distance they look like people holding trays above their heads. The pads give calves of the great shell-backed runners a square of shade to stand in. A calf will follow that shade round the stem all day, cropping whatever pushes through the rind within reach of its soft lips. At dusk its mother comes out of the glare trailing a plume of little airborne scavengers, and the two touch noses and go down to drink. Around a permanent pool the calves have worn circles deep enough that in a low sun the shadows resemble the rings of many vanished walls.

There are actual buildings, too, though one can travel days without finding one. A shelter is made by lifting three sides of a young slab of rind, bending it back while it is still supple, and propping it with a fork of deadwood. The cut edges grow downward in ropes. In time they knit into two walls and a back, and the fork is freed for use somewhere else. Inside, it is cool enough to sleep at midday. An old shelter will have a narrow seat scooped in the back wall, a stone adze on a thong, and a row of notches at the height where a seated person can feel them in the dark. These tell which way the nearest reliable seam runs. They have to be cut afresh every generation. A house left unattended slowly smooths away the directions, the seat, the opening, until all that remains is a low hump with, somewhere in its green heart, an excellent adze.

Once or twice in a season the shelf makes weather on a scale commensurate with itself. Warm breath rising from a million seams meets the cold air above the rim. By noon a cloud has laid a long blue bruise across the country, and under it every pale limb stands out with extraordinary clarity. Then rain advances in a visible wall. For a few minutes the troughs run again, rust-red with loosened cork. The crust lifts in overlapping scales to let the water through. All the hidden animals come out.

That is when you understand the white on the broken limb. Every high thing for a hundred miles is refuge. The limb goes dark from the base upward with climbing bodies; the tray trees bow until their pads touch; even a traveler standing still acquires a thick, trembling cuff of life around each leg. Wings crack open. Runners bound through sheets of water, calves tucked close in their lee. The sound, already tremendous, takes on a lower note as the flooded chambers under the ground empty their air, one after another, out toward the escarpment. At the very edge, people say, you can see the long cords swing free of the cliff and drip into sunshine with the storm still two days’ walk away.

By the following morning the water is underground. The rind has drawn tight. There may be a fish stranded in the cup of an old heel mark, silver as a shaving, attended by two patient flies. Beyond it the long bare slopes lie in ordinary yellow light. Nothing seems to move until, far off, a single square of shade slips east and a calf takes one small step after it.
