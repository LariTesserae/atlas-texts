The first indication of height is a break in the sedge no wider than a foot. Through it you can see the sunny back of a cloud a long way down. A little warm water runs over the lip, separates into drops, and is lost to sight. On either side the turf is firm. There are snails grazing quite unconcernedly along the edge.

This is the old crown of a mountain country. Seen from below, it has tremendous flanks, blue in the distance, with the white threads of falls hanging almost motionless against them. Up here there is scarcely a rise steep enough to make you change your pace. The hard parts of the mountains have worn down to long whalebacks, the hollows between them have filled, and over both has grown the same deep pelt. There are days of walking between places where stone shows through. It is a fine gray stone, smooth even on its sheltered side. In a shower it darkens without making a sound.

Most of what looks like turf is the top of a forest. Its great trees grow sideways. A trunk as thick as a cottage lies partly sunk in its own generations of leaf mould, putting up sprays of little round leaves on stalks a hand high. Roots go down from it wherever there is room. Where there is no room they cross another trunk, take hold on the far side, and draw the two together. Under your feet there may be thirty levels of these crossings, some sound and full of sap, some gone soft enough for a newt to push its face into. In warm weather there is a smell rather like the inside of a pear. The clearings are ponds. On still afternoons the leaves around their margins turn over and show their silver undersides, and for an hour or so each pond appears to occupy rather more country than it does.

Water moves crosswise through the wood. Kneeling to drink at a spring, you can sometimes feel a current going underneath it at right angles, cool against one knee. Nothing much depends on the slope of the ground until the water reaches an edge. Far inland there are whole watersheds poised on the soundness of a single buried bough. When it gives way, the change at the surface is slight: a patch of yellow rushes fails one year, a drinking pool sweetens, and some miles away a stand of thirsty red ferns begins to do well. The small blunt-headed fish of the runnels find the new way through before daylight.

The dimness is overhead. Vapour rises off so great an extent of wet leaf that by late morning the plateau has made a sky of its own, low, dove-coloured, warm enough to bare your arms in. Things close at hand have wonderfully definite colours in that light: the purple feet of a rail, the orange lining of a split seed, a bead of black dung balanced on a blade of grass. The distance goes away altogether. A herd of dun animals grazes out there, occasionally near enough for you to hear a mouth tearing off a succulent shoot. They have broad, spreading toes. Their young follow by fitting all four feet into the shallow prints of the adults. For all the life here the usual sound is a single drop striking a leaf somewhere below the surface, followed after an appreciable interval by another drop striking the same leaf.

At wide intervals there are low enclosures. The trees were persuaded, when young, to bend up out of the common weave and back into it, making a ring of arches around a dry floor. Nothing has been cut. In one enclosure a living shelf still follows the curve of the wall, just at the height of a seated person's elbow. A rail keeps her eggs there now. In another, the end of the shelf has gone on growing until it passes out through the doorway and halfway across a marsh. There is no obvious way to tell how long the makers have been gone. The paths that join the rings stay open because the dun animals like a dry walk as well as anyone did.

Toward evening the homegrown cloud thins from beneath. Long before there is any blue overhead, you can see horizontally again, quite suddenly and to an astonishing distance. The pooled basins shine between the whalebacks. Beyond them, where there seemed to be a final rim, there are other basins at nearly the same height, taking the light one after another. A rail steps out on a floating leaf and its weight lets water in over the toes of one foot. It stands drinking from this little accident while, all across the west, the high country comes into view.
