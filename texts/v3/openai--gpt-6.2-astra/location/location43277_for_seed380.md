Under the tram square there is a second square, about the size of a school gymnasium. You come into it by a stair that turns twice, so that the last thing you see of the street is a pair of shoes going by well above your head. The paving continues down both flights. At the bottom the stones are laid in a fan, a considerable piece of work almost entirely wasted on the dark. You can make it out by running your sole over the joints.

There is water over most of the fan, hardly enough in places to cover the date on a coin. Toward the middle it deepens by such easy degrees that a person wading out in good boots might go a long way before thinking to stop. Four brick piers stand in it. They carry the tram bed, and when a car goes over you can tell which pier is taking the weight by the little ruff that shakes out around its foot. Down here a tram makes almost no sound. The rails were laid on cork to spare the foundations of the music school next door.

The light comes through the glass bosses set in the sidewalk overhead. Most have gone the color of weak tea. Under one, on the lip of a disused cable trough, grows a tuft of green so fine it lies flat under the weight of a single drop. There is another tuft at the far end of the trough. That is about the extent of the garden. The air smells of wet brick and occasionally of the hot dust in a switch box. The handrail stays cold even on the days the asphalt softens upstairs.

Along the east wall are three electric pumps on high enamel plinths, each with its own flight of two little steps. Only the middle pump is still connected. It lifts seepage into the street drain at rather less than the rate at which a man could pour from a jug. Its impeller has been turning at that speed for sixty years. In that time the trickle from a packing gland has cut a groove down one of the steps and filled it in again with a cream-colored deposit. A mechanic comes down on Thursdays to put a thumb to the bearing housing. The square is so still that the click of his oilcan brings heads out from under stones twenty feet away.

What live here look at first like scraps of boiled onion. They are little translucent animals with six walking legs and a pair of long forelegs which they fold away under their chins. In the dimness nothing much distinguishes them from the wet mortar. Shade one of the glass bosses with a coat, though, and the patch of floor beneath it begins to come apart. Hundreds have been sitting there, an inch or two between neighbors, all facing the same way. They graze the nap of living matter off the stone. Their backs are quite flat. A bead of air rides on each one like a rivet.

They can take to the underside of the water as readily as to the bottom. That would be remarkable enough. The odd thing is what happens when one of the larger ones dies: its bead stays put. It will hang there weeks on end, silver from the stair, black from the pump steps. No current displaces it. The young use these points to navigate, turning at one, passing under the next, until there are regular lanes of traffic across what seems to be a quite featureless reach of water. Some of the lanes follow the old joints of the fan. Others go straight through places where a pier has since been built. At those places the animals climb the brick, traverse it sideways in single file, and come down at precisely the depth at which they left the lane.

There was a proposal once to drain the square and use it for bicycle storage. The surveyors marked the winter waterline in red at the foot of the stair. You can still see the two ends of the mark where it runs up onto dry brick. Between them the pigment has loosened and come away, grain by grain, on little feet. Far out toward the middle pump, visible only around noon, a faint pink path is taking shape across the gray.
