The tallest thing in the town is a leaf tip that comes up to the knee. Beyond it the salt flat goes on in ordinary sunshine. A hare crossing the flat will sometimes stop at the leaf, set its chin on the green rim beside it, and look down into the town. From below, the two ears stand apart on the skyline like the backs of people watching a game.

There is just the one plant. Most of it is a hollow swelling in the ground, the size of a covered market, with a long rent across the top where daylight gets in. Its inner rind has grown in galleries. There are streets with a high side for walking and a low side for drainage; there are sleeping niches and common yards and, down at the bottom, a broad green square. No one laid these out. Where feet pass often, the rind puts on a springy layer like the heel of a foot. Where something lies down in the same place night after night, the green flesh grows up around it until it has a room. The quarters of the little red bats have ceilings pitched to a point, with a little knob at the summit of each one, precisely sufficient for two feet.

For all this accommodation, the first impression is of an empty and rather solemn place. The galleries cling to the sides. Nothing crosses the middle, not even a strand of web. There is enough clear air there to fly a heron round and round with its wings out straight. Sun falls through that air in a broad stripe and shows the grain of the floor, which is leaf grain, branching and branching until the eye gives out. The floor is warm. At its lowest edge are three spoonfuls of water. A long-legged fly patrols each one.

Then the sound arrives. It arrives bodily, like the first shove of somebody behind you in a crowd. The hairs on your arms stir. All along the walls there are crickets filing their wings, lizards knocking loose the dry skins of their eggs, bats squabbling in their pointed rooms. There are thousands of pale grubs chewing in the rind. Each eats in privacy, in a snug wet bore exactly its own width, but the wall is continuous and carries the work of all those mouths to the square. Up on the flat you could hear the hare cropping the salt grass. Down here you could open your mouth and sing and have only the feeling in your throat to tell you that you were singing.

The peculiar thing is how long a noise takes to get out. A shower lasting a minute on the flat makes most of an afternoon's weather below. The rain itself is soon dealt with. It runs down the sloping streets, rinses the yards, brims the three little basins into one, and is drawn away into the flesh. The noise goes on falling. It moves visibly across the square: flies rise in a line ahead of it, then settle on the dry floor behind. A young lizard will sometimes dash out into this rain and stand with its eyes screwed shut, drinking nothing. The old ones know to drink before they hear it.

Some of the streets have been washed so often that their green has worn through to a lovely close yellow stuff, satin-smooth at the turns. On one such turn there is a row of rooms made for an animal that no longer lives here. Each has a low round entrance and, inside, seven shallow hollows in a ring. Small blue moths use them now to get out of the glare. Now and then, with no disturbance to the moths, the sound of seven bodies settling down to sleep comes out of a doorway. It is quite a domestic sound, a little pressing and turning and a last dissatisfied scrape. For a moment it can be distinguished perfectly well, even through the enormous din.

Toward evening the upper rim cools. Beads of water stand out on it at regular intervals, and the inhabitants climb up to lick them. This is when you see how populous the place is. They come in parallel files, padded feet and hooked feet and feet so light the soft streets have never registered them. A striped snake goes up with a bellyful of something that is still kicking. The bats shuffle sideways with their wings folded, taking the long way by the street although they could fly the distance in a breath. For a little while every wet bead has a face in it. From the flat all that shows is a wavering fringe along the lip of an otherwise unremarkable hole.

After dark the hare comes back. It likes the warm air lifting against its jaw. Far down in the square, in the dark between the streets, its two long sunlit ears are only now appearing in reflection in the water. The flies have gone to sleep. For as long as the water lasts, the ears stand there undisturbed.
