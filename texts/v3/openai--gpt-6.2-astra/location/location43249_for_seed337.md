The road comes into town at roof height. For half a mile it follows the rim of a clay pit, with sheep pasture on one side and, on the other, flat roofs so close below that a woman standing on one can reach up and take a parcel from the carrier. There is a regular stopping place for this. Beyond it the road turns down into the cut. From the bottom, all you see of the pasture is an occasional sheep's back moving along the sky.

The pit is broad, in fact a whole succession of pits joined at their ends. Good potter's clay lies under a shallow cap of gravel here. For generations people have taken the gravel for roads and the clay for pots, and built on the floors they have left behind. From the rim you can trace the progress of the digging by the color of the town: the old western quarter roofed in brown tile, the middle quarter pale with lime, and the eastern end still the blue-gray of wet clay. There the surveyor's cord stretches across what will be a street, at about the height of a man's chest, and the men are digging down to it from above. A little farther east the barley is still standing over the site of the market square.

Down below, it is cool enough to keep a jacket on until noon. There is plenty of daylight. It falls full on the roofs and the little courts where pots stand drying, and comes sideways into the rooms off walls that are whitewashed every spring. A potter can judge the thickness of a bowl by holding it up in his doorway. Hardly a scrap of ground is unoccupied. Under the stairs are bins for broken ware; above the bins a frame of laths for drying washed wool; under the lowest lath, on a peg driven into the stair post, hangs the one ladle shared by the families of that court. Even a stretch of wall apparently left bare will turn out to be the place where somebody spreads her sailcloth on a fine day to inspect it for holes.

The town is busiest on a clear morning after rain. Roof tiles are lifted to drain the laps between them. Damp jars get turned a quarter turn on their boards. Children carry the boards two at a time into the sun, walking with the peculiar stiff gait of people trying not to watch their feet. Very little of this carries any distance as sound. Feet go softly on clay; the partitions between courts are thick; a load going east on a sledge makes a long brushing noise and then is gone. From almost anywhere, though, one can hear the tap of the water warden's stick against the side of the main cistern. He listens for the change from a hollow note to a full one as he works his way down. People filling their morning jugs listen too.

There is a seam in the north cut that stays dark even in dry weather. A day's seepage there will scarcely wet a sleeve, but the seam runs for nearly the length of the town. A ledge no wider than a hand catches the water and leads it into covered settling jars. Maidenhair fern grows at a few of the joins. Nobody pulls it out: when a frond begins to tremble on a still day, water is escaping behind it and the joint needs packing. Otherwise there is little green below the rim. A fig tree in the oldest court has been trained flat along three house fronts; its handful of figs ripens in order from the sunny end. Now and then a wagtail comes down to the cistern steps, and its quick passage through so many occupied courts is noticed by dozens of people.

It is at the unfinished end that you feel most distinctly what sort of place this is. The new walls have no weather on them yet. Each spade has left its own shallow, shining hollow; cut clean across in the face are white rootlets still drawing on the field overhead. At the foot of the cut someone has set a door frame upright and braced it with poles. There is neither room nor house behind it. But the sill gives the diggers their level, and already, going to and fro with their baskets, they step over it rather than walk around. Toward evening, when the sun gets low enough to reach this end of the pit, the frame casts the first long, perfectly ordinary shadow of a house across the floor.
