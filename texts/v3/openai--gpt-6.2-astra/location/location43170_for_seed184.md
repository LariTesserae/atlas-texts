At the lowest tide the roofs come within a handspan of the air. From a boat you can see the ridges of red tile, the lead flashing on the chimneys, even a white cup caught by its handle on an aerial. Then the boat passes over the edge of the roofs and the bottom drops away six storeys. You are looking down Mill Street. Very little daylight gets to its paving stones.

There are nine houses on either side of the street, and a turning circle at the end for vehicles that have been gone a long time. The houses are joined all the way up by galleries, pipes, bundles of ceramic-sleeved cable. Some connections were put in before the water came and some considerably afterward. The newer pipes go straight through the upper panes of windows, with collars of green glass fused around them. There is no need to go down to street level to get from one side to the other. There is hardly room to do so. A long brown weed grows from the gutters down, and a weed of a rather brighter brown grows from the gutters of the floor below up, so that between them they have made a loose seam which runs the length of the block. Here and there the seam gapes. Through one gap you can see a landing with the number 4 in little blue tiles, and a mat still held to the floor by its two brass clips.

The mill is underneath all this. Its input is fresh water from a spring in the rock; its output, after a long passage through the works, is fresh water raised just far enough to clear the tide. It delivers the water into a pale tank on legs beyond the roofs. Once a week a tender comes and takes it away. The people on the tender oil the exposed bearings and clean the cup on the aerial, which turns out to be a rain gauge, though it has not been much use as one for a hundred years. They leave its handle pointing out to sea.

Below, the machinery has the deliberate movement of a person turning over in bed without wanting to wake anyone. A great plate in the turning circle lifts. The edges of the paving stones come apart along a six-sided line; the silt in the line draws itself into six fine threads. For an hour the plate rises, and the water which has passed through the driving chamber escapes through the mill doors, stirring the weed clear out to Number 1. Then the plate settles. One can put a palm flat on a door farther up the street and feel the stroke, though there is almost nothing to hear. The old rubber mountings have perished. In their place, between the iron of the works and the stone of the houses, grows a dense reddish felt, warm to the touch. It takes up nearly all the vibration. Where it has been scraped away for repairs the metal gives off a thin high note for months, until the felt grows back.

It is warmest at the bottom. The spring loses some of its heat through the lengths of pipe, and at each joint there is a small garden particular to the temperature there: waxy green disks at a hot joint, translucent ribbons at a cooler one, a little fringe like burnt toast where two lines cross. Toward the roofs everything grows flat and close, combed by the tide. Down at the turning circle the fronds keep their elaborate shapes for years. A person could tell time by the pale new margin on a single leaf. There are few grazers. Occasionally a silver eel comes down the street, negotiating the galleries by a route so precise it seems to have been given directions. For a while afterward, round windows in the brown seam show where it has been.

The stonework has worn into shapes that look softer than the growth on it. There are thumb-deep hollows under all the window ledges, each with its deposit of fine grey sand. The corners of the houses are rounded up to the height of the third floor. Above that height they are sharp: there was a long interval when the sea stood only halfway up the street. On the wall of Number 7 a painted advertisement ends at that old waterline. Below it the wall is bare honey-coloured brick; above it remain the head of a woman and the top half of a pear. In the weak light they appear equally large, equally remote. A wire from the mill runs through the stem of the pear to a lamp that burns continuously behind the woman's ear.

There are other lamps, but not many. Their purpose is to show from the surface that the stages of the pump are working. Seen from below they make separate dim rooms in the water, each full of suspended grains going their own way. The lowest lamp is amber. Under it, beside the lifting plate, someone laid a square of white bathroom tiles so that a dropped nut or washer could be found again. The square is still kept clean by the sweep of the outlet. Everything around it has thickened, furred over, joined onto something else; the doors have grown fast to their frames, and the little iron trees that once guarded the pavement have disappeared inside their own gardens. But on that one square there is space, and in the middle of it lies a single dark washer. Each time the mill breathes out, it stands up, rolls the breadth of a finger, and lies down again.
