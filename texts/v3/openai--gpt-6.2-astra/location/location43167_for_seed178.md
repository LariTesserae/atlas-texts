At low water the bay has corners. Ribs of black shale stick out through the eelgrass, each with a square end and a silver line along the top where the frost has taken. Gulls stand on the ends. Well inland of them, in a pasture, there is another rib with a wire fence running down it. The whole coast is coming up, a few fingerbreadths in a good year. A farmer can remember rowing over the place where she now cuts hay.

There would be a view for miles if there were light. As it is, the far shore shows itself in pieces: a yellow kitchen window, the foot of a headland picked out by surf, then a long low field of white that moves and turns out to be geese. For two hours around noon the clouds are thin enough to see their undersides. The channels turn pewter. You can make out the alder woods on the landward side of the bay, their crowns all combed one way, and the narrow tongues of meadow reaching out from the woods. Then the light goes out of the water and it is evening again.

The flood comes in hard. Out by the mouth it has a deep, fairly even roar, but in among the ribs it makes a racket like a quarry at full work. Plates of shore ice lift, tilt, run a little way on edge, and come down flat. Gravel travels in loads; you hear it dumped, a pause, then the next load. In the cold snaps the mud freezes around the roots of the sedges, and the incoming water gets underneath and takes up whole yards of turf. A piece the size of a door goes racing past, with the long green blades laid flat on it and a little brown vole running from end to end. Most of these rafts fetch up somewhere. There are little islands of meadow far out on the bars now, growing quite well, cropped short by the geese.

For all the cold there is hardly a bare patch ashore. Under the alder is a waist-deep tangle of currant and willow, and under that a mattress of moss so thick that the rain takes half a day to get through it. The springs come up comparatively warm. Long after the pools have skinned over, watercress is still green in the runs, and snipe are in there up to their knees, working in the dark. A fox follows the edge of the flood. It knows where the high ground narrows to a point and the mice will have to turn back. Farther out, seals wait in the lee of a single rock; every fish washed round it comes to them sideways.

There are six public paths across the bay. From a distance all you see of one is a pair of low brick porches, sometimes a mile apart. Step out of either porch and there is firm going underfoot, about the breadth of a cart. A sounding pole put down beside your boot goes straight through into the tide. Whatever the porches do, they do it just for the weight that's crossing; the water tears along underneath without so much as a standing ripple. The oldest porch has sunk crooked and has a slate wedged under its gate to keep it from swinging shut. Inside, above a bench worn smooth by wet coats, a board gives the wind speed at which the crossing closes. Firm footing is only so much use. Last winter the wind took a milk cart off the west path, milk, horse and all. They got the horse back.

As the tide nears its full height the noise moves inland. You can follow it with your eyes shut. First the stones go quiet under several feet of water; then comes the prolonged tearing sound of ice letting go in the ditches; finally, close by the houses, the clack and slam of the pasture gates as driftwood catches in them. People go out in pairs with lamps to clear the hinges. From the kitchen window their lights appear to be wandering over a single black expanse, though the two people are walking on opposite sides of a hedge and there is scarcely ankle-deep water in the lane.

By morning the bay will have emptied again. There will be weed in the fence, a new bank of gravel across somebody's spring, small flounders left in the hoofprints of cattle. Down in the eelgrass the square ends of the ribs will emerge one by one. Before the water has finished draining off the longest of them, there will be a gull on it, settling its feet.
