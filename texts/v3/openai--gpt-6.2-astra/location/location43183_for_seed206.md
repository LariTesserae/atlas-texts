At the foot of the marsh there is a willow root you could put your hand under, if you were willing to lie down in the sedge. It makes a low arch. The space beneath is about the size of a cupped palm, though it looks larger from either end, on account of the long clear view down the middle. Daylight comes in both ways. There is almost always a little breeze going through, warm enough that the wet sill of the opening smells of willow even at night.

The floor is root too. It went soft many years ago and received the marks of running water: three shallow flutes and, at their lower ends, a single round well. Since then it has grown a close green nap, so fine that an entire flute, wet, looks like one grass blade with the water lying along its crease. The sides of the well are steep. A bead striking the surface there raises a ring that travels half an inch, strikes felt, and is gone. Below the felt the whole place is alive and quite hard. There is no mud to stir up. Even the little pale pebbles under the arch turn out, on inspection, to be knobs of new root with their own translucent skins.

For so green a place it has very little in it. A sedge tip hangs in one entrance. There is room all around it. Two liverworts grow up opposite one another out of the flutes, their flat heads a considerable distance apart; water can pass between them without touching either. The central well is open to the reflected sky. You could watch it for a long time before you noticed that the brown seam just above the waterline was hundreds of animals standing shoulder to shoulder, with only the fans of their mouths in the water.

Most of the work is done overhead. Along the underside of the arch grow little clear bladders, each no bigger than a pinhead, attached by a neck of tough green tissue. They take up seepage from the wood. When a bladder is full its neck buckles, its two lips part, and it spits. There is a distinct *tock* as the lips meet again. One such bladder would be a curiosity. There are several thousand, in rows following the grain, and each row is sprung by the small shock of the row beside it. A run begins far back in the darkness with a sound like a thumbnail drawn along a comb. It comes forward gathering force until the whole roof is rattling. Seen from below, in the instant before the water falls, it is a ceiling of little white teeth.

This is the sound you hear through the ground if you rest your chin on the sedge. In the air it is sharper and has layers: the travelling clatter above, the continuous silk-tearing noise of spray on the nap, the deep knock of the few large drops that clear the flutes and land in the well. There are stretches of marsh all around in which one gnat seems to make a great deal of noise. Here a frog can be sitting right outside, its throat swelling and subsiding, and you cannot hear it at all.

In the brief interval after each run, springtails come out of slits in the felt. They graze where the spray has loosened the green surface. At the first click from the far end they fold themselves away again; a few are always late and go spinning down a flute, legs tucked in, to fetch up against the soft flank of a liverwort. Small flat snails stay out in all weathers. Each has polished an oval of darker green about the size of its foot and moves around this pasture so slowly that the edge has time to grow back behind it. The animals at the waterline never lift their heads. Food comes to them already sorted by weight, the coarsest crumbs stranded high on the felt, the finest carried round and round the well.

From outside, the arch looks worn nearly through. It has looked that way for a very long time. On its upper side the willow lays down a little wood each summer; underneath, the spray takes a little away. The flutes deepen into fresh tissue at much the rate their floors rise. A shed snail shell may lie for weeks in the dry space between them, bright enough to find again the next day. Then one morning it is gone, and one sees how small a part of the view it was. There is still the green interval between the liverworts, still the patch of ordinary daylight in the well. At its edge the water trembles a little before the ear can make out the first click.
