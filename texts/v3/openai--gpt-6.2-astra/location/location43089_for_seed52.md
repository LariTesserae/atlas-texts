From the lip of the old ferry slip you can look down on six miles of city. The drop is hardly the height of a house. There is still a red line on the slip where the ferry used to rub its paint off at high water. Below it, on what was the harbor floor thirty years ago, somebody has laid out a square for dancing. The tiles follow the ripples in the sand exactly. It takes a little practice to dance there without looking at your feet.

The harbor came up in one piece. Farther out, the bed of the strait is still coming up, a hand’s breadth or so in a wet year, much more in a dry one. Nobody has a good explanation for the difference. There are days when a new strip of land shows from end to end along the sea wall, black as the meat of a mussel, with sea anemones closing on it. Thus far the city has grown to meet each strip and no farther. From the slip you can see its newest district: three white streets, some allotments under net, the delicate blue uprights of a school, all ending at the same dark edge.

Between districts the ground is much as the sea left it. There are acres of fluted clay hard enough to walk on, interrupted by beds of shell that will slice through a shoe. The trees grow on the old oyster banks. Their roots make do with pockets of rain caught deep in the shells; above ground they have the extravagance of trees beside a great river. One bank carries a wood of copper figs nearly a mile long and scarcely wider than a tram. In the heat their leaves turn on their stems until they stand edgewise, and suddenly you can see all the way through the wood to a row of kitchen windows, a woman taking a yellow cloth off a line.

What looks from the ferry slip like the space *between* buildings is for the most part building too. The city buys distance from the strait. Out past the breakwater there are buoys, each fastened to nothing visible, and between adjacent buoys there is always a calm patch where the waves take rather too long to get across. That much length has been taken inshore. It is kept under tension in strips of cloudy glass. The housing department lays one of these across a piece of bare ground, fixes it at both ends, and has room enough along its thin edge for a block of flats. You could spend all afternoon indoors walking from door to door. Outside, you step over the block like a sill. The public entrances are unmistakable because of the prams and bicycles parked on apparently vacant clay.

It has made for an airy city, though not a particularly bright one. Most of the daylight goes indoors. It falls in good broad oblongs on the floors of the flats, and in the school the children grow melons on the landings. Out in the streets at noon the light is the brownish silver you get under a pier. A gull flying across town passes through alternating bands of sun and shade; the bright intervals on its belly are too brief to follow with the eye. There aren’t many gulls now. Those that still come follow the schoolchildren to the sea wall at lunch.

Towards evening the glass strips begin to sweat. For an hour there is a smell of warm pennies, then of crushed mint as the first drops hit the ground cover. Vines that have lain all day in slack coils on the clay draw themselves up against the damp edges of the houses. The municipal gardeners go out with blunt wooden paddles to ease them away from the doorways. It is quiet enough to hear the paddles working, district after district: a scrape and two pats, the soft tearing noise of a tendril that has got in too far. People bring stools outside to sit in the cooler air. Often a dozen neighbors will be sitting within arm’s reach along a glass strip, though their front doors are several streets apart on the inside.

Once the light comes back out of the houses there are a few minutes when the whole harbor floor is plainly visible. The little stepping terraces at the seaward edge cast long, sharp shadows. In the channels between the oyster banks there is here and there a skin of water, with a single reed standing upside down in it as precisely as a surveyor’s mark. Beyond the last white street, a gardener has gone out on the new land to fetch an anemone. She carries it back in a pudding bowl, walks the length of a district in seven strides, and tips it over the wall into the strait. From up on the slip you can’t hear the splash. You can see her wait, though, leaning on the wall with the empty bowl in her hands, until the little fist lets go and opens in the water.
