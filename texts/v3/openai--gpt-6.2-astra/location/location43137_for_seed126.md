From the lip of the basin you can read a day's weather on the floor below. A belt of white runs from the southwest nearly to your feet. Beyond it the ground is the color of the inside of a peach stone. By noon the white belt will have passed under you; by evening it will be climbing the far side, still in full sun long after the lip where you stood has gone dark. The basin is a shallow one. Its descent takes nine days on foot.

The ground is made of shutters. On the slopes they are long and narrow, laid across the grade; on the flats they are six-sided and about as broad as a hand. They fit so well that you can run a fingernail across half a dozen without feeling an edge. Each has a pale face that throws light back and a russet face that takes it in. A change of color seen close up is quite leisurely. One little piece of the floor sinks, turns over in its socket, and rises flush again. It gives a tap you can just hear through the sole of a bare foot. The brilliant front you saw coming toward you is several million such small decisions wide.

There used to be considerable relief here. The long low islands out on the floor were once the housings of machines, and the strings of yellow beads that go wavering over them are what remains of the teeth on enormous wheels. Heat and cold have worked on the housings for ages. Fine chips have come off them, slid down the slopes and been admitted through the shutters, to be sorted and pressed into new ones. There is very little difference in height now between the top of a buried engine and the apron that once surrounded its base. On one island a last tooth sticks up high enough to lean against. Its north side, under the overhang, still bears a little arrow and a number.

What looks like an immense expenditure of light serves something a long way down. At intervals there are openings through which you can look into the floor. There are other floors, edgewise to this one; there are rods as thin as hairs with stretches of blue daylight between them, although they are far below any opening to the sky. If you put your eye to a shaft in the evening you may see its depths all lit with a slanting morning sun. There are places on the other side of the world where the ground is cool because of what the shutters are doing here.

The air is warm enough that a bed on the open floor needs no cover. It smells faintly of struck stone. There is no dew. In the hours before sunrise a little gray material comes traveling at ankle height, flat streamers of it, moving in contrary directions over adjacent rows. Some is the powder of old surfaces; some is seed. The seed has two hooks and a flexible waist. It must get one hook into a seam and let the next turn of the shutter tuck the other one under. Miles of ground offer no purchase at all. Then there will be a narrow crescent, usually to the lee of some worn eminence, where the timing is right and a whole stand has come up together.

The growth is ankle brown at the base and almost transparent higher up. A stalk divides only once, into two little cups that face away from each other. One cup stays full of sun while the other is shaded; between them a bead of dark oil goes patiently up and down the fork. Toward the end of the season the cups go cloudy. They shed their hooks, and for a few days their shadows on the shutters are more substantial than the cups themselves. A six-legged grazer sometimes spends its whole life in one of these crescents. It takes the cloudy cups and leaves the clear ones. Its feet are wide soft disks; on the bright floor the six dull prints are easier to follow than the animal is to see.

For so active a country it is wonderfully quiet. There is no loose gravel to crunch, and the streamers pass without a hiss. Once in a while, lying with an ear against the ground, you can hear three or four low notes in succession as a load is handed from one buried housing to the next. Raise your head a little and you lose them. Overhead, in a sky too bright to look at for long, a second pale band travels with the one on the ground: light from below catching the undersides of the drifting powder.

At dusk the basin develops contours invisible at noon. The old wheel housings hold their heat. Along the line of each axle a thread of warm air rises, lifting the day's last seeds high enough to clear the rim. From up there the islands must briefly make sense again: the wheels in ranks, the channels between, the prodigious extent of the works. Down on the floor there is just a little warmth against one cheek as you cross an axle, then the cool on the other side, and many hours of level walking before you come to the next.
