From the middle of the town there is nothing to suggest its height except the trouble it takes to carry a sack of meal upstairs. The streets rise and fall a little. Beyond the last street the land goes on in much the same fashion, in long low swells, green on one side and the colour of an old spoon on the other. Then a cloud bank moves off the western edge and for an hour you can see, far below the level of the market square, the snowy tops of a quite respectable range of mountains.

There used to be mountains up here too. What remains of them is a grain in the country, as in a piece of wood worn smooth by handling. A rib of harder stone runs for six miles, never much higher than a doorstep. Houses back onto it; at the school it serves as one side of the smallest children's sandpit; out of town it parts two systems of drainage, so that water falling a handspan apart comes down on opposite sides of the world. The hollows are broad and shallow. In the deepest of them, which has room for a railway yard, three streets and a field of cabbages, you can still see over the roof of the station if you stand on a wagon.

The damp comes mostly uphill. Late in the afternoon the first pieces of cloud come loose from the western gulf and roll across the lip. They travel close to the ground, taking the hollows first. A person watching from an upstairs window can see a goods train come in apparently without wheels, the tops of the cabbages sticking up clear on either side of it. There is very little rain to hear on a roof. Instead there is the slow darkening of everything that faces west. By supper the wire fences are strung with drops. By morning they are dry enough to take the skin off a knuckle.

Almost everything green has found a way of keeping its share. There are low felted trees with leaves as narrow as comb teeth, and under them a second, softer growth fed by the drip, and under that a deep red moss which can be lifted at one corner like a sodden blanket. People leave it alone if they can. A patch stripped off to lay a siding exposed the print of a little split hoof in the mud beneath. Nobody in town had ever seen the animal that made it. In the lanes between houses the planting is so thick that two neighbours can shake hands across a hedge without being able to see each other. In sunshine, which is brief, the hedges let off steam.

The town earns its living partly from the cloud. Far out along the western lip stand rows of iron frames hung with fine slack chains. Water beads on the links and runs down into a covered channel. There are acres of them. In fog a man sent out to replace a length of chain finds his way by keeping one boot in the channel; the frames are numbered in raised figures at the height of his hand. The water passes beneath the town, fills the public cisterns, then goes over the eastern edge in a pipe. Well below the rim, where it has acquired a useful weight of fall, it turns the wheels that supply the plateau with electricity. The generating house is out of sight. Up here the only evidence of all that work is the lamp coming on over a butcher's block, or a long belt beginning to travel in the green gloom of the weaving shed.

It is a busy country to watch and a quiet one to listen to. A whole hillside changes colour as the comb trees turn their leaves edgewise to a drying wind. Trolleys of finished cloth go coasting down to the yard on greased rails. In the settling ponds pale fans of mineral open under the surface, branching visibly during the few minutes it takes a worker to eat a pear. Once in a while a solitary black bird walks out along a fan, so lightly that the brittle stuff holds it up. That will bring people to the windows. More commonly what brings them is a sound like someone drawing a fingernail slowly round the lip of a glass.

It comes from the old stone rib. With enough moisture in the ground it gives a note, very low at the western end, a little higher on the school grounds. There are other ribs out beyond the fields, and sometimes a note comes back from one of those. The railway used to send a man out to check the signals when this happened, until it was established that the answering sound had been heard here before there was any railway. Children put their milk teeth in cracks in the stone, on the understanding that what is left there will eventually be carried a great distance. No one can tell them they are wrong. The country has its own idea of how long an errand ought to take.

By evening the dimness has filled up with small differences: the yellow of lit stairwells, the pewter shine of bare ground, the almost blue light trapped among the wet chains. For a few minutes the air below the western lip is clearer than the air above it. From the end of an ordinary street, between a laundry with its shutters fastened and a wall furred over with pennywort, you can look down past the revolving cloud and see a river in sunlight, making a turn around a stand of trees thousands of feet below. Then the turn of the river goes out. Someone comes up the street with a pole to clear the laundry gutter. It has begun to run the wrong way again; a small, persistent wind from below is driving the water back along the eaves.
