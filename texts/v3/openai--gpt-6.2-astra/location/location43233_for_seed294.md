A fish going over the edge of the bank casts its shadow a little way ahead of it. For a few more strokes the shadow follows, down a slope the color of old teeth. Then the slope falls away so steeply that there is nothing within reach of the light. The fish turns back. The water out there tastes exactly like the water over the bank, but there is no sense swimming in it if you are a fish that eats things off the ground.

The bank stands high in a cold sea. Its top is broad enough that, swimming across it close to the bottom, you could spend a day with the impression of being in a plain. There are low swells, open courts of white grit, channels that fork and meet again. At intervals a rounded elevation rises out of the murk, with smaller ones waiting beyond it. Only at the edge does it become apparent what a height this place has attained. The flanks go down through blue, through a last uncertain violet, into water in which a white object held against the eye would be black.

All that height is shell. Even the grit in the courts, which rolls so freely and looks so much like sand, is made up of pieces of a wall, a hinge, the lip of an opening. The creatures that raised the bank were as wide as a thumb and lived with their mouths turned up. Each settled on the rim of an empty cup and made a cup of its own. In a fresh break on the flank one can see the generations laid out in long leaning rows. Some rows lean one way for the length of a valley, then bend the other way, recording a change in a current that has not flowed here for ages. There is no exposed stone. What looks like a great smooth boulder is a hundred thousand little rooms worn down to their common floor.

Hardly any of the cup makers live up here now. There is a bed of them in the lee of a rise, easy to miss until all their pale fringes draw in at once. A shrimp has passed over them. Behind the shrimp the fringes come out again, one cup at a time, like feeling returning to a lip.

Over the crest the water is in nearly constant disorder. A deep current strikes the bank and comes up its face; water chilled at the surface bears down on it. Neither gives way for long. On the plain a river of grit a handbreadth deep can be running east while, just above it, a school of little fish is being carried bodily west. White dust rises in tall folds. Where two folds meet they twist into a rope, tighten until one can see right through the clear water on either side, then come to pieces. For a moment a whole far slope shows, astonishing in its detail: three dark slots, a skate tipped up on one wing, the fine parallel scores left by something feeding. Then it is no farther possible to see the slope than to see through milk.

There is very little noise in this. A current a mile across changes direction without a sound. Close to the bottom there is the occasional dry tick of shell against shell. The loudest thing for hours may be a brittle star drawing one arm out of a hole too narrow for its spines.

The open courts appear almost empty because most of their inhabitants have a use for an underside. Small crabs travel across the ceilings of the slots. A gray fish rests upside down in the hollow of an old cup, held there by its belly fins, watching food go past below. The skate has buried itself by now; its two breathing holes open and shut in an otherwise immaculate bed of meal. When a hard wash lifts the loose pavement, the bank shows its population all at once. Hundreds of jointed legs fold in. Worms thin as hairs hang briefly in loops between the lifted flakes. Little round snails let themselves fall, each paying out a clear thread, and for a few seconds there is a snowfall that falls sideways. Nothing puts down leaves here. What feeds this multitude comes already in crumbs, washed up out of the dark or sinking from brighter water somewhere else.

By the time the grit settles, the river that carried it has gone elsewhere. There is a new bar across one of the slots. The skate's print has filled in. It would be easy to suppose that the whole bank travels like this, continually pulled down on one side and heaped up on the other. But beneath the depth to which a crab can dig, the cups are fused. Their corners have been rubbed off by so many passages of water that the water now finds very little to take hold of. The long swells remain where they were. On the highest of them is a shallow saddle through which every upwelling pours, and in the saddle, polished almost flush, is the curved seam between two ancient beds of shell. Whenever the dust clears it is there again: a fine gray line, just wide enough for a shrimp to set its feet in and make its way, against the whole weight of the passing sea, to the quiet side.
