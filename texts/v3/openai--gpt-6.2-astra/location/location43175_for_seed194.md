The first thing a finger laid on the salt would feel is a knock from underneath. Then, a little way off, two knocks close together. They come from a country small enough to cover with the palm of that hand. It lies in the lowest bed of the flat, in a split in a seed that has grown broad and thin under the weight of the salt. The hull has never opened to the sky.

Inside, there is just room between the two halves for a grain of barley to lie on its side. This is considerable headroom here. The tallest structure, the old dividing house in the eastern ward, comes barely halfway up. Most of the town is worked into the floor. Its streets are shallow grooves with a raised grain running down the middle; the houses are hollows under flakes of rind, each with a sill rubbed smooth on the side toward the street. There are hundreds of them. Nothing goes in or out of the doors anymore. In the dark, cool space above them there is no wing, no drifting spore, scarcely a mote of dust. A loose particle is a valuable thing and seldom stays loose for long.

Almost everything solid in the split is plant. The floor is the lower seed leaf, the vault the upper one. Both are alive. Even the little grit set into the faces of the old scraping tools consists of hard knots grown by the leaf itself. There is no spring under the town. A cut in the floor would yield a curl, like a shaving of cold soap, and no drop would gather along the cut. Nourishment moves through the leaf as an oil so stiff that a bead of it, delivered at the western gate when the gate still had an arch, has only lately reached the first turning. The keeper there has grown a lip around it to keep it from going astray.

The keepers are what remain of the population. Once, a keeper had a foot of folded fibre and could drag itself from one sill to the next, taking days to cross the street. It carried a scraper tucked into the fold. There are stretches of paving where the short drag marks survive, always in pairs: the foot set down, the tool set down beside it. Now each keeper has grafted to the spot where its work is needed. One is the lip at the turning. Another is a patch of roughness on a slope where a load must be slowed. At the dividing house three grow together into a fork that portions out the scant traffic in crumbs of meal. You could pass a fingertip over the fork and take it for the worn stump of a single stalk. Under pressure the three parts yield separately. They still have separate trades.

The knocking is the work of the streets. As the upper leaf contracts in the long cold, a strand stretched from vault to floor pulls tight, lifts a little wooden heel, and slips. The heel comes down on the raised grain of a street. The blow travels along it, shaking meal out of the old store pits; a second heel farther on takes up the work. Inside the hull the noise is enormous. The whole low ceiling serves as a sounding board. A single blow fills rooms a hundred doors away with a hard, close report, followed by the softer rattling of shutters in their grooves. Long after the shutters settle, meal continues to creep down the grade. It takes many blows to move enough to feed one sill.

Of the first town very little height remains. Towers wore down to bosses, steps to ramps; the edge of the eastern ward, once a wall, is a change in the feel of the floor from satin to felt. Yet the grades are better now than when they were cut. Meal has polished its own paths. Where it used to collect uselessly behind a threshold, the threshold has worn through. The only place in town still sharp is a tiny unused stair inside the dividing house. Its seven treads go up to where a second storey used to be. No one has had cause to take them out.

Between knocks there are intervals long enough for a keeper to grow a new scraping edge. In one of those intervals, now, a crumb rests at the head of the stair. It fell there from a split in the ceiling. It has an entire empty landing to itself, and below it seven abrupt descents for which nothing in the present town is quite prepared. The nearest keeper has begun, very slowly, to turn a fold of itself that way.
