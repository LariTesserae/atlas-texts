The first street is ankle-deep in warm water. It runs level for half a mile, between walls of red stone stippled with clear beads, and ends without a kerb at a drop of several thousand feet. From the end you can see the rest of the city. It occupies ledge after ledge of a great horseshoe of mountains. Roofs on the far arm of the horseshoe are blue with distance. Directly below, close enough to make out the little teeth on the fern leaves, is a square you could drop a plum into and never hear it land.

The noise is extraordinary. There is water going over every ledge. Some of the falls pour down into open country, but many go down *inside* the mountain, and the whole inhabited-looking face of it is full of the sound they make in there. Put your palm on a wall and you can distinguish three or four separate concussions where to the ear there is only one continuous bellow. In a narrow lane the two walls vibrate at slightly different rates. The sound seems to pass straight through your head on its way from one to the other. Out in the squares it is a relief to feel it merely pressing on your chest.

There are no inhabitants. Nothing comes to drink in the street; nothing flips a leaf over, working at its underside. The plum trees in the upper wards bear a small yellow fruit with a deep thumb-like indentation at one end, sweet enough when split to scent a whole courtyard. Not a wasp. On the pale silt that settles in the doorways the only marks are the exquisite branching tracks of water finding its way out again.

Yet it is a city, down to such matters as the widening of a landing where a crowd would naturally pause after a long flight of steps. It has come about through an agreement of sorts between the stone and the trees. Roots crossing a runnel collect a rust-coloured lime on their lower surfaces. Wood thickens above, stone thickens below; eventually there is a bridge with a living, leafy parapet. A curtain of roots hanging from that bridge makes a colonnade on the level underneath. There are whole districts in which the original trees have long since softened into red meal and washed away, leaving vaulted rooms, shady walks, niches of just the depth to admit a seated body. Other districts are still largely green. Their walls sweat. Their ceilings are translucent in patches, and the light passing through has the ordinary, homely quality of sunlight through a lettuce leaf.

High as it is, the place is hot. You see snow on peaks far below. Here on the upper rim the springs come out of the rock at the temperature of blood, and there are broad sunny terraces where the wet stone is too hot for bare feet. The heat brings out a peppery smell in the moss. Behind the last rank of houses is a marsh of immense fleshy reeds, bounded on one side by a low pink lip of mineral and on the other by the sky. Most of the city's water comes from this marsh. Its surface stands perfectly level with the lip. There is never a finger's breadth to spare.

It takes a long walk to notice what is wrong with the water. On a stairway in the eastern quarter a single torn reed leaf goes washing down ahead of you. You pass it at the seventh step, where it hangs on a sliver of stone. A quarter mile lower, as you come out under an arch, the same leaf goes by. By then you know it by a little strip of skin peeled back at its tip. You find it again on the far side of the horseshoe, standing upright in the marsh, attached at the torn place. The routes that water takes here are complete. Follow one far enough downhill and you will come to its source without once having climbed. The tremendous height is quite real; so is the level return. The older quarters have grown around this fact as comfortably as a village elsewhere might grow around a bend in a river.

There is consequently no season of ruin. The bridges have the rounded backs of bridges weathered for centuries, but will round no further. A plum lies open on a sill in the moist white sunshine, its two halves joined by a thread of flesh. Juice runs along the thread and falls. Somewhere above, a pale blossom takes in exactly that much sweetness. This happens without haste, whatever the water may sound like. From the last street on the rim one can look across acres of flashing roofs and green courts, see a solid white river hurl itself into the blue gulf between two neighborhoods, and pick out, at the very brink, a delicate bank of maidenhair. Every frond is bent flat by the wind of the fall. Each keeps its place well enough to cast a sharp, legible shadow on the red stone beneath.
