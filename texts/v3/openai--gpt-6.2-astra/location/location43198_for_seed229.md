The little wood grows upside down in the crown of a mountain arch. From below, the crowns of its seven trees look like seven islands of green fur stuck to the sky. There is room between any two for another two to grow. None do. The exposed stone between them is the color of a worn tooth, and has been scoured so smooth by flying grit that a seed hitting it will skate all the way out into daylight.

There is a way in along one foot of the arch. Someone has pecked holds for fingers and toes up to a shelf, then chipped the shelf back a little way to give a seated person room for both knees. From this seat the whole wood is within sight. The arch faces away from the sun. Far below it, a bright, dun country is continually appearing and disappearing under veils of dust. Up here there is just enough reflected light to see the red in the deepest green of the leaves. It is warm against the rock. It feels good to take one's hands out of the wind and sit on them.

What feeds the wood comes up through the mountain. The substance has no local name distinct from *amber*, though in the warmth of the living roots it is a clear, mobile oil. The seven trees mark the ends of seven seams. Each trunk comes straight down for a yard or so, as thick as a thigh, before dividing into hundreds of hanging switches. The leaves grow on these in double files, overlapping like the feathers of a wing. They are extraordinarily soft. A bent leaf keeps the impression of a thumb for several breaths before the little channels fill out again. In all this soft and lavish growth there is not a drop of water. Cut a twig and it smells like warmed orange peel; the cut closes with a bead you could burn in a lamp.

Almost nothing here stays still long enough to be counted except the trees. The mountain wind comes up through the arch in punches. First all the switches lift together and show their pale undersides. Then one side of a crown folds into the other with a clap that hurts the ears. Out come the animals. Little six-legged things with fans on their heels go spinning across the gap; blunt green fliers drive against the gust with their wings tucked, steering by unfolding one wing a crack; a long dun beast that seemed to be part of a trunk lets go with its forefeet and picks the air for tumbling grubs. For a moment the bare spaces are full of traffic. Then the leaves fall shut and one can see the seven separate crowns again. Something under the shelf says *ik, ik, ik*, at exactly the pitch of a chisel being struck. There are dozens of throats making that sound. One can feel it through the seat.

At the western end, where the arch is thinnest, a fork of pale wood has been wedged into a fissure. On the fork hangs a shallow wicker tray, mended in three places with bark cord. This is how the people below take their share of the wood. At intervals the trees shed the hard plugs that form over the mouths of the amber seams. Most go whistling away over the low country, but two or three in a day will strike the tray. Its sloping weave rolls them into a little pocket out of the wind. A plug is the size of a hazelnut and will light a house through supper. Nobody climbs the trees to cut it free. There are old cuts in the nearest trunk, and below each cut a whole dead length of branches, still hanging after many seasons, glossy and black as plaited hair.

Towards evening there comes a lull. It starts at the easternmost tree and travels the length of the arch: one crown after another sags, the switches disentangle, and for a few seconds the long green tips point straight down at the lost floor of the world. Then one hears the small domestic noises the wind has been covering all day. Teeth rasp a seed husk. A flier shuffles sideways to make room on a twig. In the wicker pocket one hard bead ticks against another. On the shelf there is time to change the hand one is sitting on before the first pale leaf turns over in the east.
