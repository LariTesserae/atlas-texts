The cloud lies below the shelf for most of the morning. From the upper end you can see across six or seven miles of wet, black ground to a little snow peak which appears to stand at the far edge of it. Then the cloud sinks a few hundred feet and the peak moves away. There is a whole blue gulf between, with another bank of cloud turning slowly in the bottom. A bird gliding out over the edge is in sunshine for a few seconds. Up on the shelf the light has the dull finish of pewter.

The ground is young lava. A lobe of it pushed out over the rim of an older mountain and stopped there, thick enough at its nose to have a cliff of its own. Farther back it spread thin. There are acres where the skin is still on it, a grey wrinkled skin which takes the print of the flow beneath as faithfully as the skin on scalded milk. Elsewhere the roof has fallen through. In these places you can see the thickness of the thing, the successive runs one above the other, the long dark vacancies where the liquid went on without them. Very little has been worn away. Even the loose bits of rock fit the places they broke from.

Water has yet to settle on a way down. It stands ankle deep in one fold, spills over into another, runs briskly for twenty yards and disappears with a sucking noise. Lower on the shelf a quantity of it comes out warm. The wet rock there is ochre, the colour running in long fingers through a skin of clear ice. A few yards to either side, snow squeaks dryly under the feet of the birds. The air is cold enough to freeze the down they leave on the rims of the pools.

On still days there is a steady discharge of vapour from a crack almost a mile long. Most of it escapes without a sound. At one end, though, there is a hole about the size of an eye, and from this comes a fine unwavering whistle. Put an ear near the ground anywhere along the crack and there is a very different noise: a heavy rolling knock that travels a little distance, stops, then begins farther on. Water is coming to the boil under a weight of water. Pressure lifts the column; cold water falls in behind it. All along that mile heat is being exchanged, dissolved stone laid down, tons of fluid shifted in a morning. On the surface the only thing visibly moving may be a drop trembling on a lip of yellow mineral.

It is by these warm leaks that most of the life gets a purchase. There is a short brown sedge with its roots in water and its blades flattened all one way by the wind. Between one patch and the next can be half an hour of bare going. At first the patches themselves look lifeless. Then the cloud lifts a little and midges rise in close pillars from the sedge, so precisely confined to the rising warm air that a single stalk bending out of it carries its little burden of flies into the cold, and there they stay, clinging. Small wading birds work the edges in dozens, probing under the stalks. A pool that could scarcely float a duck has four ducks on it, tipping up in turns. The bottom is soft with larvae.

By midday there are birds coming up out of the gulf in such numbers that for a while it seems there must be some great expanse of feeding ground just out of sight. But they have come for these same few strips. They settle shoulder to shoulder, dark backs to the wind. A falcon passes low and the whole population of one spring lifts, wheels once, and pours down on the next. From the upper end of the shelf this disturbance is no bigger than a handful of filings drawn across a slate. Very little sound comes back from it.

In the afternoon sleet makes the pools level with one another. For perhaps an hour there is a stream on the shelf, broad and shallow, running silver over the wrinkles in the stone. One loose flake rocks in the current until it turns over. Underneath, the stone is a handsome fresh blue-black, without so much as a grain of silt on it. By dusk the water has found the holes again. The cloud comes up over the nose of the flow; the distant peak goes first, then the birds, then the far end of the crack. Long after the rest is dark there is a little light down in the nearest pool, where the open water is still taking the colour of the sky.
