The path runs along the rolled edge of a leaf. On the right the leaf slopes gently away, scored with old watercourses, each with its little fringe of edible pink moss. On the left, over a lip worn smooth by hands and feet, there is a drop of perhaps six miles. In very clear weather you can see another path down there. Usually you see the backs of clouds, lit the color of a thumb held over a lamp.

The leaf was rolled while it was still soft. The holes for the lacing are there under the rim, oval holes with puckered margins, though the cord rotted out centuries ago. All the high country was made in this fashion. What looks from a distance like a mountain saddle turns out to be the place where two shoots were bent across each other and bound. The long escarpment beyond it is a wicker screen which took root at both ends and has been growing ever since. No one remembers what it was meant to screen. There is nothing on either side of it but warm air for a very long way.

Little of this work required lifting. You could once have made a turn in the course of a river with your finger and a thorn peg, provided you chose the right green bud. Someone did: near the eastern end of the path a stream rides up in a fluted trough, turns a neat quarter circle and goes west again. The peg is still there. It has sprouted a modest crown of its own, in which small gray apes sit washing fruit in the stream. They have worn five hollows in the rim and wash in the same five places even when they quarrel.

There is no earth to take a footprint. There is bark, and the felt that grows on bark, and drifts of pollen damp enough to cut into blocks; farther down there are the great pale stays, alive and quite smooth, with water traveling under their skins. Yet very little of the country is *there*. Between one stay and the next a storm can develop, spend its rain and come apart without wetting either. A seed with a good wing can go all its life without landing. The animals have the narrow occupied places in extraordinary numbers. Turn back a strip of loose rind and there are little long-faced bats packed together like the teeth of a comb, all looking up with bright offended eyes. Let the rind settle and the whole immense slope seems vacant again.

At this height there ought to be cold. Instead there is the close warmth of a hand just withdrawn from a glove. The wettest hours are before dawn, when the high screens comb water out of passing cloud. Drops collect on the woven crossings and creep downhill along the strands. Where a crossing has sagged, a pond forms, no wider than a sleeping mat but deep enough to hold fish. The fish are transparent except for a black crescent in each belly. A heron walks from crossing to crossing on feet so broad and light that the water hardly stirs; the crescents vanish one by one and can be seen for a while going down its neck. By noon the ponds have drained and the heron is standing in what appears to be a dry, slanting field, waiting for tomorrow.

There is scarcely any sound off the paths. On them one hears the suck of a lifted heel, or a faint *tik* transmitted a surprising distance through the wood. The people who tend the laces use that sound to find one another. They work with antler awls, split switches and their teeth. A tender will sometimes sit an afternoon with a knee against a knot, waiting for the slow knock that means the other worker has reached the far end of the splice. Across air the two could shout themselves hoarse and hear nothing. On the same length of cane each can tell when the other puts down a tool.

Their most delicate work is the letting out of distance. A tight splice holds its two ends a fixed journey apart: the walk from the fish ponds to the red screen has taken half a morning for as long as anyone has kept count. Untie it and the space the growing wood is owed comes due. Last wet season a lashing slipped and a woman walking home saw the baskets ahead of her dwindle to specks; by the time she caught up with them the fruit she was carrying had ripened to a pulp. No gap opened under her feet. There was simply more path, complete with worn places, moss and the pale marks where earlier walkers had rested their loads. Now there are families along that stretch who recall her stopping as a girl to drink at their doors. She is courteous to them, but has never gone that way again.

For this reason an old knot is left alone if at all possible. The paths buckle and acquire detours. Water cuts a channel through a doorstep; the household hangs a dipper there. In the dim long evening, when light comes up from the cloud floor and the undersides of things are briefly clearer than their tops, you can make out hundreds of abandoned routes above and below the one in use. Some have rolled themselves shut. Some have flowered along their whole length in long, flesh-colored bells. On one, far off, a flock of heavy birds is settling for the night. Their weight draws the ancient curve a little straighter. Here under your hand the rail turns almost imperceptibly, and from somewhere in the warm dark a loose end knocks twice against a stem.
