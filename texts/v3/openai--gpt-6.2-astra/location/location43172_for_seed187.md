There is a low place in the heath where you can stand and see the whole of it. It has the shape of the hollow left by a heel, though it is broad enough for a flock of geese to come down in without folding their wings. The lip on the west side is no higher than your waist. On the east side it dwindles so gradually that the only way to tell where the hollow ends is by the dry, rattling sound the grass makes up there. Down in the bottom the same grass stays green at the roots.

Nothing sticks up very far. The willows run along the ground. One of them reaches nearly from one end of the hollow to the other, rooting wherever its elbows touch, carrying its little round leaves an inch above the moss. You could pass it in six strides and come away with the impression that there were no trees here. A plover standing in the middle has a clear view in every direction. Its head, with the black band across the eyes, makes a surprisingly definite mark against all that open air.

Underfoot there is scarcely anything but plants. The grey wool on the hummocks is the bleached tips of moss; the hard brown rims that look like stone around the hollows are old willow wood, polished by grit and weather. Even the buff patches where the turf seems to have worn through are felted grass roots. There is rock underneath, close enough to keep the whole place shallow. It is an old shelf of it, scoured first by ice and then for so long by wind that there is no longer much for either to take hold of. The living cover fits it closely. A height difference of a hand is enough to change the colour from silver to dark bottle green, and that narrow change of colour follows the same wandering line year after year.

Water comes mostly sideways, in a fine cold mist off the inlet. It beads on the undersides of the bent grass blades. By noon the tops are pale and dry again, but there are two places beneath the willow where a bird can still get a drink. The light is ordinary northern daylight, good for seeing small things. Looking down at what seemed from the lip to be a uniform piece of green, you can see a copper beetle making its way along a twig, flies cleaning their faces on the warm sides of leaves, the parted stems where a vole has gone in. A pipit goes down into that inch of cover with food in its bill and disappears completely. There is a great deal of room here if you are small enough to use the underside of things.

The noise is out of all proportion to the view. Beyond the west lip, out of sight on a deeper arm of the inlet, is a yard where ships unload stone. When the wind is right, which is most days, the conveyor can be heard running, a long coarse roll with one bad bearing that sings above the rest. There will be a crash like a whole shingle beach sliding downhill as a hopper empties. Nothing in the hollow so much as starts. The plover goes on lifting one foot, waiting, setting it down. What sends it up is the much quieter whistle of another plover coming in low over the rim. Then for a minute the two birds make enough noise to carry clearly over the yard.

There is no path in from the works, no fence, not even a stake to mark the edge. The long way round by water has kept these two places quite separate except in the matter of sound. Late in the shift the belt stops. For a few seconds you can hear the tiny repeated knocks of last year’s grass blades striking this year’s; then the wind freshens and the whole silver upper surface hisses together. By then the mist is catching again on the west lip. The first drops run down the willow to precisely the places where there is still water from the morning.
