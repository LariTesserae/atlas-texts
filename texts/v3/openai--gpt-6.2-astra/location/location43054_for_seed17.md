The sea is halfway down the mountains. There are three thousand metres of water over the foot of the range and only nine over the pass. A boat going over the pass on a clear day has the disagreeable appearance, from below, of being about to scrape its keel on the sky. Its shadow goes over the white gravel, up one side of a boulder and down the other, and out over a blue drop where there is nothing to receive it.

The pass is one of hundreds. The range runs nearly from pole to pole, and for most of its length the crests are within a few fathoms of daylight. They are pale limestone split by bands of black flint. Some of the flint is so newly exposed that the bulbs where it fractured still fit the hollows in the opposite wall of a cleft. Down in the cleft lie the little chips that came out between. There is no weed to soften any of this. At noon you can see the chips lying there as plainly as loose teeth in a saucer.

It is very cold. For a little way on either side of noon the uppermost stone takes on a degree or two of warmth, and small transparent animals come out of its pores and flatten themselves against it. Each has a pink stomach and six feet ending in suckers. Through a lens the impression is of a crowd that has come out on the pavement. Without a lens the stone merely blushes, so faintly that on the first charts the patches were marked as iron stains.

The remarkable thing about the mountains is the noise they make. Far to the west, beyond the curve of the world, an unbroken swell comes in from an ocean with no land in it. The surface here scarcely lifts. Underneath, the whole top layer of the sea is driven back and forth across the passes. Water has to go through all those narrow seats in the rock. There are passes that boom, passes that whistle on the indraw and are silent on the return, and one long saddle with a loose slab across it that claps twice, waits, and claps twice again. You can put your hand down on the bottom anywhere along the range and feel the double clap. There is no point at which all the passes draw breath together. Submerged, the sound is enormous, enough to spoil a diver’s hearing. A person who raises their head above the water hears a few slaps against their hood and the ticking of the buckle under their chin.

Much of the water might as well be empty. A camera lowered off the east face once sent back fourteen hours of blue fading to black with a single white thread crossing the picture. Yet there are more animals along this ridge than anyone has been able to count. What confines them is the food. Sulphurous water seeps up along the bedding planes, and a floury bacterial paste forms where it meets the clean sea. Blind, blunt-headed fish crop the paste by running a hard lower lip along the seam. Behind them go little crabs picking spilled crumbs out of the lip marks. Long ivory rays feed on the crabs. All three keep so close to the seam that one can look down a mountainside and see a line of business in progress, sometimes for miles, with bare rock above it and bare rock below. At a fault the line ends. It begins again lower down, precisely where the displaced bed comes out.

Here and there on the passes stand the tide works. Each is an iron shed barely big enough to turn around in, bolted to a concrete footing, with an intake below and a short enamelled chimney reaching into the air. The chimney is for a man to breathe through when the shed is pumped dry. Inside, the two strokes of the sea drive the same shaft by means of opposing clutches. They used to run cable along the crest to collect the power. Most of it has been taken up. Where the clamps were, pairs of rusty circles march across the limestone and stop at the edge of space. A few sheds still work, driving nothing now but their own bilge pumps. The windows on either side are round and exactly in line. A ray passing behind a shed can be seen for a moment through two thicknesses of glass and a little room of air, its white wing crossing the oil can on the sill.

There is weather along the range, though it seldom travels. Over every deep notch a lens of cold water is shouldered up from below. On still mornings each lens has a low bank of fog directly over it. Viewed from a high boat deck these separate banks dwindle away northward like the steam of settlements spaced along a road. A fog bank may be only thirty paces wide. Go through it and there is the sun again, the hard glitter on the water and, below your elbow over the gunwale, the bright floor of a pass. The fog was there on the earliest surveys. It is still possible to steer by it.

Very little wears out. The swell is strong but wonderfully regular; it takes the loose grit as far as the end of its stroke and brings it back. In a hollow by one of the working sheds there is a spanner too heavy to be lifted by the water. Its handle moves a finger’s width each way. It has polished a fan in the stone, dark at the narrow end where the iron bears down, milk-white at the rim. This is what the bottom is like for a thousand miles: an uproar, the expenditure of power on a scale hard to credit, and room in the midst of it for a tool to lie almost exactly where a hand let it go.
