The green part is the thickness of a hand. It lies folded into a seam in the shore rock, where two beds of slate have come apart. From the beach it looks like a long turf bolster with both ends tucked in under the stone. A sheep could graze along the top of it without ever seeing the opening. To get in, you would have to lie down and go feet first, through cold grass and the wiry roots of thrift, until your heels found the ledge inside.

There is room to stand up there, but very little to stand on. The ledge goes halfway round an oval hollow, sloping and narrowing until it amounts to a grey line on the wall. Below it the rock falls sheer to a bed of chips. Nearly everything that occupies the hollow is pressed to one of its surfaces. The middle is just a few cubic yards of cold air.

The noise in that little volume is extraordinary. Ten paces away, on the other side of what looks like solid rock, the sea is breaking in a blind passage. You can't hear the wash pulling back over the shingle, or the small slap at the end of each wave. What gets through is the blow itself, a great close concussion followed by a drawn-out tearing noise as the air comes through a split. The torn sound dies so slowly that the next blow generally comes on top of it. A person on the ledge can feel a trouser leg flutter against the shin. You could put your cheek quite close to somebody else's and see them draw breath to speak and never know whether they did.

For all that fury the place is almost dry. There is one spoonful of water in a natural cup beside the entrance. The rim has a brown lip of moss which wicks the water over as fast as it gathers, so that nothing ever drips from it. In daylight the seam admits a blade of grey light. Little ferns grow there with their backs to the wall, all their fronds laid out sideways to get a share. There are liverworts as fat and glossy as if it rained every day. A finger's length farther in there is no green at all. On a freezing morning the outer grass is stiff enough to scratch your face, but the cup keeps its water under a lid of ice so thin that the moss grows through it.

Somebody once made three steps at the broad end of the ledge. They are slabs propped level with chocks, the kind of work that takes an afternoon if you know how to choose stones, and that will last as long as the hillside if nobody tries to improve it. From the lowest step you can reach a small recess. An oval beach pebble lies in it, wound about the waist with a pecked groove for a cord. A net weight, perhaps. It's a good place to put down something heavy when you need both hands to climb out. Nothing about the recess suggests that the person meant to leave it there for years.

The loudest hour is not high water but a little before, while there is still a roof of air in the sea passage. As the tide fills the passage that tearing note vanishes. For a while there is only a muffled pounding. Then you can hear the wind combing the grass across the mouth, a remarkably domestic sound, like a stiff brush going back and forth across a mat. Once in a while a wren works its way along the roots upside down, picking things out of them. Seen from the lowest step, its feet and pale belly cross the strip of daylight without a sound.

The steps don't shake loose. The ferns don't thrash. Even the loose end of a dead root hanging in the split has beaten against the same spot for so many tides that it has polished a little fan on the slate. When the water ebbs the noise comes back by degrees, and the root goes on polishing.
