Near the top of the ridge there is a fold in the rock that holds a wet meadow. Its floor is no wider than the wingspan of a large owl. In clear weather the ridge throws a shadow miles down the valley, but from the floor of the fold very little sky is visible: a crooked strip, about the colour of the underside of a mushroom. The rock lips have weathered round and nearly meet overhead. Between them grow willows so low and thick that their roots hang down in the dark among their leaves.

The water arrives through the walls. It stands on the slate in a skin just thick enough to bury the grain of the stone. Put a cheek close and there is a cellar chill to it, with a smell of bruised leaves. At the foot of the walls are little bays floored with rusty grit, and beyond the bays a single deep cushion of sedge and moss. What looks like a path across the cushion is the back of a root, black and polished. It ends in a pool the size of a palm. There are dozens of such pools. In each, a few pale blades of grass rise from a depth quite out of proportion to the opening.

At first there seem to be snails everywhere. Then the eye learns to separate the snails from the blunt, wet heads of salamanders, and these from the ends of curled brown fronds. The salamanders lie with their bellies in the flooded moss and their nostrils just clear of it. Under a single willow leaf there is a company of grey moths, fitted together so neatly that the edges of their wings reproduce the jagged edge of the leaf. A vole has its forefeet on the polished root; the darkness behind it is fur almost all the way back to the rock. Even the grit in the bays is occupied. Little black beetles hold to the undersides of the grains by means of feet finer than the hairs inside an ear.

Nothing has had to make a shelter here. Slate parts in flakes with a dry space under each upper edge. Roots take the weight of the moss and leave galleries. An old twist in a willow trunk has just the shape and depth of an owl sitting with its wings shut, and there is the owl, its face very faintly brighter than the wood. One eye is visible through the leaves. In it is the little bent line of sky.

The quiet is peculiar even for a place so far above the trees. Water makes a low *rrr* in a seam beneath the meadow, the sort of sound one usually hears for a moment after putting one's head on the ground. There is no second sound. No grit ticks loose. No small mouth breaks the surface of a pool. The water in the seam runs, but every bead on the wall stays precisely the size it is, fed at its upper edge and drained at its lower. There is a similar poise to the animals. The vole's weight is already on its forefeet, its whiskers already bent against a stem. It is not about to take a step.

All the hollow's great age is in its shape. There was water enough once to wear those deep pockets in the floor; the willows must once have been thin enough for light to reach the grass. But this is as far as the place goes. A yellow leaf caught halfway down one of the pools has a snail on its stalk and a clear bubble in the notch where the stalk joins the blade. There is just enough light to see the bubble by. There always is.
