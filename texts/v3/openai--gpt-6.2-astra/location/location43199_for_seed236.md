From the notch where you come over the ridge, you can see the whole town. It occupies a single shelf of apricot-colored stone, with the mountain at its back and very little at its front: a lip of rock as wide as a sleeping dog, then half a mile of air. The shelf tilted a little when it broke out of the mountain. Even from the notch you can tell which rooms would be pleasant to sleep in and which would have you rolling gently toward the door.

The rooms are leaves. A plant here puts up two or three thick blades, pale green with a fine red piping along the edges. For years they stand nearly upright. When a blade gets too heavy it eases over, and wherever its edge comes to rest it takes root. Underneath is a long low chamber with a ceiling like the palm of a hand. The red piping thickens into a row of little columns. More blades come up from the foot of each column. Down at the old end of the shelf there are six or seven stories of chambers, and the living tips at the top get the sun an hour before it reaches the street.

There is a street because of the animals. The broad-backed ones with the cream stripe are particularly set in their ways. They pass single file along the foot of the cliff, turning out at exactly the same spot to lie in a patch of late sun. A shoot that comes up in their path gets its tip eaten. So there is one good continuous street with a square at the end of it, though nobody ever intended either. Elsewhere a passage that looks promising is as likely as not to end in somebody's bedroom. Look in without putting your weight on the sill: there may be a dozen sleepers in there, their cream stripes aligned, and one small one laid crosswise over the others to fit.

There is no water on the shelf. The dark streaks under the overhang are deposits left by a warm breath that comes out between two beds of stone. A pinched-off leaf gives a little amber oil, scarcely enough to shine on a fingertip. It smells of orange peel and hot slate. The plants feed where the vapor issues; their roots run back nearly level through the mountain, following the seam. In full afternoon light you can see why they are so green above and so variously colored below. The blade raised to the sun is opaque. Under the arches, where a grazing muzzle has worn away the outer skin, the light travels some distance through the leaf and comes out the color of a peeled grape. There are whole interiors lit this way, with a deep plum-colored ceiling wherever a second leaf lies across the first.

At ankle height the place is busy. Flat golden animals go over the columns on feet too numerous and soft to hear. Something the size of a grain of barley sits in each fork of the red piping, facing out. There are little gray fliers that can do the length of the street without a wingbeat; you notice one by the slip of its shadow across the sunny wall. Most sounds don't carry beyond the room that makes them. Occasionally there is a dry *tick* from the roof and all the barley grains dip inside at once. A seed case has opened. After a while the little faces come out again.

The newest terrace is almost bare. Its broken corners are still so sharp that a shed skin, blown against one, hangs there split neatly in two. Beyond it the air is clear down to the distant plain, where the exposed edges of other beds draw long straight lines in the dust. Up here, in the lee of the wall, it is warm enough to lie still for hours. At the very edge of the rock one green blade has begun to lean out over nothing. It has a red rim, thin as a lip, and on its underside three of the golden animals are already going about their business upside down. For several summers yet this will be the last roof in town.
