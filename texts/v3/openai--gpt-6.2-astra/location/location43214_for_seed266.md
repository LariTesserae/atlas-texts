The first thing to disappear over the edge is the sound of your shoes. Out on the last few yards of pavement it seems quite loud, a scuff followed by a little roll of grit. Below the lip there is so much room that even a stone the size of your head goes down without any audible conclusion. You can see another road far below. Its switchbacks are broad enough to have buildings on the bends, and its outer wall casts a shadow. For a while it satisfies the eye as a bottom. Then, through one of its culverts, you see blue distance.

The inhabited country is the upper edge of an uplift, a thickness of banded rock tipped up out of the planet like the loose end of a floorboard. Going inland you can walk for months on what feels like level ground and end up considerably higher than you started. Going the other way takes an afternoon, by lift, provided your destination is on the near face. Some of the lift stations visible from the top belong to a different time zone.

It is pleasantly warm up here. In the shade of a freight shed one might want a jacket; on the sunny side it feels good to take off one's shoes and put both feet on the loading sill. The rock is pale, a close-grained buttery stuff with thin seams of black mica. There has never been rain enough to cut it. What has cut it is a persistent trade in loose particles between one side of the uplift and the other. An east wind brings hard red grains about the size of flour grains. A west wind brings back what is left of them. After a few million years of this, even the broadest buttresses have acquired waists. The old roads follow the grain of the country under long overhangs whose ceilings are as smooth as the undersides of spoons.

From high up, the city looks unfinished. There will be twelve blocks of apartments, a turning circle, a machine shop with a sawtooth roof, and then sixty miles of bare pavement before the next streetlamp. Yet the addresses run in order. Mail goes out every morning on the rim train. Most of the passengers know the long blank stretches well enough to sleep through them and wake, without a bell, just where the wheels change pitch on the approach to a station.

What determined the intervals was the air inside the rock. Deep in the uplift are broad, flattened cavities connected by pores. When the sun warms a few thousand square miles of stone, its buried air expands, and at certain openings on the cliff face there is a dependable outward breath. The engine houses stand over these openings. Their great wheels turn so slowly that it takes a chalk mark on a spoke to convince a visitor they are moving at all. Downstairs there are belts running at a useful speed, workshops stamping sheet metal and winding armatures, kitchens where pans stand on electric rings. The people in an apartment over an engine house can tell by the faint shiver of a spoon in a cup when the sun has reached the interior plain, though their own windows still look out into the shadow of the rim.

Nearly everything alive here lives on that breath. There is very little free water, and none to be found by digging. The common little animals have a clear oily fluid under their shells and, instead of mouths, paired felts with which they comb reactive dust out of the air. A handful of swept-up street grit, left undisturbed on a windowsill, will by noon have sorted itself into a stationary heap and perhaps thirty small walkers departing in as many directions. Their tracks are fine double lines. In the right light the pavements look as though somebody has been trying out pens.

The larger animals are easier to miss. Along the undersides of the overhangs hang things like old leather aprons, dun on the outside, rose-colored where an edge curls back. They spend most of their lives flattened to the warm stone. When the wind turns, one will loosen its forward edge, fill, and sail out over the drop. Then another lets go, and another a mile off, until the whole visible face is shedding these slow, soft quadrangles. They steer by drawing in a corner. Their young ride in the creases. From a lift car stopped between stations you can sometimes look straight into the pink fold of a passing animal and see half a dozen little black eyes looking back.

There are no leaves to stir, no running gutters, no continuous insect noise. The machines are well bedded and the trains are infrequent. A quiet shift at the outer workshops consists of the click of a relay, a few words carrying clearly from an open door across the square, and once in a while the dry flop of a sail animal landing badly on a roof. The roofs have low rounded parapets so the creatures can get off again. Older districts have steep parapets and a municipal employee with a padded pole.

By late afternoon the shadows of the buttresses lie across the lower air in immense parallel bars. Dust collects at their boundaries. Seen lengthwise, each bar has a shining red edge, and these edges extend far beyond the last buildings, describing the shape of the uplift where the rock itself is too distant to see. Somewhere along that rim the air is already beginning to cool. Over the next several hours the outward breath will falter, stop, and draw back in. There is a short interval every evening when the engine houses run on stored current and scarcely a grain moves on the pavement. People prop open their doors then. For ten minutes or so, even on the highest inhabited ledge in the country, you can eat outside without a cover on the butter.
