Lift the loose flake at the foot of the salt bluff and there is room underneath for a hand, palm up. The sound comes out through your cuff. It is astonishingly loud, a deep, even vowel of the sort made by blowing across the mouth of a jug, quite out of proportion to the little red chamber you can see between your fingers.

Most of the chamber is bare. Its floor has split into three low terraces, with the splinter of salt that came off each riser lying just below it. You could fit them all back on. A little light comes through the bluff itself, rose-colored where it has come through a yard of salt, nearly white along one thin ledge. Anything that sticks up casts two shadows: a short ordinary one and a long red one so faint it looks like a stain. It is warm clear down to the ends of the shadows. There is no cool underside to a stone here, and no damp.

On the middle terrace stand five thickets, far enough apart that each has a whole stretch of pink floor to itself. They have the dense, luxurious look of parsley gone to seed. Look from the side, though, and what seemed to be a thousand curled leaflets are the rims of little open mouths. Each plant is one continuous pocket of gold-green skin, elaborately tucked and fluted; there is no stalk inside. Its nourishment is a greasy yellow mineral that comes up through the salt in threads as fine as the lines on a fingertip. Where a thread breaks the surface, the plant buttons itself over the end. It can live there on a bead of oil so small it does not quite round up into a drop. The leaves have the lovely satin finish of things that have never been wet.

At first there seems to be just one animal, a fat cream-colored grub standing erect in the nearest thicket. Once the eye has learned that particular cream color, there are animals everywhere. Several dozen have fitted themselves side by side into a curl of leaf no bigger than a lentil. Others lie on their backs under the thinner leaves with their feet spread against the skin. The erect one has a ring of minute blunt teeth and is taking a bite. It is possible to see the green edge already severed between its teeth and the edge still attached, and the extraordinary smallness of the distance it has left to go.

It will not finish. Nothing down here gets any farther than it has got. This is easiest to see by the little stair on the far terrace. Someone has made it out of six flakes, notched with a tool and propped one upon the next, a great convenience for anything that wants to get over that sheer glassy lip. The seventh flake is just above its appointed place. Beneath it, in the space where it should fall, hangs a little scatter of scrapings. There is no hand. Whoever made the stair must have worked from outside, as you are doing, with only the tip of a tool in the chamber.

Yet the sound is not a memory of sound. Put your nail against the ledge and it goes all the way up the bone of your thumb. Those little feet are beating the leaves like drumheads; the folded mouths are singing; a sound hard and bright as a pebble striking crockery comes from the seventh step. Here the blow and the note it makes occupy the same instant, and the instant has room in it for the whole length of the note. From outside, with your ear to the salt, you could listen to it for an afternoon. From inside, not even the grub has had to wait.
