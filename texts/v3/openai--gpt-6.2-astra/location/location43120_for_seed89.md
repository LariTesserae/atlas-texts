Halfway along the underside of the summit, the cliff has a lip. Almost nothing of it is visible from above. A bird dropping past the edge of the ice field can fold one wing, tip in under the overhang, and disappear into a warm dark place about the size of a walnut grove. The lip is broad at either end and narrow in the middle, where the bellies of nesting birds hang out a little over the drop.

The warmth comes through a seam at the back. Deep in the mountain there is still hot rock, and water that has got down to it comes slowly back as vapor, smelling faintly of eggs. A few paces forward of the seam the floor is dry and pleasant to lie on. Farther forward still, where the outside air gets in, the roof sweats. Drops gather on the same blunt teeth of stone and fall into the same hollows they have been falling into for a great many years. Some of the hollows are deep enough to drown a chick. Each has a collar of white mineral with a low place in it where the water spills. Beyond these the floor is dusty again, right up to the edge.

The birds are small for the noise they make. They have charcoal backs and feet the color of raw ginger. Each pair keeps one of the shallow dishes that weathering has scooped in the ledge; there is no nesting material except what comes off the birds themselves. In the dark an arriving bird finds its dish by calling twice and listening for its mate. There are hundreds doing this. The calls come back off the roof with their two notes run together, and under that goes the steady baby noise, like a heap of green wood spitting on a fire. Every so often a bird lands a dish or two out of place. A short, quite distinct argument can then be heard some way down the face of the mountain.

From the entrance there seems to be no unoccupied stone. Yet between any two birds there is room for a good deal of life. Flat brown beetles go sideways under the feathers, eating shed skin. A pale cricket with ludicrously long feelers stands in each spillway and takes what comes over the sill. In the dry dust, tiny things with pincers wait under the curled edges of eggshells. Almost the only green is a strip of liverwort no wider than a thumb along one tooth of the roof. It gets perhaps twelve minutes of sun on a clear morning. When the sun reaches it, the whole wet strip shines in the black overhead, and the beads hanging off it cast little moving lights on the backs of the birds below.

Most afternoons the cloud below the lip rises to its level. For an hour there is no telling by sight which way is out. Insects come up in the cloud, wind-stunned and scarcely flying, and the birds on the outermost dishes need only lean forward to pick them off. Those farther back must leave their places. There is a slow exchange then, well-fed birds working inward on foot to relieve their mates, hungry birds shuffling toward the open air. The young stay exactly where they are. The sound hardly alters. One could spend the whole hour there and think that nothing much had happened, though nearly every adult would have changed places and thousands of insects, carried up from a valley none of the chicks will ever see, would have gone into the rock in the form of droppings.

Long after the cloud sinks again, the lip keeps its weather. The roof goes on dripping. The crickets brace their hind legs against the current, such as it is. At the narrow waist of the ledge a sleeping bird has its breast in the warm and its tail feathers stirring in air cold enough for snow.
