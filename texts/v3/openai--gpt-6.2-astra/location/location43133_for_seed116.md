The brass cap at the top of the tide gauge is just warm enough to clear itself of snow. It stands on a summit where planes pass below in clear weather. On the underside of the cap, invisible to anyone climbing the ladder, is a round window the size of a watch face. Put your cheek against the last rung and you can look up through it into the sea.

It is about as broad as a soup plate. A daylight pipe brings sun down through the post above it, so the water has the ordinary greenish light of a harbor at noon. There are shadows under ledges. There is even a long pale shoal, made of porcelain ground off the wall by the float in the days when the float still traveled its whole course. It travels scarcely a finger's breadth now. Most of the machinery of the old gauge has worn into such easy shapes that it is difficult to see what work it once did. The ratchet is a row of low, comfortable saddles. Between two teeth a translucent shrimp sits fanning eggs.

A pipe rises into this bowl from very far below. The water that comes up it is warm and tastes of the iron flanges it has passed on the way. Somewhere under the permanent cloud deck an engine is still lifting it. You hear the engine much better here than you would in the valley: the hollow post rings against your cheek and the ladder bothers your teeth. At each delivery the shoal lifts a little white dust. The shrimp all turn their heads the same way. For several seconds nothing else seems to happen, and then, right around the rim, a forest lays itself flat.

The forest grows on a single unraveling length of braided packing. Its copper strands carry little sleeves of weed, dark at the base, lettuce-green at the tips, with amber bladders that hold the leaves out in the open water. The braid has come loose in just three places. Here it hangs in deep bights, so thickly grown that a fish can go in at the top and take half a minute to work its way out below. Elsewhere the bowl is bare. A grain knocked loose from one of those green capes may roll a long way across the white floor without touching so much as a hair.

Much of the life here is visible only when the water stops pushing. Then the leaves stand up and the animals come out to mend things. A little brown snail rasps a clear lane down the glass. Worms with red combs for heads lean from clay tubes and catch the dust settling out of the water; what they cannot eat they use to build another course of wall. There are flat silver hunters that wait under the float for the shadows of smaller fish to cross the porcelain. Seen from the window they are almost perfectly concealed, except for the movement of swallowed prey down their bright sides. Now and then one strikes the glass in pursuit of its own underside. The sound, in that great iron resonator, is like a man dropping a spanner four rungs up.

Only a bead of air remains in the whole chamber. It runs in a groove in the roof, out of sight until the post sways in the high wind. Then it comes down the curve of the cap like a quicksilver oval, taking the surface of the sea with it. A colony of minute black beetles goes wherever it goes. They hang by their feet from that traveling shore. For them a gale means a journey into a fresh bed of leaves; for the shrimp below it means a brief immense patch of brightness running over the eggs.

There must have been hundreds of gauges once. The number on the cap is seven hundred and something, the last figure rubbed away by weather. This one keeps taking its readings. Every evening, as the metal cools and contracts, the float touches a contact polished clean by generations of grazing mouths. Far down the post a bell sounds once. At that note the fish abandon the shoal for the copper woods. They do it even on midsummer evenings when the sun is still pouring down the daylight pipe and every grain on the bottom can be counted. A little later the return valve opens. Water slips out through the saddles of the ratchet, carrying the day's loose porcelain, and leaves behind, on the sheltered side of each tooth, a fresh crescent of fine white beach.
