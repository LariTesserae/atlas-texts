The town is laid out around a hole in the mountain. From the roof of the washhouse you can look across and see a woman in the dye yard wringing out a sleeve; to get there on foot takes the better part of an hour. The street goes round the rim in a long, nearly level curl. There was a bridge once. The two handsome stone approaches are still there, with little booths where the bridge keepers used to sell hot pears. Beyond each booth the paving ends in daylight.

It is a small place, scarcely bigger across than the shadow of a cloud. Most of what lies within its boundary is air. Swifts come up through it in late summer, one or two at a time, dark against the white walls of the shaft, then silver for an instant as they cross the level of the street. In clear weather you can look a long way down without seeing anything that would give you an idea of the depth. A dropped pear makes no report that anyone has heard.

There is plenty else to hear. At that height the wind hardly ever leaves off pressing against the mountain, and somewhere below the town the shaft narrows to a throat. On the west side you hear a note like the sound you get blowing over a jug. At the washhouse it is a great loose clattering, as though a cartload of crockery were coming up the shaft and never quite arriving. Conversations are conducted very close together. The potter and her son work on opposite sides of a table with their toes touching under it; she has only to lift her big toe and he turns the board a quarter turn. Indoors, behind thick felt hung in the doorways, the noise is lower but more intimate. It sets the water trembling in the cups.

Everything that grows here grows on the outside of the curl. Low pear trees are trained flat against sun-warmed walls. Ferns come up waist-high in the lanes between houses, and there are stairways so deep in soft green growth that people find the steps by the worn handrail. Water stands just under the skin of the ground. Kneel in a garden to tie up a branch and for a moment the earth looks perfectly firm; then two dark patches spread around your knees. It is cold water. Even on a bright afternoon, with the stone sills full of drying socks and the pears smelling sweet in their little paper hoods, it leaves a deep ache in the fingers.

On the inside of the curl almost nothing grows. That is where the mountain is being made. A clear solution sweats from the lip and runs a few handbreadths down the wall before the wind takes the water out of it. What stays behind is stone, pale buff when wet, white as an eggshell when dry. It forms in fine upright blades. The oldest of the bridge booths has a window looking straight down, and a child can lie on the floor and see blades there thinner than the edge of a leaf, with not a chip out of them. There are no ravines, no heaps of fallen rubble. Give a rock long enough here and instead of wearing hollow it puts on another coat. The old bridge did not fall. Its piers grew thick and heavy beyond what the timbers could carry, and the townspeople took it apart while it was still safe to walk on. Its beams are the lintels of the public baths now. From the hot pool you can see, overhead, the square holes for the pegs that once held the parapet.

Keeping a town in such a place is mostly a matter of keeping openings open. Each ward has a crew with mallets and narrow chisels to clear the mouths of drains. They cut out the new stone in curved pieces, still soft enough to scratch with a nail, and set the pieces on racks to harden. Almost every house has a roof tiled with the former insides of pipes. When rain comes the roofs darken in bands, cream and honey and pink, according to which spring the tiles came from. Then the rain passes on below the town. For a while the whole curl sits in plain cool sunshine, with a storm going on somewhere under its floor.

Near the pear booths there is a brass stud set into the pavement. It was put there when the bridge was built, one exact arm's length from the brink. These days there is room between the stud and the drop for a bench, a trough of water, and a narrow bed of chives. The bench faces inward. People sit there to peel fruit or mend a cuff, seldom looking down; there is usually something to see on the other side, a sheet coming off a line, a friend stepping out into the light. Under their heels the white lip goes on thickening, too slowly to watch. Out at its newest edge there is not yet a place for so much as a seed to lodge.
