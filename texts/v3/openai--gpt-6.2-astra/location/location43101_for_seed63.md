The way in is through a notch near the summit. On the outer side of the notch the wind is hard enough to roll pebbles uphill. Six paces in, a pinch of dust dropped from the hand falls straight down. The roof here is a single tilted slab, propped on the lip of an old volcanic throat. Beneath it lies a room rather longer than a threshing floor, hot at one end, hotter at the other. There is just enough light to see the white rim of the step by which one enters. The next white rim, which looks very like another step, draws back at the touch of a foot.

It is the lip of an animal. There are thousands of them, fitted edge to edge over the floor. In the dark they feel like shallow earthenware dishes, warm and slightly rough on the outside, with a rolled edge as smooth as a tooth. Each has a soft underside which it sets down over a crack in the rock. A bitter gas comes up through the crack; quite a different air works in from the notch. The dish makes its living where the two meet. When lifted, it folds almost in half, and the naked place it leaves smells for an instant like a freshly struck match.

There is no water running here, nor any mark of its having run. What looks at first like a system of little watercourses in the floor is a record of feet and lips and patient bellies. The dishes move. A crack clogs with yellow meal; another opens a hair's breadth farther down the slope; over the course of a hot season a whole bed travels the length of an arm. The rock under the oldest beds is polished into long flutes. Stray grains blown in from the summit do the cutting, carried under the animals like grit under a thumb. In places the flutes go up a wall.

The great crowd is on the dishes themselves. Little six-legged things graze the meal that cakes on the rims. They are fat behind and very narrow in front, so that several can feed from the same nick, their rumps sticking out like the cloves of a bulb. Flat hunters pass under them. The hunters have fringes so long and fine that a grazer can be halfway onto a hunter's back before it comes within reach of the mouth. Smaller creatures still live in those fringes. Shake one over a pale stone and the dust that falls from it sorts itself by size and runs in three directions. Even on the underside of the roof, beyond any easy reach of the gas, the stone is upholstered with sleepers hanging by their hind hooks, each with its face tucked into the hollow under the one above. A dead sleeper stays in place. By morning there will be eggs in the clean case of its back.

All this goes on with very little noise. Now and then a dish settles badly and rocks against its neighbour: two small knocks, widely spaced, as of someone testing a melon. The loudest sound is at the entrance, where the wind on the far side of the stone can be heard drawing breath through its teeth. It is possible to sit on the step for a long time believing that nothing much is happening. Then the wind drops, the pale thread of daylight overhead wanders a little in the rising heat, and one sees that the entire pattern of backs below it is different.

At the hotter end stand three short walls of loosely laid stone. Whoever laid them made room enough between two of them for a person to lie down, though it is difficult to imagine wanting to lie there now. The upper stones have slipped. The bottom courses are still square, and are useful to the animals in a way that the builders can hardly have intended. When the sun leaves the outer face of the mountain, cooling air pours in low through the notch and divides against these walls. A still pocket forms behind each one. Into these pockets come the young dishes, which for the first few days of their lives are light enough to blow away.

They arrive upside down. In the faint backlight from the entrance one can sometimes see a dozen at once go skidding by on their little convex shells, legs gathered to their bellies. They collect behind the walls in drifts. For a while there is the dry, almost pleasant rustle of their efforts to turn over. The first to succeed set off toward the smell of an unoccupied seam. Others climb onto their sisters to get the purchase they need, and so the drift unpacks from the edges inward until only a few late ones are left spinning on the bare stone.

Outside, by then, the summit casts a shadow miles long across a country of bare ridges. Inside, the change from day to night amounts to the loss of that one white line on the step. The floor stays hot. In the space freed by a hunter's passing, two grazers ease themselves apart to admit a third.
