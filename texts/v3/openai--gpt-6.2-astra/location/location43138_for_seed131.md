At the foot of the condenser house there is a square of wired glass flush with the pavement. Kneel there in daylight and put a hand up to keep your reflection off it. A little way below your face is what looks like the green shore of a pond. The shore curves out of sight at either end. The whole pond, ends included, would fit in a kitchen sink.

It is the old catch box beneath a pipe elbow. It has a floor of cast iron, four tiled walls, and a waste pipe set low in one corner. The water stands just deep enough to cover the heads of the floor bolts. Close to the glass the light is ordinary pavement light; down at the bolts it is amber from the iron. There is very little in the middle. A flake of white enamel that came off the elbow years ago lies on the bottom with a clear space of a hand’s breadth on every side of it. Light passing through the wire in the glass lays a faint square across it.

The shore is made of washers. Whoever changed the gasket used to let the old washers fall, and they rolled down the slight pitch of the floor until they fetched up in the same two corners. Rust has joined their rims. Lime has filled the steps between them, building one low bank with a long shallow saddle in it. Along the wet edge the lime is polished almost to the feel of soap. There was once lettering cast into the floor here. All that remains of it is the bowl of a P, a little round well in which the green is particularly dark.

What looks from above like a continuous sward comes apart, up close, into liverwort and two kinds of moss. The liverwort grows flat in the saddle where it is always well rinsed. The moss makes fat cushions on the higher washer rims; each has a fine bright halo of new leaves and a belly full of warm brown water. A single fern has got a root into the grout. Its longest frond reaches well away from the bank, and beneath it there is an astonishing amount of room: bare tile, clear air, the fern’s shadow, then bare tile again. Occasionally a pale springtail comes out along the underside of the frond. It can go to the very tip without meeting anything.

The sound is out of all proportion. The elbow carries the return flow from the condenser, and one of its feet is bolted straight through the wall of the box. Every stroke of the circulation pump is in there. Heard standing up, it is a tolerable whir among the other works noises. With an ear on the iron rim of the glass it is a solid, hammering roar. The ear on the rim cannot hear the hand tapping the glass beside it. Yet the fern hardly stirs. The only visible effect is along the waterline, where the reflection of each white tile quivers into a comb of very fine teeth.

The pipe sweats in all seasons. Water gathers underneath it, travels down the bolted foot, and comes out among the moss without ever making a drop. What comes in goes out over the lip of the waste. There is a brown line on the tiles so narrow it might have been ruled there in pencil: the high water and the low water of as many years as anyone has worked here. On the old drain drawing the box is shown empty, a rectangle with an arrow through it. The arrow still gives the course of the water exactly. It goes along the foot of the green bank, past the white flake, and away through a notch worn smooth enough that nothing passing over it makes a ripple.
