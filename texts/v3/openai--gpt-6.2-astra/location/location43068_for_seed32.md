To see the court you have to lie down in the colonnade and look through the chip at the foot of the last pillar. Both your eyes won't fit. With one eye to the chip you see what looks like a considerable distance: a white pavement, low walls running across it, a far wall too bright to look at comfortably. Then a grain of red grog in the nearest paving slab gives the scale away. The whole court would fit under your hand.

It lies below the level of the colonnade, between the runner on which the pillar sits and the lining of an air passage. Whoever bedded the runner in plaster laid it down on a close combing of ridges. Most of the ridges flattened out and joined. Here, where the underside of the runner had a hollow in it, they kept their shape. They are the low walls. Between them you can see the square impressions of the cloth used to press the plaster smooth. These are the paving slabs. There isn't a stone in the place. Even the loose red grain is a bit of one fired brick ground up to make another.

A gap no thicker than a thumbnail separates the crests of the ridges from the roof. It lets the light all the way in. Outside, the colonnade faces a high wall finished in white lime; whatever part of the sky is bright, that wall gives back much the same glare. Inside, light strikes the first plaster crest, fills its fluted side, strikes the next. In the last furrow it has the soft, penetrating whiteness of flour. You expect a little warmth from so much light. The pillar against your cheek is cool.

At the ends of three furrows there is a growth like green felt cut very short with scissors. Its color is all on the tips. Along one edge, where the felt turns up, you see that the underside is the same grey as the plaster; it has taken a skin of the floor up with it. Nothing grazes it. Nothing has crossed the little stretch of white before it and left a track. Down there it is dry enough that a single shed filament, pale green and bent in two places, holds its shape from end to end. The filament lies athwart the farthest lane like a fallen branch.

The sound is enormous. You hear very little of it standing up. At the chip it is a full open *ah*, with a hard buzzing note lodged in it. The air passage beyond the lining runs the length of the building; the draft that keeps the rooms above sweet is sounding against a lip of tile. Put your ear where your eye was and you can pick out a lower note, transmitted through the runner itself, that you feel in the small bones around your ear. Yet there is no wind in the court. The filament does not stir. It is strange to listen awhile and then look in again and find that shining, heavily scored surface intact. By ear alone you would have said it was being milled to powder.

There is hardly any unclaimed room. Where two ridges shoulder together, brick meal fills the angle; where the floor cups a little, the felt has spread to the very rim of the cup. Even the clear lanes are only clear in the middle. Along their edges the plaster has fretted into little beads which lie caught exactly in the hollows of the cloth print. Long ago there must have been a time when they could roll. Now each has a dull side turned to the light and a polished side fitted to its seat.

Someone has rubbed the outside of the chip quite smooth, perhaps trying to get a better view. The smoothing ends just within it. Beyond the reach of a fingertip the first ridge still bears, wonderfully large and distinct, the whorl of the thumb that made it.
