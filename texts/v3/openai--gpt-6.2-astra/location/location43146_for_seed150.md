From below, the town is a dark seam between two bands of snow. It occupies a shelf halfway up a cirque wall, a little wider at one end than the other, just where the mountain goats used to turn off the scree. A slab came away from the wall two winters ago. One end of the shelf still has the old rounded lip, deep in heather; the other ends in a clean gray break. Roots stick out of the break in storeys. Some still hold stones in the shapes of their forks. The finest have already put on white growing tips and turned down.

The town is made of willow. There may be fifty willows or only one; no trunk stands clear enough to count. They grow sideways here. A stem as thick as a thigh lies in a bed of its own fallen leaves, puts up a spray of twigs, and, a little farther on, puts down another root. Snow presses the spray over into the next one. In this way the roofs get thicker each winter while the ceilings stay about knee-high. Underneath are chambers with smooth earthen floors, joined by low passages. Above, there is hardly space to lay a palm without folding a leaf. The leaves are very small, blue on top and woolly underneath. When wind comes up the wall the whole upper town turns the color of a hare’s belly, beginning at the broken end.

What gives it the look of a settlement is the water. It comes out of the cliff in half a dozen places, and everything grows in accordance with where it has to go. There is a main street of black, wet moss along the foot of the wall. Shorter streets run off it toward the brink. At each crossing the willows have rooted on both banks, leaving the middle open under a low arch; in the slack water behind the roots, sedges make dense little square plots. None of the streets is broad enough to walk. Yet from a boulder above, you can pick out the quarters of the place, the covered ways, even a pale, rather handsome square where a sheet of ice has lifted the moss off its bed. There is no stump, cut mark, peg or fastening anywhere on the shelf.

Very little sun gets in. For a few minutes in the afternoon a reflection off the opposite snowfield reaches the undersides of the roofs. It is then possible to see how much is growing down there: clear green cups in the moss, red hairs along a root, tiny folded leaves coming straight out of bark so old it has gone silver. The air smells of wet wood. Even in summer there is ice in the back chambers. Water runs over it in a skin so thin that the only sign of movement is a grain of pollen travelling slowly across what looks like a solid floor.

The place has long quiet intervals. A drop falls onto a leaf and stays there. Then several drops find the same leaf, the stem gives, and there is a short run of ticking sounds all the way down through the roofs. Sometimes a wren goes in at the broken edge. For a while its whereabouts can be followed by the separate patches of leaves that shake. It comes out surprisingly far away, on the rock side, and the shelf settles behind it.

Toward evening the seep at the upper end begins to freeze first. Its little street goes dull; water wells up behind the blockage and takes the next passage instead. By dark a quarter that was dry all day is full of the sound of dripping, and another has fallen still. At the fresh break, the last overflow gathers on the exposed roots. It freezes there in clear beads, each with a white tip visible inside it. Beyond them there is nothing for a long way down.
