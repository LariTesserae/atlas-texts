There is a country under the sill of the freight scale. You can see into it by lying on the loading floor with one ear against the boards. The sill is a little bowed. At either end it sits flush on the stone, but in the middle there is clearance enough for a small animal to stand on another animal's back, which a great many of them do. Daylight comes through the slots in the iron tread and falls in plain white bars across the ground. In that good light the first impression is of a gravel yard in which every stone has somewhere to go.

The ground itself is older than the scale, older than the rock from which the scale house was cut. It is the exposed back of a bed of grey quartzite worn almost level before the next bed was laid on it. A few harder grains stand proud. The hollows between them hold a soft black meal of graphite, husks, iron filings and dung. No mud has ever set here. A track stays sharp until something crosses it, which is seldom more than a second. The highest point, a brass screw head toward the west end, has been polished by passing bellies until the slot has all but disappeared.

Most of the traffic is the little grey stock the weighmen call beans. Six legs tuck so neatly under a bean that, working its way uphill over its fellows, it seems to roll by a series of changes of mind. They eat the bearing grease. Grease comes down an old wick at the east abutment, nowhere near fast enough to drip; the beans rasp it out from between the cotton fibres and leave them white. Well-fed beans have a dark saddle visible through the shell. They travel out into the cold in this condition and come back pale. Along the way are animals that comb their legs for crumbs, animals that puncture the saddle, minute riders that eat the puncturers' eggs. A dead bean seldom reaches the ground whole. Its empty shell does, and rolls wonderfully far in a draught, taking the legs out from under the live ones.

It is cool even at noon. The stone draws the warmth from a hand laid flat on the loading floor. There is nothing green, no damp seam, nothing that has to lift a leaf into those handsome bars of sun. The breeding chambers are almost offensively clean: a row of eggs gummed inside a shed shell, the shell wedged lip down in a scratch in the rock. What the young need from the world is enclosed with them. The first meal is the gum.

When a truck wheel takes the tread, the sky comes down a little. Not far. The scale has stops. But before the iron touches them it squeezes a flat wind through the country, and everything that can grip does so. Everything else goes west. There are pale long-jawed creatures that spend their adult lives facing east behind the screw head, forelegs spread, catching what blows by. They have no eyes. On quiet Sundays they can be seen slowly feeling up and down the sheltered side of the brass, as though there might be some adjustment they could make.

What sounds from upstairs like one substantial clank is quite otherwise down here. First the wick bracket sings. Then comes the prolonged scramble of claws finding purchase, a noise like coarse cloth being torn, then a terrific double knock with a distinct silence in it. In the silence the suspended grit hangs bright enough for each grain to cast its own shadow. After the second knock the light bars widen again. The beans let go by degrees. For a while all movement is across the prevailing direction of traffic: the overturned getting upright, the robbed looking for grease, mothers hauling displaced shells back to the particular scratches that fit them.

There are only a few works of metal in all that inhabited space. Besides the screw and the bracket there is a bent piece of copper wire which some repairman pushed through the slot and lost. Beneath its low arch a narrow road has been kept bare clear down to the old stone. Even when the floor on either side is three animals deep, the road stays open. Sit through a shift change, with the scale at rest and boot heels going away across the boards, and you can see why. Out from the hollow copper bend comes a single jointed feeler. It sweeps the road from verge to verge, gathering up the dust and everything small enough to go with it. Then it withdraws, quite without haste. The beans crowd up to the edge again.
