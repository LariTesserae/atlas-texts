At street level there is a low tiled roof with twelve square holes in it. People walking home from market rest their baskets on the ridge and get a warm wind up their sleeves. The street goes around the roof. The stairs go down beside it, turn under the eaves, and come out halfway up the wall of a sunken wash court. From the landing you can look straight into a pigeon nest built in the handle of a broken jar.

There is room below for six washing benches, though there are nine. To get around the last three you have to step on the ends of them. Nobody minds. The ends are bare of linen and worn down in the middle to little saddles. Above them, poles run from wall to wall at several heights, hung with shirts, swaddling squares, long aprons stiff with paste. In the morning the light comes in through the roof holes in squares about the size of a folded sheet. A white apron can light the whole underside of a bench. Hang a brown blanket in its place and the crickets under there begin to sing.

Everything down to the bed of the drain has passed through somebody's hands. The piers are brick; the basins are made in sections of fired clay, stitched at the joints with little lead cramps. Even the hump which looks so much like a long-buried boulder under the lowest step is the shoulder of an old oil jar. It was set there to carry the stair when the court was first dug. The tread broke over it years ago, and now the jar is the tread. It has a lovely sure grip under a wet sole, all those fine parallel ridges left by the potter's fingers.

Only one bench at a time gets water. A woman at the head of the court lifts a wooden peg from a trough, counts out a basinful, and puts the peg back. For a moment there is a quick, broad, slapping sound, as if something very large has landed flat on the tiles. Then the little runnels empty. For much of the day the floor is quite dry except under the benches, where a lost button lies magnified in a teaspoon of water. Washing here is mostly beating and turning, beating and turning. Nine paddles going at once make a noise that can be felt in the landing rail. Instructions are given by pointing with the chin. Babies taken home from the court notoriously cannot sleep in a quiet house.

Heat comes through the back wall from the bread ovens in the next cellar. By afternoon pigeons have loosened their wings away from their bodies. Geckos come out along the courses of brick, where each has a narrow level road and need never cross another's path unless it wants to. There are also sparrows, flour moths from the bakery, a colony of handsome black beetles under the peg trough, and one remarkably fat toad. Nobody knows how the toad got down the stairs. At the first slap of a filling basin it backs into the hollow under a footrest; the wash drives small insects out of the floor joints and practically into its mouth. Of green things there is very little: a pad of liverwort on a leaking joint and a fig seedling high up in a pigeon pot, still living on what the pigeons have brought it.

When the beating stops, the court takes a while to grow quiet. Someone wrings out a twist of blue cloth; somebody else hauls on a pole rope that squeals in its wooden eye. Water goes on ticking from a hem into a glazed bowl long after the people who hung it have climbed to the street. Toward evening the last square of sun reaches the far wall. There, beneath layers of limewash rubbed thin by leaning backs, you can make out the red outline of a much smaller basin and the holes for a bench at the height of your knee. The floor has been tiled over so many times that what used to be a comfortable working height is now just right for a child to sit on. A child usually does, heels knocking the wall, waiting for the blue cloth to come down so he can help his mother fold it without letting either end touch the ground.
