There is a street on the harbour plan that is mostly water. It runs for about sixty paces under the cold store, between two rows of concrete piers, and ends at a screen of upright iron bars. A kerb runs the whole way down the left side. On the kerb are three enamel door numbers, legible from a boat, though all three doors have been bricked up. The address still in use is Number 8, Pump Room. Its door is halfway up a flight of yellow steps.

The street was cut through new harbour fill. You can see the stuff in section below the kerb: fist-sized pieces of blue quarry stone with edges sharp enough to skin a knee, and among them, here and there, half a white tile. Where the drain from the ice plant comes in, the issuing water has washed the grit out from between the stones. It makes a little clear bay with a bottom of clean sand. A shoal of fingerlings hangs there, every head pointed at the mouth of the pipe. They seem to be making no effort at all until a bit of thread goes by and shows how fast the water is running.

It is hot enough below the cold store that the yellow handrail stays warm even where it is wet. Overhead, the pipes carrying cold brine have thick jackets of cork, tarred and bound with wire. At each valve there is a bare inch of metal. Water gathers there and falls, always on the same spots. One drop strikes the lid of a switch box with a surprisingly good bell note, quite distinct through the heavy running sound of the pumps. Further in, the drops fall into water and make no sound you can pick out. There is daylight at either end of the street, a broad greenish light at the bars and a hard white rectangle over the steps. In the middle you find your way by the little red lamps on the pump-room wall, doubled beneath you.

There is very little growing here. One joint in the steps holds a tuft of grass, flattened sideways by the hose the attendant drags across it. The stone at the open end is green to the depth of a hand below water. Yet almost anywhere you stop and look, something is feeding. Small translucent prawns pick over a drowned moth. Crabs no bigger than shirt buttons work sideways along the wet mortar with one claw held to the mouth. High on a cable tray a night heron stands well out of reach of the drops. It has a long wait between fish, but need never go far for one. There are pale fans of old scales on the tray beside its feet.

Outside the bars the tide lifts coal lighters several feet up and lets them down again. Here the level scarcely varies. The harbour is on the far side of a sill; the pumps take the drainage of six blocks and deliver it over that sill, and the water left in the street stands at the lip of the intake. There is a dark pencil line on the lowest yellow riser, put there by somebody checking the level years ago. The surface trembles against it. Even when the two pump motors exchange duty, the change amounts to a moment of rougher vibration under the soles, then the return of the same deep note, now coming through the pier on the right instead of the left.

From above, this whole place occupies the space between the receiving bay and the office where the drivers sign for their loads. A man crossing it can hear, through one square of iron grating, a rush like a downpour, and two strides later hear only trucks again. Below, the square is a patch of pearly light with the occasional shoe darkening it. The heron does not look up.
