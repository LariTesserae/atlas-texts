Behind the summit lift’s brass load plate there is a shallow country, two handspans wide. Its floor is the upper face of a porcelain insulator. The porcelain was stamped with three terraces to make a longer journey for any stray current trying to cross it. Seen edge-on, these are considerable uplands. A few stairs in the middle lead nowhere: the die slipped when the clay was soft, and for the width of a thumbnail there are two impressions of the same ascent.

Daylight gets in through the little window where the load limit used to be. It is good, ordinary light. You can tell cream from white in it, and see which of the copper filings are copper all the way through and which are merely copper-plated steel. The window looks onto the bright side of a cloud bank, with nothing higher than the lift house to cast a shadow. There is enough sky in this place to make its smallness rather difficult to judge.

The terraces end in a cut bank of greenish glass. Beyond that lies the flat back of the load plate, scored in reverse with the figure 8. Glass dust has collected in the two bowls of the numeral, but almost nowhere else. Along the bank the edges are as sharp as on the morning the fitter snapped off the excess glass with pliers. At one point the two sides of a break stand a hair apart. Every tooth on one side still has its socket opposite.

It is cold enough here that the grease on the window catch has kept the whorls of the fitter’s thumb. There is no frost. The compartment was filled with dry insulating gas and pinched shut through a little tin nipple, now a landmark on the highest terrace. Nothing has ever run downhill. On the flats that look most like old lake beds, inspection under a lens reveals the fine parallel strokes of a polishing wheel.

There are four small growths on the porcelain. They were printed there as dots of green varnish to show where the field was strongest. Over the years the varnish sent out stiff, divided leaves, feeding on charge leaking through its roots of powdered tin. The largest is low enough to pass under a fingernail without touching it. Each leaf grew until its tip discharged exactly what its stem could draw, and there it stopped. There are wide stretches where the unbroken glaze offers no purchase. Nothing grazes the leaves or shelters beneath them. Their undersides are as clean and green as their tops.

The astonishing thing is the noise. The lift has not moved in years, but its holding coil is never switched off. Its hum comes up through the porcelain with the force of a factory floor, meets the brass overhead, and comes back down. In the lower bowl of the 8 it would be impossible to hear someone shouting from the upper bowl. Yet a flake of plating finer than an eyelash stands on end between them. It occupies a seam where the two vibrations cancel. The leaves grow in such seams too. What looks at first like a scattering of unrelated things is a map of the few places quiet enough for anything to stand.

Outside, the lift house has doors, a clock, a button to call the car. In here the current holds, the cloud gives light, the green tips spend exactly what they receive. On the middle terrace the second little staircase remains beside the first, down to the last sharp tread, as though the press had lifted only a moment ago and the clay had yet to decide which impression to keep.
