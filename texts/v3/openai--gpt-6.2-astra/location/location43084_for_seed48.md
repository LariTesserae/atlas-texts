The street is easiest to see just after the rain. It runs straight across half a mile of shallow water, two parallel curbs with the crowns of the cobbles showing between them. At the far end it climbs out beside a pump house. There is a good stone doorstep there, worn hollow on the right, and three feet of water on the side the door used to open onto. In an hour the tide will cover the street again.

This was a salt town. From the pump house roof you can still make out its wards: the long narrow rectangles where the workers lived, the square of the covered market, the much larger squares of the settling pans. The houses were worth taking down when people left. The footings weren't. Most of what remains above waist height belongs to the salt works, and most of that is still in use. A pipe on rusted trestles crosses the old market at a slant, taking brine to a bank of evaporators too far away to see in this weather. Every so often a drop falls from a joint. Under each bad joint there is a little white collar on the mud.

It's hot even in the rain. The rail on the pump house steps is warm on the underside where the water hasn't touched it. Low cloud has sat over the coast all afternoon; there's enough light to see for miles and hardly enough to tell one colour from another. Out towards the sea a maintenance lorry is creeping along a bund with its headlights on. For a long time it seems to make no progress. Then it reaches a sluice and its reflection drops a whole lorry length into the lower water on the other side.

Nothing much takes root here. On the high ground the salt comes up through the soil in a crust that breaks like the top of a pie. Lower down, the bottom of each pan is lined with slick brown film. Tip a cup into what looks like clear water and the cup is suddenly full of legs. Brine shrimp hang on their backs in it, working away with all their limbs. In the less salty channels nearer the sea there are mullet, packed so closely under the lip of an outfall that their backs have rubbed a bright patch on the pipe. The channels drain at different rates. The fish know which ones to leave first.

So do the birds. What looked from the steps like a pale deposit along a distant curb lifts a few inches and settles again: several thousand little sandpipers shifting to make room for the incoming water. Close up, the exposed mud is busy enough to give you vertigo. Small crabs are bringing pellets up out of their holes. Flies stand thick on the pellets. A bird goes along taking the flies and leaving behind a line of perfect little clay beads. Yet from the next bund all this amounts to a strip of grey about the width of your finger. There are whole acres of water with nothing breaking the surface but rain.

The older sea walls have lost their corners. Where the tide gets round an end it has cut a smooth curved passage, deep on the outside of the bend, with a fan of clean shell grit on the inside. Nobody bothers to square those ends off anymore. The crew puts its new gates where the water already goes. At the turn of the tide a motor starts in the pump house; you feel it through the step before you hear it. One gate rises a handspan. For a minute there is a steady pouring sound and the mullet turn together into the flow. Then the levels come even, the sound dies away, and rain dimples the water on both sides of the gate alike.

By dusk the old street has gone. A ribbon of sandpipers passes low over its course on the way to their roost, wingtips nearly touching the water. The lorry comes back with one man driving and another asleep against the glass. Behind them, one by one across the flooded wards, amber lamps come on over the valves.
