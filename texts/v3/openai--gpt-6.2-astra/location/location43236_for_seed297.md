The floor of the basin is so nearly level that from its southern lip you can see the tops of the yellow reeds for miles, but scarcely a foot of the ground they grow in. The reeds give way in broad patches to something lower and much darker. Three pale ridges cross the growth at a slant. They stand no higher than a man's shoulder, and all three end abruptly in mid-basin. Beyond them the yellow resumes, right up to a far rim softened by distance and by nothing else. Close at hand the rim is sharp enough to cut a sole.

It is warm down among the reeds. There is no smell of damp earth under them, no darkening of the dust even at the base of a stem. The ground is a cream mineral that splits into thin, slightly curved plates. Where two plates have thrust up against one another, their broken edges fit tooth for tooth across the gap. Nothing has worn away since the thrust. Looking down into one of these cracks, you see that the green which seemed so dark from the lip is actually an accumulation of colors: lime at the top, then bottle green, then the deep blue of leaves that receive only the light passed on by other leaves. Little apricot-colored fans grow upside down on the undersides of the plates. Even the narrowest crack has its fringe.

None of this growth contains water. A reed broken low down has a cross section fine as ivory, with a lilac point at the center. No sap wells up. The lilac point is the exposed end of a filament which runs down through the mineral and up into every blade in the clump. It carries the *condition* of being nourished, rather than nourishment itself. Deep in the plate, where the root hairs touch certain grains, that condition holds naturally. A leaf a yard above the ground can partake of it by being, in one very restricted respect, in exactly the same place as the grain. In all other respects the leaf is out here in the light. It casts a narrow shadow; its edge has a tiny saw to it that you can feel by drawing a thumb one way but not the other.

This economy of distance is a property of the basin, like the cleavage of its stone. The plants merely make an exquisite use of it. Along the middle ridge, where the effect is strongest, there are whole thickets of a red-stemmed shrub which share a single flower. You can walk from bush to bush and find the flower each time at a different height, once level with your knee and once deep inside the twigs. A small gray moth sits well down in its throat. At the first bush you see the moth's folded back, at the next its powdery forehead. There is just the one moth. It has found perhaps the most spacious shelter any small animal ever had.

Away from the ridges there is little to hear. Now and then comes a faint double tick, as of a seed striking stone and bouncing once. If you stop to listen, the interval between the two ticks opens up enough to admit a smaller sound, a dry furry scrape. Under the lowest canopy a beetle is working its way over a chipped edge. It has been getting over that edge for as long as the edge has existed. This does not seem to trouble it. Its forefeet have a firm hold; there is light on the other side.

From above, the place has the look of a country in its first long afternoon. There is room between the clumps to walk quite easily, though from a few yards off the layers close up and the way you came is all leaves. The sun is plain white, the shadows fall in one direction, the distant rim stays dependably distant. Only after a long while do you notice that the golden cast on the western half of the reeds is not a trick of low sun. Those blades are showing the undersides they turned up at the first passage of warm air across the basin. Farther east some are still in the act of turning. The air rests against them with just enough pressure to lift them, and no need, apparently, to go on.
