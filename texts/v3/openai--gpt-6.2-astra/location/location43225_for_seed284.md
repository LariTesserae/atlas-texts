From the valley the summit looks chipped. A thin piece of its western face has come away and is lodged against the rest, leaving a black mark the length of a fingernail at arm’s reach. The mark is the town. It gets eleven minutes of direct sun in August. A person standing in its highest street during those eleven minutes could cover the sun with a thumb and still see, on either side of the thumb, the unlit windows of the houses opposite.

The streets are hung between the two faces of the split. There are nineteen of them, counting the little one that serves only the enamel works and a house where three sisters mend armatures. The upper streets have grated floors. Lower down, where people object to filings in their supper, the floors are made of thin overlapping plates. From underneath, a family walking home looks like a little travelling dent in a fish’s belly. There is nowhere in town to put a ladder without first obtaining the consent of a neighbor.

The Y-shaped break at the bottom of the cleft is quite new. There are people who remember it being a single line. Inside its arms the stone is pale green and so finely jagged that a wool sleeve brushed against it leaves a permanent scrap. No runnel has worn it smooth. The clouds spend most of the year a mile below, rubbing their soft backs on the wooded parts of the mountain. Up here there is hardly enough loose moisture to put a bloom on a cold glass. Water comes in soldered tins on the lift and goes from the kitchens to the washhouse to the recovery still, whose keeper knows to within half a cup what the whole population has been drinking. A tin with a dent in its rim is opened over a basin.

What the town has instead is wind. It comes through the fork in the rock from two different valleys at once. A hand put through the grating can feel a fierce upward current on the fingers and a downward one at the wrist. There are narrow wheels in all the useful draughts. Some are no larger than coat buttons; some reach through four streets, turning behind guards of perforated brass. Their output goes to the winding shops, where copper thread almost too fine to see is wound onto ceramic spools for instruments used far below. The wheels have to be moved as the currents move. A wheel tender sits with her knees in a sling, watching six little tufts of white wool, and sends her machinery sliding up and down on rails. On a changeable afternoon the entire works seems to be going somewhere. Wheel guards pass the windows, lamps retreat into the ceiling, a floor leaf lifts to let a shaft through and slaps down again. The sisters take their cups off the sill without looking up.

Very little of this carries as sound. The air is thin, the bearings are good, and every piece of metal that might chatter has been given a little leather tongue to bite on. The strongest impression, especially down by the fork, is of having lost one’s hearing while retaining the sense of touch. Then somebody quite close by cracks an egg against the lip of a pan, and the small domestic knock is perfectly distinct.

The egg will have been brought up on the lift. Nothing here lays an egg that big. The commonest animals are dun-colored, six-legged things called cuffs, which live in the warm clearance behind the bearing collars. A cuff can flatten itself to the thickness of a visiting card. When a wheel moves, its cuffs pour out and run just ahead of the shadow of the guard until it settles; then they fold themselves into the new clearance, hundreds to a collar. They eat the black skin that grows on old lubricant. Their own cast skins, translucent and faintly oily, are eaten by little ivory moths that never fly. The moths have serviceable wings, but a moth that opens them in a street here is generally lost to the valley. They walk up the walls in pairs, feeling ahead with the folded tips. There are also red hunting mites, and blind silver crickets, and a kind of short-legged mouse that builds nests of rejected copper. An occupied nest is easy to recognize: all the ends of the wire are turned neatly in.

At shift change the tender parks the big wheels one at a time. For several minutes the animal traffic is astonishing. Cuffs cross the crossings in dark bands. Mice hang head down from the grates and scoop them up with both paws. Moths hurry along the undertreads of the stairs, safe from feet but not from the crickets waiting at the bolts. A child sitting very still on the lowest step can watch almost the whole economy of the mountain pass over one boot. Then a green lamp comes on, the new tender tests the wool with a breath, and everything vanishes into the machinery again.

It is cool even in the houses. People favor short thick jackets with polished elbows, and put their chairs where waste heat comes through the party wall. In the evening, light from the enamel furnace gets into the grain of the fresh break. A green seam shows first; then a hundred lesser fractures take it up, so that for a while one can see a little way *into* the mountain, as into a piece of flawed glass. This is when the surveyor makes his rounds. He pencils a date beside the end of each illuminated line. Most dates stay good for years. Occasionally he has to reach an inch farther than he did the night before.

Beyond the last houses there is a small public landing with no machine on it at all. People go there for the long view: not out across the clouds, which cannot be seen from the landing, but straight up through the split. There are all nineteen streets in foreshortening, their warm undersides and cold handrails, the turning wheels continually exchanging places, and high above the highest roof a narrow strip of ordinary sky. Once in a while something crosses it too quickly to identify. For days afterward people say it was a bird.
