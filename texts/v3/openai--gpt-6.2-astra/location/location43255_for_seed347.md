The first roofs are level with the grass. From the sheep track on the rim you could step straight onto one, though the turf on it is greener than the turf underfoot, and trembles a little from the weight of a gull. Beyond it the roofs go down in steps. There is a whole town down there, in the hollow left by a lake.

The lake went out through its eastern bank within living memory. The cut is still raw. It shows a depth of blue clay, a band of gravel, then more clay so dark it looks wet even where the wind has dried it. At the bottom a river runs away between banks much too grand for it. A few willows have got their toes into the gravel. Their trunks are no thicker than wrists. Farther in, out of the wind, willow growth is so dense that a hare can cross from one end of the town to the other under cover.

People built first along the shingle ridges of the old shore. They laid flat stones edge to edge and built low houses on them, each house sharing its uphill wall with the yard above. From across the hollow you can read the history of the water in those long, nearly level streets. A lane will go half a mile without rising the height of a doorstep, then turn sharply down a flight of six steps to another street that does the same. All the stone is local, picked out of the exposed beds, and a great deal of it is still sharp enough to catch wool. In the newest quarter people hang fleeces over the corners where the children run past.

It is cold enough most mornings for ice to take the little square outside the meeting house. Under the ice are grass blades, a lost red berry, the distinct print of a duck's foot in silt. By noon it all comes loose. Water from higher ground goes through the town in shallow, stone-lined runs, under thresholds, through notches in the garden walls, sometimes right across a kitchen floor in a groove older than the house. Nobody blocks a run. Even a handful of leaves held there for an hour means water finding another way, and the other way is quite likely to be through somebody's bed of onions. A person walking home will stoop to clear a notch without breaking off a conversation.

The hollow gets the full northern daylight. At midday you can see clear across it to the pale scrape on the opposite bank where a slice of earth came away last spring. Yet there are places down in the town where hardly any sky is visible. Rowan branches meet over the lanes. Ferns grow out of the retaining walls in successive green shelves; below them are liverworts and the little round leaves of pennywort, each with a bead of meltwater at its center. A roof left untended for a season comes up in rushes. The busiest nesting place for ducks is the roof of the house where people gather to settle disputes. In spring the ducklings drop one by one into the soft black bed beside the door, to the considerable inconvenience of whoever has just sown it.

Below the last street the lake persists, though from above it looks like a meadow. The sedge stands in water to half its height. There are brown trout in lanes scarcely wider than the fish, and snipe that stay still so long their reflections grow complete again around their legs. A fox works the dry crest of an old gravel bar. Its passage shows first in a string of small movements well ahead of it: a vole dropping off a tussock, a duck drawing her neck low, two teal sliding together into the shadow of a bank. The fox passes without finding anything. For a while the open water keeps rocking against the stems.

For so many lives in one place there is very little noise. There are no wheels on those steps. Loads go on shoulders; doors open into banks of earth; the thick wet roofs take the edge off voices. A whole shower can pass over with scarcely a sound until the house runs begin to fill. Then, at intervals down the slope, there is the mild cluck of water falling the depth of one stone. In the evening someone high up on the old shore empties a basin. Long after the door has shut, a floating onion skin is still making its way through the lower streets, turning bright side up in each small fall, on its way back to what is left of the lake.
