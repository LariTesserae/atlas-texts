The edge of the plain is a fresh break, six hundred feet of cream-colored rock with a lip so sharp it casts a hairline of shadow on its own face. Nothing has fallen from it. At the foot there ought to be a sloping apron of rubble, a softer version of the cliff leaning against the original; instead the lower country comes level to the wall, and the two meet in a clean inside corner. From up here one can follow that corner for miles. It goes around three headlands and disappears behind a grove of copper-colored leaves.

Most of the high plain is bare. Its surface has the fine grain and faint drag of unglazed porcelain. The sun is pleasant on it. There is no glare except in certain long seams where the mineral takes a polish in the very act of growing. These seams run across the plain in widely separated pairs. Between one pair and the next there may be an hour's walk over absolutely vacant ground, with a good clear view of another shelf of the same pale stone far to the east, poised a little higher in the warm, almost colorless sky.

The groves occur where a seam turns. From a distance they have the full, rumpled outlines of great stands of cabbage: blue-green in the hollows, russet where the outer leaves turn up to the light. Up close, there are leaves as long as a body, wonderfully thin, with a soft pile on the underside. They overlap so deeply that under the fifth or sixth layer it is dark. Smaller plants live there, pale apricot things with forked stems, rooted entirely in the pile of their neighbors. At the very edge of a grove, where there is no room for another whole leaf, little green tongues come out flat along the stone. The abundance stops within the width of a hand. Beyond it there is not so much as a speck of lichen.

There is no water in the leaves. The heavy substance that fills their ribs is a clear oil, warm all the way down to the root. Split sunlight passes through it and lays a narrow yellow stripe on the leaf below. The roots reach both seams of a pair, and the difference between the minerals on either side drives a current through the plant. Its thickest parts are buried. Down there, thousands of discs turn on oiled spindles, passing fine mineral powder inward from the root tips. One can see the whole operation in section where the grove overhangs the cliff. There are discs no bigger than seeds, then broad, closely fitted wheels of amber tissue, and at the center a slow pale screw that presses the powder into new ribs. All the plants in a grove share one such center. There is nothing crude or provisional about the fit of its parts, though no one has fitted them. On the exposed face a single drop of oil clings to a spindle end. It turns with the spindle and never drops.

For all this work the grove makes very little sound. Close to the ground there is a fine intermittent *tik*, rather like the noise a cooling stone makes after sunset. Out on the plain one hears nothing. No grit travels over the porcelain. No small creature stirs in the miles of deep, comfortable shade under the leaves. Even the soft pile keeps its nap, brushed in one direction only, the direction in which the leaf first unfolded.

It is difficult to say whether this is old country or country in the first instant of its existence. The cliff has the delicate fracture marks of a thing broken this morning, yet the plants at its edge have worked enough stone to make a mountain. Their discarded mineral travels back along the second seam and crystallizes precisely where the first has been drawn away. A leaf is fed from the base and taken back at the tip without its shadow shifting on the ground. Perhaps the whole high plain works this way. Far out on the eastern shelf is a long white ridge, scarcely higher than a step at one end, rising at the other into a magnificent unfinished headland. It looks as if one more thrust from below would bring it all the way into the sun. The light rests on its narrow crest. In the lee of it, visible across all that empty air, a single grove fills the angle of warm shade exactly.
