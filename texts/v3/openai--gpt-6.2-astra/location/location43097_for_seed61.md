A paving stone rocks a little when stepped on. Under its low end is a town. The whole place, including the quarry from which it was cut, would fit in the hollow of two cupped hands. From the street above, the only sign of it is a damp crescent on the stone that lasts about an hour longer than the rest of the rain.

The town occupies a pocket in blue limestone. At the top of the pocket a seam lets water in; at the bottom another seam takes it away. Between the two there used to be enough of a fall to strike a hollow in the floor. The hollow is still there, round, with one white pebble in it. But someone long ago put a little slanting slab across the fall. Below this there are now sixteen more slabs, each resting on the roof of a house, passing the water along from roof to roof until it arrives at the bottom with scarcely enough force to turn the pebble over.

These roofs make a great deal of noise. Some are slate, some are fired clay, one is the shoulder blade of an animal nobody here has ever seen entire. Each takes the patter in its own way. The clay makes a fat plocking sound that carries through all the houses that share its wall. The bone hums. Down on the floor, where the streets are scarcely wider than saw cuts, the combined sound has the volume and steadiness of heavy surf. There is a quiet place behind the quarry, but it is inconvenient to get to, and after a little while there one becomes aware of a high ringing in one's head and is glad to get back.

Most of the houses have a dry room and a wet room. The wet rooms face the street. Their sills are worn concave from setting down jars in the same place, and the jars themselves have flat places on their bellies to keep them from rolling on the sills. Everything fits like that. A flight of three steps is also the lid of a settling tank; the underside of a bridge is grooved to drain the ceiling of the room below. Where there was once a public square, a very broad, shallow bowl has been worn into the paving. There is just room to go around it single file. The light that comes in past the rocking stone lies in the bowl, grey even at noon, bright enough to work by if the work is pale. This is where handles are fitted to awls and the fine cracks in crockery are rubbed with white clay.

Nothing green grows here. Along the waterline there is a skin the color of skim milk, grazed so thin in places that the blue of the stone shows through. A single blind limpet has been working its way along the same retaining wall for longer than the wall has had a parapet. Its shell is the largest unobstructed surface in the lower town. In the dimness it looks like another roof, except for the clean swath behind it, no wider than a thread.

It is cool enough that a handprint on a door stays clouded after the hand is gone. There are people, though one mostly knows them by such marks. In all that racket they signal through the stone: a heel pressed to one end of a threshold makes the loose latch at the other end lift slightly, and someone inside knows to open up. It is common to sit with a bare foot against a neighbor's wall. Children have to be taught not to drum their heels; it sets half a street answering doors.

Up above, weather comes in spells and the paving dries out between them. Down here the limestone holds the rain and yields it so grudgingly that the sixteenth roof receives much the same allowance in a dry month as in a wet one. The town's oldest job is keeping it that way. When a lip begins to wear low, a potter lays a bead of clay along it; when a passage silts, the silt goes into the next bead. There is very little left of the first slanting slab except its slope. Sometimes, in the night, the white pebble turns in its hollow. Through the loud continuous water comes the small hard knock of stone on stone. People in bed feel it against their backs, and know the drains are clear.
