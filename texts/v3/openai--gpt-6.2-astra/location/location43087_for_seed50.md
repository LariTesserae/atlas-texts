The top of the pass is broad enough for two people to sit and eat, provided neither minds having their feet over a considerable drop. In the pale stone between the two sitting places there is a square of water about the size of a thumbnail. It is there in fine weather. The edges are chipped; somebody once sat up here with a smaller, harder stone and made a window.

Look down through it at noon and you can see the whole country. It extends a little way under either seat. The floor is white lime, the roof scarcely a finger above it, translucent enough that the shadow of a passing bird goes clean across the floor. Almost all that space is water, clear, cool, empty. At first it looks as still as the water in a spirit level. Then you notice a single black seed going round and round so fast that what you took for a fine grey ring is the track it makes in your sight.

The water comes up the mountain. This is the peculiarity of the pass; the streams on both faces run the usual way, but whatever soaks into the rock goes up. There are hairline mouths in the floor of the little country. First one and then another emits a jet of water, hard enough to strike the roof and flatten out against it like a shirt spread to dry. Where two such sheets meet they fold downward. There are wholesale reversals of the current. The black seed will stand absolutely still for an instant and then go hurtling back the way it came. Water escapes through the square window in a succession of little jumps. On sunny days walkers sometimes feel it on their ankles and look up for rain.

Out on the pass the sound of all this is a rather agreeable burr, such as a grasshopper might make in the pocket of a coat. Inside it is immense. The roof rings. Each mouth in the floor has a slightly different note, and when several are open the notes beat against one another until there are distinct blows of silence in the noise. A creature living here could have no use for a call. There is a little flat snail, seldom seen except in the clear light of late summer, which locates another of its kind by following the clean streak it has eaten in the green fur under the roof. Two streaks sometimes meet. For a while thereafter there is only one, twice as wide.

Very little of the floor is old enough to have a shape. The rising water carries lime and lays it down in soft lips around the mouths. A lip grows higher on its sheltered side, curls, and begins to enclose a yard of perfectly quiet water no larger than the hollow on the back of a lentil. By evening the yard may have a high scalloped wall. The next mouth to open may be inside it. Then the entire delicate enclosure comes loose and travels about the country, an itinerant piece of geography, until it lodges somewhere under the low roof. Some of these fragments are still recognizable as cups. Most are white splinters with a fresh, almost fleshy pink at the break. There is no sand.

Beside the window a peg of dark juniper has been driven into a crack. A horsehair tied to it hangs into the water, with three knots at unequal intervals. No one remembers quite what was measured. Lime has grown over the lowest knot and made a white pear of it. This is the one reliable landmark below. Everything else may have moved between one visit and the next; the pear swings violently, but it stays put.

In the afternoon the western seat warms through. The green fur under it gives off a few bright bubbles, which roll along the ceiling and gather against the horsehair. For a minute or two there is air down there, a silver patch with an underside as unsteady as a flame. In that patch you can see, reflected very small and wonderfully distinct, the peaks on the other side of the valley. Then the hair twitches, the air comes out through the window with a loud wet syllable, and the little country is submerged again.
