From the road along the sea wall you can look down through the roofs of the pump houses. There are three of them, several miles apart, each with a square of glass over its engine. The road is about level with the deck of a shrimp boat passing on the seaward side. On the landward side a flight of thirty steps goes down to the flats. After rain only the top twenty or so are usable. The oil drum chained to the bottom rail is a float; when it comes within reach, the watchman shuts the gate across the steps.

At the foot of the wall it is warm, wet, and curiously dark for a place with so much sky. Cloud off the sea comes over the coping in long low bellies, shedding a little water without quite becoming rain. The flats send back hardly any light. They are the brown of baking chocolate, even under water. A flicker of silver half a mile out will turn out to be a flock of sandpipers wheeling; when they settle, that whole reach goes dark again.

Almost all the ground down here is new. A cut at the far end of the basin admits water from the river when the river is high. It comes in heavy with silt and spreads until its current is too weak to carry much of anything. Close to the cut there are fans of fine sand, firm enough for a fox to cross. Farther out, a surveyor pushing a graduated rod through what looks like solid ground finds six inches of water, eight inches of something that will neither splash nor hold a hole, and then, quite distinctly, the grit of last winter's deposit. There are no banks yet to the little rivers on the flats. They have edges like the edges of spilled milk. Some finish miles short of the pumps, in a broad stain out of which the water must find its own way underground.

From the wall the basin looks vacant. Down on it there is scarcely a free square inch. The shallowest pools are thick with glass shrimp. Worms draw rosettes of black thread on the mud; little crabs dismantle the rosettes, pellet by pellet. In the water left in a fox print, a dozen fry keep turning together, too small to disturb the outline of the toes. There is very little green. A few clumps of rushes have taken on the sand fans, catching enough grit between their stems to stand on small raised feet. At the muddy end of the basin the nearest thing to a meadow is a faint bronze nap on the ooze. The sandpipers feed on it with a delicate sewing motion. Behind them come starlings examining every puncture.

The middle pump house has a brick apron where it is possible to sit dry, though not to talk. Inside, two old diesels drive screws so large that each blade lifts a sheet of brown water broad enough to cover the apron. The sheets fall on the other side of the wall. The noise has a body to it: the bench shakes through the seat of your trousers, and the loose skin under a resting gull's beak trembles. More than a mile away, at the bird counters' hut, people still point rather than try to make themselves heard. The engines run at one speed for days. For all that commotion the only visible result on the flats may be a handspan of bare mud around the foot of a post by evening.

There used to be a plan to make fields here. On the inside door of the hut is a faded drawing of straight blue drains meeting a road at tidy intervals, with a space left white for a village. The drawing is still of some use: its red crosses mark the boreholes, and these have iron caps you can tie a boat to. But on the ground there are only the pump houses, the hut, and a shed for storing lengths of pipe. Twice a year a crew comes down in a flat-bottomed skiff to paint a fresh white band on each borehole cap, just clear of the mud. In the shed they keep the measurements in pencil on a doorjamb. At the oldest borehole the ground has risen the length of a man's forearm since the first mark.

Toward evening the cloud sometimes lifts clear of the sea wall. Light gets in almost horizontally through the legs of the gulls. Then all across the basin you can see, by their thread-thin shadows, ridges that have been there all day under your eye: a branching sill of sand, the lip of a former pool, a wide low back of mud joining two beds of rushes that last year stood apart. Water still covers the join, but a heron walking from one bed to the other barely wets the joints of its toes. It goes that way every evening now.
