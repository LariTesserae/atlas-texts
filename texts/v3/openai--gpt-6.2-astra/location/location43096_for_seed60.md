At the bottom of the lane there is a gate with its bottom hinge a good foot above the ground. A white tile on the gatepost says LOW WATER. Beyond it, about the size of a bowling green, lies a warm, rather lumpy orchard. The gate opens easily in February. In September there is no opening it at all.

The trees stand in five rows. Between them grows something with the flat, overlapping leaves of a leek, though each leaf is broad enough to wrap a loaf in. The old leaves haven't rotted. They lie in layers, crosswise from one year's growth to the next, and if you put your heel through the top layer there is a glimpse of pale green down there, fresh as the inside of a cabbage. The stuff bears weight surprisingly well. A person walking across it makes a depression that follows a little behind them, as if someone were carrying a heavy sack along underneath. Water comes up in the heel marks. It is clear at first and then fills with the white dust that lies over everything here, even in wet weather.

There is a brick shed in the middle row. Halfway up its wall a cast-iron spout pours a stout rope of water into a bed of mint. The mint has been flattened into the shape of a parting in hair. It extends in this condition for twelve feet, at which point the water vanishes and the mint stands up again. This, and a patch of little round leaves that continually turn over in one corner of the orchard, are the only running water to be seen. But the sound is considerable. It comes especially strongly from the trees. Put an ear to one of their wide, almost horizontal limbs and you hear gravel rolling in a tin pail. Move along to the next limb and it is the hollow gulp of a washtub emptying. At a distance the whole place sounds like a laundry on a busy morning.

The shed is still at work. On the dry side of the wall is an electric motor, an oiler with a thumb of amber oil in its glass, and a broad leather belt running down through a slot in the floor. The current comes in on two brown china knobs high under the eaves. Everything else in the shed has had to accommodate the orchard. The inspection bench is at an angle that sends a pencil rolling smartly into the corner. A root has grown through the handle of a drawer and, in thickening, pulled the drawer open. In it are a square of blue chalk and a folded piece of newspaper so damp that the print has gone through six thicknesses. On the exposed square one can still read PEARS.

There are no pears now. In the ordinary cream-coloured daylight of the basin you can see all the way up into the crowns. The little spurs where fruit ought to hang have lengthened, gone green again, and come back down. On the older trees they have reached the leaf floor and knitted into it. Some have thickened into fluted columns; others are still soft red cords, beaded with ants on the rare afternoons when the ants come out. There is very little other traffic here. Once a year or so a heron stands on the gatepost and looks a long time at the sound of all that water.

The orchard was planted on a raft. That becomes plain at the far end, where a recent fall has lifted a corner of the leaf bed. There are the original oak baulks, black and square, with iron dogs driven into their ends. Under them hangs a yard of fine, apricot-coloured root. Under the root is deep brown water, entirely shaded, giving back a perfect small reflection of the bright rent overhead. Even the shed floats. The gatepost does not. From the post one can judge how much water the winter has brought, and how much the motor has managed to send away. It would have emptied the basin long ago if the mint bed still led to the culvert. Now it simply lifts water out of the dark, gives it twelve feet of sun, and takes it back.

In a still hour there is another movement, too slight to feel through the soles. Look from the shed door toward the gate and note which trunk just hides the white tile. Eat lunch there and look again. A sliver of tile will have appeared on the other side. The orchard is turning under the thrust of the spout. It has worn the basin nearly round in doing so. All along the low bank, at the height of a seated person's shoulder, the clay has been rubbed to a dark satin by the passing leaves. There is one glazed brick set into that clay. Every few weeks the farthest-reaching branch goes by it with a long, domestic squeak, like a finger cleaning the rim of a plate.
