There is a lake in the cleft between two summits. It would hold a little orchard, with room for a path down the middle. Most of the time it stands on edge. From the lower summit you look across a few yards of wet stone into a sheer face of warm, brown-green water. Behind that face, a long way in, a pale leaf goes slowly up. It turns over and goes down again. There is no tree on either summit that could have dropped it.

The stone underfoot is black and so freshly split that in places the edges are still thin enough to show red against the sky. There is no gravel. Nothing has yet had time to become gravel. There are slabs as big as doors and splinters that will go through the skin, and in the cracks a little rust-colored paste that smells of blood when the rain hits it. A few rushes grow sideways out of one sheltered seam. Their roots are white and much longer than the rushes, trailing out into the upright lake.

Its noise reaches the valley as a single low note. Up here it comes apart. There is the thrashing sound at the nearer face, like a thousand hands rinsing heavy cloth; the high tearing noise where the top of the lake drags against the saddle; and underneath, at intervals of no particular length, a blow so deep that the rushes jump and drops come off them all at once. You can stand within reach of another person and see them begin to shout, see them give it up. Even with your ears stopped you feel the noise in the soft place under your tongue.

At first it looks as though the whole lake is about to fall. In fact, only parts of it fall, and seldom in the direction of the valley. A patch the size of a bed will suddenly find its own downhill. Water pitches across water. A hollow opens, with a ceiling of bubbles and a brief, perfectly dry floor of black rock. For an instant there is a little daylight in there, the dull lilac daylight of the high cloud. Then a second current finds the hollow and the floor goes dark. The deep blows come when two such hollows meet and crush the air between them.

There are three low walls in the cleft, built without mortar. They make no enclosure that can be understood from the shore. One runs up a rib of stone and ends at a notch; one is nearly always submerged; the third consists of only four courses, neatly laid, in a place so awkward to reach that it is hard to credit anybody with the patience. But once or twice in an afternoon the water lays itself flat from the notch to the four courses. Then the walls are plainly the sides of a little landing, and the notch is worn smooth where a knee would rest while someone leaned down to fill a pot. It is the only smooth stone here. A hand that knew the lake well could still put a pot into it. There would be time to fill it, though not to dip twice.

For all the agitation, very little lives in the water. Under the landing are a few limpets the color of sheep’s milk, each with a dark rim of flesh firmly clenched around its inch of rock. Wherever the lake throws itself, a film stays with them. Once, in a hollow that opened almost to the middle, there was a single transparent animal hanging by two threads. Its long fine legs were folded against its belly. When the hollow shut, it unfolded them, quite leisurely, and stood on the returning water as on a floor. You might watch the rest of the day and see nothing else move of its own accord.

Toward evening the cleft fills with mist from below. The lake is warmer than the mist. At their meeting there is a narrow strip of clear air, and through it, for a few minutes, you can see straight down past the mountain to a stream shining in the valley, fields already in shadow, the white crowns of flowering trees. Then the current brings the pale leaf round again. This close it is broad and veined, with a bite gone from one side and a bead of air caught in the bite. The water heaves; for once the leaf comes free. It settles on the top course of the little wall. All night, in that terrific noise, with tons of warm water flinging themselves from height to height, it lies there drying.
