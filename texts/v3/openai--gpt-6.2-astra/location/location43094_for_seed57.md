A brick has come out of the heel of the old quay. Its socket is well below low water. You could fit a hand in there, though not with the fingers spread. From the channel it looks like another patch of black weed on a black wall; only when the weed lifts can you see the upper lip, a straight line with a tooth of pale mortar still hanging from it.

Inside, the floor falls away to the left. There is a little scree of brick chips there, red if anything ever brought a light to them. They still have corners fine enough to catch on skin. One has landed on its point and stayed there. Behind the scree the mortar has split, exposing the small stones it was made with: three white and one green, bedded close together, with the round impression of a fifth. That is the deepest part. The water in the little round bed is appreciably colder than the water at the mouth.

Weed grows thickest along the lip, where a blade can put its tip out into the dim reach of the channel. Inside it grows differently. It spends very little on thickness. A single blade, folded and folded again, occupies most of the ceiling; the water between its folds would scarcely wet a lip. Farther back are soft tufts the colour of tea leaves after the tea has been poured off. Fine clear threads run from tuft to tuft. On the underside of every chip there is a pelt which has taken the shape of the grit beneath it. Almost nothing swims here. Once in a while a white jointed thing comes in sideways, eats a clean half-moon out of a blade, and backs out the way it came. Its meal leaves a window, good for a week or so.

The tide has an odd difficulty with the place. At the mouth it keeps harbour time. A little way in, each fold of weed keeps the tide it had when the fold was made. The long blade overhead has a winter low water on one side and, on the other, the high water of a warm night when the channel was full of drifting seed. Both sides are submerged. It is the pull that differs. On the winter side grains hang slantwise, intent on going out; on the summer side a seed presses steadily in, its husk long since come apart, the two empty halves still arriving. A fold sometimes wears through. Then two tides settle their small difference. The blade gives a twitch and the brick standing on its point makes a single dry tick underwater.

Deep in the round hollow, before the weeds and before the quay, there is still some of the water that used to lie in the print of a hoof in the salt mud. It has no tide at all. It holds the faint uprush of cold that comes through mud from below. The clear threads have found it. They go down into the print in such numbers that the hollow looks full of combed hair, all drawn towards a part in the middle. Nothing moves there fast enough to see except at the very ends, where now and then a thread slips into its own reflection and comes up growing the other way.

Outside, a boat settling against the quay can make the whole wall grumble. In the socket the sound is felt mostly as a easing of pressure: the leaves part a fraction, silt rises off the scree, the four stones show. Then everything beds down again. Over many such disturbances the chip on its point has begun to lean. When it falls it will roof over a patch of pelt no larger than a nail. There will be another inside then, with a little of this dark for its weather.
