From the summit there is one more pass to cross. It lies in the hollow on the underside of the old surveyor’s level, between the brass rim and a screw that has backed out half a turn. Leave the screw alone. Enough daylight gets in along its thread to see the pass, if you lie down on the summit stones and give your eye time.

It is about as long as a grain of barley. The near slope is blue shale, broken into little overlapping plates; the far slope is something paler and more massive, with a blunt projecting shoulder. Between them lies a saddle of loose black grit. The sense of elevation is strongest there. On either side the ground falls away so steeply that the slopes, though very short, have room to turn blue with distance before they end against the brass. A speck cast down one of them would take the better part of a morning to reach the rim. From above you could stop it with the tip of a pin.

The level was built to settle a disagreement between two mountains about which was higher. Its makers could remove a difference in height from the objects that possessed it and keep it for reference. There should have been nothing in the hollow but that difference, much as there is nothing between the jaws of a caliper but a width. But a little cutting dust was left in the instrument when the back went on. Given the height, and all the time since, the dust has made quite a country.

There is no ice. What looks like a shelf of old snow under the far shoulder is powdered porcelain from the screw sleeve. It has lain there through thousands of winters without acquiring a crust. Nothing puts down a root to hold it. The cold is deepest a little way above the ground, in a thin band that follows the lie of the land. An upright flake of shale will sometimes split clean across at that height; its upper half falls and makes a low wall, and the stump stands in the shelter of its own ruin. This accounts for much of the terracing on the near slope. The rest is the work of a single long grain of corundum. Whenever the summit contracts in the cold, the brass pushes this grain a fraction farther up the pass. On warming, it rolls back by a slightly different route. It has worn a braid of paths into the shale, fine and various as the tracks around a village well. There has never been anything here to walk them.

Two works of the surveyors are still visible. At the saddle there is a silver staple with one foot exposed, its squared inside corner still bright. Far below, at the limit of sight, stands a black post with a white head. The post marks zero. On rare afternoons the light entering the screw thread reaches its head and comes back up the slope, illuminating the undersides of the shale plates. For a few minutes the pass has the appearance it might have if you were lying on your back in a valley below it, looking up. The staple is a high arch against a strip of pewter sky. Then the sun moves on outside, and you are looking down again into a piece of equipment small enough to close your hand around.

Very little sound gets out. Once, waiting with your cheek against the stones, you might hear a faint double tick. The first is the corundum coming to rest. The second, much fainter, comes from the other mountain, beyond sight from this one, where a little height has just been given back.
