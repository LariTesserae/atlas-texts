From the top of the stair you can see daylight on the back of a crab half a mile away. It is a big crab, and the light down here is quite good. The stair goes on for another forty steps before it leaves the rock wall and becomes a ladder, and the ladder ends in a clay jar sunk to its rim in the floor. There is room in the jar to stand and reach up and help the next person down. Outside it, almost everything ankle-high is moving.

This is the country under the salt shelf. Above it is a white, waterless plain that hurts the eyes at noon. Below it the light is the yellow of a clean fleece, except where fractures in the salt admit the blue of the sky in long, perfectly straight lines. Some of those lines cross several miles of country: a hump of shell gravel, a steaming gut of water, the clay roof of a shelter, another hump. You can watch a cloud go over all of them. The shelf is thick enough to bear a laden donkey and thin enough in places for someone below to count its four dark feet.

Fresh water comes up warm through the bottom. It makes trouble wherever it goes. A spring will work away at the foot of a salt pillar until the pillar settles, the roof lets down a little, and a broad pan of brine that has stood quiet for months tips out over dry ground. Then there is a run. The first sign is generally a noise like somebody emptying a sack of buttons. Thousands of little white crabs are coming over the gravel ridge, some still clutching bits of meat, some travelling with their young buttoned all down their backs. Behind them comes a shining front of water no deeper than a finger. By the time it reaches the ladder it may be deep enough to swim a man in his jar.

Between runs the greater part of the floor is dry. It has hardly had time to become a floor. There are stretches of soft mineral rind with the mould of bubbles underneath, rafts of shells stuck together in the positions in which their owners died, and acres of fresh pink sediment so finely creased by the draining water that a moth landing there spoils a pattern. Nothing has grown over any of it. Around the mouths of the springs there is a greasy grey skin that feeds on what comes up dissolved in the water. A thumbnail drawn through it leaves a dark furrow; by morning the furrow is closed. This is what supports the country. Little rasping mouths crop it from underneath. Bigger mouths prise the raspers loose. What escapes being eaten alive gets eaten very thoroughly dead.

The animals are easiest to tell apart when they stop. What looked like coarse sand blowing sideways resolves into flies with their wings folded flat. What looked like the same sand blowing the other way is a procession of shrimp on the wet underside of a ledge, visible through the ledge. The big crabs stand high on lilac legs and have a habit of carrying a smaller animal in the crook of one claw for hours before eating it. On the pillars, pale lizards hang head down with their eyes shut, opening one eye at the sound of a fly striking salt. Even the dry middles of the gravel humps are full. Put your ear to one and there is a busy, papery tearing from the grubs inside the shells. Stand up and you lose it in the slap of water and the great communal crackle of feet.

The few shelters are built on knobs of black rock that come through from below the salt. Each consists of a potter’s kiln, a sleeping platform, and a sloping rack on which the catch is spread to dry in the breath of a hot fissure. The people who come down the ladders bring their fuel with them; a bundle of thornwood is worth carrying much farther than a jar of water. They make wonderful jars here. Some have little staircases of thumb dents up their sides so that anything that falls in can get out again. Those are for drinking water. The smooth-sided ones are for supper.

Toward evening, when the blue lines have gone from the floor but the roof still holds a considerable light, the flies rise. They hang beneath the salt in dark, slack loops, millions to a loop. This is the one time of day you can see the shape of the air: a narrow updraught above each spring, a slow roll out across the warm flats, and at the far end of the country a steady pull toward an opening nobody on this side has reached. For a few minutes the ground seems almost bare. You notice a new island out beyond the last kiln, oval, pink, scarcely higher than the water, with a crab already standing on it. Then somewhere in the distance a pillar gives a small, decisive knock. Every lizard opens its eyes.
