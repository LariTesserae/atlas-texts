Under the axle works there is a field about the size of the works canteen. When the number three press came down for the first time, its concrete foot sank nine inches and stayed there. Those are the missing nine inches, the foreman used to say, pointing through the inspection hatch. In fact there is room down there for a man to stand upright, and for quite a high shelf of rock above his head, with animals living on both faces of it.

The press broke a seam of honey-coloured spar. Seen from the hatch, the field looks rather like a bed someone has tried to make with sheets of glass. Slabs have ridden up over one another; long triangular spaces run in under them. Some of the edges are so fine that they vanish seen end on. There has been no water down here to take the edges off. On the nearest one there is still a little curl of blue cotton from the sleeve of the man who installed the light. The light itself is well back in its wire cage, the colour of an electric cooker just after you switch it off. A slab will carry that colour along its edge for yards into what looks like absolute black.

It is warm. On opening the hatch one gets the smell of hot sewing-machine oil, quite pleasant, and a noise of such volume that the first instinct is to shut it again. There is the low note of the works flywheel, carried down a single iron pile, but most of what one hears is feet. The common animal here has a body like a spectacle lens and a fringe of little jointed feet all round it. It stands on the spar and plays its feet over the surface faster than the eye can follow. Each foot has a hard heel. In a hand's breadth of stone there may be forty of these creatures, all facing different ways, each occupying just the patch on which its body will fit. The sharp high tearing sound they make together is audible in the canteen through the legs of the chairs.

For a long time the men thought the creatures were trying to get somewhere and slipping. Then an electrician put his test pencil to the spar, and its little bulb came on. The stone takes a charge under pressure. Every turn of the wheel upstairs squeezes it a little; the feet draw off the charge. A creature lifted on the end of a wooden rule goes quite still, light and dry as a watch glass. Set it down anywhere on the field and it begins again. On the undersides of the slabs the animals are milky white, and on top they are smoke brown. Along an edge one sometimes finds a two-coloured individual folded neatly in half.

There are things like plants, too, though very few. One grows around the base of the iron pile. It has six leaves, black on the outside and lined with a fine copper nap; they open away from one another just far enough to hold five little pockets of warm air. In each pocket lives a pale, soft-backed mite the size of a plum stone. From time to time a mite leans out into the din and combs the nap with its mouth. This is about the largest gesture one can see in the field. Most of the business of living goes on within the thickness of a fingernail. What looks like a dusty thumbprint on a slab resolves, under the electrician's lens, into rows of minute backs with narrower backs fitted into the intervals between them.

Beyond the pile stands a three-legged survey stool. There is nowhere for a fourth leg to go. The surveyor left a plumb line hanging from its seat and scratched a cross in the stone below the bob. It was supposed that the chamber would settle. Every year somebody opens the hatch and sights across: the brass point hangs over the cross with just enough clearance to see a thread of orange light between. Even the blue cotton is there. People who know the field best say that a still photograph gives a truer idea of it than a film, except that in the photograph you cannot feel the sound in your teeth. The wheel turns, the stone takes the weight, the little heels come down by the million. All that labour, and the point of the bob has never touched.
