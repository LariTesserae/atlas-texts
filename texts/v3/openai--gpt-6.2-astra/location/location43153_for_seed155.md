From the south rim you can see most of the city, clear across to the comb mills on the north rim, where it is raining. In between is a plain the color of the underside of an olive leaf. The streets show as parallel changes in the nap. There are very few things that stick up: the mills, a stand of chimney trees, the white back of the reservoir. A tram crossing the plain is visible chiefly by its shadow. It looks rather exposed out there until it reaches a stop and forty people come up out of the grass.

The plain is one of the old summits. Far below it, younger peaks have the usual blue ribs and scraps of snow caught in their gullies. Up here the work of weather is almost finished. The difference between a hill and a valley can be the thickness of a hand, and a person pouring out a cup of tea at a crossroads may be sending it to one of two distant seas. Nothing like rock comes to the surface. On the rim, where one might hope to see a section through the mountain, there are layers of compressed wood, green even in the deepest visible seam. Little leaves no bigger than fish scales grow straight out of the end grain. The oldest ones take years to shut over the mark of a fingernail.

The city was made by parting those layers. A street door is a weight on a hinge of living fiber; lift it and a stair turns down into the yellow light of a shopping arcade. Below the shops are kitchens and the district heating mains, then the tram depots with their great sliding roofs. Indoors there is a perpetual need for barbers. They go round with a gauge and a pair of long shears, cutting the ceilings back to regulation height. People who can afford it have a recess cut beside a bed and allow the fine white growth to come down as far as the pillow. It smells of raw almonds and is warm against the ear. On the surface an entire residential quarter amounts to a few hundred neatly spaced doormats and the breath coming out of holes.

It is a loud place in broad daylight. The sound that carries best is the *tak-tak-tak* of the comb mills stripping the year’s outer fiber from the plain. Long toothed drums travel on cables, turning the silvery growth over to its dark side. A half-mile swath of country changes color in the time it takes to eat lunch. Behind the drums walk the inspectors in buttoned coats, feeling the ground through the soles of their feet for a skipped patch. Fiber goes to the spinning halls; the short waste is pressed into brake blocks, cups, switchboard panels, the hard translucent glazing used over basement classrooms. Very little has to be brought up the mountain. Copper does. A coil of good wire in a shop window will have children pressed to the glass to see that astonishing orange.

What the mills cannot strip is a hollow. There are shallow depressions everywhere, some scarcely wide enough to sit in, others the size of a ward. They travel slowly against the wind. Surveyors discovered this before the first cable was laid: a brass peg set at the lowest point of a hollow would be halfway up its slope next morning, without the surrounding fibers having moved at all. It is the lowness itself that goes traveling. It will cross a road, a room, a sleeping body. In the old theater, which lies on one of the regular tracks, the stage sags in the middle every ninth afternoon and the actors lean toward each other like people talking over a cradle.

Most of the water on the summit rides in these hollows. There may be no rain on a district for weeks, and then an inch of bright water comes sliding through the grass, carrying husks and the reflection of a cloud. At the laundries they keep an eye on the level boards. As soon as a hollow passes under the floor, the intake bells begin to knock and the women swing the long spouts down. Farther out there are places where it is left to pass undisturbed. Once or twice a year a gray bird will come up out of the valleys and walk alongside a moving pool, stepping in from time to time to rinse its bill. Its feet make black stars in the silvery nap. People cut the cable power to a whole field rather than frighten one of those birds.

By late afternoon the rain on the north rim has ended. The air up here is thin and cool enough that the shade of a passing tram makes the skin tighten on the backs of the hands. One by one the mills disengage. In the intervals between them you can hear dishes being stacked somewhere underfoot, then a burst of music as a door goes up three streets away. The last drum stops with a single tooth still buried. For a while the whole open reach of the summit seems empty. Then the evening kitchen vents open, all across the plain at once, and thousands of little rectangles stand upright in the slant light, each with its green side out and its gold side in.
