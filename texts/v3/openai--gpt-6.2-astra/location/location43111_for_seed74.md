At the bottom of the hollow is a town with seven houses and a works. You can see from one end of it to the other. The houses stand well apart, and the space between them looks at first like a common shorn very close by sheep. It is green even under the eaves. If you kneel there, though, you find a grain running through the green, and here and there a knot big enough to sit on. The whole floor of the hollow is wood. What looks like turf is the wood in leaf.

Three streets cross the common. On two of them the grain runs lengthwise, offering a good dry footing; the third was laid out across the grain, to serve a building long since taken down. In damp weather that street is as slippery as a fish. It still has its little blue enamel street sign. The town has kept a surprising amount of street furniture: drain grates, lamp standards, a public scale with a brass pan that will hold a sack of meal or one quite comfortable child. All of it was installed with room for adjustment. A lamppost stands in a collar rather than a socket, so that as the wood thickens underneath, somebody can come along with a spanner and set the light back at the height people want it.

There are no great trees. Growth here tends to run sideways for a long time before it finds a reason to rise. The houses supply such a reason. Each was begun on a frame of iron hoops; now only the tips of the hoops show above the roofs, like the handles on so many old flatirons. The walls are solid and gently bellied, the window reveals deep enough to keep a jug of milk cool through the afternoon. Where a householder wants more room, it is easier to move a window outward by a handspan each spring than to cut a new one. One house has a bay which took a marriage to make. Its three panes face east, south and west, and on a clear day the patch of sun they admit travels the full length of the kitchen table.

The works occupies the lowest ground. It makes packing felt from the pale underbark shed by the streets. Through its open loading doors you can see a comb wide enough to dress a horse, two steel rollers, and a roll of finished felt with the soft, close spiral of a cut leek. The machinery runs off an electric motor hung from the rafters to keep it level. Most days the rollers turn for hours. Their bearings are good, and a works whose walls are a yard of cork makes a poor advertisement for its own industry: out on the common the only sign of production is a square of light going regularly dark and bright as a belt passes the high window. At noon the motor stops. Then you can hear a woman inside tearing the paper off her lunch.

Rain comes in short visits, seldom enough at once to soak a coat through. Its effects last all day. First the roofs darken. Then little lips along the edges of the roads swell and tip water through the grates. Underfoot, cups no bigger than thimbles fill in a sequence you can follow downhill, one gleam appearing a few inches beyond the last. Late in the afternoon, long after the sky has cleared, a shallow cistern behind the works receives its inch of water. This is where you might see the hollow's one wagtail. It comes over the rim without company, stands on the enamel depth gauge, and takes whatever the water has brought up. There may be weeks between sightings. No one has bothered to net the cistern.

In the evening the cold collects early on the common. People going home from the works walk a little uphill out of it, each by a separate street or path, leaving very little impression of a crowd. The freshest underbark lies in loose curls at the roadside, ready for the morning cart. Beneath it the exposed surface is the colour of cream. By morning it will have greened over; by the end of the week a stranger could not say which strip had been harvested. Yet the town keeps roughly the shape it has had for two hundred years. The cart always turns at the same wide bend. The road crew leaves the same knots uncut for seats. And at the bend, where two generations of carters have put a hand out to steady themselves as they climb aboard, a living rail has worn smooth, dark, and just the right thickness for a hand.
