From the uphill lip you can see the whole shelf: three islands of green in a sheet of shallow water, a whitewashed booth at the far end, and a low stone curb beyond which there is nothing until the clouds. It takes about a minute to walk the length of it. Usually you go barefoot. The rock under the water is soft with a nap of rust-colored weed, and the few places where it breaks the surface are hot enough to make you hurry.

It is a new shelf. A piece of the mountain came down within living memory, taking the end of a road with it. There is still a white stripe on the last yard of road, tipped up at an angle; the booth used to be level with it. What the fall uncovered was a bed of black stone full of narrow pink seams. Warm water sweats out along the seams. Even at this height, where you have to stop for breath after the climb, the heat is considerable. A wet handprint on the booth door dries while you are trying the latch.

Almost everything grows on the three islands. They began as scraps of roadside turf and are now deep enough to take a walking stick up to the handle. There are little palms in there, and coarse sweet grass with a cutting edge, and a vine whose pale green flowers open underwater. The palms on the smallest island all lean outward. At noon their shadows reach just far enough past the roots to shelter a single brown rail. It walks around the island as the shade moves. Sometimes you see the bend and release of the grass stems for ten minutes before you see the bird.

The booth was a rain station. Its roof has peeled back at one corner, and inside are a stool, a row of coat pegs, and a square opening in the floor about the size of a bread tin. The opening still gives onto the old watershed. If you kneel there on a clear morning you can see rain falling into a ditch that went over the cliff forty years ago. A white roadside flower gets beaten flat, recovers, gets beaten flat again. Nothing repeats exactly. The ditch continues to have weather.

For part of each afternoon the two drainages connect. The first sign is a dimple out in the open water. Then dozens of dimples form, each directly over a pink seam, and the water starts to go down. There is no whirl to it. A grass seed floating over a dimple stays where it is while the surface subsides around it, until it comes to rest on the stone. In the booth the ditch fills with warm, clear water. You can put an arm through the opening and feel the current tug at the hair on your wrist. The people who maintain the road still come up once a year to clear the ditch with a long-handled rake. If it clogs, the shelf floods to the height of the coat pegs.

Drawdown lasts less than an hour. This is when the shelf has a smell to it: hot pennies, trodden mint, the faint shellfish smell of the weed lying flat. Tiny snails shut their doors one after another with a sound like grit falling on paper. The rail steps delicately out of the grass. It has learned to turn over the strands of weed with one long toe.

Then the black rock darkens along the seams. Separate beads of water swell until they touch; whole stretches of the shelf turn silver at once. From below, if there is a gap in the cloud and you happen to be looking up, you can see that little flash near the broken end of the road. A moment later the water is deep enough to show only sky, and the place is quite difficult to pick out on the mountain at all.
