At the inland edge of the lagoon, the fish go on for half a mile past what looks like the shore. Their backs lift the round leaves of a creeping herb; a bulge travels through the green and subsides, and a little farther on another leaf goes up. There is only a hand’s depth of water under the herb. The fish turn on their sides to get through. Beyond the reach of the fish the same herb grows on mud, and beyond the mud it grows on solid ground, without any change in the size or spacing of its leaves.

The solid ground is shell. A low shelf of it extends around three sides of the lagoon, broad enough to carry a forest. At the waterline it is still being made. Fingernail-sized shellfish grow there in clumps, the young fastening on the backs of the old, the gaps between them filling with a fine white lime. In the shade of the bank a shrimp can swim through the ground. A little higher up, the very same stuff is hard enough to ring when a nut falls on it. No stream has cut a bed in it. Water comes out wherever there is room, sometimes from a hundred little mouths in a patch no wider than a leaf, each with its own beard of dark green weed.

It is hot even in those wet hollows. A toad resting in one has its belly in warm water and its back in warm air, and the bead that gathers on its upper eyelid stays there until it blinks. Overhead, the trees fork low and spread wide. Their crowns meet across intervals that are quite considerable down among the trunks. In the brown light below them grow translucent gingers, long jointed ferns, and a palm with scarcely any stem, just an immense pleated fan opening straight out of the shell. Drops striking these fans make a heavy, leisurely pattering. From a distance the forest always sounds as though it has just stopped raining.

The animals are easiest to see by looking at one place for a long time. What seems to be the mottling on a palm rib resolves into seven or eight small lizards, lying head to tail with their feet folded close. The dark tidemark on a trunk is made of snails. A low bough over the lagoon has so many little herons on it that the ones near the trunk pass crabs outward in their bills to the young on the thin end. Under that bough the bottom is pink with shed crab legs. There are broad clear spaces farther out on the water, but even there, on looking closely, the surface is stippled with the nostrils of animals taking the air.

The loudest sound comes from out there. A brown frog floats with just its throat showing and gives a single hollow knock. There are enough of them across the shallows to make a continuous knocking, mellow out in the open, startlingly sharp where one is tucked under the bank. Insects keep up a much finer sound in the crowns. Between the two one can still hear the shellfish at a seep closing, a soft run of clicks as the shadow of a feeding bird passes over them.

From the far side of the lagoon the whole shelf looks almost level with the water, a long dark breadth broken here and there by the pale undersides of leaves. A skin of low cloud holds the brightness down even at midday. Nothing much alters the outline. A tree that grows too heavy for the shell tips slowly into the shallows, where its crown takes root while the old trunk is still in leaf. The shell under it softens in the water; a few yards away another white lip is hardening. Year after year there is the same breadth of forest, the same narrow margin of bright weed. At any point along it a fish can come gliding in from the dim open water, turn on its side, and vanish a long way into the land.
