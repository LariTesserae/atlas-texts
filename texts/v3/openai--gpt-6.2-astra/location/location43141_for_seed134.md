There is one place on the ridge where the glacier has lifted clear of the rock without breaking. Under it is a pocket of water about as long as a whale. The downhill lip is blue ice, frozen fast to granite; the uphill end is a jumble of slabs that were all one slab until quite recently. A white seam of quartz runs across the breaks. One could fit the pieces together by following that line alone. It is very light here. The snow has blown off the back of the glacier, and the sun, crossing a high cold sky, sends a broad patch of ordinary daylight traveling over the bottom.

There is scarcely room for the light to fall. Animals hang belly-up against the ice, lie stacked in the cracks, occupy the narrow intervals between the stacks. Some look like squat, translucent pears with a black stone showing in each; some are jointed lengths of white gristle, blunt at both ends, which go by contracting a segment in the middle and waiting for the rest of the body to catch up. A fine gray fur on the granite resolves, in the traveling light, into separate little backs. Each back has a crease and each crease opens occasionally to show a clean pink interior. There is no weed, not even the green blur that grows where sunlight reaches wet stone. When one of the little animals moves on, the patch it leaves is bare and bright as a fresh chip.

The sound is considerable. Even with nothing much moving there is a loaded, turning sound, as of heavy work going on beyond a wall. It comes from the pears. The black stone in each is a polished grain of the mountain, held in a muscular socket; water enters through the narrow end, turns the grain against a ring of smaller grains, and goes out in two milky threads. They live on what comes up through a fissure under the slabs: warmish water carrying a taste of iron, and in it the pale soft specks that live on iron. The pears grind the specks free of their rusty crusts. Thousands work at once. The low note belongs to the rock they are all holding on to. Over it one can distinguish a rather sweet clinking, irregular, close at hand. A grain has worn too small for its socket. Somewhere in the crowd a pear is trying out replacements.

Everything that eats here gets something passed on to it. The pink mouths take the waste milk; the jointed creatures ease their blunt noses under the mouths and eat the eggs laid in the hollows underneath. Against the roof are broad animals like clear soles of feet, through which the daylight comes slightly yellow. They catch what rises. Once in a long while one peels loose and settles, enclosing under itself whatever happens to be on that handbreadth of floor. For days afterward there is a clear, foot-shaped window in the ceiling. The other soles take most of a summer to close it.

The pocket tips a little from year to year. There is no perceptible swing, only evidence: a level of fine red meal left on a ledge which now slopes too steeply to hold it; a family of grinders well above the fissure still facing the way the iron water used to come. As the ice bears down on one edge it lifts on the other. Water pressed out of the narrowing cracks goes into the new ones. On the uphill side, where the granite has just parted, there are recesses as thin and brilliant as the inside of a split crystal. A few of the white jointed animals have got in sideways. In all that populous water these are the only ones whose bodies can be seen entire.

Late in the day the traveling patch reaches the downhill lip. For a little while the ice there becomes transparent. Beyond it is air, and far below, across a gap almost painful to look through after the press of bodies, the brown flank of another mountain. Nothing swims toward the view. The light draws a last yellow edge on a socket, a grain goes round in it, and the ridge keeps up its work.
