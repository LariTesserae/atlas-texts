From the floor, the roof can be mistaken for a sky with very few stars. There are long walks on which the same three grains of luminous spar remain overhead. On the fourth or fifth day a black edge cuts off one grain, then another: one has come within sight, so to speak, of a pillar. Much later the pillar itself can be felt, first as a warmth against one cheek, then as a wall leaning out over the road. The road goes round its foot. This involuntary bend is the first thing to show how great the pillar is. It takes half a day to get back on course.

The whole country is a parting between two beds of rock. In places the parting is barely wide enough for the little ivory-backed animals to pass through on their bellies; elsewhere it has opened to a height at which a falling stone would arrive well ahead of the sound of its breaking loose. There are matching features above and below. A long ridge of black, beautifully fluted stone has its hollow somewhere up in the dark. A chip knocked from the ridge still lies where it came down when the beds drew apart. Almost nothing has happened to it since. There is no rain to darken one face more than the others, no frost to widen the white nick at its tip. Pick it up and it has a fine edge and a comfortable warmth, as if someone had just finished making it and put it down.

There are no roots in the cracks. The grey nap that lies along certain seams consists of animals, each smaller than a grain of meal, their backs all canted the same way. The stone here leaks a sweetish gas, too slowly to be felt as a draught. The grey animals hold still with their feeding fringes down in the seam. An individual dislodged by a fingertip goes briskly about on six red feet until it finds a vacancy. Along the best seams there may be no vacancy for miles.

Much of the larger life is flat. There are soft black grazers that slip under the nap and leave a bare thumbprint behind them, and things like articulated saucers that settle over a grazer and wait for it to come out against a particular part of the rim. Most remarkable are the ivory backs. What seems to be a single low ledge beside the road, good for sitting on, may be several hundred of them fitted shoulder to shoulder. They lie there for the heat that comes through the rock. Put a hand down gently among them and a space opens about the hand, with no scramble and scarcely a click; the adjustment travels a long way along the ledge. They do not object to a load. What they dislike is the difference in temperature between flesh and stone.

The air has weather of a sort. A low country full of feeding seams has a permanent bank of pearl-grey dust over it. The grains are charged; they hang apart from one another, and the bank has an underside so level that a short traveller can pass beneath it without seeing dust at all. A tall one comes out with two grey brows. There are banks drawn on the oldest route tablets, and their boundaries are still accurate to a pace. Where the floor steps down sharply the dust ends in midair. Seen by a carried light, that end looks substantial enough to lean a ladder against.

Few lights are carried. On the frequented ways there is a finger groove cut into the stone at hip height. It follows a ledge where it can, crosses a gap on a slab propped up for the purpose, and occasionally leads the hand into a shallow recess. In the recess there will be a seat, a polished cup for the elbow, and a piece of fired clay on a cord. The clay is a map, meant to be read with the other hand while sitting down. Its maker has not attempted to represent distance. A stretch of easy going that lasts a month is one smooth inch; a nasty little crossing among blades of upturned spar takes up most of the back of the tablet. On one tablet a hollow has been worn nearly through at a complicated fork. Generations of people have sat in the same warm seat, trying to make up their minds.

A long way off these roads, in a chamber with no luminous grains at all, there is a small roofless house. Six fitted slabs, a bench, a lintel too low for anyone to pass without bowing. It stands at the exact place where two echoes of a footfall arrive together. Outside, one hears almost nothing: the brush of a fringe on stone, one's clothes shifting, one's own jaw working. Inside, a tap of the heel gives a deep, agreeable double note. The floor in front of the bench has one worn oval. There is no other house for an immense distance.

It is tempting, in such a place, to call the silence a hush and expect something to follow it. Nothing follows. The warmth comes up through the soles at the same rate. The grey mouths eat; a saucer settles. High above them the three grains shine by virtue of something in the bed in which they are set, and not because it is evening, or morning, or any particular hour at all.
