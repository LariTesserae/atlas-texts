Walking out from the landing, you would swear the country had been rained on an hour ago. The road is dry down the middle, but the little axe nicks in its edging are full of water. Every leaf carries just enough weight to make its tip hang down. There is that extraordinary light after rain in a hot country, when you can see a bird turn its head on a post half a mile away. An hour later the road gives exactly the same impression.

It is a low country, scarcely higher at its highest point than a man standing up in a canoe. Most of it is laid out in great shallow trays. Their rims are walking paths; their bottoms hold water, or beds of leaf mould, or a close green turf so wet it takes the print of the stitching on a sandal. From the landing you can see across a dozen trays to a white storehouse, the sole building in that direction. A grove grows beside it, quite black underneath. Between here and there is an immense allowance of room.

There is no earth. Push a stick down through the turf and it goes through a weave of fine rush, then a layer of nutshells that crackle, then stops on something hard. The tray bottoms are slabs of fired clay, each small enough to have been carried under one arm. This is true of the country as far as anyone has walked. Even the long rise that conceals the eastern distance is sherd laid upon sherd and faced with lime. The people who laid it used their thumbs to smooth the joints. In places the little whorls are sharp enough to take an ink impression from. The rise looks newly finished, although the oldest woman at the landing remembers fitting her thumb into those same marks as a girl and finding, as she does now, that somebody had remarkably small hands.

The planting is lavish where there is any planting at all. Out of a single square of basket-work by the road rise fans big enough to shade a bed, ropes of pepper, fleshy pink cups holding warm water, and a fig with roots laced back into the basket like the ends of a darn. Beyond it are four acres of plain yellow tiling. A narrow runnel winds about on this expanse, making turn after apparently foolish turn to serve one white lily at its far end. Everything the lily needs comes all that way. There is no appreciable current; the pollen on the water lies in long clean lines.

At first there seem to be very few animals. Then the eye learns the scale of their accommodations. Each of the thousand pale knobs on the lily stalk is the back of a little frog. What looks like a fringe sewn around a fan leaf is a row of bats holding on with their toes, their folded wings the reddish brown of oiled wood. Under the walking rims are entire galleries of fish in the shade. They have a habit of lying with one eye just clear of the water, looking sideways across its brilliant skin. In the heat very little calls for muscular effort. A bird on a post can open its beak and let the minute flies walk in after the moisture. You hear the dry click when it shuts.

The odd thing becomes plain if you sit at the runnel and wet your feet. Up close, all the ordinary business goes on. A frog springs off; a fish turns; the rings widen and lift the lily. Look away as far as the storehouse, then look back. The frog is still swimming, but the place on the stalk that its body kept green against the sun is occupied by a frog of precisely that green. Look for the gap in the pollen where your ankle went in. There isn't one. Yet your ankle is yellow to the bone. Nothing here begrudges you what you take, and nothing quite consents to have been taken from.

This is why there are so few houses. One grove gives figs enough for the whole district. One wet square supplies all the rush that people care to split and plait in this heat. At the landing a man sits making a basket out of lengths cut this morning; across the water you can pick out the rush he cut by the pale sweet flesh of its tip, shoulder-high among the others. He will show you the cut but sees no particular interest in it. What interests him is how they got the trays laid in the first place. He has tried lifting one of the little slabs. It comes up readily enough. Under it, fitted to the same space, is the slab, with a coarse hair fired into the glaze near one corner. The one in his hands has the hair too.

By the landing there is a shelter with a roof of split fans and a bench pleasantly damp at either end. People come there to nap in the whiteness of the day. From the bench you can hear, over very long intervals, a drop fall from the roof into a clay saucer. It is practically the only sound that carries from one tray to the next. The saucer is full to a convex lip. Wasps stand all round it drinking, their six little dents apiece in the water; there is room for one more drop, as there has always been.
