The first thing a sounding pole touches is grass. There are six feet of it in some places, all lying one way under the water, so that the pole travels a little sideways before its iron shoe finds the floor. Then comes a small hard knock. A hand resting on the floor a few yards off would feel it. Very little of the sound reaches the surface.

This was a salt coast once. For as long as anyone kept a reckoning here, people carried mud in baskets. They enclosed a piece of the shallows with two wicker fences, tipped the dredgings between them, and faced the seaward side with whatever flat stone they could bring by punt. Inside they made pans for salt, beds for rushes, catchments for sweet water. The only considerable height a person ever attained in this country was the height of a windmill. From such a height one could see other mills, but not the end of the works. There were whole districts where a good straight willow rod was a more useful inheritance than a lump of iron.

It is all under one water now. The old divisions make scarcely any difference to its level, though they still determine nearly everything about its movement. A tide coming inland has to find the openings where the gates were. At one of these openings a single brown blade of weed will stand out horizontal for hours. A few paces to either side, the silt is as fine and undisturbed as the dust inside a box.

In winter there is ice from shore to shore, with snow enough on it to put most of the beds in darkness. The water has a greenish light just under a new crack. It falls on what looks like a woven pavement: long black straps of last summer’s grass, crossing shorter olive straps, crossing the pale narrow leaves that have grown in the shelter of both. There is a great deal of living green down in that nap. Pull it apart and the inner shoots are tender as the heart of a leek. Their roots have gone into the basket-work, where some of the willow has lasted better than the stone. A stem no thicker than a finger still has its bark on the underside. On top it has been worn flush with the mud.

There are deeper places. The navigable cuts remain as long gaps in the grass where the bottom drops out of reach of a pole. Almost nothing grows there. Dead leaves slide over the lip and come to rest in drifts that preserve each fold and curl until the weight of the next fall presses them flat. There are no burrows in these banks, no shoals waiting in the lee of a sill. In clear water at the turn of the tide one can watch a single seed sink the whole way to the bottom without anything coming to meet it.

Out toward the middle of the works, the pans are so broad that the ice has weather of its own. Snow is scoured from one district and laid down over another. Under the bare ice spring begins weeks earlier. A mile-wide bed puts up fresh blades while the next bed, under its white cover, is still living on last year’s roots. When the thaw finally comes, the difference is visible from the surface: a dark rectangle beside a light one, and beyond it another, slightly out of square. The people who set those lines had no means of sighting very far. A bank would start straight, ease off to one side, then take a modest correcting bend when its builders came within view of the bank they meant to join.

Of the mills there are only the footings, low circles of fitted stone with the upper faces hollowed a little by sand. Nothing has fallen far. A gate slab lies a hand’s breadth below its threshold. A broken pot has settled into the bed it was made from. Even where a wall has disappeared, its course can be followed by the grass, which is shorter on the old hard crown and very long on the side that used to be a ditch. In the brief open season a swell passes over both. The long grass heaves up first, the short grass barely stirs, and for a moment a bank that has been level for centuries stands under the water again.
