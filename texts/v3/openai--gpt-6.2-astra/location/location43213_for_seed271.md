The hollow lies just above the level of the river flats. You could walk its length in a minute, though from the upper end you can’t quite see where it finishes: the low bank on the left turns inward and hides the last few yards of floor. There is one tamarisk on the lip of that bank. From below, its thin crown looks quite full against the pale sky. From the flats you can see daylight all the way through it.

Most of the floor is dry. It is made of fine, fawn-colored earth that comes away on a palm, with flat stones set into it so deeply that only their upper faces show. They were once part of the ledge along the right-hand bank. There the rock still lies in beds, a hard gray layer over a softer yellow one, and the yellow has worn back far enough to leave a strip of shade underneath. The air in the hollow is warm, even in that shade. Very little direct sun gets in; the light comes mostly off the bank opposite and gives the gray rock a muted honey color.

Under the ledge is a seep. At first there is only a darkening of the stone, about the height of a hand above the ground. Farther down, the dark band widens, and a few wiry stems of maidenhair hang from a joint. Below them is a basin scarcely wider than a doorstep, its near edge made of three stones laid end to end. Someone took a little trouble over the middle stone. Its ends have been knocked square to fit between the other two, and its top is level enough to rest a full cup on. There is no longer a path to it. The water stands a thumb’s depth behind the sill, clear over yellow grit except in one corner where a film of green clings to the bottom.

What little sound there is comes from here. A bead gathers underneath the lowest fern tip and falls with a small distinct knock into the basin. There is time between drops to hear how thoroughly the banks cut off the wind on the flats. Nothing stirs among the stems. Out beyond the reach of the damp there are two tufts of coarse grass, several yards apart, with last year’s blades still folded into their centers.

Around the turn at the lower end, the hollow opens without any particular threshold. The banks dwindle to the height of a knee. In a long rain, water must leave by this route: there is a narrow tongue of washed grit laid out on the flat. In ordinary weather the seep goes no farther than the sill. A little moisture works through the joints between the three stones and darkens the earth immediately below them. It has done so for long enough that each joint has its own small white rim of lime, and below each rim the same half-moon of damp, no larger than the heel of a hand.
