The lowest door in the ward has a tide mark halfway up its glass. The sea is still close enough to smell, especially in the entrance hall, where the mailboxes have furred green around their locks. But there has been no tide at that door in sixty years. Someone has written, in small figures at the end of the mark, the height of a child.

The ward occupies about as much ground as a covered market. It has a laundry, a school with two classrooms, a public oven, three courts, and apartments for a few hundred people. From the upper landings one can see the level flash of the winter bay through gaps scarcely wider than a hand. Most of the view is of other landings. Windows are staggered so that a room gets its share of sky rather than a look into somebody’s bed. On a clear day there is quite ordinary daylight even at the bottom: a pale square on the oven door, the shadow of a hand going across it for a loaf.

There wasn’t land here when the ward was laid out. The first things installed were the lintels, each held at a surveyed height above the shallows by a pair of little black shoes. The shoes do something to distance. A lintel so fitted remains a lintel’s height above a floor whether there is a floor or not. For years people came to work in boats, ducking under the successive doorheads of the school. They bolted walls to them and hung stairs down from them. There are old photographs of the bottom stair dipping in the chop, with red weed wound around its newel.

Now the interesting work is underfoot. Through the inspection glass in the east court you can see a bed of grey grit, a little shell in it, then another bed of grit at a slight angle to the first. Farther down, the beds are white and solid. The ward is acquiring the ground it would have had to stand on if it had been built in the usual order. This takes a long time. For one whole winter the only change visible through the glass was that the little shell opened and a grain fell in. In the laundry cellar, on the other hand, nearly a foot of blue clay arrived between one washday and the next. They had to raise the tables.

What disconcerts visitors is that the ground comes with its past. A woman taking up a loose paving brick found a plum stone underneath, split and whittled into a whistle. After washing it she could see the nicks where the maker had kept missing the same cut. Nobody in the ward grows plums. In the school there is a drawer for things like this, things too particular to throw away on the grounds that they never happened.

It is cold enough most of the year that the iron handrails wear sleeves of plaited cord. Moisture collects in a few favored places. There will be one wet riser in an otherwise dry staircase, or a bead under a balcony that fattens all morning and drops at lunch. In the north court a leak has begun to cut a channel in the new earth. So far it is no longer than a spoon. Children lie on their stomachs to see where the water will turn when it reaches a certain yellow pebble. Nobody is allowed to move the pebble, not even to settle a bet.

A little black moss grows in the cord on the handrails, on the undersides where palms don’t rub. In the warmth behind the oven flue there are six or seven blades of grass. Once a sandpiper came in through the freight passage and spent an afternoon probing the seam between two paving slabs. Its feet, lifted high at each step, were the color of mustard. So many people stood still to watch it that the lights on the passage timer went out, and somebody had to wave.

For so close-built a place the ward carries very little noise. The black shoes take up most vibrations that pass from wall to wall. You can watch a man on the opposite landing replace a pane of glass and hear only the dry working of your own fingers inside your gloves. Sounds that travel by air do get through. Toward evening, as the oven cools, its sheet-metal hood gives a small pop. Doors open, not because this is any kind of signal, but because by then the bread is cool enough to cut.

At night a low light stays on beneath the inspection glass. From an upstairs window it looks like a lit room in a basement. Sometimes a resident coming home late will stop there with a bag of groceries and look down for a minute before going in. The beds of grit are gradually bowing under the weight of the courts. There is a hairline crack in the white stuff now. Sooner or later the little black shoes can be taken off the lintels; the engineers say the ward will settle less than the thickness of a coin. In the meantime, down in the grainy light, the shell stands open. There is room in it for another grain.
