The low country is pale green and ruled in furrows, and at first the furrows give a false impression of its size. What looks like a furrow a person could step across has a railway laid along its floor. What looks like stubble on the further lip is a stand of chimney trees with little galleries winding up them, green at the foot and white as a pared apple where the upper air has polished them. There is no smoke. Now and then a chimney opens its crown and lets go a column of clear gas. For a mile above it the sky wrinkles, and through the wrinkle one can see the next furrow, usually hidden by the curve of the world.

It is cool down on the railway. The rails are two black runners grafted to a bed of grey felt. Both give a little under the hand; the black stuff has the firmness of a thumbnail, the felt the short dense pile of a peach. Even in the warmth of a shut fist no damp comes out of either. There is a substance the plants use for sap which travels through them as a faintly golden gas. A cut runner smells for a moment of quince. Then the lips of the cut grow white and knit up, and the smell is gone.

Most of what the country makes is light. Flat leaves the size of station roofs are held on edge above the furrows, in such numbers that from below one sees only a succession of bright narrow blades. They turn by fractions, passing the sun from face to face and down into the workings. Go in through any of the doors at the base of a chimney and there is the same extravagant daylight six galleries down, falling on spindle beds, on switchboards with their small hard buds in rows, on a whole reserve of folded track asleep in its rack. Here and there a leaf has been poorly aimed. On the gallery wall opposite it is a white oval worn an inch deep by centuries of afternoons.

There must once have been a great trade in parts. A coupling grown at the eastern rim will fit a haulage vine on the far side of the pole. Each piece comes away with the same shallow notch for a thumb, though nothing living here now has a thumb. The notch is often the last feature to go. There are fields of worn-out machine stock on which a kindly little silver moss feeds, and all through the moss are these hollows, each holding just so much shade. A rare brown moth sleeps in one. Its wings have the fine parallel ridges of the boards on which its ancestors slept; when it flies it shows an astonishing pink underside, a colour for which there seems to be no provision anywhere in the works.

Around midday the trains come through. They carry no light of their own and make so little noise that the first sign of one is the shadow of its load moving along the bank. The wagons have broad petal sides and ride the runners by a travelling muscular contraction, like the passage of a swallow down a throat. They carry bright rolls of leaf, bales of backing felt, occasional loads of new doors still shut in their frames. Where two trains meet, the bank slowly parts and makes a siding. Sometimes it keeps the siding afterwards. The inhabited thickness of the land is full of such accommodations: chambers for loads too wide to pass, elbows put into straight corridors for the sake of one ancient delivery. Their corners have rounded with age. Soft straw-coloured roots hang in the elbows and comb stray gold out of the draught.

Weather comes chiefly from the west, where enormous acreages of leaf shut for servicing at about the same hour. The displaced air moves east as a low, cold front. You can see it coming for much of a morning: a dark green hem drawn across the almost white plain, each leaf showing its back a little before the leaf beside it. The chimney galleries take the pressure off. There is a little ticking in them, as of somebody walking away with grains of sand in the tread of one shoe. It passes. Behind the front every blade stands at a fresh angle; daylight reaches places it has not reached for weeks. The silver moss puts up its minute square flowers all at once.

Distance is less reliable after the front. The country was laid out to keep any two working stations a day's haul apart, and it still observes the specification, though there are fewer working stations than there were. On the railway this is scarcely noticeable. On foot one can spend the afternoon beside a single fallen chimney, making good progress past successive galleries, and look back at evening to see its foot still quite close. Leave the track and the effect goes away. From the top of the bank the fallen tree is perhaps sixty yards long; a brown moth can fly its length in a minute. The long way and the short way cast the same shadow.

At the bottom of the oldest furrows there are inspection wells. Their rims have been worn satin-smooth by opening and shutting. Looking down one, past tier after tier of leaf-lit machinery, one expects at last some foundation different in kind from the rest: gravel, a footing of stone, something on which the first bed of felt was spread. What comes into view instead is the pale underside of another field. Little dark swellings move along it. They are the roots of trains passing overhead in that country, keeping to a timetable which brings one of them under the well precisely as the local train goes by. For an instant the two fields draw snugly together. A breath of quince rises out of the well. Then the bright interval opens again, several miles deep, quiet enough to hear the moth settle back into its notch.
