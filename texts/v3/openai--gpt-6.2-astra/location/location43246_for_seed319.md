After rain, the rails of the high tram stand out dry for a mile. The iron holds yesterday’s heat. Between the rails there are two inches of warm water, quite clear, through which you can read the numbers cast into the sleeper plates. A woman coming off shift walks home on one rail with her lunch tin held out for balance. She has a long way to go. The white blocks just ahead of her are a pumping station; the white blocks where she lives are two stations farther on, almost the same size in the clear air.

The city occupies a shelf halfway up a mountain. From the tram you can look across its whole breadth to the lip, where the tops of clouds sometimes show, rounded and dirty on the underside like heads of cauliflower at the end of market day. Behind the city the mountain continues in long steps of pinkish limestone. Water comes down through the steps rather than over them. It issues from bedding planes in sheets so thin that a wet cliff looks dry until a fly lights on it and goes sliding down, still on its feet.

Almost everything on the shelf has been built on. This is difficult to believe until you get off the tram. What appeared to be open country between the stations is an expanse of paved settling beds, some full, some drained, with a street along the top of each dividing wall. The little humps far out on the limestone are iron inspection covers under a century’s deposit of lime. There are acres of roof here that nobody has had occasion to stand on in living memory. In the older quarters the distinction between a roof and the ground has lost its usefulness. A grocer sweeps water out of his doorway into a groove worn in the crown of a municipal cistern. The water takes the corner past the post office and disappears down a hole originally intended for a lamp standard.

The noise begins well before the first houses. There is no particular moment of entering it. You discover that you are in it when the person beside you on the tram mouths something and you lean close enough to smell the pear she has eaten and still hear nothing she says. Under the shelf, water from the entire back wall of the mountain is dropping through galleries to the power works below. The noise comes up through the beds. A full bed gives out a steep, steady hiss; an empty one booms. Between two empty beds you can feel the cloth of your trouser legs beating against your shins, although the air is perfectly still. At the lip you can see the tailwater departing far below in a broad white elbow. It looks almost motionless. A service launch tied to the bank stays in the same place all afternoon, a minute black stitch in all that white.

The works were cut to take advantage of a fall the water was already making. There are abandoned stairways leading down to natural chambers whose walls are as evenly fluted as the inside of a turbine casing. Elsewhere it is the machinery that has taken on the shape of the rock. Lime has grown over the stationary parts; where a shaft still turns, a delicately exact circular clearance remains. The fit is so good that the engineers have stopped installing collars in certain places. A new bearing runs badly for its first few years. Everyone waits for the mountain to finish it.

Very little grows in the city. In the lee of a parapet there may be a cushion of gray rushes, the stalks hard and jointed and full of sweetish water. Children chew them on the walk from school. One fig tree grows above the hot laundry, its roots occupying a disused conduit all the way to the freight office. It produces six or seven figs a year, splendid to look at and salty enough to spoil a meal. Most of the visible green is a skin on submerged stone, coming loose in the afternoons in slips as fine as cigarette papers. Occasionally a white bird walks a whole settling bed, placing its feet with extravagant care, picking minute things out of that skin. Its reflection is much easier to see than the bird itself.

By four the water in the streets is warm to the ankle. Shutters go up on the shaded sides of the houses. Men sit outside the bathhouse in undershirts playing a game with brass counters on a felt board; the counters have little pegs underneath so that the vibration will not walk them out of place. From across the bed you can tell when somebody wins. All four men lay their palms flat on the table, then one reaches slowly out and sweeps the brass toward him. There is an unexpected ease to people’s movements here. Nothing is gained by slamming a door, calling after a friend, or hurrying to answer a knock. Callers pull a wire that raises a red wooden fish in the kitchen. The fish stays up until somebody has time to come.

Toward evening the low sun finds all the wet edges at once. For a few minutes the plan of the city can be seen with extraordinary precision: the great rectangles, the tram curve, the stair streets descending the face of the shelf, even the outlines of houses taken down generations ago, their foundations still diverting the surface film a finger’s breadth to either side. Then the light goes off the paving and stays only in the high windows. In one of them the woman from the tram is washing her lunch tin. She props it open on the sill to dry. Away below her, over a distance that takes the better part of an hour to walk, a sluice keeper eases a wheel through half a turn. Sometime in the night the bed beneath her window will fill, and without waking she will turn over in her sleep as the note of the room changes.
