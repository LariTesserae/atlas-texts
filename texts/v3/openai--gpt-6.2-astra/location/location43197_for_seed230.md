For thirty paces the street has a roof. The upper town is built against a rib of limestone, and where the rib overhangs, the houses stop just short of it. Their front doors open onto a covered walk with bare rock on one side and whitewashed walls on the other. At either end there is ordinary daylight. Even in the middle it is light enough to sit on a doorstep and pick the stones out of lentils. The air there is cool on the backs of the hands.

The street widens halfway along to make room for a cistern. A seam in the overhang lets out water, very little at a time. Someone long ago cut a groove beneath the seam and set a length of hollow elder in it, so that instead of spreading into a dark stain and being lost, the water falls clear of the wall. A squat stone housing keeps dirt out of the cistern. There is a wooden lid and, beside it, a shelf at hip height where a person can stand a full jar before taking it up on one shoulder. The shelf has two shallow dishes worn into it, a little one and a big one, each smooth enough to show the grain of the stone.

This is as much of a square as the upper town possesses. Four lanes meet here. There is a bakehouse, a room where wool is weighed, and a broad flight of steps going up to another rank of houses whose backs form the wall of the lane above. From below, the place looks densely piled. Up here there is a surprising amount of room. A loaded donkey can turn at the cistern without backing into a doorway. Across the square a woman sits mending a grain sieve, quite alone in a patch of sun large enough to thresh in. She has drawn a loose end of willow through the rim and is working it back under the weave with a horn spoon, the handle dark from her thumb. You can hear it catch and slip. From the bakehouse comes the occasional knock of the wooden peel against the oven mouth.

Beyond the last parapet there is nothing at eye level for a long way. The opposite slopes are lower; one looks down on the pale backs of them and on a footpath that disappears beneath the lip of the town. Wind moving up those slopes passes over the roof of the walk. A few stiff grasses grow where the parapet meets the paving, out of reach of a broom. By the elder spout there is moss, no wider than a hand laid flat. Sometimes a small brown bird comes in at the bright end of the street, drinks from the groove above the spout, and goes out by the same opening. It leaves two fine scratches in the damp lime.

The outer steps have worn low in their middles. When one cracks, the masons lift it with bars and bed it again in grit; they keep the worn side up because the steps on either side are worn to match. Much of the town is maintained in this fashion. The bakehouse has had three thresholds, one set into the hollow of the last. The cistern lid is newer than its hinges. Even the elder spout, which looks of a piece with the moss and the seam, can be pulled out and replaced by a child standing on the jar shelf. In rain the uncovered lanes darken all the way to their edges, but under the overhang the paving stays pale. Long after the rain has passed down into the valley, there is still the same small sound inside the stone housing, a drop falling from no great height into water not far below.
