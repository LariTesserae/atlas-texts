The herons land in the dark. You can tell a new one has come in by the way a whole bed of ferns dips and sheds its drops. Then a long blue foot comes through the fern, feels about for something firm, and takes the weight off the leaves. There is plenty of firm ground here, though none of it is earth.

This is the roost on the warm side of the high ridge. It fills the head of a valley and runs out along both shoulders, thinning at the ends to a few nests in a tree. In the middle the trees are quite buried. Generations of birds have laid sticks across their forks, then across the sticks, then in the hollows where two loads of sticks sagged together. What you might take in the dusk for a hillside is the overlapping margins of these nests. The oldest parts will bear a little grove of fig trees. The newest will scarcely bear the bird that made them. Far below, where the last light still strikes the opposite slope, a single white bird flying home is visible for a long time before it disappears into the black lip of the roost.

Under that lip it is warm enough to smell the peppery oil of a bruised leaf. Ferns grow shoulder high along the walks the birds have worn. There are curtains of fine roots, mats of soft liverwort, creepers with pale flowers that open into the dark interior rather than toward the sky. Moths find them there. The ground under this growth is a deep brown felt of leaf mould, feathers and crumbled wood; here and there, in the worn wall of a gully, a stick still shows the fork that kept it from rolling away when it was first laid down. The gullies are round-bottomed and surprisingly dry. Rain comes in short heavy visits. When it comes, water runs down a thousand sloping twigs and gathers in the low, tight cups of abandoned nests. Days later a tree frog can still sit in one with just its eyes out, while a few feet off the dust is deep enough to take the print of a moth's wing.

There is hardly a moment when it would be possible to hear rain begin. A heron chick makes a noise like a stiff reed dragged across teeth. There are thousands of chicks. Under their rasping goes the full throated racket of the frogs, and under that, almost too steady to distinguish, the whir of insects in the warm cavities of the banks. From time to time all the chicks within reach of one returning bird cry out together. The sound has an edge you feel in your ear long after the bird has folded its wings. Yet down at foot level a snail is going from one side of a feather to the other, grazing the green film off its quill. Very little in the roost is in a hurry to get anywhere.

The broadest clearings are where old sections have fallen through. From the rim of one you can look across a fair stretch of country: low fern-covered rolls, the dark humps of figs, a pale trail of droppings following the crest of a rise. Look down instead and the bank has an underside. Roots hang free into a depth full of flying things. Now and then a loosened twig turns slowly down through them and goes out of sight without a sound anyone could hear.

Such a clearing lasts for years. Birds favour its airy edge for their young; each season the edge thickens a little and leans farther out. At the far end of this one the two sides have nearly met. Already a vine has crossed on the back of a stick, and on the vine, beak tucked into the feathers of its breast, a small rail is sleeping. By the time there is a walk across, there will be no sign of which side was which. The herons will come in at evening as they have always done, set one foot down through the leaves, and test the ground.
