Just under the sheep track, where a hoof has peeled up a corner of turf, there is a room about the size of a bread drawer. It is brighter than the field. Put your cheek to the ground and the first thing you see is the far wall, white as the inside of a broken crock, with a very small red animal walking up it. Then your eye adjusts and you see the country between.

There is a plain of clay, quite smooth except where grit has come down from the roof. Two roots cross it a finger's length apart. In the fork of one root stands a green shoot with its pair of seed leaves still stuck together at the tips. It has been furnished with a great deal of light and very little else. No other green thing has come up. The air down here is cool enough that the underside of each root wears a fine row of beads, though the clay between them has dried to the color of mustard.

The red animal on the wall is a mite. There are not many places a mite can get to on that wall yet. Most of it is still daylight. Here daylight enters through the lifted corner of turf, fills the room, and very slowly sets, beginning at the edges. In places it has the consistency of the skin on cooled milk. The mite tests it with a foreleg and goes on up wherever the skin will bear it. Behind the mite, its tracks stay dark for a little while. This is how you can tell the white substance from chalk, of which there are also several perfectly ordinary chips on the floor.

In one corner is half a doorway. Someone has stood three splinters of snail shell on end and laid a fourth across two of them. The opening admits a whisker of air from a neighboring crack. Every few minutes a sowbug comes through backward, towing a crumb of dark stuff almost big enough to plug the door. There must be a substantial work going on there in the dark. On this side there is the one door and a heap which could still be covered with a daisy petal. The sowbugs leave their loads and go back in. The lintel rocks slightly each time one rubs against it; it makes the little dry tick that, from above, you might take for grit in your ear.

For a while nothing seems to use the plain. Then one of the beads on the roots grows heavy enough to drop. It does not splash. It stands trembling on the clay, a round clear hill with a flattened foot, and inside it the mustard ground appears deeply rutted and immensely far away. Little white springtails come out from under the chalk chips. There are dozens under each. They crowd around the foot of the drop, drinking at the edge, and their bellies turn the color of whatever is behind them. A threadworm comes down the root in the wet track the bead has left. Two pale jaws protrude from a hole, close on a springtail, and withdraw. In a minute the hill is a low lens; in another it is a dark sixpence of mud. The springtails disperse so completely that the distances open up again. One could watch the room at noon on three successive days and describe it quite honestly as an empty place containing two roots.

Toward evening, as the shade of the track moves over the opening, the unset light draws out in long strands. It clings especially to hairs and feelers. Beetles that have spent the day tucked into a seam overhead come down trailing brightness behind them, and for a few minutes you can see where everything is going. A broad strand leads under the shell door. A very fine one runs up the stem of the seedling and through the tiny opening between its leaves. On the high white wall the mite is sitting quite still, all eight feet now on solid ground.

By dark the room is narrower by the thickness of a sheet of paper. The day's light is on the walls, cool and hard and faintly ridged where the animals went over it before it set. There is no wind to scour those ridges and not much water to wear them away. In time the two walls may come together. But tonight one of the seed leaves has pulled free of the other. As it opens, slow and stiff at the joint, it bears against the roof. Above it, on the sheep track, a grain of earth rolls out from under the turf and comes to rest a little way downhill.
