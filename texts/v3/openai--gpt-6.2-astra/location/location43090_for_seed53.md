From below, the pass looks like a single notch cut in a white ridge. Up in the notch there is room to walk about. It is perhaps forty paces long and no wider than a house, with a view at either end into a different kind of weather. To the east the cloud lies far below, flat and dazzling. To the west, wisps come up the cliff and spill over the sill at ankle height. A person crossing from one end to the other would get the cuffs of their trousers wet on an otherwise clear day.

The floor is grey limestone, mostly occupied by fins of the same stone. Some reach to the knee, some well above the head. There are places where a shoulder will fit between two fins but a boot, down where they thicken, will not. Their upper edges have weathered into comfortable round grips. In a few of the narrower passages the facing walls carry matching bands of little white shells; the two halves of an old seabed are still close enough to touch at once. Looking straight down between them, one sees a slot of dense blue shade with a bead of water at the bottom.

For most of the morning the water stays where it is. Frost makes a white nap on the west faces of the fins, right up to a sharp line where the fog gives out. The east faces are dry and almost too bright to look at. Then the sun gets into the notch lengthwise. For a few minutes every west face is hung with drops, each with a bright point well inside it. They grow heavy and run together. Little dark maps spread on the floor. What looks at first like a general wetting of the stone sorts itself into three or four persistent threads, all headed for a hollow under a leaning slab.

Someone has done a little work here. One of the threads would naturally go east, out over the precipice. A groove about the breadth of a thumb turns it back. The groove is rough at its upper end, where the small overlapping blows of a chisel are still distinct; lower down it is as smoothly lined as the inside of a cup. Under the slab stands a low wall of six fitted stones. Behind it cold water gathers, hardly enough to wash in, plenty to drink. There is a flat place for a knee and another for setting something down. From the knee place the whole imposing white ridge disappears. There is just the roof close over one's hair, the water, and one brilliant triangle of sky reflected in it.

Nothing grows even there. The dark fringe at the waterline is powdered stone, fine enough to lift in a little smoke when a drop strikes it. No wings come flickering through the slots. The sound during the thaw is a small succession of knocks under the slab, quite wooden in tone, though there is no wood anywhere in the pass. Out in the open one can barely hear it. The wind mostly passes overhead, drawing a long loose scarf of mist off the western lip without stirring the water behind the wall.

By midafternoon one fin shades the next and the supply stops, beginning at the westernmost groove and ending with a last drop that clings under the roof. A skin forms on the pool, clear at first, then marked with fine white strokes where it grips the six stones. Higher up, water caught in the bedding planes freezes too. Once in a while a scale of limestone comes free and slips edgewise down a slot, making a quick, surprisingly domestic sound, like a plate being put away. There are thousands of these scales packed upright below the fins. Near the top they are sharp and pale. Farther down, where the melt has passed over them summer after summer, their corners are gone, and they fit against one another with scarcely a dark line showing.
