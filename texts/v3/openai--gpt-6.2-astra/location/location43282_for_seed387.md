In the dye works district there is a round iron cover in the pavement that never dries. The wet extends just as far as the raised letters of the foundry name. Below the cover is a shaft, and off the shaft, well below the reach of a man lying on his belly with a lamp, is a room the size of a tram shelter. It is full to the crown of its brick arch with warm water.

Every street in the district has something to do with this room. The long main from the north sheds runs under its floor. The wash houses return their water through a stone throat in the east wall. Three generations of fitters have brought in smaller pipes wherever they could get a drill through, and stopped up other pipes, sometimes very nicely, with threaded brass caps, sometimes by driving in a piece of oak that has since gone as black and hard as the pipe. All this crowds the walls. In the middle there is practically nothing. A grain of white grit crossing the room is an event one could follow from beginning to end, if it ever took the same route twice.

The light comes down the shaft through the hole for the cover key. Most of the day it falls on one brick. At noon for a few minutes it reaches a patch of floor worn pale as the sole of a bare foot. This is where the wash-house return strikes when all its valves are open. The floor there is basalt. It has taken ninety years of rinse water to make a hollow deep enough to lay a thumb in.

The remarkable thing about the place is how little the eye can account for the noise. There will be a concussion fit to bring down the arch, and all that happens is that a shred of joint packing lifts, considers the water, and lies down again. The main carries the beat of the lift pumps from six streets away. A loose flap somewhere under the wash houses claps on its seat with the speed and fury of a cupboard door in a burning house. Through the hanger bolts comes the fine continuous scream of a shaft turning in a dry bearing above ground. In the water these sounds have no distance between them. Even in the lee of the stone throat, where a flake of scale can hang almost motionless, there is no quiet.

At the change of shift the sheds draw hot water in a different order. For perhaps half a minute the whole circulation of the room comes undone. Water that has lain along the ceiling all morning rolls down the west wall, carrying its rusty warmth with it. Two clear streams of different temperatures fold through one another; seen through them, the courses of brickwork come unlaid and slide sideways. The white grain shoots up the shaft into daylight and comes back by the floor. Then the new draw establishes itself. There is a fast road along the crown, a slow one back between the two lowest pipes, and a little wheel of water turning on its side inside the thumb hollow. The boundaries are exact. A thread caught on a bolt may have one end streaming east and the other west for eight hours.

There is no green on anything. Once in a long while a blind, nearly transparent shrimp comes in from the old well line. It works its way along the mortar with its forelegs, eating the soft grey skin that grows around the oak plugs. What keeps it there is the roughness of the brick: close enough to touch the wall, it can walk; a feeler’s length out, it would be gone. Sometimes the small cast shell remains caught in the mortar after the animal has moved on. The water opens and closes it gently by the split along its back.

Up on the pavement the cover gives a slight kick under the heel at ten past two. People who take that way home know the spot. In cold weather a shoe left on it while its owner laces the other one will be warm through the sole. Nothing else comes up from the room except a bead of water through the keyhole, which swells at each stroke of the north pumps and spills a little farther round the letters.
