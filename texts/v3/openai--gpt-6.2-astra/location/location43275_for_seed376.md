The wind has combed the top of the pass down to a nap. All the leaves there are thumb-long, silver on the back, and they lie so neatly one over the next that a drop of water can run a hundred yards downhill without touching a stem. Beyond the pass the ground falls away in broad steps, right down to the blue margin of a lake. Silver changes to bottle green and then to the rusty color of last year’s needles. It looks like a good place to see the weather coming. Often what looks like a squall crossing the lower valley is something the valley itself is doing.

There is rock under all this, but it shows only in the highest notch, a tooth of grey stone with a seam of pink through it. Everywhere else the stuff that takes the weight is woody. Roots as thick as thighs lie across the slope, level after level of them, their uphill sides packed with leaf mould. On the downhill side they have a lip worn smooth as a sled runner. Every spring for a very long time a little ice has gone over that lip carrying a little grit. Smaller plants live in the long hollows behind the roots. There is a moss that grows in tight apricot knobs and is dry and warm to the touch even when the shade beside it is white with frost. There are narrow-leaved things whose flowers hang *under* the lip, pale little purses in the dark.

Most of the work goes on there, out of the light. The big roots are hollow in sections. At one end of each section is a collar of muscle-like green tissue, wonderfully slow to tighten and very quick to let go. In the cold hours before sunrise the leaves on the pass collect a fine nap of ice. When the sun clears the notch, melt goes ticking down through the leaves. A collar swells shut behind each charge of water; the next collar opens ahead. By midmorning the whole sunny side is pumping. It takes the yield of an immense area of nearly dry leaf to put a trickle in one root. Yet down by the lake there are bladders big enough to float a fallen tree, strained almost transparent with the morning’s delivery.

Seen from the pass at that hour, the valley is in extraordinary motion. A dark band goes across the silver as thousands of leaves stand on edge. Three more bands follow at different speeds. A green terrace several acres wide rises in the middle, splits, and lays its two halves down with their yellow undersides exposed. The long snag that has stood crooked on the lake margin all winter travels, still upright, the width of a meadow. Under it a full bladder is rolling into an empty space, squeezing the next one aside. In the wake of that disturbance whole beds of the little purse flowers come out into daylight, bobbing on their threads. By the time the shadow of the notch reaches them they will be under cover again.

There is hardly a sound. The leaves are felted where they rub. The roots move in beds of wet pulp, and the moving parts fit one another because they have grown one another’s shape. Close up there is sometimes a damp kiss at the opening of a collar. Farther off, nothing. One can watch a mile of hillside turn over in the time it takes a small grey bird to scrutinize a bud, take something off it, and wipe its beak. The bird makes the more distinct noise.

Towards evening the water has settled into scattered pockets. The lake itself, which from above looked quite deep and blue, proves to be a few inches of clear water held on a dark raft of stems. Here and there a stem has put up a red tip, and each tip has its own little ring of ice by sundown. In a dry week the rings last after the water is gone. They hang there like loose glass washers until a warm morning drops them all.

The old shapes of the valley persist through all this business. The silver saddle is where it was; the broad steps still catch the low light on their rounded brows. Even the snag comes back nearly to its former place as the bladders empty. Only a fine line of yellow flowers, caught this time on the wrong side of a closing fold, stays out on the open slope. In the last light they make visible, all the way from the pass to the lake, one of the seams along which this apparently solid country gives.
