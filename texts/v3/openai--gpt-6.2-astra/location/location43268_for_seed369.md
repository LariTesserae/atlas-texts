Halfway round the high shoulder of the mountain there is a shelf wide enough for three trees. They grow a good distance apart. From the foot of any one of them you can see the whole shelf: the low bank at its back, green to the top, the pale floor with its few islands of earth, the lip beyond which there is nothing green for a very long way down. Cloud moves below the lip. On a clear afternoon you can look over it into the red forks of a dry riverbed.

Up here the water comes out sideways. A seam about knee-high runs the length of the bank, and all along it beads of water swell in the moss until their weight draws a little green tongue down. Then the bead is gone and the tongue lifts. There are thousands of these, each keeping its own time. Below them the bank is furred with fine roots the colour of unbleached silk. The air there smells of wet bark and has the warmth of the inside of a folded leaf. Half a dozen steps away, out on the bare stone, the sun is strong enough to dry your shoulders.

The trees have short trunks and immense crowns. All the vegetable extravagance of the place has gone into those crowns and the narrow bank. Ferns grow in the forks, and up through the ferns grow soft canes with sprays of round orange fruit, so translucent that the seeds show through as dark commas. Old leaves remain caught up there until they are earth. A tree can shed a handful of quite good black soil in a high wind. Where that soil lands on the shelf it makes a little fan; where it lands in one of the shallow hollows it stays, and a patch of bright flat grass grows there, abruptly bounded, like the hair on the back of a knuckle. The rest is open stone. You can follow a single fallen orange fruit with your eye almost anywhere it rolls.

The surprising thing is the volume of the water. There is hardly enough moving in sight to carry a leaf, yet the place is full of a deep pouring sound. Under the shelf are cavities worn smooth inside, with mouths opening on the precipice. The little rills find cracks in the floor and fall through into those rooms. Along the outer lip the sound comes up through the soles of your feet; a few paces back it seems to issue from the leafy crowns overhead. Sometimes two of the cavities sound together, very nearly on one note. A slow throbbing then passes over the whole shelf. Loose seeds tremble in their husks. When one cavity fills to its outlet the throbbing stops, and what had seemed a great commotion resolves into the small distinct tick of drops on stone.

There is a bird that visits for the fruit, slate-coloured except for the bare yellow skin round its eyes. Most days there is no bird. Its visits are known by the little crescents of peel stuck to the rock, which dry in the sun and lift at both tips. The seeds lodge farther off, in seams too thin to admit a root for more than a season. Every summer a few pairs of tender leaves stand out in the middle of all that pale space. Every summer they disappear.

Toward evening the shadow of the mountain climbs up from below and touches the underside of the shelf long before the light goes off the trees. For an hour the bank is cool and dripping and the highest orange fruit still has sunlight passing clean through it. Then the bare floor begins to darken in long wandering lines. Water that was vanishing into the warm rock all afternoon can travel a little farther now. The lines join, bend round the grass islands, reach the lip. By last light a fringe of drops hangs there above the valley, each with a minute upside-down crown of leaves inside it, each taking a long while to fall.
