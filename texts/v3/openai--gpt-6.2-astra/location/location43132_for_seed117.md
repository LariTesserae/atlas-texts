At the foot of the slipway there is a pocket of shore that keeps its water even when the tide is out. You could throw a glove across it. A coat button dropped here, on the other hand, might travel quite a distance. The floor is blue limestone, worn into an assembly of cups with holes in their sides. What looks like the bottom of a cup is often the roof of a larger one. At the lowest tide you can put your cheek near a hole no bigger than a buttonhole and feel a steady draught coming up, cold and smelling of pennies.

A repair shed once stood over the pocket. Three cast-iron feet remain, and a length of roof caught between the slipway and the shore wall. This makes a low ceiling over perhaps half the water. The rest gets the thin daylight that comes down between the high sides of the slip. Under the roof the light is mostly secondhand: a moving white patch on the underside of a flange, a nickel gleam far back in a hole. Rain has been coming through the same rivet holes for so long that each one has a dimple waiting for it in the stone below. A few of the dimples have met and run together. Others still hold exactly one drop, trembling in its own skin.

There is scarcely any green. Where fresh water seeps through the shore wall it brings up a gray paste, very fine, which the little snails eat. You can tell a snail's age roughly by the breadth of the clean track it leaves. The widest tracks stop at the edge of the salt water; the narrow ones go blundering in and double back. There are mussels the color of stove lids packed upright in every seam, with a second, smaller generation growing on their hinges. In the spaces between them live translucent shrimp. For much of the day you see only their work: a crumb going sideways up a steep face, resting, going on. Even the apparently bare stone has feelers laid flat along it, each in a groove of its own.

The last piece of machinery is a tide float inside one of the iron feet. It used to run an indicator in the shed. The dial went away with the walls, but the float still lifts a greasy rod about the thickness of a knitting needle. Shortly before the sea reaches the mouth of the pocket, water comes in underground and the rod rises. It gives a little tick where a bent part passes the guide. In the still air under the roof it's as distinct as somebody setting down a cup.

At that sound the rock comes loose in places. What seemed to be six or seven chips of limestone kick off and hang in the water: little flatfish, pale on both sides from living here. The shrimp come out too. For a minute there seems to be some mistake in this arrangement, prey and hunters alike deserting good cover. Then the first charge of sea water arrives through the floor. It drives old grit, shell splinters and a considerable number of worms out of the cups. The flatfish take the worms in midwater. The shrimp settle behind them to pick up what their mouths have cut in two. By the time the tide comes openly over the sill, most of the feeding is finished. Nothing much accompanies it but the faint sandy sizzle of the cups clearing their throats.

Once flooded, the pocket looks simpler. The rock partitions disappear under a single sheet of pewter-colored water, and the three iron feet might be bollards on an ordinary quay. Only in one corner, where the roof dips low, is there any sign of the country underneath. Air works out of the drowned chambers there, a bubble at a time. The bubbles creep along the underside of the corrugations and gather at the torn edge in bright rows. Long after the rain has stopped, long after the sea outside has turned and begun to fall, the edge goes on shedding them.
