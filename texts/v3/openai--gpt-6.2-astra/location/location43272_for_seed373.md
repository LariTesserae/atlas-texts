There are three ways onto the shelf, all cut as flights of steps in the pale rock. Two come up through the floor and have little collars of brass around them. The third comes round the outside of the cliff. On that one you can follow the weathering of a single step from the place under the overhang where the chisel marks are still sharp to the outer corner where a million years of blown grit have taken the corner off altogether. At the head of the stairs is a space about the size of a village green. It looks quite empty. Then the ground six feet in front of you goes over like the page of a book, and you see the garden on the other side.

The shelf is a bed of mica. Not mica as it occurs elsewhere in the mountains, in bright flakes that stick to your thumb, but great leaves of it, some as big as a roof, attached to the mountain along one edge. The garden grows on the undersides. There are soft green brushes, crimson fans with white margins, lengths of yellow fleece so thick that little paths have been worn through them. In the cold clear daylight you can see the shadows of the paths through the mica before the wind lifts it. Almost everything alive here lives within a hand’s breadth of a surface. Above that narrow green country there is half a mile of perfectly empty blue to the nearest thing, a tooth of rock on the other side of the pass.

Nothing drinks. If you pinch a green brush it comes apart into fine cool wires, with a smell like a pocketful of change. Each wire has a split foot, one toe touching the mica and the other a dark grain trapped in it. The brushes live on the small persistent difference between the two. In old growth the grains have been eaten clean away. There the fleece is a pale buttery colour, full of grubs that roll up scraps of exhausted root and push them over the edge. They are prodigious little housekeepers. Below the cliff is a fan of their tailings, pink in the distance, big enough to have turned the course of a glacier back when there were glaciers.

The noise at the top of the steps is almost unbearable. The wind by itself makes very little. It gets under one leaf and lifts it; the leaf steals the wind from its neighbour, which falls flat. The blow passes through the bed, and twenty yards away another leaf springs up, throwing a handful of red animals into the light. There is a deep concussion you feel in your heels and on top of it the ripping, rattling treble of all the little leaves. Conversations here are conducted by writing with a finger in the dust on the stair collars. Most of the writing is the same two or three names, and below the names drawings of feet pointing one way or another.

The red animals land running. They have six feet apiece and nearly circular bodies and fit the little paths exactly. For a while they all run the same way. Then a shadow crosses them and they part down the middle to let through a long white creature on legs like eyelashes. It goes by carrying a piece of green brush many times the size of its head. Further in, where two leaves have stayed shut for perhaps a century, there are whole generations of animals that have never had to hold on. When that seam opens, what shows for an instant is not green at all but a fur of tiny cream backs, and then they have all gone round to the dark again.

At the outer lip stands a single narrow building of grey blocks. The roof has slipped down inside it. On the surviving lintel are three black studs, warm to the touch, and in the lee of each stud a little parcel of air is still having yesterday’s weather. Dust goes into one of these places, describes the same short hook over and over, and comes out hours later. Nobody has managed to enlarge them. They are just large enough for the gold moths to lay their eggs in while the rest of the shelf is in an uproar. You can watch a moth feel for the boundary with one antenna, back herself carefully across it, and become, from the waist down, perfectly still.

From the ridge opposite, the whole disturbance looks quite gentle: a silver patch turning green, a green patch turning silver. The outline scarcely alters. The same three notches have been drawn in maps of the lip for as long as there have been maps. Up close there isn’t a square yard you could count on to stay the same while you crossed it. Even on the sheltered stairs, with your back against solid mountain, something will touch the back of your hand: a scrap of yellow fleece, travelling fast, with a family of red runners hanging on to it and one of them already trying to make a path.
