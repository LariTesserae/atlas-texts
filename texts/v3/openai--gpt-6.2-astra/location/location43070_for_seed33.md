The first street is easy to miss. It goes along the crown of a ridge, a pale strip the width of a cart, with a lip on either side where the powder has worked its way out of the paving. Beyond the lip on the left is a drop of perhaps three feet, onto a shelf strewn with chips. Beyond the one on the right you can see the curvature of the world. The air down there is brown, banded with other browns, and so far below that the weather in it looks like a property of the stone you are standing on: a vein of umber that very slowly forks.

Go along the street a little way and it becomes clear that this is a city. There are side streets. There are little courts set back from them in groups of four, each with a high blind wall to take the west wind, each with a stone seat in the lee. Across a mile of gulf you can pick out exactly the same arrangement on the next ridge. You can follow it, ridge to ridge, quite a long way south. There is a bend repeated in all the streets, even on those ridges where a straight course would have been easier. Something once stood in the way of that bend, or still does.

The stone here is a dull violet on a fresh break, biscuit-colored where it has been exposed. The seats are worn into shallow saddles. Grit travels along them all afternoon, creeping up one end, spilling off the other. The wear goes on under roofs and inside closed rooms; if you put your ear against a wall you hear the grains passing on the inner side, with a sound like a thumb moving over the page of a book. There is no rain to give the dust a crust. It gathers in deep, exquisitely loose drifts from which the least disturbance will release a single straight column of dust, rising intact until its foot leaves the ground.

In the middle of the day the light improves enough to see the bridges. Most of them are very short: two or three paving stones projecting over an abyss, with two or three more to receive them on a cliff so remote that its whole face will fit behind a fingernail. For a few minutes, as the dim sun goes behind the upper ridge, the intervening distance wears thin. It wears unevenly. First the far abutment is close enough to see a crack in it, then half of it is close enough to touch, while the other half remains a blue speck. A narrow animal waits here, folded on six chalky feet. It is as flat as a sandal and almost the same color as the road. When the crack runs all the way across, the animal steps over. There is no leap. The great brown depths are still perfectly visible under its belly.

This is how the city was made. Nothing quarried the rock or laid out the courts. For ages certain places have been arriving beside certain other places and rubbing against them for part of the day. The paving stones are flat because they have had other paving stones pressed to them. The little courts are the impressions of towers on a ridge beyond sight; the seats are what is left of the towers’ cornices. A whole quarter on the east is a descending flight of broad steps, each step worn by the underside of the one above it. At dusk they lie briefly flush and make a square. It is a handsome piece of civic space, wide enough for a multitude, with a warm patch at one corner where two south-facing walls have spent the afternoon against each other. Usually there is nothing in it.

There is a living skin in that warm corner. In daylight it contracts into pepper-colored points in the pores of the stone. As the square forms, the points open, each into a soft black mouth smaller than the head of a pin. They eat the pale mineral flour still warm from the grinding of the streets. Now and then one of the flat animals comes to graze, moving its chin sideways along the wall. The track it leaves is violet and clean, and lasts for years. On some walls you can count the visits: three tracks at the foot of a cornice, a fourth high overhead from a time when a different street came here. Nothing grows tall. There is no advantage to height in a place where the sky above a thing may, for an hour each evening, be a ceiling fitted to within a grain of its back.

By night the ridges have drawn apart again. Cold comes out of the paving through the soles of the feet. A few chips, loosened during the day, tip off an edge and turn slowly down into the thicker air. No sound comes back. From here the city seems less a city of houses than one of thresholds: miles and miles of places worn smooth by meeting, with scarcely anything obliged to stay. But on the first ridge, in the sheltered seat beside the road, there is a small detail that makes it feel inhabited. One of the animals has folded itself there for the night. Its six feet fit into six hollows in the stone. They are deeper than the marks of a single season.
