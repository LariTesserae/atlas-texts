To find the place you would have to lift the sorrel, dig down past its white runners, and split what looks like a second, much paler root. It would be a pity to do this. The root is hollow. While it is closed, there is a whole afternoon inside it.

The hollow is about the size of a plum. Two lobes that used to fit together have grown steadily apart, leaving a valley with a high wall at either end. You can still match the notch on one wall to the tooth on the other. Nothing has yet blunted the tooth. Even the little pock in its tip has its answering bead on the opposite slope. The slopes are cream-colored, turning green toward their tops, where light comes through the flesh. There is enough light to see the finest things by: a split in a fiber, the shadow of a hair, the faint yellow dust a bud leaves behind when it changes its mind about opening. The light is so ordinary one keeps looking for the way out to it.

Everything that looks like ground here is capable of growing over a cut. What looks like loose gravel at the foot of the slope is a bed of hard little seeds, each held to the next by a nearly invisible neck. In the warm hours they roll a fraction against one another without coming free. The bed settles with a grinding noise. Higher up, a whole sheet of pale rind peels back and goes green on its underside. This takes several days. For all that time its edge gives off a loud, intermittent ripping sound, as if someone just beyond sight were struggling with a particularly long piece of cloth.

There is very little *in* the valley. No thicket rises from its floor. No vines cross the intervening air. Growth takes place almost entirely away from the center, into the surrounding root, and whatever space the lobes win from one another stays empty. A single gnat came out of the seed bed once. Its passage from slope to slope was an event: for a moment the eye had something by which to judge the distance. It flew for most of an hour. On the return trip it scarcely had room to spread its wings. Distance here seems to be a property of departures. Going back to the exact spot you left costs almost nothing.

The rain makes use of this. Moisture beads on the cooler tooth and sometimes a drop gets heavy enough to fall. It goes down slowly, bright and slightly out of round, through a prodigious depth of warm air. There is time for its shadow to swell on the seeds below. Then comes the slap, astonishingly loud. Most of the water goes straight back to the tooth; you can see it hanging there while the seeds are still rocking. A little stays behind in their seams. That little is enough. By evening there will be half a dozen green points along the course the splash took, and by morning they will have bent down and put their tips into the floor. In this way the floor gets thicker without the valley ever filling up.

On the wall beneath the notch, a patch of tissue has learned a simple use for one of its own thorns. As the patch swells it presses the thorn sideways into the rind; as it slackens the thorn slips free. A new slit opens every few days, just deep enough to bleed a clear gum. Nothing comes to eat the gum. It dries in an amber lip that hums when the wall is under strain. There are lips of several ages there now. Just before the lobes move apart again, you can hear the strain pass down the wall, first a high note, then two low ones sounding together, then a crack that sets the whole seed bed rolling.

Afterward there is a still interval. The newly exposed strip at the bottom of the wall is almost white. It has never been part of a surface before. Warmth brings a faint pink into it, and the two sides draw apart by another hair. Above them the sorrel stands in a handbreadth of poor soil, its three leaves folding at the first touch of evening cool. Down here that much movement of a stem sends a long green shadow sweeping from one end of the valley to the other. The light clears. The drop is still on the tooth.
