There is a shelf of pale stone high on the warm side of the ridge. It takes perhaps a hundred paces to cross. From below, when the cloud lifts, it looks quite level. Up here it has the shape of a leaf held in the palm, a little hollow along the middle and a slow twist toward the tip. The pools at one end catch the morning sun while those at the other are still reflecting the green underside of a rock ledge.

Water comes up through the floor of the pools. You can see where it enters because the grains of white sand at the bottom stand on end, quiver a little, and lie down pointing another way. For all that comes in, very little seems to go out. The lip of the shelf is thick and rounded, perpetually dark along its lower edge. Water eases over it and follows the curve of the stone out of sight. A whole afternoon's overflow can pass without the sound of a drop.

Most of the shelf is open. The green is gathered in perhaps half a dozen places where the rock has worn through to a soft red seam. There it grows deep: ferns with stalks as thick as thumbs, beds of moss into which an entire fallen frond can sink, little trees with smooth, slightly sweating bark. Each bed has an abrupt margin. A fern will lean far enough over bare stone to shade a patch of it the size of a pool, but no seedling takes hold in the shade. Between one bed and the next there is room for the sun to heat the stone and bring out its faint smell of wet chalk. Nothing stirs under the leaves. Even the oldest fronds, long since turned the color of tea, keep the fine lace of their edges intact.

Late in the day the pools begin to empty uphill. First a silver line runs out from a pool toward the nearest bed of green. Other lines join it, fine enough that the stone still feels dry if touched between them. They follow almost invisible grooves, each one ending at a pale, exposed root. The roots darken. A little later the fern stalks straighten, lifting their whole weight of leaf scarcely the width of a finger. The grooves are very old. Where a tree has died and the moss has closed over its place, water still climbs to the spot and stands there in a clear bead until morning.

At the tip of the shelf the rock has weathered thin enough to let light through. In the last hour of sun a band of honey color appears underfoot, with the shadows of buried seams suspended in it. Here, once in a long while, there is a sound: a single dry tick as a flake parts from the rim. It will stay where it is through many wet seasons, rocking in its hollow when the stone warms, wearing both its own underside and the hollow smooth. Beyond it there is nothing at the same height for a long way. The cloud lies low enough now to show the tops of distant ridges. On the shelf the pools have gone shallow and warm, and in each, uncannily distinct in the quiet light, a few grains of sand stand up.
