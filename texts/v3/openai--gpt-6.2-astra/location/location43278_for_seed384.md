The lower ward fits inside a ring of stone about the size of a gas holder. From the top it looks like a well in the sea floor, with a few iron rungs going down the inside and then a depth to which the eye doesn’t follow. Ice covers the sea nine months of the year. In the other three there is usually enough light to see the first landing, where a blue enamel sign asks people coming up to give way to people going down.

There are six streets in the well. They used to be terraces, with houses cut back into the stone and a common stair that went round and round the wall. Whole families lived above their place of work, which in this case meant below the next family’s place of work. A little of that inconvenience is still apparent. A great cast-iron bend from the refrigerating plant passes through the long front room of number eight. Somebody fitted a shelf to the bend and tiled the wall behind it in yellow. There are four brass hooks for cups.

Everything is under water now, even the insides of the bulbs. The water is very cold and so clear in places that a shop window, its glass long gone, still seems to have glass in it. Elsewhere clarity ends at a level line. Put a hand through that line and the fingers grow wide and run together. It is the top of a pool of brine. There are dozens of these pools hung at different heights in the ward, wherever a level floor and a decent doorsill have survived. The sill of the public baths has worn down in the middle, and brine goes over it in a glossy tongue, down the steps and into the ordinary seawater of the street. A grain of plaster takes a long time to fall through that tongue. For a moment on each step it goes uphill.

Most of the plant is still working. There is a difference in pressure between the sea inside the ring and the sea outside, which the old engineers took off through a pair of galleries in the rock. On a rising tide the lower gallery drives a wheel; on a falling tide the upper one does. The changeover takes nearly an hour. A ponderous leather flap lifts from its seat, showing an underside the pale pink of a heel. Belts advance the width of a nail. In the meter house a row of black figures turn over behind their little panes, faithfully charging the ward for the power it makes.

Very little of this can be heard. The belts are wet, and the wheels run in beds of silt worn to their precise dimensions. Now and then there is the small, intimate sound of two plates settling together in a rack. It travels through the handrail. Hold the rail at the first landing long enough and you will feel it in your wrist.

The charge on the meters is mostly for light. At any one time there is enough current to light one street, very poorly. A distributor at the foot of the well gives each its turn. For several minutes the yellow tiles of number eight are visible; the cup hooks throw four long shadows up the wall. Then the light is down at the baths, picking out the white depth marks and the dark little cubicles where people once went to be private about taking off their clothes. In the lit water one sees how little space there is between things. A drain discharges against the back of a clock. The stair has been notched to clear a balcony. Anywhere a man might once have squeezed sideways, the sea has packed some additional substance: flakes of zinc, wool from pipe lagging, silt fine as face powder, all laid down in legible bands.

Only at the lip of the well is there anything green, a short nap on the stone that lies flat at high water. One white fish lives on the second landing, or several white fish use it singly. When the light comes it eases itself into the hollow worn by ascending feet. There is room there for its whole body, with the tail turned a little to one side.

Below that, things are kept clean by the brine. On the lowest street it stands chest-high and perfectly dark. The doors swell shut above its surface and shrink below it, so each opens, if at all, by bending in the middle. Behind the last of them is the distributor. Its arm goes slowly from stud to stud in a circle scored deep into the copper. There was once a seventh stud, for a street the engineers intended to cut and never did. The arm still stops there. For that interval the well is dark from top to bottom; the white fish leaves its footprint, and all the electricity of the ward goes out through two capped wires into a little warmth in the stone.
