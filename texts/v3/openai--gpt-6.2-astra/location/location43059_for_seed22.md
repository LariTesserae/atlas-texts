From the survey stair you can see clear across the low country to another stair, thirty miles off. In the dimness it looks like a stitch of white thread. Between the two there seems to be nothing that could account for the noise. You have to put your mouth to somebody’s ear to tell them where to look.

The plain was a bay until quite recently. Things that lived under water are still standing in the positions in which they died: fans of brittle lime, the spires of enormous tube worms, a reef with one smooth face where the tide used to rub its shoulder. The bay floor came up in long level strips. Between one strip and the next there is generally a drop of a foot or two, with edges sharp enough to take the rubber off a wheel. Here and there the uplift pulled a seam apart instead. A seam can run straight for miles, then turn aside to accommodate something as small as a buried shell. Most are too narrow to fall into. A few contain whole districts of the city.

You come on a district almost without warning. First there is a line of swallows going down into the ground. Then the surface runs out at your feet, and across an interval of warm blue air a woman is out on a balcony beating flour from her apron. Below her are eight or nine storeys of windows, striped awnings, a school yard with its ball net drawn tight over the top. Below these, where the walls draw close enough to shade each other all day, are the gardens. The leaves are so big and so densely fitted together that a dropped coin may lie for years within sight of its owner. Little long-fingered animals make a living turning such leaves over. They pick off the snails and let everything else fall. In the lower town it is considered lucky, but by no means extraordinary, to be struck by money.

There is very little water to spare. Most mornings the city gets its allowance as a bloom of grey beads on the underside of the plain. The roots up there have grown flat, with the delicate branching pattern of frost on a pane. By breakfast they have taken the beads in. Once or twice a season rain falls out on the exposed lime and darkens it in great travelling patches; people go up in lifts with their collars open for the pleasure of being wet all over. Within an hour the rock is dry again. Yet down in the seams there are tree ferns, gourds furry as peaches, banks of red flowers with standing room in each throat for a small bird. In the grocers’ quarter they have to cut the growth back from the lamps twice a week.

What you hear on the stair is the city making its crossings. When the bay rose, the opposite halves of certain shells remained in contact in a way not quite accounted for by their positions. Tap one and its partner rings, sometimes on the far side of the plain. The first crossings were hardly more than speaking tubes. Now there are tiled municipal thresholds through which a cart loaded with melons can pass entire, coming out thirty miles away with its wheels still rattling on the same four loose tiles. For the instant of passage, though, the intervening stone has to take up the length of the journey and give it back. You can see it happening from above. A pale shiver travels along a terrace. Dust stands up in a line. Then comes a sustained brazen bawl that you feel through the soft parts of your face. On a market morning there may be six hundred crossings in an hour. The districts have agreed on a silent interval at noon, principally for the sake of the schools.

It is worth staying up on the plain for that interval. The last load goes through; the stone gives a few diminishing ticks. For the first time you can hear the dry patter which has been going on all along at the bottom of the survey stair. Out of holes no wider than nail heads, thousands of amber beetles are backing into the feeble daylight, each towing a little white parcel of fungus to air. On the next ledge a fox the colour of the lime is waiting with its chin between its paws. It does not trouble with the beetles. It is watching their holes for the quick green tongues of the lizards that eat them.

Far out, halfway to the other stair, one dark tree stands on the skyline. In the hush you hear its birds: a rich, disorderly chuckling, as though a large household were just waking up. Then the tree thins, parts in the middle, and goes streaming away in two directions. There was never a tree there. The flocks settle out of sight in separate seams. At noon precisely a child somewhere below begins to play a scale on a horn, gets the seventh note wrong, and has time to try it twice more before the stone starts up again.
