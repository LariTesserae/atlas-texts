The flat part of the mountain is the wettest. Rain goes quickly off the enclosing peaks, leaving them pale and almost dry even under a cloud. It comes out again on the high shelf between them. There the limestone is dark to a depth of several feet. A broken face shows the line: dove gray above, blue below, as distinct as the waterline on a bathing animal.

From the western shoulder the shelf looks like a single level of silver, four or five miles across. At its far end it narrows and turns out of sight behind a bluff. Down on the shelf there is hardly a level place the size of a hand. The stone is divided into close-set backs with rounded crowns, each with a runnel pressed up against the next. Some backs are long enough to follow for half a mile. Others end bluntly after a few paces, and their two runnels join around the nose in a little thickening of clear water. The sun picks out every crown. In the spaces between, it lights up the bottoms of the streams so well that their depth is difficult to judge. What looks like a shallow white scratch can be a cleft into which a leg would go up to the hip.

There is no single fall to account for the noise. Under the backs are chambers, and water pouring through the chambers moves a great deal of air. At every narrow opening there is a rough aspirated note. Water dropping from one level to the next adds the harder sound of stones being emptied out in loads. Across the whole shelf these sounds are so numerous and so nearly continuous that a cry would carry no distance at all. Yet within an inch or two of the ear one can hear a single drop strike. A lip of rock shelters a patch of air; incline a little toward it and the drop is there, small and hollow and distinct. Lift the head and the mountain shuts over it again.

It is cool even on the most brilliant afternoons. The water has been a long time inside the peaks. Where it wells out it brings up pale grains that dance at the mouth of the spring, sinking at the sides, rising again in the middle. Nothing takes root among them. There is no turf on the crowns, no trailing weed in the channels, not so much as a loose leaf to travel the length of a runnel and show how fast it goes. The few dark stains are iron. They continue into the stone wherever a crown has split.

In one of the deeper pools a little colorless shrimp sometimes comes up from below. Seen from overhead it is mostly a pair of curved shadows, one cast by its body and one by the long feeler with which it keeps contact with the wall. It works its way along the seam where a thin skin of bacteria grows in the mineral seep. A few inches on, the seam dips under an overhang and the shrimp goes with it. There may be a considerable inhabited depth to the shelf. Very little of it ever has occasion to come into the light.

At the eastern bluff the water runs sideways into a bedding plane and disappears, all but a trickle that slips over the edge. From there one can see the lower clouds passing far down in the valley. Up here the flood is always at much the same height. The mountain holds whole seasons of rain in its interior and gives them up through the same small mouths; limestone worn away on the backs is laid down again in smooth pale collars at the springs. It would take a very long acquaintance to notice that a collar had grown. Even the dancing grains have their appointed little hollow. What first seems a place in the midst of some immense event—the glare, the din, the ground brimful of running water—is the mountain in its settled condition. On the quietest day it has, it sounds like this.
