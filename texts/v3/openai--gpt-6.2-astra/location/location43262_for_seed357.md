From a distance the country looks recently ploughed. Long green ridges run across it, each trailing a brown line of water on its uphill side. There is a mile or more between one ridge and the next. Only at the eastern edge, where a great bite is missing, can you see what the furrows are made of. They are folds in a leaf bed nearly a hundred feet thick. Its torn edge hangs out in ordinary daylight, layer under pale green layer, and a shower falls from it into clouds so far below that the drops disappear before they reach them.

Up here it is hot enough that rain on the broad flats gives off a smell of vegetables lifted from a pot. A little wind goes a long way. There are few upright things to interrupt it: a stand of tall cups on one horizon, a white stalk on another. Everything else grows sideways. The leaf you put your foot on may be half a mile long, grey with sun on its upper face and lined on the underside with short, wet roots that take hold of the leaf beneath. It is possible to cross an entire watershed without stepping off it. What looks like cropped grass in the distance is the nap of its skin.

The ground keeps making ground. Along the rim of the missing section, fresh runners extend into the air. They sag under their own weight until the wind blows two together; their damp tips adhere. A web collects between them, at first so fine that rain goes straight through. Then come the little leaves, round as coins, each one overlapping the gap between two others. Water begins to stand on them. By the time a pool is deep enough to reflect the sky, there will be another web growing in its shade. Nothing here remembers the shape of the land underneath. There may not be any land underneath. The oldest layer visible in the cut is as neatly joined as the newest one.

Water travels badly in this country. It gathers behind a fold for days until there is a long warm lake with a clean shore, and the cups on the horizon stand upside down in it. Then some afternoon, under a perfectly clear sky, a seam lets go. The first sound is a sticky succession of pops. The lake narrows visibly; a moving hump passes under the plain. Far off, a sheet of water comes up through the turf, white at the base, brown at the crest, high enough to hide the cups. For several minutes the noise is tremendous. Whole acres roll over in the flood and lie with their pale roots in the sun. The released water finds a different fold to lean against. By evening there is a new lake, and along the old shore a line of wet husks still marks a level that no longer means anything.

The tall cups grow where this has happened many times. They start as the curled corners of overturned leaves. Roots that would dry out in the open air survive inside the curl; one leaf takes hold of the next, and in a few seasons a loose green scroll has become a column wide enough to enclose a pond. From near its foot you can hear the water shifting overhead. In gusts the cups shed it over one lip in a single heavy rope. There is a patch below each lip where the ceaseless beating has washed away the skin of the plain and exposed its strong yellow fibres. These are among the few places where you can get a good grip.

No wings come to skim the new water. Nothing crawls out of the stranded husks. In a lull, with sun drying one shoulder and a hot drip falling steadily on the other, you can distinguish the sounds usually lost in the general uproar: the rub of two wet surfaces sliding apart, the little tick of a root pulling free. Once in a while a ridge shifts so slowly that its movement is apparent only against that solitary white stalk. The stalk shows a little more of its length, then a little less. By late afternoon it has vanished. You would swear the ground between you and it had stayed level all day.

Toward dusk the heat persists but the wind cools. The flats bead over with moisture almost at once. Out along the torn eastern rim the new webs shine, and for a short while it is possible to see exactly how much has been added since morning: a narrow scalloped margin, scarcely the width of a hand, running for miles above the drop. In one place two runners have missed each other. Through the opening there is a glimpse of the lower clouds turning pink. Water collects at the tip of either runner, weighs it down, falls; the tips spring up again, each at a slightly different moment, close enough now to brush.
