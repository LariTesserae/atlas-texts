There is a fig tree growing below the pasture. From the nearer side you see only its crown, a patch of broad leaves among the fine upright grass. Go round to the far side and the whole crown sinks out of sight. Here a lip of limestone overhangs the hollow, and you can lie flat on the warm turf and look down past your wrists into a wood about the size of a courtyard.

The floor slopes toward one end. Nobody seeing the place dry would have trouble guessing which end: the loose things have all gone there. Fig pips, shells bleached to the color of oats, the little elbows of dead branches, even the round pellets of earth the ants bring up, lie sorted into shallow fans. At the upper end there are bare stone shelves, worn smooth in the middle and rough under their overhanging edges. Rain comes in over these shelves in a sheet a finger deep. It lasts barely longer than the rain itself. For several days afterward, though, one joint in the rock stays black. If you lay a hand on it your palm comes away wet and smelling of pennies.

Most of the green is along that joint. Ferns grow sideways out of it, then turn up; their oldest fronds hang straight down, so the bank has a crisp brown petticoat beneath all the soft green fiddleheads. Farther out, where the earth is dry enough to sift, the fig has everything to itself. Its trunk divided so long ago, and the limbs have put down so many roots of their own, that there is no obvious place to stand and say: here is where the tree began. A lizard can run halfway round the hollow at the height of your shoulder without jumping a gap. In the dimmest part of the canopy the figs are pale yellow. You find them first by their smell, a warm, almost buttery sweetness made sharp in places by a fruit split open and beginning to sour.

It is hot down here well after the light goes. The pasture wind passes overhead; occasionally it turns a few leaves silver at the top of the tree without stirring a single leaf below. Along the western wall are three steps, a landing no wider than a foot, and then plain rock again. Whoever cut the rest of the stair cut it in softer stone. Its course can still be followed by the geckos pressed belly-flat into a slanting succession of dents. On the surviving landing a bird makes a nest each year, binding dry grass to the rock with little daubs of mud. The old daubs remain in a row, higher or lower according to how thick a pad of grass she began with.

For so full a place there is astonishingly little to hear. A falling fig generally lodges in the litter without a sound. Its arrival is announced instead by a change of traffic: first one ant going uphill with a fleck of pink pulp, then six, then a continuous double line along the underside of a root. Tiny flies poise above the tear in the skin. A skink waits beside it, the flies keeping almost exactly the reach of its tongue away from its head. All afternoon a tortoise the same dusty ocher as the floor works its way toward the fruit. It reaches it with a dry fern caught crosswise on its shell and eats the whole thing, ants and all. The homeward line goes on carrying pulp for a while after the outward line has lost its destination.

Only at one hour is there anything like open daylight on the floor. A bar of sun gets past the southern lip and moves over the fans of shells. Moths on the limestone walk sideways ahead of it. The geckos withdraw into their dents. For a few minutes the tortoise stands with its forefeet in the light and the rest of it in shade. Then the bar climbs the opposite bank, picks out, quite high up, a small white shell wedged in the fork of a root—the mark of a flood no living fern here has seen—and goes out among the grass.
