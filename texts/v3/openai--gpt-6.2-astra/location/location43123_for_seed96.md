From either rim you can see the whole catchment, except for the water. You see the white roofs that cover it. They descend by shallow steps for perhaps three miles, meet along the bottom of the valley, and climb the farther side. A cloud lying low enough to touch the uppermost roofs casts a surprisingly small, dark shadow in all that white. The rest of the cloud hangs over the outer edge, in sunlight, with nothing under it for a long way down.

The roofs are earthenware. Each tile has a slight hollow, about the size of the hollow made by cupping two hands, and a lip that fits into the hollow of its neighbor. Up close they are not especially white. Some are pink as the inside of a cheek; some show the dirty yellow of clay imperfectly fired. Their brightness comes from a thin coat of tin-colored glaze and the finer coat of water over that. On a clear morning the opposite slope hurts to look at. Look instead at the tiles beside your foot and you can see where someone steadied each one with a thumb to dip it.

There is scarcely a level piece of ground. Even the little landings where six or seven stairways come together tip toward a drain. Between the stairways, cisterns share walls; under the cisterns, squat piers stand shoulder to shoulder on broad fired feet. At the rim, where a section has been left unfinished, the feet themselves can be seen resting on an older roof. Its tiles are a different shape, long and narrow, dark blue. No one walking here would have any particular reason to suppose there is earth below that one either.

Most days the wet comes sideways. A bank of cloud moves through the saddle and snags on the combs set up along the ridges. These are ranks of unglazed clay teeth, taller than a person, cold enough that water thickens on their windward sides. By noon the teeth are streaked dark from point to socket. The drops slide into the first cups and pass from lip to lip. There is no splashing. The fit is close, and the water lets go of one surface only when it has taken hold of the next. You could spend an hour watching a little fleet of white mineral flakes travel the length of a roof without hearing anything but your own sleeves rubbing when you shift your arms against the chill.

At the bottom there ought to be a river. Instead there is a street a pace wide, paved in red, with a continuous bench on either side. It winds a little, accommodating the bellies of the great jars sunk along it. Here the workmanship is rougher and more intimate. The jars were too big to turn. On their insides the clay ropes are still distinct, each course pressed down with four fingers, a pause, four fingers again. You can lean over an inspection mouth and see those marks continue down into perfectly transparent water. Much deeper than seems possible, the last few turns of the pot are lit by a round patch of sky.

Nothing drinks here. There are no green threads in the cups, no drowned wings to lift out of the jars. Now and then a patch of glaze goes cloudy from beneath, and in the course of a season a small fan of crystals forces its way through. These fans make the only cover on the slopes. In the younger eastern wards there are none yet. The stair edges there can cut a bare heel; the potters’ grit still lies in little crescents behind the lips, not enough wet days having passed to wash it all down.

Late in the afternoon a sound carries up from the red street: one low *tok*, sometimes another a minute later. Under certain benches there are stone floats, hollowed and sealed with pitch, that lift against the undersides of the seats when a jar is full. It is a modest signal for so much stored weight. Nothing opens automatically. The plugs are still in place, their square heads polished by hands that must once have come down at that sound. All evening the upper rows continue feeding the lower rows, until at last a full cup can find no room in the cup below. Then, very slowly, one roof after another loses its pattern of small bright hollows. From the rim you see a smooth patch gathering at the bottom of the valley, red showing through at first, then only the pale sky.
