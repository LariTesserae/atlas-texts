The lip of the plain is about the height of a doorstep. You can tell you have come to it because your leading foot finds nothing for an instant and then goes ankle-deep in warm water. Behind you the land is dry for a long way; ahead, seventeen miles of new ground slope by almost imperceptible degrees toward a sea that has not quite finished leaving. There is no surf to give you its direction. You have to kneel and put your hand in the water. The cooler side of a standing stone is usually the seaward side.

There is no light, not even from the electric works. A lamp draws current, warms its housing, and casts nothing. This made surprisingly little difference to the works, which were built to be run by three people and are now run by two. The gauges have hands you can touch. Along the mains, every hundred yards, a flanged joint rises clear of the mud, with a number cast in relief on its upper face. The numbers have an industrial neatness that is a pleasure to come upon after a mile of feeling things that might be either stone or flesh.

Most of the ground is shell lime, deposited in beds offshore and lifted all at once within living memory. It hasn't had time to acquire the shapes one expects of a country. A ridge ends in a square break, like the broken end of a biscuit. There are straight-sided gullies no wider than a wrist and deep enough to take a sounding line to its knot. Rain fills them from below before the first drops reach the surface. For a while afterward the whole plain sweats. Water gathers under the rims of shells still set in the rock and hangs there in fat, warm beads; a person coming out of the dry country can drink without stooping much, simply by running a cup along the underside of a ledge.

Very little grows upright. Here and there a clump of rushes, each blade thick as a finger, has taken hold in a crack. The tips are always worn off. Small climbing snails spend their lives going up them and eating the tender last inch, then coming down to lay their eggs in the wet. One such clump may be the only vegetation in half a mile. It will be heavy with snails, and with soft crabs picking among the eggs, and with the narrow, muscular eels that come out of the gullies at high water and knot themselves around the stems to prise the crabs loose. Stand still beside a clump and presently there will be an eel investigating the warm seam between your boot and trouser leg. It does not mean any great harm. It is accustomed to a world in which scarcely anything has an inside that cannot be got at with patience.

Much of this goes on without a sound. The crabs have felted feet. The eels slide down the wet blades rather than dropping. Out in the open pools are rafts of little diving birds, packed so close that the outer birds have to paddle while the inner ones sleep with their feet drawn up against their bellies. They feed on shrimp that come to the underside of the raft for shelter. Once in a while the whole company turns itself over in sections, the sleepers going under and the divers coming up, and there is a noise like somebody easing a damp sheet off a table. When it stops you can hear individual drops falling from the pipe flanges again.

There are six pump houses, far apart, each on a concrete plinth with a narrow iron stair. They were put there to drain the plain for grazing. The difficulty is that water removed from one part of the plain does not necessarily turn up somewhere else. Often it turns up at the same place, earlier. After the western pumps had run for a season, people began to remember a pond beside the western stair. They remembered fishing in it as children. One of the fitters, a man who had poured the plinth himself, went down on his knees and found in the concrete the paired depressions where he now remembered setting a bait tin while the cement was soft. His name was scratched between them in the laborious capitals he had used at nine.

The pumps are kept at a very low speed now. A year's work gives the worst-flooded district perhaps a finger's breadth of freeboard, and produces a past shallow enough for most people to live with. There are compensations. A bed of mussels that had barely begun to establish itself below the eastern works is now, and to a limited extent always has been, a famous bed. Its oldest shells are larger than soup plates. On rest days people come out along the main, counting the joints with the soles of their shoes, and sit shucking mussels on the stair. Nobody speaks much while eating. In that wide dark a voice carries to places the speaker has no sense of addressing.

Beyond the last house the main dives and doesn't come up. The lime gives way to a level floor of clay, still marked with sharp little ripples from the day it rose. Here the warmth of the land runs out. There is a band a few yards across in which one foot is warm and the other cold, and beyond that the water deepens so gradually that the birds can go on sleeping far out at sea. This is where the attendants come, once a month, to find out how the work is going. They walk abreast with a knotted cord between them until the cold reaches their knees. Then they stand a minute, letting the mud close over their toes, trying to remember where they stood last time.
