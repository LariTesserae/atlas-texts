There is a tide mark on the leg of the bench outside the pay office. Usually the water there is only a saucerful, with a little yellow pollen caught against the iron foot. But twice a year or so the river comes up the drain, crosses the paving, and stands a handbreadth deep under the bench. The mark is quite level, surprisingly sharp. The bench itself has settled at an angle.

From that seat you can see most of the mill quarter. It occupies the last bend of a creek before the creek loses its banks in the reed beds. There are two long press sheds, a boiler house, a landing, and three rows of dwellings built close behind one another on a low ridge of brick rubble. Between the rows are narrow lanes where a handcart can pass if the washerwomen take their tubs indoors. Everything taller than a roof is green. The ridge ends at the office; beyond it, what looks from the bench like a stretch of perfectly good meadow is a mat of sedge over water nearly shoulder deep.

The mill makes board from reeds. Cut bundles come in on shallow barges, their pale ends sticking out over the bows. On the landing there is a distinct change of smell, from the sour, cool mud under the planks to the sweet hay smell of a bundle just opened. A toothed roller takes the stalks into the first shed. Farther along, the stuff travels as a continuous sheet of wet brown felt, so frail at the edges that a drop from the roof will put a hole in it. By the time it comes out at the other end it can be stacked on a pallet and struck square with a mallet. The houses have ceilings of this board. In the older kitchens, through forty years of distemper, you can still see the straight seam marking the width of the machine.

It is a loud little place. The cutter makes a clattering rush that goes on so long the ear ceases to pick the strokes apart. Underneath it is the pump, two heavy beats and a lighter one, audible through the soles of your feet on the landing. People don't try to talk across the lane while the mill is running. They walk the few steps to the other doorway and talk there. At noon the drive stops. For a minute you hear water dropping from a hundred places, then someone scraping a chair back, someone else blowing on a spoonful of soup.

Very little ground stays bare. There are ferns in the slots left for the flood gates, and a fig rooted behind a downpipe has lifted the pipe away from the wall without breaking either of its brackets. The space beneath the high steam main is a favorite place for growing beans. Strings run up from the beds to the steel trestles, and for much of the year the pods hang at just the height of a child's face. Even the disused siding has a crop: close soft grass between the rails, cut with kitchen shears and laid on the muddy steps so that people can wipe their shoes. In the warm, wet air, severed runners sometimes take hold there. A step will acquire a green fringe before anyone thinks to sweep it.

Most afternoons are bright. Light comes off the open creek and goes a long way under the shed roofs, touching the undersides of belts and the pale, upturned palms of men checking a sheet as it passes. In the lanes the light is greener and shifts less. Leaves from opposite yards rest on one another over the washing lines. A single grey heron sometimes stands in the little square of clear water by the intake. Its departure, when it comes, is startling: for an instant there seems to be far too much wing to get out between the pipe and the reeds.

What changes here is mostly a matter of inches. Each dry season the landing has to be extended a plank or two over the silt; every few years a doorsill is raised and the door cut shorter. At the back of the pay office there are three generations of sill, the lowest now well below the herb bed. No one has pulled out the old stone. On warm evenings, after the last sheet has been laid on its stack and the vibration has gone out of the bench, a trickle still finds its way along the groove worn in that buried threshold. It comes out among the mint stems, runs clear for a foot or so, and soaks away.
