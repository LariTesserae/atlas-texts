From the lower air the plateau looks like the worn end of a broom. Pale fibers hang down for a mile, all combed in much the same direction by the wind. There are birds that spend their whole lives on the underside, pitching out into the dusk and coming back to catch a fiber in each foot. From above, the same place looks almost bare. There is a low rim to the west, three long depressions like the marks of fingers in dough, and a scattering of dark green roofs. You can see the whole town and quite a distance beyond it. It takes the better part of a day to walk from the first roof to the last.

The bare ground is leaf. Peel up a corner where the wind has worried it loose and there is another leaf, a little darker, with a different grain. Six layers down it is still green. In the oldest cuts the layers are too fine to count; they show up as a gloss on the cut face, the way the pages of a gilt-edged book can look like one piece of metal. A low light comes through the high dust and slides a long way across those slopes. Little takes a shadow except at its very foot. A deer standing half a mile off may be visible only as four dark commas.

There is hardly any water. The deer have a clear oil behind their eyes that stays limber in the cold; the leaves, if bruised, exude a white grease that can be rolled off between finger and thumb. Nothing here has the knack of drinking. On the rare occasions when a grain of ice falls out of the upper air, it lies about like a bit of broken crockery until it vanishes. The weather that matters is the change of electrical charge between the upper and lower faces of the plateau. Toward evening the underside goes positive. Every loose fiber lifts. The birds down there let go all at once.

You hear that change coming. At first there is a sound like someone a few streets over dragging a chest of drawers. Then the whole western depression starts up, a coarse sustained note with a hiccup in it where an old split crosses the grain. Powder jumps off the ground in little straight lines. Out on the open leaf the deer bend their front knees and put their jaws down. They eat the small animals jarred out of the seams: fat gray jointed things, most of them blind, that have spent the quiet part of the day shaving wax from the undersides of the leaves. For ten minutes the slopes are stippled with them. Then the charge settles, the seams shut, and there are the deer again, apparently grazing on nothing.

The town was laid out to make use of that note. Its streets are widely separated parallel bands of fiber, under tension, held apart at intervals by buildings grown in the shape of low bridges. The buildings need the street as much as the street needs them. Cut a band and two wards of the town would relax into shallow cups. As it is, the evening vibration passes from roof to roof, shedding particular pitches in particular rooms. Hutches of yellow seed warm up; worn floor coverings put out a fresh nap; in the municipal ovens a hard brown meal lifts and splits open into loaves. The noise indoors is tremendous. People who eat together in the evening sit with their ankles touching under the table, and a whole vocabulary of courtesies can be expressed with the side of a shoe.

The longer streets do something less easily accounted for. A folded coat set down at the western exchange can be picked up, still creased and still warm at the elbows, at the eastern exchange before the sound has reached it. This works only with things that have lain on the plateau long enough to acquire its grain. A visitor's coat will sit where it was put. An old coat with a new button will arrive without the button. For this reason there are trays under the exchange benches full of pins, lenses, replacement teeth, the bright little odds and ends that have not yet become local.

The town used to have tall buildings. What remain of them are pale ovals in the pavement, visible from a roof in slant light. They were never demolished. The wind took a few cells a year from their upper surfaces, and the living floors beneath kept inserting a few; eventually the lintels came level with the street. There is a district in the north where, by long custom, you step over a certain line rather than on it. The line was the sill of a house whose people liked to sit in the doorway. There is no account of how long ago that was. The deer step over it too, probably because it is a slightly stiffer part of the leaf.

After the evening note dies back there is enough light for an hour to go out toward the rim. The going is easy except near the three depressions, where the ground has worn so smooth that walkers take off their shoes and carry them. Below, the released birds are circling upward on the lee side. One by one they clear the edge, fly a little way across the level ground, then fold their wings and coast on their bellies with a dry, diminishing hiss. They leave two faint tracks apiece from the tips of their trailing feet. By morning the top leaf will have grown thick enough to lift those tracks off on a skin, and the wind will take it, snag it for a while on a roof, send it on over the edge. There are miles of such skins hanging underneath the town. In the dark, the birds find their old places among them by touch.
