The first thing you learn on the upper shelf is not to judge the depth of water by its sound. A film hardly thick enough to cover the tread of a boot can make a noise like surf. There are animals underneath, in the hairline space between water and stone, and a great many of them are calling.

The shelf runs along the flank of a mountain, above the weather that moves through the lower valleys. It is about an hour’s walk across and much too long to walk in a night, though night here lasts eleven days. From the outer parapet you can sometimes see lightning going off soundlessly in the clouds below. It shows you the underside of the next bluff, a tremendous clean fracture with hardly a ledge on it. The whole shelf came up in one piece not very long ago. Its streams are still trying out several beds apiece. There are streets in the city that have had three names, for three different streams, without anybody having moved a door.

Most of the water comes through the mountain. It beads up on the fresh black faces of the rock, salty enough to stay liquid in the cold, and goes downhill by such small degrees that the surveyors use floats to find the slope. Where a seep dries there is a white lip sharp enough to cut skin. Where it persists there is life. First a grey scum that eats what the water brings out of the deep; then the little callers, flat as nail parings, with a hard knob on the belly that they strike against the ground. They feed with the rest of their bodies while they knock. Sometimes a whole pavement falls into step, and the soles of your feet begin to itch. No one has found a place on the shelf where something green will grow.

The city occupies the widest part of the shelf. Its oldest houses are narrow, four or five rooms one behind the other, set on combs of blunt ceramic teeth so the water can pass beneath. Over the years the spaces between have filled with bathhouses, salting rooms, public kitchens, stair towers, covered walks, and the low round sheds where people go to have silence. The sheds are a municipal service. Inside one you can hear your own clothes creak when you sit down. Outside, in addition to the countless ticking mouths underfoot, there is the boom of water in the bridge piers and the nearly continuous hoarse complaint of the great white animals on the roofs.

There is no good name for these animals in any language spoken down below. From a distance they look like loads of laundry left out in the frost. Up close each proves to be a warm, rather heavy bag with six small feet gathered at one end. They spend most of their lives lying across something: a ridge of stone, the coping of a wall, the curved back of another of their kind. Their fringes hang into the water and catch the little flat animals that stray off the bottom. In a particularly good place one may remain for thirty years. The noise is mostly territorial. Two beasts on opposite sides of a gutter will keep up an argument well into the lives of the grandchildren of the person who put the gutter there.

Much of the civic engineering has to do with leaving them where they are. A roof due for replacement is uncoupled from its weight, slid sideways with all its occupants, and kept at the same height on a temporary address until the work is finished. Weight is handled in three exchanges along the inner wall of the city. There are no lights on the exchanges and nothing much to see inside, just rows of black ingots in shallow pans. On a heavy working day a cup set down on the sill of the nearest kitchen takes two hands to lift. Residents of that block bake exceptionally close-grained bread.

For all the crowding, the city has a generous prospect. At the end of nearly every cross street the handrail stops and there is open air, miles of it, with the far settlements audible across the gap. In the dark it is possible to know them better than by sight. One has a deep iron ferry bell. Another stands over a hollow portion of the shelf and its millions of callers produce a single low note, like a finger going round the rim of a wet glass. On visits people are often surprised by how small that town is. They had imagined something on the scale of a capital.

Change here is measured with brass studs. A stud in a doorstep records where the water reached on the night the house was opened. A row of them up an abutment records the slow lift of the mountain. Children are taken to the east ward to see a stream that has finally chosen a bed. It is only a groove as wide as a thumb, with a pale deposit on either bank, but all the water from a public square now finds its way into it. The children squat in the cold and put their fingers there. For most of them it is the first time they have felt water on the shelf moving fast enough to push back.
