At noon the shadow of a roof post lies six hundred metres long on the floor of the sea. It runs down a slope of pale concrete, crosses two expansion joints, and kinks where the floor drops a step. Fish hang in the shadow. As it moves they move with it, keeping on the dark side of a line no wider, at its far end, than a road.

The roof is wired glass on steel. From below, its framework is usually too high to make out; there is daylight, and a surface that wrinkles the daylight, and occasionally a bead of darkness that travels steadily against the run of the waves. That will be a glazing crew on the gantry. They can have rain up there while it is quite bright below. A shower roughens the glass and takes the sharp margins off every shadow for miles. Then a swath of clear sun comes through and the shadows acquire edges again, one after another, from west to east.

It was dry here less than thirty years ago. Along the deeper grades there are still sweep marks from the brooms the finishing crews used on the wet cement. The dams dividing one filling section from the next were taken down to within a yard of the bottom and no farther. Their stumps make long, low reefs with beautifully square ends. Beyond them is more floor, then the pale upward pitch of a service bank, then water too blue to see through. A sounding chart of the whole sea comes in nine bound volumes. For most purposes a single page will do. A fishing boat can work the length of that page all summer without finding cause to turn it.

There is very little to interrupt the water. The columns stand far apart. The hoists, access ladders, and conduits are gathered against them, so that between one column and the next even a large animal has nowhere to stop out of the current. Shoals of silver fry pass like handfuls of filings drawn rapidly across a table. Behind them comes a long interval when there is absolutely nothing. Then, a good way off to one side, a seal goes down headfirst, kicking once at the surface. It has seen something from above that was invisible from below.

What carries in that emptiness is the sound. There is the low turning note of the circulation works, conducted through the floor, and above it the much closer rattle of mussel shells knocking together on the instrument cables. A loose dogging pin on a gate can be heard three bays away. On the mornings when the northern works reverse, the blades slow almost to a standstill. For a minute one hears the sea itself: grit ticking down a bank, the scrape of a skate uncovering a buried clam. Then the motors take hold in the opposite direction. The note climbs, steadies, and all those small sounds disappear without anything having ceased to make them.

The reversal sends a front through the lower water. It is visible where it lifts the fine white deposit off the floor. The silt rolls forward to the lip of a trench and pours over like flour; small flatfish rise out of it, travel a few feet on the advancing edge, and settle again. By afternoon the front has reached the post fields. There the cold bottom water piles against the footings and boils up toward the daylight. Gulls assemble on the inspection walks well before anything reaches the surface. An old gull will sit through the first few upwellings, which bring little but shreds of green weed. The younger birds wear themselves out diving. When the shrimp come up, the old gull steps off the rail.

The green weed grows only where it can get a purchase. Here and there a rectangle of the floor is furred with it, precisely the size of a slab: a different aggregate in that day's batch, rough enough to hold the first threads. The neighboring slabs are nearly bare. Crabs patrol the four edges. There are leagues of this country in which a crab's entire living is to be found along the seam between one afternoon's work and the next morning's.

Toward evening the light goes amber beneath the glass. The surface wind, which has a whole inland sea in which to get up, drives short hard waves eastward. They slap under the service walks and burst through the gratings, wetting the boots of people on their way to the late shift. Down on the floor it is still quite light. The roof-post shadows have stretched out of sight, and the fish that followed them have gathered at the old dividing dam. They turn into the current along its top, holding their places with little strokes of their tails. On one face the concrete is scoured clean. On the other, in the lee, a bank of shell grit has been accumulating for thirty years. It has nearly reached the crest. Another winter or two and this particular reef will have a gentle side, the first for a long distance in any direction.
