The easiest way to see across the basin is to wait for the water to stand up. It happens first at the western edge, where a sheet of brown water lifts out of the flooded woods, taking its duckweed with it. For a few seconds there is a view underneath: several miles of smooth green cane, the white roof of a pumping station, and beyond it a line of clay bluffs so new that the cane roots stick straight out of their faces, pale and wet as peeled willow. Then the sheet comes down. The sound arrives through the ground before the air. It makes the water jump in the cups of the leaves.

Most of the time the basin is submerged to a depth of three or four feet. The bottom is warm, almost flesh-warm where the black silt lies thick, and the water above it is a degree or two cooler. Everything that grows here grows at several levels. There are mats of minute red ferns at the surface; beneath them are long, exceedingly clear leaves that can only be seen by the rows of bubbles caught along their edges. Down in the dark the cane makes a second, horizontal forest of jointed roots. There are no fish among them. Nothing grazes the ferns. A leaf can wear away to lace from the constant drag of the water and still not have a single bite taken out of it.

There used to be one channel through the basin. The dredge that kept it open still lies there, its ladder down and its buckets going round. You can tell when one comes up full by a change in the engine note audible even through the general din: a deep, reluctant *umm* at intervals of eleven seconds. It is a small machine to hear from so far away. There are only six or seven works in the entire flooded tract, all built of the same yellow brick, all running. At the southern station a belt wide enough to sleep on passes out through a slot in the wall, travels half a mile between the trunks of the cane, then enters the water at a shallow angle. Once an hour its seam goes by, a double row of copper staples polished pink. Water thrown from the seam falls as a little travelling shower. The ferns along that route are a brighter red.

The difficulty with keeping a channel open is that a hollow here need not stay where it was dug. The dredge may take up a bucket of clay at noon and leave a clean bucket-shaped hole in the bottom a quarter mile east. For a long time the men at the works dealt with this by setting out numbered stakes and dredging by the numbers rather than by sight. Some of the stakes survive. Under the roots at Fourteen there is still a neat flight of steps meant for the door of the southern station. One of the steps is worn in the middle. It was in the right place for years.

Lately much larger pieces of the bottom have begun to shift. What looks from a distance like a low island of solid cane will sometimes split without a tremor, the two halves drawing apart to disclose a narrow chocolate-coloured trench. Warm water pours over both lips. For a moment there is enough light to see the strata: black mud, blue clay, a fine yellow bed of sand, then black mud again full of perfectly preserved leaves. The edges are ruled sharp. Before they have time to slump, the trench is full and the little red ferns are racing down its length on the new current. Somewhere else a corresponding ridge is rising. Whole groves come up on these ridges streaming water from their crowns. The submerged leaves, too thin to support themselves in air, plaster themselves in brilliant green strips against the stems.

From above, if there were any height from which to look, it might be possible to watch these exchanges pass across the basin like figures in a calculation. Down among the cane there is only the terrific local business of it. A few yards away tons of water are falling. A root mass tears slowly in two with a noise like a warehouse door being dragged over concrete. The motors hold their separate pitches; the sheet-iron roofs rattle; rain shaken out of the standing water thrashes a million broad leaves at once. There are intervals when a bank of reeds folds over under the weight and the dim sky shows through, a soft untroubled violet. Not a wing crosses the opening.

Toward evening the current sometimes brings things back to the works. A handrail slides sideways through the mud and fits against a landing. Two brick piers rise under the loose span of a pipe. At the southern door the water sinks, and for a little while there are steps there again, not the worn ones from Fourteen but three broad green steps cut in fresh clay. Water drains down them into the station. Inside, under a single shaded bulb, the flywheel throws its enormous shadow up the wall and draws it back. The steps last ten minutes, long enough for a thin drift of red fern to catch on each tread and mark it clearly in the dusk. Then the floor of the basin moves on, and the open door gives straight onto deep water, with the light still burning on the other side.
