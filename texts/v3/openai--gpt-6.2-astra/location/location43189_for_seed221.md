There is a little country behind the number plate of the cloud condenser. The plate says 7. From the gantry on the outside you can put a finger through the upper stroke of the seven and feel the cold of the water, but not reach the water itself. There is a draught out of that hole that keeps a fingertip cold for some time afterward. The gantry is the last one up the tower. On a clear day the other condensers are visible below it, each making a small square shadow on the clouds.

The floor inside is about the size of a folded handkerchief. It was punched out of nickel sheet, folded twice to give it a fall, and soldered badly at one corner. The solder stands up in little silver lobes. Between them are deep sounds like water running beneath a bridge. This is misleading as to distance: the water is right there, scarcely a hair below the lip. It wells through the floor under pressure and lies against the nickel in a tight skin. Where a burr stands up, the skin climbs the burr. At the tops of several of these eminences there is a bead of water with a dry valley on either side.

The seven admits what daylight there is. It gives the place one white horizon and one oblique shaft full of legs. The legs belong to pale mites which live on the back of the plate in such numbers that you can see the shapes of the stamped letters on the *other* side, picked out by the deeper fur in the hollows. Most have one foot down in the wet and the others locked with the feet of their neighbours. Smaller things work their way through this trellis. There are jointed, transparent grazers, with a single green speck showing where the last meal has got to; there are blunt worms that go about with bubbles fitted over their heads. A patch of green the size of a pinprick under the washer feeds a surprising extent of the ceiling. Elsewhere what looks like vegetation is animal: the little stand of red reeds on the sill will all draw in their horns if the sill is touched.

It is very nearly freezing. The water is kept liquid by salt and the slight expenditure of heat at the bearing behind the wall. Ice has nevertheless taken on the exact form of the screw thread at the coldest end. You could back the screw out and find a clear nut left in place. Nothing here has had time to weather, although the tower has been in service longer than anyone now working on it. There is a comb on the moving face of the condenser which passes the underside of the floor and shaves off precisely as much mineral as the water has put down. Even the solder lobes, examined closely, have the fine scored finish of pieces just now taken from a lathe. The country is forever at the point of being unpacked. There ought to be a scrap of oily paper still adhering to it somewhere.

The noise is hard to account for from outside. On the gantry there is a steady, rather pleasant electrical hum. In here a whole sheet of metal is being sounded at arm’s length or less, and the water is transmitting the sound straight into every body in contact with it. The mites have no use for ears. The animals which need to tell each other anything do it by altering the pull of a foot. A tug started near the washer can go all the way round the plate without a single animal changing its address. Seen in the slant light, this passage of news looks like the nap on velvet being brushed the wrong way. In the roar, the bubble worms graze upside down on the skin of the pools, heads knocking softly together, a sound that has probably never been heard.

There is a shore at the badly soldered corner where everything brought in from outside must eventually arrive. A blue fibre from a fitter’s cuff has lain there for years. Hundreds of little mouths keep it clean. The last turn of the fibre sticks out into the draught from the seven and trembles so rapidly that it appears to end in a small blue fan. It was a fan when the plate was first screwed on. It is a fan now. Beyond it, sometimes, an enormous blurred pink darkness closes off the day, stays long enough to feel the cold, and goes away.
