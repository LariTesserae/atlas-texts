The light comes in at ankle height. A slab has slipped down across the mouth of the hollow, leaving a long slot underneath. On the far side of the slot there is a shingle beach and, beyond it, a line of grey sea. From inside, the beach looks immensely bright. A stone no bigger than a knuckle stands out black on it, with a little blue shadow all its own.

The hollow is in the foot of a limestone bluff. It is about as big as the ground floor of a house, though it takes some time to make this out. There is no point from which one can see all the floor. Great squat bosses of yellowish stone stand almost shoulder to shoulder under the low roof. Between them are basins, some the size and shape of cupped hands, others big enough to lie down in. All are full. The stone dividing one basin from the next is rounded on top, wet on both sides, and in places scarcely wider than a boot sole.

A warm spring feeds the place. It comes up under a ledge at the back, without a bubble or any other sign by which to find its exact source. A hand put into the water there meets a soft steady push. The warmth is pleasant, particularly after coming in on hands and knees over the cold beach stones. It has got into the walls. Even quite high up, where the stone is dry enough to take a chalk mark, it feels like a wall that has had the afternoon sun on it.

Water passes over the lips of the basins in such thin sheets that they look solid. A grain of sand set on one will travel a surprising distance before it falls. Nearly all the sound comes from one place: under a shelf by the entrance a thread of water falls the length of a finger into a hollow in the rock. It makes a small, rather domestic ticking sound. Farther in, the bosses cut it off. There one can hear the grit in a wet sandal as its wearer shifts their weight.

There are two steps pecked into the side of a boss. They give access to a shelf just wide enough to sit on, with one's feet in a deep basin and one's head out of the way of the roof. The lower step has worn into a scoop. On the shelf is a shallow earthenware bowl with one edge broken off, mended once through a pair of drilled holes. The binding is gone. Somebody has put the bowl on its side and chocked it with a pebble so that it will drain. This small provision for keeping something dry seems especially painstaking here.

Nothing green grows in the hollow. The olive colour deep in the basins belongs to the stone; rubbed with a thumb, it leaves the thumb quite clean. Occasionally there is a little grey crab in the drain toward the beach. It fits sideways into a seam so closely that when it draws its claws in, the seam looks empty again. There is very little else that could find a meal here.

The water leaves under the shingle well above the reach of an ordinary tide. Rain on the bluff, wind on the shore, a hot day that makes the beach painful to walk on—these make their differences on the other side of the slab. In here the water stands at its pale line on the sitting shelf. Each basin has a low place in its rim worn to just the width that will pass what it receives. No basin empties to fill the next. Seen from the two steps, the one thing in the hollow that is in continual motion is also the one thing always in its place: the little tilted square of daylight lying flat on the brimming water by the door.
