The warmest place on the pass is under a black flake of rock no bigger than a spread hand. The pass takes snow in winter, but the wind scours this particular brow bare. There is a white patch on the black, which from the trail looks like the remains of a snowball. It is salt. A thread of hot air comes up through a split in the brow and, on its way out from under the flake, deposits a grain or two of white at the lip. The patch is years of weather.

Inside, there is hardly height enough for a beetle to turn over. The ceiling is the dull purple of an old bruise. The floor is quite different stone, black and sparkling, so recently parted from the ceiling that every knob below still has its socket above. At one end the two surfaces are still attached. There the whole country runs out in a series of narrowing rooms, then a thinness into which even a mite can put only its feet. At the open end a blade of daylight admits the shapes of things but almost none of their colour. What looks like a heap of buckwheat is a crowd of small brown beetles with their heads tucked down.

There is a quarry on the other side of the mountain. Nothing of it can be seen from here. Its crushing mill starts up while the pass is still dark, and the first a walker knows of it is a faint tingling in a boot set flat on the rock. Under the flake it is immense. The two faces of the stone make a drum with scarcely any air inside it. A broad continuous roar fills the chamber, overlaid by a fine hard chatter where one loose crystal knocks in its socket. Grain after grain of salt stands up, drops, stands up again. The beetles keep still. The mill has been running every morning for longer than any of them has lived.

They come for the heat and for the things the wind brings. The pass is a sieve for insects blown up from the lower valleys; wings and husks collect in every lee, but here a living fly can crawl in out of the bitter air and be warm straight through in a minute. There are flies deep in the pocket with their legs folded, and flies at the lip so dry that a touch will take the dust off an eye. Mites work along the joints of the dead ones. The little beetles go in under the cases of larger beetles and emerge powdered orange. One pale spider occupies the only space tall enough to let it hang clear of the ground. There is no web to speak of, just three lines drawn tight across the approach. At noon the flake gets too hot for feet near the seam, and the crowd backs out a little. The movement takes half an hour and travels through them by touch; the outermost beetle wakes in the cold stripe of light without having gone there of its own accord.

There is no pool, not even a stain where a pool has dried. Moisture in this place is a private possession, carried in a soft abdomen or sealed inside an egg. The salt keeps its corners. Down at the blind end, heat working up from the mountain and cold working down from the sky are lifting another hair’s breadth of stone. Every few winters a fresh row of sockets opens. For a while the new ground stays beautifully clean, with little needles of glass left upright where they tore free. Then a feeler comes through, sweeping gently from side to side.

Late in the day the mill stops. It is possible then to hear the crowd itself: not voices, but the minute rasp of shell passing shell, and now and then the surprisingly distinct snip of the spider cutting a line. The brow loses the sun. Cold reaches a little way in at the lip and stops. By dark the beetles have closed up over the warm seam again, so closely fitted that nothing of the glittering floor shows between them.
