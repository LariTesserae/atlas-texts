The place to stand is on the lip of a white basin. The lip is warm under your bare feet, and narrow enough that if you stand with your toes in one country your heels are wet with the water of the next. Both countries are about knee-deep. One has a floor of little blue hexagons, the other a floor of pink ribs, spaced to admit a finger. Well out in the pink water, something the size and color of a freight car is lying down. The line along its side slowly parts into two rows of eyelashes.

Seen from a height, these basins make up a plain. There are oval settling pans, miles long, with footbridges across their narrow ends; there are trays like the drawers of a type case, each with its own pool; there are broad sheets of shallow water on which the only sign of division is a perfectly straight line where white birds can stand ankle-deep. The dividing lips seldom rise more than a hand’s breadth above the water. A little spray goes over them. A few drops keep finding their way down the dry side of a spillway which has never yet had to take a flood. The drops follow one another down the same bright track without wearing so much as a groove.

Nothing here was dug. The hollow was made first. You can see how it was done in the clear pans, where the coved sides turn in under the water and the floor panels meet on a row of dark fasteners. Under the panels are the return mains, under the mains the frames that carry them, and under the frames, a long way down, another set of pans receiving the warmth of all that machinery. Deep inspection lamps shine green through a hundred thicknesses of water. There is no last thickness through which you could put a drill and bring up a curl of earth.

The light overhead comes through milky vaults so high that it has room to become daylight on the way down. There are actual clouds under the vaults. A bank of rain hangs over the western strainers, several days’ walk away. You can see its blue foot and the white notch in it where a warm exhaust divides the falling water. The showers stay there. If you want rain on your face you must go to it. Halfway there, at a particular expansion joint in the walkway, you will begin to feel the cool on the backs of your hands.

It takes a while to see how crowded the plain is. What looked like the fine gray stipple fired into the glaze is a company of snails, each no bigger than the head of a rivet, polishing the ceramic with transparent tongues. The birds on the submerged wall stand breast to back for the whole visible length of it. Under each bird, sheltered between its feet, is a fan of little fish facing the overflow. There are broad diving animals with pleated necks that come up very slowly between these fans, take a mouthful of air without scattering anything, and fold themselves down again. In the clear water you can watch one going through layer after layer of smaller animals, each layer coming apart at its nose and fastening up at its tail. Almost none of them call. Close to your ear a bird combs one feather through its bill: a small, dry zip. The next sound is water dropping off the end of the feather.

There is nothing green to eat. The mains sweat a soft, nutritious scale, cream-colored and faintly salty, which comes away in the mouths of the grazers. Animal heat and the waste of digestion go back through the pink ribs. Downstairs, slow wheels lift the recovered salts into the feed again. The wheels are very large; a man could spend his working life watching one tooth emerge from its bath of oil. The fast work is done by the mouths.

Now and then one of the freight-car animals gets up. Its underside is white, intricately stamped with the pattern of the floor. For a minute the uncovered ribs give off steam in the shape of the body that was lying on them. Before the shape has quite dissolved, another animal eases out of the crowd and fits itself to the warm print. This is about as much disturbance as the plain permits. There is motion everywhere, considerable appetite, even the pleasure of finding a place still warm from somebody else. Yet the water is always at the same scratch on the gauge. The rain keeps its notch. The white lip on which you stand has the keen, slightly disagreeable corner of something taken out of its mold this morning. On its underside, where you have to kneel and put a hand into the warm water to feel them, the maker’s little raised letters are sharp enough to read.
