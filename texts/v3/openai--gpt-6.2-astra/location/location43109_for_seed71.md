The plumb lines begin to lean about half an hour before the river comes back. In the city of Olt each bridge has one: a flax cord, a bored pebble, a little white notch in the pier to show where the pebble ought to hang. On a still morning you can walk from bridge to bridge and see the stones all leaning the same way. Then you come to a bridge where the stone hangs true. Beyond it a woman is kneeling in the dry watercourse, setting out trays of seedlings. She has seen the water return for sixty years. She knows to within the breadth of a tray where it will turn.

Olt stands very high, though there is nothing in the view from Olt to tell you so. The plateau has been worn down almost to a plane. What remains of whole ranges shows through the turf in long oval patches, pink granite surrounded by a rim of darker, harder stuff. You could sweep one clean with a broom. It takes six summers of walking to reach the southern edge, where the air grows abruptly deep underfoot and there are clouds a mile below the last paving stones. Up here the sun has an ordinary white light. It warms the back of a hand quite pleasantly. Take the hand out of the sun and the cold gets into the knuckles at once.

From a roof the city looks at first like nothing much: three grey courts, some bleaching cloth, a garden going yellow at the tips. Then the eye picks up the continuation of a street on the far side of a league of bare rock. There are the same paired kerbs; there is the little widening for carts to pass. The houses grow in knots along it. A thousand people may share an oven court, living so close that a loaf can go home over the roofs without ever coming down to street level. The next oven is a day's walk away. In the intervals there are buried conduits, bridges, folds of cultivated ground, and great level yards of unknown purpose which the pavers still keep in repair. It is one city. A paving apprentice can go west until her hair is white and still recognize the mark for a loose slab, chalked at the corner in the fashion she learned at home.

The water is seldom more than ankle deep. Most of the year it is frost underneath the stones, or beads hanging in the felt of leaves. When a wet wind crosses the plateau, the hollows fill. For a few hours they remain hollows; then, under the weight of water, some begin to acquire neighbours from a long way off. Two gutters laid parallel may empty into different summers. This is why the first practice bridges children build in shelf-stone have no arches. The important thing to learn is where to put the far end.

At Olt the returning river comes quite simply round a corner. One moment there is fine dry grit in the channel, and the next a tongue of brown water is curling the grit over like the edge of a rug. It brings bits of rush, translucent plates of ice, sometimes a square of turf still green from a district where it is afternoon. For the length of the flood the districts touch. You can stand on the bridge in the cold morning and watch a laundry drying in low copper sunlight on the opposite bank. People cross if they have business there. They carry their own bread. No one takes a child across on the promise of being home by evening.

Where there is water the growth is extravagant. The channel walls wear six or seven layers of plants: black gripping threads against the stone, then little tongues of liverwort, a shag of sour edible leaves, and above these the pale folded cups that open in sequence as the water rises. Whole hedges lie flat in winter and lift in a day on stems like bent green fingers. There is nothing to browse them. There are no flies over the standing pools, no birds to come down on the seed heads and shake them. Pollen travels on the cuffs of passersby or goes off in ochre sheets on a gust. In sheltered courts people pollinate the heavy flour flowers by drawing a frayed rope slowly across the beds, two people to a rope, walking barefoot so as to feel if they have strayed onto a stem.

Much is happening at any hour and very little of it carries to the ear. A frost seam works its way across a flagstone. A stand of red reeds comes into seed all down a watercourse between breakfast and noon. The sun eases a lace of ice off a north wall; it slips whole into the deep moss at the foot. Out in the open the sounds that tell of habitation are small and remarkably distinct: the scrape as a potter draws grit off his turning slab, three blows spaced well apart as someone beds a kerb, the flap of a wet shirt being shaken straight. A voice sometimes comes over the water from so far away that the words belong to no language spoken in Olt. Usually it has the unhurried rise and drop of somebody counting.

By late morning the river is drawing back. The laundry across from the bridge goes first, copper light and all, leaving a low bank furred with frost. Then the water shrinks to a thread. The woman with the trays has not moved them. Every seedling has had its roots soaked; not one has been lifted out of the grit. She carries them up two trays at a time to the warm side of the oven court. Under the bridge the flax cord stirs a little, and the pebble comes to rest against the old white notch.
