There is a town on the floor of a drowned valley. It has low wards of adjoining chambers, two broad ways that meet at a slant, and, at the lowest point of the crossing, a square into which an upwelling rolls fine white grit. Very little of the town stands empty. A shrimp lives in the hinge socket of a shell; a worm lives in the shrimp’s cast skin until the skin comes apart. The water is warm enough that a skin lasts scarcely a day.

Overhead is a roof of limestone, high above the crossing but so low at the valley edges that a ray going there to feed must go flat, its back just clear of the stone. Only at the seaward end does any daylight get in. From well inside the valley it amounts to a dilution of the dark, the sort of difference an eye can find by looking away and lose by looking straight at it. There are a few wine-colored felts of weed out there. Farther in, what feels like grass brushing the sand is the fringe of an animal, and what folds up its twigs at a touch is an animal too.

The first builders were great fan-shaped clams. A fan lodged upright in the valley clay makes a lee where another can take hold. In time there were banks of them, their broad lips all set across the flow. The dark of the inner valley suited them as well as the mouth: the tide brought food in loose brown flocks from the sunlit shoals outside. Even now, under a hand laid against what seems a solid wall, one of its stones might close.

But most of the lower courses are dead. Pale tube worms have stitched them together, generation over generation, and little patches of living sponge seal the seams. Water that could no longer pass through the banks went around their ends. It scoured lanes down to clay, widened the bends, undercut the corners until slabs fell out and lay across the narrower passages. There are bridges a skate can get under but not turn around under. There are round rooms as smooth within as the hollow of an ear, worn out by a single trapped pebble. Through one long district runs a waist-high seam of old shell lips, all facing a way the tide no longer goes.

At slack water the place seems scarcely inhabited. Grit settles in the square. A few blind gobies hang head down beside a wall, keeping their places with fins so nearly transparent that the fish seem pinned there. Then a thread of cooler water enters along the bottom of the seaward way. Before it has reached the crossing, the mouths of the tube worms farther in are opening. By the time the brown flocks arrive, whole streets are feathered. Crabs climb to the crests of the banks and hold their little hairy wrists out. What passes one pair of wrists passes a thousand more. Close to the valley head the water runs clear.

There is almost no sound to this. Now and then a shell tips off a ledge and lands with a china knock, surprisingly far away. The loudest thing is often a fish quite close by, cracking one small snail in its throat. Twice in a tide there is a long hush even of such noises. A broad ray comes in from the mouth, recognizable at a distance by the push of water that travels ahead of its face. Every exposed wrist draws back. It quarters the square, blowing pits in the grit, and works its way along the low outer wards. Behind it the pits fill, the feathers come out, and a procession of small flatfish follows in the stirred mud for whatever the ray has missed.

At the very back, beyond the last bank, the valley narrows to a bedding crack. Warm fresh water seeps down it from somewhere on land. Being light, it keeps to the roof; there is a ceiling river there, shallow and winding, running out to sea above the heads of creatures that have never left salt water. Where the two waters meet, the stone is eaten into delicate flutings. Tiny white grazers spend their lives upside down along that wavering shore. Below them stretch the packed roofs of the town, then the bare crossing with its turning grit, then more roofs, diminishing toward the faint gray of the mouth. Only a few body lengths separate one shore from the other. A shell dropped from the upper one would take a long time to arrive, rocking from lip to lip through all that busy dark.
