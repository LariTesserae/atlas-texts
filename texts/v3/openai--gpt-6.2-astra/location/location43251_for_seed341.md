There is a square in the lower city where the rain comes up through the paving. It begins at one joint, usually the long crooked one outside the municipal laundry. A dark line runs the length of the joint; then a bead of water travels along it, fattening as it goes, until it rolls free into the dust. Within a minute all the joints are running. The smell is of hot brick soaked through.

The square is small enough that a sheet hung out of the laundry loft can cast shade on the opposite pavement. Lofts surround it on three sides. Their upper stories lean out on iron brackets, and a pair of water mains crosses the remaining strip of sky. Even at noon the light down here is the color of the underside of a leaf. There is a great deal of open floor. The few things that stand on it—a pump housing, a stone bench, three pollarded figs—have room around them as objects do in a room being scrubbed. Nothing is left on the ground that needs to stay dry.

Underfoot lies a shallow bed of gravel, the former margin of a river. The river itself is behind six streets and a wall now, but a rise in its level travels through the gravel and arrives here scarcely an hour later. The paving was laid in a gradual dish to receive it. So many years of water have passed over the lip of the central drain that the aggregate stands out of the cement in little knobs, pink, black and milky white, each polished on its upstream face. The oldest water mark on the pump housing is at knee height. Someone set a brass shelf just above it, exactly the size of a pair of shoes.

Greenery keeps to the edges until you look up. Then there are whole gardens in the air: ferns massed in the mouths of disused downpipes, fig roots crossing from one building to another along the mains, a fleshy vine that has filled a fire balcony to the rail. Its leaves overlap like spoon bowls. After rain upstairs, they empty one into the next for hours. Down below, the vacant spaces stay vacant. No fin stirs in the drain, no small feet print the silt. A fig has split open on the bench. Its red interior is quite still. In this heat it will sour by evening, and the next rise will carry it away.

What makes it difficult to sit here is the noise. The pumps in the basement of the laundry serve the entire ward. First comes the slap of a belt taking up its slack; then a deep revolving note sets in, and the bench begins to tick against its loose left foot. The falling water adds a high continuous tearing sound. Up in the loft a white sheet bellies out, falls back, bellies again. It is possible to see the rhythm of the engines better there than anywhere in the pumphouse. When both engines are running, the sheet stands almost straight out into the square in the hot draft from the vents.

At the height of a rise the drain changes its mind. For a little while it sends up a clear, round-shouldered mound of water, smooth enough to hold the reflection of an entire window. That window shivers on the mound while everything around it rushes inward. Then the basement pumps gain on the river. The mound flattens, dimples, and lets go with a gulp that can be heard through the soles. Water slips off the paving in broad skins. In a few minutes there is only the crooked wet joint, the smell of bruised fig, and, on the brass shelf, a thin deposit of river sand laid down long ago and still beyond the reach of any ordinary high water.
