A piece of the plateau rises in the night, usually by less than the thickness of a leaf. In the morning there is a white line at the foot of a cliff, so bright it hurts to look at it. Little red mites come down in files to eat the salt off the new stone. By noon the line is pink and seems to be moving sideways. A rise of a finger’s breadth is a great event. It leaves a terrace broad enough for birds to nest on, and for the next several hundred miles the same narrow terrace is full of birds.

The plateau stands above most rain. From its southern lip you can see the tops of thunderstorms going by below, bulging up like great cauliflowers, their insides lighting violet in broad daylight. Up here the sky is a hard, washed blue. There are shallow basins of white grit, fields of slate tilted almost on edge, and innumerable blunt pillars of a translucent stone the color of butter. The pillars have risen through the slate and carried pieces of it up on their backs. Some are half a mile high. There has not yet been time to round the corners of those black caps. Even at a distance where a whole pillar looks no bigger than a tooth, its shadow has a cut-paper edge.

Much of what looks like bare stone from that distance is foliage. The common tree grows flat, with its trunk lying in a crack and its branches flattened to either side, each overlapping its neighbor just enough to exclude the wind. A forest of these trees has the nap and luster of a green pelt brushed all one way. Under the leaves it is dark and comparatively warm. There are soft brown flowers down there that never see the sky, and blind, long-whiskered beasts no longer than a thumb that spend their lives pushing through the flowers, coming out yellow at one end of a branch and going in brown at the other. An old tree may be a hundred yards wide and nowhere high enough to shade a standing bird.

Water comes mostly from the animals. Every evening a skin of frost forms above each occupied burrow, white at the middle and thinning to a dusting at the edge. Morning light penetrates the leaves and loosens the frost from below. For perhaps ten minutes there is a pattering in the forests. The trees take nearly all of it. What escapes collects in deep slots between the slate blades, pools so narrow that a drinking animal has to turn its head sideways to get its tongue in. Once in a long while a thunderhead snags on the southern lip and spills actual rain over the edge. Its course across the plateau can be traced years later by a winding band of tall blue sedge, stiff with the empty cases of the flies that hatched there in one afternoon.

There is a noise everywhere like heavy axles turning under load. It is strongest in the pillar country. There, great pale animals hang around the columns in close-fitting rings, heads down, rumps to the light. Each head consists chiefly of a pair of broad mineral teeth. As the soft stone comes up from below, the animals crop it. You can put an ear to the ground a day’s walk from the nearest colony and hear the catch and grind of their chewing conducted through the rock. Up close, much of the noise is a chord too low to hear properly; it makes the tips of grass quiver and tick against one another even on windless days.

Their backs are the source of the extraordinary brightness of the place. Each carries ranks of clear plates, silvered underneath, which tip very slowly after the sun. In the morning all the east faces of all the pillars light up together. The plates gather heat enough to keep the animal’s mouth working against the cold stone; a loose one lying on the ground will melt itself a little socket in the frost. In exchange for warmth and powdered mineral the trees grow tapered shoots straight into the folds of the animals’ bellies. Sap goes in; a fine chalky meal comes out. Fierce little six-legged birds shoulder one another aside to get at the meal while it is still wet. There are millions of them on a single slope. When one is crowded off, it flings out two pairs of legs and sails on the skin between them to another back, screaming the whole way. The scream is the thin high sound that carries over the grinding.

Seen from the cap of a pillar, the country has the look of an industry in full operation. There are bright ranks of collectors, deep vibrations at regular intervals, ground stone pouring continuously from a thousand mouths, green belts carrying the product away. There is waste heat enough to make the distant pillars waver. Yet nothing here was assembled. A young grinding animal, no bigger than a plum, has the same clear plates on its back and the same big blunt teeth. It has to spend its first winter on the sunny side of a pebble. Most of the warm pebbles are occupied.

For all the racket, a whole afternoon can pass without any large thing moving. The birds settle. The plates turn a fraction. In the far distance a bank of white appears to advance across the green, but this is sunlight passing from one colony to the next as the world turns. The eye keeps trying to find a road through it all, a clean run of scree or an empty ridge; every possible route resolves, on looking closer, into backs or leaves or shining occupied ledges. Only in the blue shadow directly north of the tallest pillars is there anything like open ground. There the frost lies undisturbed, and a few narrow tracks cross it, widely spaced, belonging to something light enough to walk on the crust. They go from shadow to shadow. Whatever makes them has all that cold quiet country to itself, a strip at a time.
