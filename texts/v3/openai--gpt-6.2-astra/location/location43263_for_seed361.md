There is room for six people in the hollow, if two sit on the lip with their feet hanging over the plateau. From below there is nothing to see. The whole summit looks like the back of a hand laid flat against the sky. It has been wearing down so long that the little gutter out of the hollow has no perceptible slope; a bead of water in it can go either way, according to the wind.

At midday the stone is warm through the seat of your trousers. It is a fine-grained gray stone with much harder, almond-shaped inclusions. In the floor of the hollow these stand a finger's breadth proud. Along the lip even the almonds have been worn flat. There are three clumps of vegetation: a fern bed no bigger than a washbasin, a glossy thicket growing out of the gutter, and a single plant with broad, cupped leaves, each bearing the round brown waterline of a vanished pool. Nothing much grows between them. One can see a single red mite make a journey of several minutes over the bare floor.

The noise is astonishing. It comes up through your hands if you lean on them. Under the floor are thin partings in the stone, and all the partings are full of animals. The largest are the size of apple pips, flattened, with ivory backs and a ridge of teeth on one foreleg. They brace their backs against the ceiling and rasp the ridge across the floor. A male that finds a good sounding place can possess it for most of his life, though he may have to share the back half of his body with a tenant that combs him for parasites. There are thousands of males. In the heated hours the hollow rings like a workshop where everyone is filing metal and nobody ever has to stop to turn a piece in the vise.

Other creatures hunt by these vibrations. A thread-thin black lizard lays its chin on an almond of rock, waits, then runs to the fern bed and thrusts its entire head down a crack. Sometimes it gets the tenant instead. The tenant comes apart in its mouth in a quantity of bitter yellow dust. From time to time a lizard backs out sneezing extravagantly in the ordinary sunshine.

There is one made thing, easily mistaken for another patch of bare stone. Four dull staples enclose a rectangle about the size of a door laid down. Inside it the weather is a little late. A cloud passes over the hollow; the rectangle stays bright for nine more breaths. When the rest of the floor is bright again there is a neat oblong of shade. No one has found anything beneath the staples. One has come loose and turns freely in its socket. The delay persists, except in the loose corner, where light wavers in a fashion trying to the eyes. It is best seen indirectly, by watching the little ferns on that edge fold and unfold their sensitive tips.

Rain comes seldom, in short, hard showers that rake the summit sideways. The first drops send a running silence through the rock. Then a sheet of water skims the floor, divides around the still dry rectangle and goes out over the lip. For nine breaths after the shower the rectangle has its own rain, lovely to see in clear air. This second, smaller runoff is the important one. The gutter is already wet; instead of breaking up into beads the water travels all the way along it into the glossy thicket. Out of the thicket come hundreds of translucent shrimp, swimming *up* that shallow flow. They have just enough time to reach the partings in the floor before the connection dries. Within the hour you can see their pale fans working in the cracks, sifting the yellow dust and the cast skins out of the water.

Late in the day the great rasping note comes unstitched. Individual strokes become audible, then pairs answering from opposite walls. Wind up here smells of hot mineral and very distant fires. Long after the lower country is in shadow, there is light enough on the lip to see the mite again, or another just like it, arrived at the foot of a fern. It stands up on its hind legs. The lowest frond is a little too high. It walks on around the rim of the bed until it finds one that touches.
