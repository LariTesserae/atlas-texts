On the highest bench of the mountain the water lies within a finger’s depth of the grass. A rail walking out into a meadow leaves a row of three-toed pools, each with a little plug of brown water rising in it, clearing from the edges inward. Beyond the bird there are miles of wet green level ground. Only at the far end, where a stream goes over the lip and its white thread can be seen against a bank of cloud far below, is there much sense of the height of the place.

It is hot up here even before sunrise. Under the bench run beds of black volcanic stone, some tight as glass, others so full of holes that a hillside’s worth of rain can go into them without leaving so much as a puddle. The water comes up again warm. In places it comes up too hot for anything but a slick orange skin to grow about the mouth of the spring. A few yards off, where the hot runnels mingle with rainwater, there are beds of sedge taller than a rail and a little palm with ragged leaves that grows with its whole trunk under water. Its crown seems to have been laid loose on the surface. When the current changes it leans the other way, trailing a comb of fine bubbles.

There are long low ridges across the bench. They were the fronts of separate flows once, steep and sharp; now they have the rounded profiles of sleeping cattle, and trees grow along their backs with enough space between them to see clear across the marsh. In ordinary daylight the distances are beautifully legible: a yellow meadow, a dark ridge, three silver reaches of the same stream, then a farther ridge streaked with the white droppings of cliff birds. The foliage is thickest in the seams. There a seed can fall through five or six layers of leaves and come to rest in the fork of a fern, still a good way from soil. Tiny crabs live in those forks. They go about their work upside down as readily as right side up, painstakingly stripping the soft parts from fallen flowers.

For an hour or so in the morning the place sounds like very little. There is the dry click of the crabs, though one has to be almost under a fern to hear it. There is a sucking noise when a grazing bird pulls up a bulb. Then somewhere out in the middle of the bench comes a soft double knock. A patch of pond surface lifts, spills over its rim and settles. Another patch does the same a long way off. Belowground, steam has been pressing on a column of water until the column clears a bend in its passage; water pours out above, cold water slips in below, the steam collapses, and the whole business begins again. Some of the vents have worn such easy passages that they keep time for days. Put an ear to an exposed rib of stone and the bench is busy from end to end, pumping, drawing, knocking over. Stand up and there is a dragonfly poised above its reflection, neither of them visibly stirring.

Nothing on the meadow stays flooded to quite the same depth. A rainstorm on the upper cone may make itself known here the following afternoon, in perfectly clear weather, by a slow rise that brings the small fish up among the grass heads. The rails know where to wait. So does a long tawny cat that sleeps through the hot part of the day in the shade of the ridge trees. It seldom has to enter the water. It walks the narrow strips of dry ground left between the rising pools, pausing wherever two strips run together; most things with fur or feathers come that way sooner or later.

Toward evening the wind usually turns uphill. It brings up a cool smell of bare stone from the enormous face below the bench. For a few minutes the grass all shows its pale undersides and the steam is laid flat, and one can see to the very edge. The stream there has cut back into the lip year by year, making a green notch with dripping walls and a single crooked tree rooted deep in one side. Its branches spread out into empty air. Little black birds fly up out of the notch at dusk, wet from bathing in the last shallow inch of the stream. They settle all along those branches to dry, with the waterfall going down through cloud beneath their feet and the warm meadow at their backs still bright enough to feed in.
