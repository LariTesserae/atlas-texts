At the east end of the freight line, the rails go on for half a mile beyond the floor. They rest on a trellis of pale branches. From the last sleeper you can look down into a space the size of a market hall, where leaves as broad as sails are slowly opening, edge toward edge. Once they touch, they will knit. A surveyor has already put a blue peg into the tip of one leaf to mark the corner of a future school. For the time being, a little flock of six-legged animals roosts upside down on the peg.

This is how the basin is built, from its edges inward, a new underground district every eighty years or so. The older districts stand on a deck of living wood thick enough to take a foundry. The newest will shake a little under the weight of a lunch basket. There is scarcely a bare mineral surface anywhere. Even the ceiling is faced with the backs of leaves, close-set and silver, except at intervals where shafts go up through three hundred feet of rock. The shafts are lined with the same silver tissue. At noon each delivers a remarkably faithful patch of the day above: warm yellow sun, the traveling shadow of a cloud, sometimes the tiny, bewildering shadow of a bird. Banks of electric lamps fill in between. By one in the afternoon you can sit anywhere on the basin floor and mend a watch.

For a town so entirely overgrown, it has long views. The plant wears most of its bulk flat. Between the station and the winding works there are two miles of smooth green-gray rind, marked only by tram poles and the occasional hump where a new root has passed beneath. A motorman can see someone waiting at the works stop five minutes before he reaches them. In the distance, beyond the rows of low brick-red workshops, the rind rises in shallow pleats. Each pleat has a street inside it. You enter at one end past a row of coat hooks and come out at the other among loading cranes, having passed a bakery, a machine shop and forty or fifty front doors. In cold weather these streets draw shut along the top. The hooks are there because it is appreciably warmer inside.

Nothing drips. A coat left on its hook for a generation would collect a fine nap of dust on the shoulders and never acquire a spot of mildew. Cut the rind and a clear oil wells up, carrying little plates of salt that slide over one another and seal the cut. The grazing animals have narrow, opposed teeth with which they pinch out a plate before the next one slides into place. Along the tramway at dusk there are hundreds of them, intent on their tiny meals. Their chief enemy is a rust-colored glider that lets go of the ceiling and steers by twisting its tail. Every so often a whole acre of grazers turns its pale underside upward at once. The glider, suddenly deprived of the sight of any one back, generally misses.

The winding works makes use of the same slow pull that opens the great leaves. Behind its windows you can see drums taller than houses, each with a strap running out through a slot in the wall to a growing rib. Over a week the rib draws the strap perhaps the length of a hand. There is enough force in that hand's breadth to lift a train. Gearing turns it into electricity; the electricity runs the trams and lamps and the presses that stamp fallen leaf sheath into household goods. People eat off plates with the fine branching veins still visible under their food. If you break one across a vein it comes apart in two clean pieces. If you break it lengthwise it may unravel all over the table.

Most of this makes very little noise. The works gives off a steady note you feel in the bench at the tram stop before you hear it. Wood catches the clatter of wheels. The commonest sound out in the open is a light, widely scattered ticking as the ceiling turns its silver scales to follow the sun. On a clear day the adjustment travels west in a discernible front. Shift workers lying on the warm housings of the generators can tell, with their eyes shut, when the light has reached the far side of town.

Only at the unfinished east end does the basin smell strongly of anything: a bitter, fresh smell like the inside of a nut. There the edges of two leaves sometimes fail to meet. One slips beneath its neighbor and goes on growing in the dark, making a second floor a few feet below the first. The planners used to count these laps as waste. Now, before they stake out a district, they send someone under with a lamp to see what has been given to them. The last overlap yielded a parcel tunnel, a sheltered winter walk, and a room of no practical use, long and low, with a gold seam running the whole length of its ceiling. They put a bench in it and left it alone.
