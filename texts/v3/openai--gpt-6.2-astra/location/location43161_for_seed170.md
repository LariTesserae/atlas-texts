Up on the lime shelf the wind carries a little grit at the height of your ankles and nothing higher. Every standing stone has a waist. Some have worn through and settled straight down onto their own pedestals, so neatly that only a line of white meal shows where the break occurred. Between one stone and the next it can be half an hour’s walk. There is ample daylight, a plain blue sky, and a noise like a hundred freight wagons being emptied of gravel just over the hill.

Over the hill the shelf continues, dipping very slightly east. What looked from a distance like a dark lake is a bed of plum-colored garnets. The wind has sorted them by size. At the near lip they are as fine as flour; at the far lip there are beads big enough to pick up individually, hot enough to pass quickly from hand to hand. The noise is greater here. It comes from the garnets themselves, though none appear to move.

It takes a while to see an animal. The first one is usually visible in the shade of a stone: a low, six-legged thing about the length of a jackrabbit, with a deep chest and a pale, blunt face. It is walking very slowly out of the shade. Before its nose reaches the sun, another nose comes through the back of its head. Then there are dozens of noses, the ears at different angles, a darker individual with a notch in its lip, a small one pushing sideways among the legs. They do not crowd each other or ooze together. Each has its own precise hide. They occupy almost exactly the same stretch of ground.

Most of the space on the shelf is vacant. Most of the animals in the world live here.

The herds follow routes so old that what wears a route deeper is no longer the passage of feet but the passage of days. In one place Tuesday’s herd walks abreast of a herd from a summer not yet arrived. In another a mother has stopped to listen and a thousand years of her descendants have to make a little jog around her. Seen in the open sun, all those bodies in register look like a single animal drawn in dark pencil, the line heavily gone over at the shoulder and almost erased at the rump. Shade separates them. A cloud crossing the shelf can disclose, in the time it takes its edge to pass over one apparently solitary grazer, a migration of stupefying size. For miles the bare ground stays bare.

What they graze is the size difference between the garnets. Each mouth has two tough belts of gum running in opposite directions. The coarse grains turn one belt; the fines pack into the grooves of the other. Down both flanks, underneath the short dun hair, little knobs advance in sequence as the load goes through. There is a warm electrical smell about a feeding herd, and the sound at close quarters resolves into distinct operations: intake, a pause while something engages, the long rattling run, the dry soft puff of discharge. Behind a grazer lies a teaspoonful of uniform pink sand. The wind takes it apart again. An entire afternoon’s feeding may be undone by a single breath that brings two different weights of grit rolling back together.

There are no buildings, not even a wall to get behind when the shelf is loud. The nearest thing to shelter is the western scarp, where the lime curls under in a long lip. From here one looks down through a great depth of hot clear air to the lower country. Nothing rises from it. No cloud develops along the rim. Once in a long while a white rind detaches from the underlip and falls, flashing at long intervals as it turns. Long after it has dwindled out of sight, animals all along the scarp lift their faces. The concussion comes up through their feet before it comes up through the air.

Toward evening the shadows lengthen east across the garnet beds. This is when it becomes possible to see how very young some of the animals are. They have oversize joints and a peculiar deliberateness about placing a foot; one puts its foot down wrong and stands looking at it. In the next band of light that same knee belongs to an old, steady beast with the hair rubbed off it. The red sun takes hours getting through the dust at the horizon. All that time the shelf ticks and grinds, giving off the heat it has stored, while along one narrow track the young animal has the shade to itself. At last it picks its foot up out of the hot beads and tries again.
