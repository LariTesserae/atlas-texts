From the ridge path the shelf looks just big enough to turn a loaded mule on. Only from its upper end can you see how it widens under the overhang, and how much grows there. A green strip runs the length of the wall. Beyond it is a floor of pale stone, tipped a little toward the light, with three or four big flakes standing up on edge. Then the floor stops. There are mornings when the cloud is so far down that it looks like snow lying in the fields of a lower country.

The wall used to stand farther out. You can match a black vein in one of the flakes to its other half overhead. The break is still bright and sugary; if you sit on the flake it leaves grit on the seat of your trousers. Nothing has had time to round off here. Even the tiny runnels in the floor go by elbows, following cracks.

At sunrise every leaf along the wall wears a hard white rim. The tallest plants are willows no higher than a boot, with thick old trunks going sideways under the moss. Between them grow pleated ferns, mats of pink campion, and a little round-leaved herb that smells of apples when trodden on. They have soil to grow in because for years the swifts have nested in the split above them. Everything dropped from that split stays in the lee of the rock: grass stems, droppings, the husks of beetles, the occasional whole bird. Dig a thumb into the green and there is a depth of black, rather warm earth quite out of keeping with the cold stone an arm's length away.

For most of the morning the only water is locked up in the leaf rims. Then sunlight reaches a brown patch on the roof, and drops start to hang from it, swelling for a long time before they fall. Someone has laid a split length of wood beneath the patch. One end rests on a cairn of two stones; the other feeds a hollow pecked into the floor, just deep enough to dip a cup. The wood is worn almost paper-thin at the end. When it is running, a finch can drink there without once lifting its head. By early afternoon the patch has given up all the night's rime. The last bead stays on the underside of the spout and shrinks away.

Those few wet hours are busy. Little flies climb out of the moss and stand cleaning their faces. A vole works along the willow trunks, biting off buds and leaving each pair of outer scales neatly together. The swifts come up from below the shelf at such speed that by the time their shadows cross the floor the birds themselves are in the roof. There are dozens of them up there. Once in a while a young one shows its wide pale mouth at the seam, and the apparently solid wall acquires, for a second, a small pink hinge. Nearly all this goes on without a sound that carries. You can hear a finch's claws slip on the lip of the cup. The wind is blowing hard enough to flatten the grass on the ridge, but here it passes several yards out, in the blue.

At the wide end are the remains of a shelter: a low curve of knee-high stones and one peg of dark horn driven into a joint. The peg is still polished on top where a loop used to hang. Inside the curve the ground is bare except for a square of exceptionally fine turf. Voles have burrowed under it until it gives a little under the palm. It is the only soft place to sit. From there, in the fierce reflected light, one can watch a small falcon hanging out beyond the brink, so nearly level with the eye that the soft buff feathers under its chin are visible. It holds its place with minute corrections of either wing. A swift slips in under it. Another takes a wider course. For a moment the falcon tips, and all three go out of sight.

Shade rises up the wall long before the peaks opposite lose their sun. The flies go back among the leaves. Ice forms first in the marks of the pecking tool around the shallow cup, a white dot in each blow, so that at dusk you can see how it was made considerably better than you could at noon.
