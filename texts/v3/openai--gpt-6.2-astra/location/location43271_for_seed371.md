The grove lies in the split end of a ridge, a thousand feet above the last ground that will hold a tree upright. From lip to lip the cleft is no wider than the reach of a fallen spruce. It is quite possible to cross the ridge in daylight and take the green in the crack for a stain. Down among the stems there is barely enough light to see the opposite wall. The sky is a crooked strip, the color of the back of a spoon.

Nearly everything that grows here comes out of the same long seam in the stone. A pine has lain along it for so long that its trunk is buried in its own needles. What look like separate little pines farther down are its branches, rooted where they touch. There are willows no taller than grass with astonishingly thick wrists. Ferns occupy the intervals between the willow stems, and there is a second, finer growth of them tucked up under the fronds of the first. Only the topmost needles ever stand clear in the wind. Lower down, the plants lean on one another in the dark. A whole bank of green can move several inches before any one stem has to bend very far.

Most of the stone is dry. Flakes come off it with a white edge, and there is still grey flour in the angles of the freshest breaks. At night the ridge catches passing cloud. Droplets collect on the needles and travel along their undersides until they reach a twig; the twigs conduct them inward. The ground directly under the pine trunk is black and damp even when the exposed tips of the branches are rattling dry. There is one spoonful of open water in the heel of a detached slab. A gust puts a shiver on it. Between gusts it holds a little more sky than can be seen by looking up.

The great noise in the cleft comes from below. A slit at the foot of the wall goes clean through to the windward face of the mountain. When the wind bears against that face, air issues from the slit in a hard level sheet. It drives the loose needles before it, flattens the ferns from beneath and sets a thin plate of stone chattering against its bed. There is a bass note to this, a rapid knocking over it, and over that the high tearing sound of air going through leaves. With eyes shut one might judge there was a considerable machine at work quite close by. Then the pressure drops. The plate gives two or three separate knocks. A needle slides down a frond with a small, perfectly audible tick.

The pauses are seldom long enough for everything to settle. Air spilling in over the lip meets air still rising from below; the willow leaves turn their pale sides out in patches that run first one way, then the other. Cold grit comes skimming across the green. Now and then a piece too heavy for the wind to carry falls from the wall, cuts straight through the ferns and stays where it lands. The newest one is a wedge of pale rock with a single green smear on its point. Its socket, just overhead, would fit it exactly.

Nothing calls here or takes flight when the branches heave. In a lull the small pale movements that persist at the back of the cleft turn out to be the tips of grasses, caught under a pine bough and slowly working free. The last tip slips loose and stands upright in the dimness. Then the stone begins to knock again.
