The easiest way to see how low the country lies is to watch the lip of a puddle give way. Water the depth of a thumb spills out and keeps going until there is a dark streak halfway to the horizon. In an hour a second horizon appears in the streak, upside down and rather clearer than the first. On either side the pavement is already drying. It dries to the color of the inside of a water jar, a warm dun pink, except where salt has come up white in the whorls of a thumbprint.

There are thumbprints everywhere. The slabs are hand-sized and roughly square, made by pressing wet clay into a four-sided frame, striking off the excess with a cord, and tipping the result onto the ground. Here and there someone forgot to wet the frame and a corner pulled away with it. Such faults are easy to pick out in the low light. For hundreds of miles every raised edge has a fine shadow, blue on its western side. The light comes through a ceiling of unglazed porcelain so high and thin that the shadows of the ceiling joists take most of a day to pass overhead. The joists themselves are beyond sight. On a particularly clear afternoon you can just make out the holes where the potter pricked an air bubble.

Almost everything that stands above ankle height is a kiln. They stand a long way apart. One will be a squat beehive of yellow brick with a throat black enough to look wet; the next, a day’s journey off, four battered walls around a square of trembling air. Much of the heat comes from below, through courses of brick laid crosswise to the pavement. In a kiln the courses turn up on edge and deliver their heat to the chamber. There is no fuel to gather here. No tree has left a fleck of charcoal in the clay, no beetle a case, no animal a little crescent of bone. Break open a slab and what looks like grit in the middle proves under a lens to be broken pottery, tempered with finer pieces of broken pottery in its turn.

By late afternoon the underside of the ceiling is wet. The first drops fall so far apart that each has time to make a complete little landscape: a crown of thrown-up slip, a moat, a streak of clear water running away to the east. Then a seam in the porcelain begins to shed water along its whole length. A gray curtain crosses the plain. A kiln fifteen miles away goes dark from the foot upward and presently the pavement underfoot is bobbing. The squares ride very gently against one another. There is a small expensive sound, like a cup set down carelessly in the next room. Beyond that, very little. Whole districts are in motion, and a drop falling into the mouth of a kiln is still audible.

It is during these floods that the plain gets made. A floating slab tips, exposing a patch of soft red clay directly beneath. Four narrow strips of fired earth come together around the patch. A wet cord draws itself taut between two corners, sweeps sideways, and leaves the clay level. There is a short pause. Then the impression of a thumb deepens at one edge and the square turns out of its mold, moving with the peculiar deftness of a thing done all day by someone thinking of supper. The heat takes it almost at once. It is possible to watch every part of this operation except the part where there ought to be a hand.

The new square occupies as much room as the old one. This matters surprisingly little close by. Two kilns that stood a mile apart in the morning stand a mile and one slab apart at night; the difference is hardly worth walking back to check. But let a rain seam travel the length of the country, let every lifted slab turn out another, and by morning an inland sea has acquired a southern shore which is also, for several hours, its northern shore. From a kiln door you can see the back of your own kiln across thirty miles of rain. Water pours downhill both ways into the same gutter. The gutter takes it. Down in the dark the water can be heard pattering onto something hollow, moving farther and farther away without ever falling very far.

Toward evening the ceiling takes on the dull red translucence of an ear held against a lamp. The water draws back into the joints. For a while the tops of the kilns put out little caps of mist, each with its own slow turn to it, and between them lie enormous tracts of utterly empty, newly washed floor. Nothing comes out to feed in the pools. Nothing has worn a path between one door and another. At the edge of the last pool to dry there is usually a low ridge where the flood has pushed up a quantity of slip. It looks soft enough to smooth away with a heel. By dark it will have been pinched into a row of shallow bowls, each one still attached to the next by a little waist of clay. They stand there cooling through the night. In the morning, some distance off, the country will have need of bowls.
