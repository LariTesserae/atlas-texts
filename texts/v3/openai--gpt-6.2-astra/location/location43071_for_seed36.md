There are no banks to the river yet. There is a long wet place, a little lower than the rest of the country, where the roots lie bare and pink in a finger’s depth of water. Follow it for half a mile and it goes under a stand of stems so closely set that even the water must get through by wetting one stem and then the next. Beyond the stand it opens again, wide enough to reflect the roof.

The roof is the underside of a salt bed. On top, in the sun, it must be dazzling. Down here the light is the color of milk in a green glass. It comes through several feet of crystal, strongest along the seams; you can look straight up without squinting, though there is light enough to see the red ribs in a leaf across the valley. Some of the taller plants have reached the salt and spread their leaves flat against it. Seen from below, their crowns resemble the hands of people shading their eyes to look in.

It is hot. Where the ground is dry it smells of the inside of a seed sack, and where it is damp there is a smell like a split melon. Both are smells of the ground itself. What looks at a distance like a succession of low earthen ridges is the exposed portion of an enormous rootstock. There is hardly a handful of earth in the whole valley. Stand in a hollow between two ridges and your heels settle into a fine nap of root hairs. There is something pleasant and a little embarrassing about the fit.

For all the press of growth, the view is long. Most things grow sideways here. A whole wood of jointed canes lies at the height of your hip, putting up a leaf at each joint; beyond it are acres of round, plated backs with little orchards of shoots on them. In the far distance, where the salt roof descends, roof and country meet in a brilliant line. The plants along that line have only a few inches of room. They grow as green writing in the cracks.

In three or four places above the wet ground there are small enclosures. Each consists of a circle of canes that were bent inward while tender and have grown fast where they cross. A gap has been left on the dry side, just wide enough for shoulders. Inside, the ground cover is a different variety, short and silvery, soft even in the heat. Somebody went to the trouble of settling it here. No cut ends, no ashes, nothing that might tell you how long ago. In one enclosure a sand-colored lizard is lying on its belly with its toes spread luxuriously apart. You might pass through the other three and another mile of country without seeing a second animal.

Water hangs from the leaves that touch the cooler seams of the roof. Occasionally a drop lets go. There is a light slap somewhere among the canes, followed a good while later by a much smaller tick as the water reaches the next leaf down. These sounds carry astonishingly far. The silence between them has no insect whine in it, no wind. After a while you can tell which patches of foliage beneath the roof are wet without looking up.

The wet places never quite fill, and the roots never quite cover them. At the edge of the long pool you can see pale knobs under the skin of the rootstock, each with the crease where a new shoot will open. They look a day from breaking through. Yet the water has left no mark on them, not even a tide line of green. Everything here has got as far as the moment before a country begins to have a history: a roof without a fallen slab, a hillside without a gully, the first soft lip of a riverbank. There is no telling how long a place can stay that new.
