The duck stair is cut into the lip of an old rice terrace. Its treads are very broad and only a hand high. Water runs down one side of it; on the other, a woman is coming up with two baskets of eggs, holding them out from her hips to keep them level. She stops on the landing. She can feel the ducks coming before she sees them. The woven hurdle at the head of the stair bulges, lifts, and lets through a hundred dirty white breasts at once. For a minute the woman stands in the middle of a noise like somebody vigorously washing clothes. Then the stair is empty except for a little brown drake trying to swallow a snail.

There is a whole market town here, folded into a notch of the mountain small enough that a shout from the lower spillway can stop a man emptying a ditch at the top. From across the valley it looks like a patch of alder scrub. Even standing on the stair in good daylight it is difficult to say where the houses are. What looks like one long green bank has twelve separate front doors, each with a pair of straw sandals hanging up to drain. The roofs pitch back against the hillside. Years of patching with fresh rushes have made them thick enough to grow a crop of their own: grass near the ridgepoles, ferns lower down, and at the eaves a very fine red-stemmed weed whose roots hang in the rain chains. The town goats know which roofs will take their weight. Visitors do not.

It is late in the growing season. The little shelves of rice below the houses are yellowing, though the air up here is cool enough that the egg woman wears a felt jacket. In gaps in the roofs one can see other mountains, blue all the way to their feet. The light on them is steady. Here a passing cloud will turn every leaf the same flat silver for a moment, and then the sun comes out and there are shadows again, thousands of small, distinct, trembling shadows. Nothing has quite dried from yesterday. A hand trailed along a railing comes away wet at the cuff.

The firmest things in town are the terrace walls. They were laid with the rounded faces of the stones turned in, so that from outside a wall offers a thousand little ledges to anything that wants to grow there. Pennywort runs in overlapping scales down to the water. There are pear trees espaliered against the houses, their branches tied so close to the plaster that when somebody picks a pear the knot gives a little knock indoors. In the lanes, where no topsoil could survive the feet, people lay split lengths of bamboo crosswise and jam sweet flag into the muddy edges to hold them. The leaves get trodden and give off a clean, fierce smell. Beneath all this is the mountain's grey stone, exposed in one place only, a hump worn smooth at the turn into the market. On wet days a child sits there with an armful of cut bracken, throwing a fresh handful under the feet of each laden mule.

There are animals enough for a place ten times the size. The ducks have been brought in from all the outlying paddies to glean a field just cut. Finches have come for the same reason, in flocks so close that their turns show up in the corner of the eye as a kind of blinking. Every patch of water left by a hoof has its boatmen rowing upside down. Swallows shoot under the market awnings after flies and come out between the legs of people sitting on the low wall. Under that wall, in wet chambers no wider than a fist, frogs keep up a tremendous rattling call. One stops when a foot comes down overhead; its two neighbors carry on. At night, when the shutters are tied and the mules stand asleep with their chins against their ropes, the progress of a late walker can be followed by the moving gap in the sound.

Most of the day's business is done to the beat of two wooden grain stamps at the foot of the stair. Water tips a scoop, the beam rises, the scoop spills, the head falls into the mortar. The stamps rarely strike together. People have grown so used to talking between the blows that when both beams are propped up at noon there is an odd, hesitant quality to the bargaining. You can hear the actual articles for sale then: little crabs scrabbling in a crock, green walnuts being rolled across a board to show how full they are. One stall sells nothing but lengths of hazel already bent to the common curve of the town's basket handles. There is hardly room for two customers before it, but its owner has been there forty years.

In the afternoon a boy at the top calls down that the spring has gone brown. It has rained somewhere above them, beyond the visible ridge. Without shutting up shop the crab seller reaches back and pulls a wooden peg. His neighbor lifts a wicker screen. All along the lane people make these small adjustments, some with a toe while their hands are busy. By the time the first cold surge comes through, the house channels have been closed and the broad old floodcourse is open. For a little while a stream as wide as a cart goes tearing down the duck stair. Straw spins at the landings. The grain stamps begin to gallop until somebody hooks their scoops clear. Children lean out over the rail to see what the mountain is sending down today: cones, a piece of honeycomb, a thrashing clump of grass with a perfectly still mouse on top. At the bottom the water spreads into the cut field and loses its brown color among the stubble.

Before the last tread is clear, the egg woman is on it again, this time going home with salt in one basket and the other over her head against the dripping. She pauses to tuck a loose stone back into the lip of the landing. It fits in one position only. By morning there will be silt around it, and duck prints in the silt, and very likely one of those red weeds taking hold in the seam.
