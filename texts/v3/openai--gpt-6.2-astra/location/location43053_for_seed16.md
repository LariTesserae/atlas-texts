The entrance lies at the foot of a black stone curb. Fine dust has polished the curb to the shape of a well-used doorstep. Behind it is a slit about the width of a hand, and behind the slit, lower than the lowest street of the buried town, is a long, hot room. Almost the whole room is empty. A little light comes through the slit and lies on the opposite wall in a shape like a bent thumb. Everything that grows here grows just below it.

The room was once a junction for air. Four square flues come down through the old masonry, each from a different quarter. You can tell them apart by the workmanship. One has a lining of fitted black tiles; one is a rough bore with chisel marks and a ledge where two parties digging from opposite ends missed their meeting by an inch. On hot afternoons the taller shafts draw air up and the shorter ones bring it down. The builders hung thin stone vanes at the mouths to keep the flow in one direction. Three are gone. The fourth still swings, and when it falls back against its stop the report is loud enough to dislodge grains from the ceiling. Afterward you hear each grain land.

Nothing here has ever been wet. The town was built of split stone locked with wedges, its joints packed with a yellow earth that fuses under heat. In a few places the packing has squeezed out in little hard lips. The wind would long ago have scoured them flush except that the local growth favors precisely that yellow stuff. A root no thicker than a hair enters the lip lengthwise, branches in it, and splits it into leaves of stone. In the opened seam the true leaves come up: dark red blades, pale and tacky at the base, fine-toothed along one side. Their living sap is a clear oil. A bruised blade gives off the smell of a hot orange peel.

Seen from the entrance there seems very little of this garden, just a dark hem along a wall. Close to, it is deep enough to lose sight of the masonry. Low, tightly furled shoots push up under a canopy of older blades. Spent leaves hang on by their teeth and catch the fine mineral meal that comes down the rough bore. For a few minutes every afternoon the bent patch of light reaches the garden. The change begins at one end: a blade untwists, knocks its neighbor loose, and a stretch of foliage turns its red undersides to the light with the soft, deliberate rasp of a pack of cards being spread. The far end is still opening when the first leaves begin to close.

There is a narrow bare place halfway along the bed. Here the surviving vane directs a hard jet against the wall. It has cut a shallow oval into the stone and will cut no deeper; the hollow now turns the moving grit cleanly out again. On either side of the oval the growth is exceptionally handsome. Its folded blades stand in two thick ranks with a clean path between, as though somebody still came down from the town to tend this one short border.

At the end of the day the flues reverse in order of height. For a while air comes into the room from all four quarters, hot from the tile roofs, bitter with furnace earth, carrying a trace of the pale dust that has spent centuries working its way down the stairs of the northern houses. The vane lifts nearly level. The plants flatten. Out through the handwide slit goes a single sustained note that can just be heard above ground, if one happens to be standing at the curb. Then the tallest flue takes the draft, the note cuts off, and down in the dark the stone falls against its stop.
