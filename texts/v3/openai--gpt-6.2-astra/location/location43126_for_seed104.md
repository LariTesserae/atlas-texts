The first sign of the height is a leaf going over the edge. It drifts out quite level, a coppery oval with a bite missing, and for a while it keeps pace with the other leaves. Then the water carrying it thins to a skin, the skin breaks, and the leaf begins the long, unhurried tumble down to the clouds.

Up here there is a sea. Its bed is the crown of a mountain worn so broad and flat that weather can spend a month crossing it. Hardly anything of the old relief survives. Where there was a range there is a long shoal of pink stone, deep enough at one end to float a tree, shallow enough at the other for the tips of the sedges to write on the surface. The divides between drowned river systems are as low as the raised seams of a blanket. A change of wind sends warm water washing over them and joins forests that have stood in separate waters for the better part of a season.

Most of the light comes through green. Shoals far out in the sea support groves of bladder trees, rooted in the stone, their trunks dividing underwater into pale supple fingers. The fingers bear the floats, and the floats bear small thickets of red leaves in the air. Below these are streamers of weed, and on the streamers grow finer plants like eyelashes. There is scarcely a handbreadth of bare water in the groves. Yet nothing much stirs there of its own accord. One can follow what looks like a slender fish for a long way through the dusk and find it is the loose end of a root, nosing from side to side in a current too slow to feel.

The sea has rain both above and below. Ordinary rain comes in off the rim, smelling of the enormous warm drop beneath. The other kind begins in the hollows of the bed. Little round beads of clear water work their way out of the stone and rise through the darker water without mixing with it. On a day of heavy up-rain the whole forest leans. The beads gather under the leaves until they are large enough to tip them, then go rolling upward again. At the surface they hang for a moment like glass buttons on the underside of a cloth. Where enough collect, a patch of sea lifts bodily into the air.

These lifted waters travel inland at the pace of shade. Some are no bigger than a pool; some have a dark horizon of their own and take all afternoon to pass overhead. From below, one sees roots, seeds, a little silt caught up in them, the occasional perfect impression of a stone that has stayed behind. A lifted sea will settle wherever the ground remembers being low. This is not necessarily where it is low now. There are places where a river runs a few fathoms above its former bed, following every turn with great fidelity, while on the actual stone underneath, soft yellow moss grows in a dry and wonderfully sheltered strip.

In one such strip stand seven posts of black wood. They are set in pairs except for the last one. Each has a polished notch, at about the height of a bent elbow; on one there is still a twist of fibre pinched deep into the grain. The notches face the empty riverbed. Whatever used to be tied up here had a narrow bow. On wet days the wood gives off a faint sweet smell. Nothing else of the landing remains, and the only sound is an intermittent tick as the moss sheds ripe seed against the posts.

Once in a great while an animal comes up through the canopy to breathe. It has a wide grey back covered in tiny rooted plants. It parts the red leaves so gently that they close behind it still holding their beads of water. For several minutes after it sinks, a clear oval persists in the surface growth. Light goes down through that opening all the way to the pink floor, and there, suddenly visible in their thousands, are the shallow winding tracks of things that move too slowly ever to be seen moving.

Toward evening the outer shoals drain. Water slips over the lip of the mountain for hundreds of miles without a roar; the sound of its fall belongs to a country far below. The wet stone emerges warm, finely pitted, smelling a little of iron. In every pit a minute garden unfolds to take advantage of the brief exposure. Beyond the rim the upper branches of storms shine in the dusk. Then somewhere far inland a suspended lake finds its old valley and comes down. Hours later the returning water reaches the shoals, its leading edge no higher than a snail, quietly lifting each folded stem in turn.
