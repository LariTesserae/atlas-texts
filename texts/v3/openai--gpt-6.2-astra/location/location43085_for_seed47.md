The first sign of a storm is that all the grit gets up on one edge. For a few seconds the plain looks darker, as if a cloud had come across the sun. Then each grain falls over on its other face, and a line of silver runs out across the brown country, faster than anything could run after it. There is a little dry tick at your feet. From the rest of the visible world there is no sound at all.

This is high country, though you could spend a lifetime crossing it without climbing anything taller than a house. It is the worn top of a continental shelf that stands nineteen miles above the lower lands. What used to be mountain chains can be traced by the changes underfoot: eighty miles of green-black stone, a narrow belt of garnet gravel, and then the pale, fine stuff that once lay under the roots of the mountains. All have been brought to the same level. In places the garnets still stick up a little. The low evening sun finds every one of them.

The height is easiest to understand at the western lip. The last few miles slope so gently that the blue coming into view ahead might be a lake. It is air. A second horizon lies far down inside it, with the shadows of lowland clouds moving across a dun surface. Up here there are no clouds. There is scarcely enough gas to blur a distant object, let alone carry a smell or stir a coat. The sunlight feels good on the back of a hand. Put that hand flat on the stone and the cold comes through the glove.

The weather travels through the shelf itself. Deep down, layers of heavy rock creep in opposite directions, catching and slipping. The old survey houses relieve some of the strain. Each is a square room no bigger than a shepherd’s hut, with a door on either side and a floor cut out of the living rock. Stand inside one during a slip and for a moment there is no weight on either foot. The room is passing a very small part of the shelf’s load to another room, perhaps beyond the horizon. Nobody has managed to make two loose pieces of their floor behave this way. It seems to matter that it is all one stone.

There are many fewer houses than there used to be. Around the site of a missing one the stress runs wild. Silver fronts overtake one another, split, double back. A field of flat pebbles begins to turn, each pebble in its own shallow socket, making thousands of little suns wink on and off. A mile away a slab the size of a town rises by the thickness of a fingernail and sheds, all along one side, a falling curtain of red meal. For half an hour there may be no spot on the skyline that holds still under a survey glass. Then the load shifts east. The slab subsides into the bed it has polished for itself, the pebbles settle, and there is the same old plain. Even in the worst country a footprint can last for years. It blinks silver with everything else, right down to the maker’s chipped left heel.

Very little grows here. The best place to look is under a stone with one loose end. Sometimes there is a black fan attached to its underside, thin enough for light to come through it brown. Its ribs are laid across the line of slip; every time the stone rocks, the fan draws a little charge. A large one, old enough to overlap the edges of a palm, may have a snail on it. These have clear shells full of fine yellow branching tubes. They scrape the dead margins of the fans and leave a neat scallop, extraordinarily delicate work for a creature whose whole world spends several hours a day being shaken. Between one inhabited stone and the next there can be a week’s walk.

At dusk the houses become visible from a long way off. They have no lamps. It is just that their roof slabs stay precisely level while the land around them tips, so that for a minute all the roofs west of you hold the same peach-colored piece of sky. There might be three such lights. You pick the nearest and walk toward it over the darkening gravel. By the time you reach the door the color is gone. Inside, somebody long ago cut a sleeping hollow in the floor and rounded off the stone at one end to fit a neck. Lie there, and in the silence you can feel the whole night’s weather going on beneath your head: a quick lift, a long ease, a shiver from some enormous distance, too slight to wake a sleeper but quite distinct if you happen to be awake.
