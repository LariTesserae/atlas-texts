Every seventh stair on the south rain tower has a little brass door in its riser. Behind the highest of these doors is a catch basin about the size of a bread drawer. Open it on a clear afternoon and a breath of warm air wets your lip. For a moment nothing inside is visible. Then you make out a beach, dark red, with something pale running along it in a great hurry.

The beach is ground-up gasket. The whole basin was once lined with the stuff. A few centuries of wash have taken it off the walls and sorted it by weight: fine red meal in the shallows, curls like apple peel farther up, and at the head of the beach three black bolts too heavy for any flood to budge. Beyond the bolts stands a bank of cooling fins. Their lower corners, originally square, have worn into arches. Water moves through the arches in adjoining rooms, each with its own level, ceiling, smell. In one the water is scarcely deeper than the skin on boiled milk. In another a loose washer has been turning on edge for so long that it has cut itself a round, deep well.

The noise is astonishing. You feel for a fault in the stair before you understand that this is its ordinary sound. Most of it comes from a white ceramic tongue, as long as a thumbnail, that beats against the underside of the tread. The tongue returns water to a cistern twelve floors overhead. Nothing flows upward: the water gathers on the tongue, the tongue strikes, and the underside of the distant cistern is wet. That small economy of distance cost the builders a great deal in noise. With the door shut you hear a comfortable hum through your shoes. With it open you cannot hear the person on the stair below asking what you have found.

Life goes on in the intervals between strokes. The pale runner is a six-legged crab carrying a piece of shed shell. It takes the shell behind the middle bolt, where there are scores of crabs, most no larger than a grain of rice. They wedge the pieces into the red grit to shore up their burrows. Flat brown swimmers hang under the surface of the well. When the washer comes around, they let go together and tumble in its wake, mouths open to catch what it scrapes from the sides. A colony of midges occupies the dry screw threads above the door. Their young live almost directly below them, in a runnel that follows the old maker’s stamp; the deepest and safest part of the runnel is the bottom of the numeral nine.

There is very little green. A tuft of fine weed grows where a hairline crack in the riser admits a blade of afternoon light. Its tips are kept neatly cropped by the crabs. Most food comes down from higher in the tower: pollen, grease, dead things from the gutters, dissolved traces of whatever the rain has rinsed off the roof. On hot days the stair itself exudes a clear oil. By evening the swimmers have eaten the cloudy film that grows on it, and the water is clear enough to see the tiny bright crescents their mouths have polished into the fins.

Up here, well above the roofs of the town, a change of weather arrives first as a change of pressure. Before a storm the water in the washer-well rises without a drop yet coming in. Midges walk uphill along the threads. The crabs finish whatever trip they are making and stay behind the bolts. Then the first roof water arrives, warm from its long passage down the sunward skin of the tower. For half a minute all the little rooms become one room. Red sand pours through the arches; the tuft of weed lies flat. A brown swimmer that has spent its whole life in the dark goes sailing past the open door, every bone in its head showing for an instant in the daylight.

By the time the rain reaches the streets below, the basin has drained back to its familiar shore. The washer finds its groove. One crab comes out to investigate a length of fresh copper wire stranded at the high-water mark. It takes hold of one end and backs up the beach with it, stopping whenever it steps on the other end. In the dimness behind the bolts, half a dozen others are already coming down to help.
