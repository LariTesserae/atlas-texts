The river is still on the far side of the trees. What looks like its near bank is the bank of a former channel, a clay lip worn so round that a ripe fig landing on it can roll all the way to the bottom. Down there a few pools remain. Most of the bed is dry enough for leaves to lie loose in it. On a map the bend would be a mile of pale, unfilled space between two arms of forest. On the ground there is scarcely room to set a foot. Little brown frogs sit in the curled leaves, each in the small allowance of damp kept by the leaf against the clay.

It is the last hot week before the rains. Even well after midnight a stone turned over on the bank is warm on both sides. There is no moon yet. The sky shows where the old channel goes, a winding vacancy among the crowns, but gives no useful light below the level of a branch. The separate places announce themselves by smell: split figs on the slope, the peppery leaves of low shrubs on the crest, and, some way out in the dark, the clean smell of moving water.

The trees grow high on old banks like this one all across the plain. Their roots reach the gravel that still carries water underground. In the hollows the growth is lower but just as close: thickets of cane, broad-leaved gingers, vines lying over shrubs and putting down roots on the far side. A view across the plain by day would take in several miles of green, with what seem to be long swells running through it. The ground accounts for very little of that height. It is the difference between a fig tree and a stand of ginger, repeated along the courses the river has taken.

There are flying foxes in the figs. For all their size, a dozen can be busy overhead for some time before one gives itself away by dropping a sucked twist of fruit. Below them, land crabs carry the pulp off sideways in pieces. On the bare clay between two pools there is a continuous low traffic of beetles, crickets, and the geckos that eat them. Very little of this carries to the ear. Now and then comes the sound of something small and hard rolling over a dry leaf. At the last deep pool a night heron has been standing so long in one place that a moth has settled on its back. The water by its shins is thick with prawns; each time it takes one, the others clear a little circle that closes almost at once.

Far upriver a pump runs at night. It cannot be heard from the crest. Down in the bed, with an ear close to the hard bank, there is a steady twin beat, quite distinct from anything the insects make. It keeps the same pace for hours. Then it stops, and the absence is so slight that it takes a while to be sure. There is no track here leading toward it, no cut in the trees. A seed coming down from that direction could pass through the bend and go on to the estuary without touching a made thing.

Near morning a low moon puts a little light into the abandoned channel. For a few minutes the pools shine separately along its length. Between them are pale stretches where deer have been down to lick the clay. Several deer are there now; what looked in the dark like open ground turns out to have been full of bodies standing with their heads lowered. One lifts its head, chews, and goes back to the bank. In a week or two the first rain will dissolve the edges of the lick. Later the river will rise enough to enter at the lower end of the channel and fill it backwards, floating the leaves up into the ginger stems. But tonight the pools are still separate. At the deepest one the heron changes feet, lowering the lifted foot so slowly that the moth stays where it is.
