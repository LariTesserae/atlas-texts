The pool is on the underside of the summit. From the ice field below, on a clear morning, you might see it as a wink under the dark lip of the mountain. There is no place on the summit itself from which you could look down and see it. It lies in a hollow about the size of a cupped hand, full to the brim, its free surface facing the ice six thousand feet below.

The mountain split here last winter. Between the two lips of the split there are still threads of red stone that would fit exactly into the grooves opposite them if the mountain ever closed its mouth. Hot water comes beading out of one such groove. It crawls uphill into the hollow, bringing a little rotten-egg smell and enough heat to keep a patch of the overhang bare in any weather. Elsewhere on the peak the snow has set hard and blue. Here the rock is too hot to lay a cheek against. Daylight, reflected from the snow far below, enters the pool from beneath; a white wavering light plays over every detail of the stone.

What looks at first like grit drifting in the water resolves into a great many kinds of feet. Some are broad and almost perfectly clear, with a spot of yellow fat at each ankle. They belong to beasts so thin that only when two cross one another does the shape of either become apparent. Some feet are black hooks carried in rows along the sides of soft pink purses. The purses graze on the greasy bacterial skin that forms wherever the spring water meets air. They leave clear tracks in it, just the width of a hair. At the hottest end of the hollow there are white shells packed lip to lip, each with a fan of gills open in the welling water. Their young ride those fans for a while. Every so often a fan folds and sends a dozen offspring bowling through the crowd.

There is one loose black grain in the hollow, conspicuous against the red. A little blunt animal with a mouth on the end of each arm picks it up and works it under the rim of a shell. For a long time nothing happens. Then there is a click, scarcely louder than the noise your tongue makes coming away from a tooth. The animal eats. When it has moved on, another of its kind feels along the empty shell until it finds the grain. There are plenty of flakes of red stone lying about, but these break. The black one passes from mouth to mouth all day. It is the oldest thing here apart from the mountain.

Around noon a cloud drags across the peak. The pool loses the bright country reflected in its face and takes on the plain grey look of water in a stone. Mist condenses on the sharp outer lip. Each bead slides inward until the hollow can hold no more. Then the surface bulges toward the ice field, and all the animals within reach of the bulge begin going somewhere in a hurry. The purses dig in their hooks. The clear beasts run straight out onto the bulge and flatten themselves there. They are the hunters of this place; they know which way things fall.

For the breadth of a thumbnail below the pool, down is toward the mountain. Beyond that it is toward the ice. The boundary is not quite level. A drop coming loose at one end hangs, quivers, and goes back into the hollow; at the other it departs so suddenly that it leaves a little pit where it was. Between these places there is a narrow run where drops go sideways. For a moment each is a separate round pond rolling along the underside of the rock. The clear hunters ride its skin. Inside, a pink purse or a handful of new shells turns over slowly, with the whole bright ice field magnified behind it. Just before the drop comes home the hunters push their feet through and feed.

From time to time one of these small ponds misses the hollow on its return. It travels a little farther along the overhang, into red stone that has never been wet before. Most come to nothing. But there is one, a finger’s width east of the main pool, that has lasted since yesterday. It has no spring of its own. A single pink animal is making the circuit of its shore, eating a film too fine to see. In the afternoon light its track goes all the way around. Where the ends meet, the first stretch has clouded over again, and the animal goes on.
