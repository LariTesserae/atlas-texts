The valley is loudest where it hasn't grown a floor yet. There the white tips of the floor-beets knock against one another in open air. Each knock is quite a small sound, like a knuckle on a door. There are six miles of them. Viewed from the older ground, their pale, busy undersides look like a crowd waving from a station. Somewhere down in the middle of all that waving is the place where the two sides will catch, and the valley will have another acre you can stand on.

Above it lies a skin of yellow stone, seldom more than twice the height of a house overhead. The stone lets daylight through. Shadows of things on the surface pass clear across the valley ceiling: a wandering elbow of dune, the fork of a fallen branch, once in a great while the long-legged shadow of an animal no one down here has seen except in silhouette. At noon there is light enough to thread a needle. It is warm and smells of freshly pared cork.

Nothing drips. A cup left mouth-up for a year collects a finger of husks, some amber-colored animal teeth, a little dust as fine as face powder. All the growing things carry a thin oil instead of sap. It goes stiff on contact with air. Break a frond and a bead comes out, trembles, and sets hard enough to tap with a fingernail. Old breaks have strings of these beads on them. In the ceiling groves the strings hang fifteen or twenty feet, clear at their lower ends, dark at their attachments, recording a succession of accidents all the way back to when the grove was a single leaf.

On the finished floor, if finished is the word, one can walk for half a day among plants without seeing much that resembles a plant. There are russet flats with a nap like the back of a moth. There are low, hinged ridges that open in the warmth and expose ranks of orange teeth; the teeth shed a flour, and the flour takes root wherever it falls. What looks like a blue clay bank is a single folded cotyledon, big enough to have a path worn along its fold. The few patches of naked stone are startling. They feel indecently still underfoot.

Animals occupy practically every interval. Pale runners with sixes of small feet go up and down the bead strings, gnawing the bitter rind off the oldest beads. A herd of broad-backed grazers works across the russet flats on its knees. Their young ride underneath, facing backward, combing the nap for the white jumping things disturbed by their parents' passage. When the herd turns, the white things turn too, a sheet of sparks blowing uphill. In the space it takes to look at this, three or four other arrangements have passed through it: copper wings pursuing the sparks, flat black snouts rising from the nap to take the wings, a company of little shell-browed thieves prying at the snouts for whatever has not yet been swallowed. A grazer will sometimes stop chewing and hold its mouth wide. The thieves come in, clean the grinding plates, and leave by the same door. For those few seconds the grazer's expression is one of appalled hospitality.

People have put up shelters here and there on the stone patches. The walls are plaited husk, the roofs a single sheet of bark weighted at the corners with stone adzes gone too blunt to sharpen again. Each threshold has two polished knee hollows. To speak to someone inside, you kneel, lay the point of your chin against the doorpost, and talk with your teeth almost closed. The person inside holds the post between finger and thumb. By air a shout does not carry the length of a bed. By good close-grained wood you can tell one sister's voice from another's.

The great noise travels. One moment it is out in the unfinished acres; the next it is directly overhead. A band of ceiling trees has reached the end of its purchase on the stone. Their hooks let go in succession with a ripping report, and the whole grove swings down, still attached at one end. For a moment its bead strings lie horizontal. The runners cling on with all six feet, their tails pointing the wrong way. Then comes the concussion of the grove settling into the flats, a smell of bruised oranges, a high continuous whine from the crowded wings of everything that has had to get out at once. The grazers are already kneeling along the fresh edge. By evening there will be a strip eaten clean enough to walk through. By morning the upturned hooks will have opened into little yellow fans, avid for the light now reaching them from an entirely unexpected direction.

A path here is consequently named for where it starts, never for where it goes. The path from the Split Post went for years to a shelf of fine red toolstone. Last summer it climbed, so gently that nobody noticed the change at first, into a canopy full of nesting runners. People who need toolstone now go out by the old shell midden, and the children take the path from the Split Post with pockets full of husk flour to see if a runner will come down and eat from a hand.

Toward dusk the light turns the color of the inside of an eggshell. Far out on the unfinished ground, a patch of knocking ceases. Even through the chewing, the shrilling, the groan of the displaced grove, people notice that little omission. Some go to the edge to look. Two white fans have meshed. A cloud of flour hangs beneath them. Already a grazer has put one knee on the join and is considering, with the weight of its whole warm body, whether there is ground there.
