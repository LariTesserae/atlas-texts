At noon the mountain has a green top and a black middle and no visible foot. Its foot is somewhere in the white air below. There are six or seven miles of nearly level country up here, tipped just enough to let you see most of it from the western lip: long meadows, belts of low timber, the zinc roof of a works, more meadows, a second roof so bright it leaves a blue rectangle in your sight when you look away. From that distance it seems possible to walk straight across.

On the ground you find the seams. The whole cap of the mountain is coming apart in slabs. Some of the openings are narrow enough to trap a coin; some could take a house standing upright without scraping either wall. Their edges are still square. Under a fringe of roots the stone is a glossy greenish black, with little round hollows cut in half on either face. If you lie down on one edge you can pick out the matching hollows opposite. There is hardly any rubble below. Nearly everything that has broken off is still falling, in the mountain’s sense of the word: a block that was level with the meadow when the works was built now stands a hand’s breadth lower.

The woods grow into these openings as readily as they grow up. There are white trunks visible thirty feet down, bent out from the walls and then turned neatly toward the sky. Above them, in much better light, grow tiers of plum-colored ferns, and above those a wiry grass whose seed heads interlock across the narrower gaps. In June the grass puts out small yellow flowers on the undersides of the heads. You can cross a whole field without seeing a flower, then go down a maintenance stair and find yourself under a yellow ceiling. It smells faintly of cut pears there. Almost nothing stirs in it. Once in a while a pale moth works its way along upside down, taking most of an afternoon to visit a patch the size of a bedsheet.

Rain is uncommon. The damp comes up at night. Cloud rises into the seams, catches in the hanging woods and leaves the lowest branches loaded with drops. Each little tree has a wet side and a dry side; moss follows the wet side out along a limb to the very last twig. The topsoil, meanwhile, is dry enough to dust your shoes. A plant that means to live on the meadow must spend its first few seasons getting a root over an edge. Where the stone is unbroken there are bare clearings in full sun, warm to touch in spite of the cold air, with the woods packed around them as close as people around a stove.

The works make surveying glass. Below each zinc roof is a room of electric furnaces and, below that, a much longer room where the cast glass cools. These lower rooms lie half on one slab and half on another. Their floors overlap like the sliding leaves of a dining table. Everything that crosses a seam has some such allowance: a loop in a cable, an extra length of gutter tucked inside the next, a hinged step halfway down a flight of stairs. At the back of the western works is a door that once led out onto a loading platform. The platform has moved six inches away. Someone has painted the intervening six inches of daylight Safety Orange on the official plan.

There is very little to hear. The furnaces draw current without a flame; the carts run on broad rubber wheels. Even in the casting room what you notice is the small dry click of a worker lifting her dark glasses onto her forehead. Finished panes go out upright on a cableway, one rack at a time. From across the meadow you can tell when a load is leaving because a section of distant woods begins to travel slowly through the nearer woods, undiminished in detail but upside down.

Late in the day the glare goes off the roofs. For a little while you can see through the upper windows of both works and out the other side, to the far rim with its thin, crowded trees. Then the first cloud comes level with the lowest branches. The black cuts in the country whiten from the bottom up. By the time the workers walk home, the wide seams look full enough to step on and the narrow ones have vanished altogether. The paths are marked with waist-high posts, close set, each carrying a smear of white enamel on the side toward solid ground. People go home with one hand trailing in the grass and the other brushing those little patches of paint, which keep a surprising amount of the day’s warmth.
