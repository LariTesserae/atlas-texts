At street level there are four low roofs of wired glass behind a brick parapet. You can see the ridge vents, all propped open the same hand’s breadth, and the top of a standpipe with its red paint gone pink. The roofs belong to one yard. To get into it you go down the steps beside the standpipe, past the level of the street drain, and open a swollen green door. The steps continue on the other side. For the last six you have to lift a fern frond out of the way, and its weight, particularly if the sprays have been on, comes as a surprise.

This was the propagation yard for the municipal parks, built down in an old tank to save the cost of heating four exposed walls. It occupies rather less than the space of a small public square. From the landing you can see almost none of it. There is a close view of the underside of a banana leaf, with the daylight coming through it the colour of cooking oil; beyond that, another leaf with its edge in tatters; beyond that, where you might expect at last to see the glass, the black bottoms of pots on an overhead rack. Water gathers on the lip of each pot and falls into the leaves below. Some of the leaves have acquired a permanent notch at the place where the water runs out.

Down on the floor the plan of the yard becomes clearer. There is a main walk of blue engineering brick, wide enough for a loaded barrow. Four narrow walks turn off it at right angles, each with a stopcock at the corner, each ending at a tiled wall. Iron benches run the length of these little streets. The old ceramic numbers are still there, though in places you would have to put your hand deep amongst wet stems to feel one. Above the benches are shelves, above the shelves are the rods that once carried shade cloth, and on the rods another garden has established itself in the felt of old roots and dust. Plants that were originally set on a shelf in six-inch pots now occupy the whole height of the house. Their roots go down through the slats into the beds beneath. The pots are still in there, mostly in two or three pieces, held in their original relation by the pressure of the roots.

The warmth is strongest at ankle height. Two thick heating pipes go out along one edge of the main walk and come back along the other; on a cold morning the brick dries in two straight bands, while the middle stays dark and slick. Against the outer walls there is always water. It comes through the mortar in beads, whatever the weather above, and trickles down a green skin into the perimeter gutter. The wall tiles were white once. Across every row there is now a fine brown writing of roots in the joints. Here and there a tile has come away entire, leaving a neat square bed in which liverwort grows as level as if somebody had gone round with a knife and trimmed it.

It is difficult to talk in the yard. In a mesh enclosure under the landing a pair of circulating pumps run day and night. Their note goes through the metalwork so that if you catch hold of a bench you can feel it in the heel of your hand. Over that is the much higher, rougher sound of water pouring from the return pipe into the open filter tank. There is no single place from which this sound seems to come. The low glass throws it back, the tiled ends of the walks throw it back, and even in the last house, where coarse palm leaves part stiffly in front of you and close almost soundlessly behind, you hear the whole volume of it. Nothing calls or scurries in answer. On the soft silt beside the gutter, the only marks are the little pits made by water falling from a height.

The parks no longer take many plants from here. An attendant comes down to oil the pumps, clear the intakes and cut a passage along the brick. The cuttings go into a slatted bin at the far end, where they settle, heat, and diminish until there is room again. Elsewhere very little is removed. Down under the benches, last year’s leaves lie flat on the leaves of the year before; below those is a black, sweet-smelling mould, deep enough now to have buried the lowest crossbars. In one bay an iron bench has given way altogether. Its top inclines perhaps a foot from end to end. The palms that went down with it have long since turned upright, each trunk preserving near its base the same slight bend.

On a rainy afternoon the glass darkens before anything else changes. For a while the pumps and the falling water are all you hear. Then the street drain fills, and behind the west wall there is a long hollow rush, remarkably close, as though someone had opened a door onto a river. A little later the beads on the wall join up. Water threads across the main walk in the shallow hollows worn by barrow wheels and disappears under the ferns. By evening the drain has quieted. In the last of the light two narrow streams are still crossing the bricks, faithfully following the tracks of wheels that have not passed that way in years.
