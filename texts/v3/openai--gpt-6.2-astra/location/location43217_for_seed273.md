In the east orchard, ladders lie flat between the rows. The pear trees grow in troughs scarcely deeper than a boot is long; their branches are trained out along wires, and a picker standing beside a tree can reach its farthest fruit. The ladders are for getting down. At intervals along each row there is a square lid, warm to the touch even early in the morning. Lift one and the pears rattle a little on their stems. Below is a windy space full of crossed ironwork, pigeons, and the pale blue backs of clouds a long way off.

The orchard occupies the crown of the easternmost of seven viaducts. Seen from the end of any one of them, the others look like cultivated ridges, green at the top, red where the arcades show through, dim at the foot. Their foundations are nearly a mile below the pear wires. Cross streets span the gaps at several levels. By following the topmost street west you can walk for two hours in practically level sunshine, through a belt of market gardens, a town of whitewashed engine houses with dwellings in their upper stories, and acres of trellis under which the light is the yellow-green of the inside of a grape. At the western end the street turns a corner around a ticket office. There the whole extent of your walk is visible, right back to the silver water tank among the pears. Usually there is a hawk sitting on the ticket office, watching the same view for quite different reasons.

It is the heat of the works that makes the growing season so long. Warm return water runs east through pipes bedded in the tiled vaults, and the gardeners plant according to how far it has come. Near the generating halls there are figs, almost black on the side that faces the tiles; farther on, beans, quinces, pears. The air itself is thin and seldom quite warm enough for sitting still. On a bench above a return pipe, however, you can eat your lunch in your shirtsleeves and watch a late snow falling, dry as chaff, off the shady side of the street.

There is surprisingly little water to be seen. A rain shower darkens the paving in long stripes, according to the widths of awnings overhead, and is gone down brass slots before the smell of hot brick has passed. Most of the useful damp comes at night. The roofs of the sheds have deep corrugations; what beads there runs down to the ends, gathers weight on a little tooth of enamel, and falls into a covered channel. There are old men who can tell from the intervals between drops whether to open their allotment valves in the morning. In the daytime the channels are betrayingly green along their seams. Ferns have picked them out, street by street, with the accuracy of a plumber's plan.

Even the earth here has come through someone's hands. Break a clod and there will be some white grit of crockery in it, a curl of old packing straw, a little red dust from a brick saw. A century of leaf fall has made a dark crumbly inch over the original mixture. Beetles work in it. On sunny walls, lizards wait beside the small round holes that solitary bees have bored in the mortar. At the end of summer the young starlings come up out of the lower galleries in such numbers that people put sheets over the laundry for the quarter hour it takes the flock to settle. From inside a shed you hear them arrive on the roof, first two or three distinct sets of feet, then a long coarse brushing sound, as if somebody were pushing all the dust in the world toward the gutter.

Under everything goes the deliberate double knock of the lift engines. The pistons are huge and slow; between one stroke and the next there is time to hear a conversation quite plainly from across the tracks. Anyone who has lived here long remembers a week when one of the engines was out of service. It was then, they say, that you could hear how hard the wind blew. Usually the wind only shows itself in small things: a pear leaf white side up, the permanent eastward lean of a hedge, the deep polish on one lip of each slot through which a hoist cable passes. Most of the wear has this look of having found its proper shape. New stair treads are cut with a shallow hollow already in them so that nobody coming up in the dark will stumble at the repair.

Late in the afternoon the top walks are still dazzling, but a cool violet shadow has filled the streets one level down. Families carry their chairs out there. A few peaches of the heat-loving kind, wrapped separately in scraps of timetable, travel east in the empty picking baskets. Far below, the last goods lift of the day emerges from the cloud, wet on every bar and rail, and rises through storey after storey of dry, sun-warmed masonry. By the time it reaches the orchard the iron has almost dried. The children who have been waiting for it put their fingers beneath the carriage sill, where there are still a few big cold drops to be had.
