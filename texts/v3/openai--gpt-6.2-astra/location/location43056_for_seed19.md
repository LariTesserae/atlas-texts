The first sign of the height is a moon in the wrong place. It stands in a blue notch between two low gray rises, a little below the level of your boots. What looks like a saddle between the rises is a break in the tableland. You can walk up to it over fine, firm gravel. Beyond the last stone there is air clear down to a belt of cloud, and through a rent in that, a country dimmed almost to the color of tin. The moon is setting for somebody in that country. Up here it has stood in the notch a long time.

The top is broad enough to have districts. There is the ocher gravel, the white pavement, the long black beds where a harder stone shows through. The remains of a range of mountains run down the middle. Each mountain amounts now to a mild inconvenience on a walk: a little uphill work on one side, a little downhill on the other. In the pavement are channels broad enough to have taken rivers. Even the little flutes in their floors are preserved. There has been nothing in them for ages but light. A handful of the white stuff feels cool and has practically no weight; rubbed between finger and thumb it breaks into hollow, six-sided husks. Much of what looks like salt from a distance is animal.

Once the eye makes out the first back, it is difficult to find bare ground. There are flat creatures the size of mittens fitted together along the river floors, dun on dun, each with one satin-black eye on a short stem. On their backs stand flocks of small gray birds. The birds stand close enough for the fine hooks of their wing feathers to catch in those of their neighbors. Every so often the joined nap of them changes direction around a much larger animal, a four-footed thing with heavy cheeks and a bare pink muzzle. Its feet are hidden. There are hundreds of the pink muzzles out there in the daylight, each with its neat pair of dark nostrils. Nothing appears to breathe.

It is not quite stillness. At the near end of a black bed a bird has got hold of a red grub. Twenty paces on there is a bird with the grub half out of the rock, and beyond it another with the grub curling round its bill. The nick in the lower bill is the same in all three. Follow the bed far enough and you find the bird with its head tucked down, and farther still the small pointed egg it came out of. Things that happen at this height take up ground. They do not take up time. If you sit beside the egg until your hip goes numb, you will see no change in it. You can get up and walk to the hatching in a minute or two. There is a little grit stuck to the transparent wet skin of the chick, the only damp thing for miles.

This accounts for some of the crowding. A single one of the big cheeked grazers occupies most of an acre in the course of getting down on its knees. Between its forelegs, in a crack that shelters a few russet blades, a colony of pale mites has room for its whole history. The grass itself is scarce. It grows in narrow slots in the rock, puts up a stiff comb, flowers brown, and ends a yard or so along the slot in a pinch of chaff. There are whole reaches of white pavement without so much as that. The grazers cross them with the same bit of grass working slowly out of the corners of their mouths.

There are shelters in the crooks of the old rivers, seldom more than one in sight at a time. They are made of slabs propped on edge, with a roof of overlapping shoulder blades pinned in place with bone. At one of them a low sill has been polished by people sitting on it. Beside the door hang three little stone adzes on rawhide loops: one blunt, one newly chipped, one chipped down almost to the haft. A thumbprint in old resin on the haft is identical on all three. Inside, the floor has been swept. Someone went to the trouble of choosing a place for a house where an afternoon spent quietly indoors would occupy no more space than the house itself.

Sound carries badly, or rather it stays where it was made. There is a patch of air near the sill, no bigger than a cupped hand, in which you can hear the dry draw of a blade being sharpened. A pace away it is gone. Out among the animals you can put an ear close to a great pink nose and, if you have chosen the right one, hear a long soft intake of breath. Most of the plateau is as quiet as the inside of a cupboard. The light is quite ordinary. It brings out ocher in the stone and a little green on the necks of the gray birds. It is cool enough to keep your coat on.

From the sill you can see nearly to the far rim. The paths show clearly from here, narrow strips of darker gravel winding for miles through all that close-set life. They take considerable detours to avoid a nesting or the last few feet of an animal lying down. Only one goes straight. It runs over the flattened spine of the old range to the notch with the moon in it. Along that path are the prints of a child's heels, deep at first and then lighter and farther apart. Where the child began to run there is still, at about the height of your lowered hand, a small place full of laughter.
