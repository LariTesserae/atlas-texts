At the foot of the old fig, the ground is still being made. Two flat roots run out from the trunk and meet again a stride away. Between them is a little court, low enough that a fallen fig rolls into it and stays. Silt from the last rain lies in pale fans on the floor. At the thin end of one fan a scrap of leaf projects, still green; at the thick end there is already a fern coming up. The rest is smooth and almost bare. Nothing has had time to wear a way across it.

From above, the court looks densely planted. This is mostly the work of three ferns. Their fronds reach across from separate footholds on the roots and overlap by just a few leaflets. Underneath there is a surprising stretch of open ground, dim even at noon. The air is warm there. Only close to the bark, where the shade lasts all day, can a snail travel without leaving a bright account of its journey. There is one standing body of water, in the curled lip of a brown leaf. It would hardly wet the fig if the fig rolled into it, but a beetle drinking there has to brace its hind feet well up the bank.

The busiest quarter is built into one side of the court. Termites have plastered the lower slope of the root with earth, roofing over the ways by which they go up to a length of dead wood wedged against the trunk. Some of the roofs are scarcely wider than a grass stem. Others join into broad covered landings, go round the loose edge of a scale of bark, and divide again. A whole population passes within an inch of the open floor without venturing onto it. In one place the plaster ends. The edge is dark with fresh work, and now and then a pale head appears, sets down a grain, and is gone. By evening the gap between two roofs will be a small blind arch. The ants that hunt along the seam will have to hunt elsewhere.

Rain arrives here some time after it has begun outside. First the fern tips sag. Then one tip lets fall a fat drop, always in much the same place, and for a moment there is a round hole in the silt with beaded walls. A woodlouse backs under a twig. Springtails scatter so lightly that the eye takes them for grains thrown out by the drop. There is a soft tap, a long pause, another tap. Even when all the broad fig leaves overhead are draining, very little water reaches the court. Most of it follows the other side of the trunk on down the slope.

Afterward, the damp patch brings out more life than seemed possible in so much empty space. Minute snails nose along the rim. A spider no bigger than a fig seed waits with its first pair of legs resting on the wet earth. The fallen fruit has split at its softest point, and small flies walk in and out through the split, folding their clear wings to pass. By the next warm afternoon the patch has shrunk to the size of a coin and the animals have drawn in with it or gone back under cover. A dusting of fine earth remains around its edge. The next leaf to fall will lie a little higher than the last one. Already the fig is caught fast by its lower half; what was a loose thing in the court is becoming part of the floor.
