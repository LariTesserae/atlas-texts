The first sign of the height is a hole in the reeds. Water goes into it ankle-deep, over a lip of brown stone worn smooth as a heel. If you lie down and put your face close, you can see out under the lip. There is a whole afternoon going on below: the white tops of storm clouds, a gap of blue, and through the gap a red river making a bend around a town. The water from the reeds comes apart in the wind long before it gets to the clouds.

Up here the land is nearly level. Three or four green basins lie end to end along the crown of the mountain, divided by ridges low enough for a child to walk over. Beyond the last is a blunt black summit with a wet shine on its south face. Heat comes up through this rock. A freshly turned stone dries on top in minutes while its underside goes on sweating. Pools in deep shade are as warm as the inside of a cheek. In the shallows the mud is full of little pale snails that come out at night and graze the print of a foot until, by morning, the toes have run together.

There are bells along the ridges. From a distance they look like solitary shade trees, each with a small, exceptionally dark crown. Close up, the crown is a shallow copper-colored dish held on three legs, high enough to stand under. Nothing hangs from it. It is the air that rings. Here on the crown of the mountain there is roughly a half mile of air that belongs, by pressure and weight, down at the red river. What the bells do with the difference is not well understood. On the outer slope you can take six steps out of the ferns and find yourself gasping in cold gravel; come back those six steps and sweat runs into your eyes. Each bell has a little house beside it, generally of split cane, for the person who keeps the legs clear of climbing roots. There are only nine houses. At dusk the keepers can see one another's cook fires all down the chain of basins.

What looks from the ridges like an even pelt of green is quite various underfoot. On the hotter ground grow groves of a plant like celery blown up to the size of a pear tree. Its stalks are translucent at noon, and you can see, high inside them, the dark commas of grubs. There are slick lawns of liverwort between the groves. On firmer islands there is room for broad-leaved trees with heavy yellow fruit that smells faintly of bread. Animals wait under these for days. One is a long, low browser with splayed feet and a narrow white stripe from forehead to tail; in the dappled shade you notice the stripe first, apparently laid across several separate patches of darkness. When a fruit finally falls, the animal gets up, eats it without haste, and lies down in a slightly different place. Small green birds turn over the bruised leaves where it has lain.

All day there is a considerable noise. Water slipping through the beds of hollow stems makes a granular rattling, as though somebody were forever tipping rice back into a bin. The green birds have a shockingly metallic cry. Under these is the note of the nearest bell, too low to hear distinctly until you rest the side of your head against one of its legs. Then there are intervals in it. The other bells are sounding too, some across miles of open marsh, and for a little while their notes coincide. The surface of the pools draws up into fine parallel seams. Water mites crowd onto the seams to mate. A keeper shelling beans on her doorstep can tell without looking up when the next basin has gone into tune: the loose skins shuffle over the bottom of her bowl.

Very little here moves fast. The water seems to have been considering the same routes for an immense time. It has rounded the ridges, scooped seats in the black rock, abandoned some channels so gradually that trees growing in them have roots shaped to a current that is no longer there. Even the edge of the livable air wanders slowly, a few yards in the course of a long life. On one slope are the remains of a grove overtaken by cold. Its bleached stalks still stand above the gravel, full of neat empty cells. On another, warmth has gained ground. Beetles are working their way out among stones that have never had anything living under them before.

Toward evening the black summit loses its shine from the bottom up. The reeds cast long, quite ordinary shadows. Down through the hole at the end of the marsh, lightning has begun in the clouds; from here it looks soft and local, a light carried from room to room. Nothing of its thunder reaches the basins. A keeper comes along the worn lip with a basket to gather snails, testing each handful in her palm and putting the small ones back. Behind her, for as far as the next ridge, the stalks are full of birds settling down. In front of her there is scarcely a yard of wet grass left, then all that sky.
