The shelf is small enough that a bird can call from its upper end and startle a bird at its lower end into flight. This happens every eleven breaths or so. A plump grey bird lets go of a sedge stem, falls a little way over the precipice, then comes up level with the ledge, its feet already open for the same stem. From below, if there were anywhere below to stand, you could recognize this shelf by the one small pair of wings that keeps appearing under it.

It lies high on the shaded side of the mountain. A sheet of rock has come away, leaving a flat floor and a back wall that fit one another in the mind, knob for socket, long shallow groove for rib. The edges are still keen. The wall is charcoal coloured except where it is wet, which is almost everywhere; there it has the dark purplish lustre of the inside of a mussel shell. The light comes up from a bank of cloud a considerable distance down. Consequently the undersides of leaves are wonderfully distinct, each little branching vein lit cool green, while it is hard to see what rests on top of them.

There are five deep pockets in the floor. Each could hold a little pond, but each is so full of growing things that its water is only visible when something swims. Then a line opens in the floating liverwort, showing an inch of black, and closes softly from both sides. Alders grow out sideways from the pockets and bend up at their ends. Their roots wear green sleeves a handspan thick. There are ferns with stalks like red glass and a soft grass whose blades, laid flat in one direction by the passage of mice, are silver, and laid the other way are green. Between one pocket and the next there is a good deal of bare stone. A mouse takes some time to make that crossing. You can hear its nails.

Underfoot there is a much bigger sound. At first it seems to come from elsewhere on the mountain, from some enormous works in full production a mile inside the rock. There is the low steady note of a shaft turning under load; laid over it, a double knock, a pause, a rush as of a thousand small hard objects tipped into a bin. In the pause the ears go on supplying the knock. Against the back wall it is loud enough to feel in the cartilage of the nose.

The works are easiest to see in the body of a midge. There are plenty of midges. One can settle on a light-coloured stone and be, for several seconds, quite a good window. Through the clear, nearly empty abdomen you see a long wet floor, lit at intervals, receding to a distance for which there is no allowance in the thickness of the insect. Along it ranks of dark wheels turn in baths of water. Above each wheel a bead swells at the tip of a root; the wheel lifts it whole and carries it out of sight. No two beads have quite the same shape. The double knock is the sound of their release. When the midge lifts off, the floor goes with it, without so much as a wobble in the lights. There is no entrance in the mountain. A split stone shows only fresh grey grain.

What is being made here appears to be the wetness of the shelf. A bead delivered somewhere in that immense interior arrives on the outside as the cold drop that hangs from the lowest alder leaf. It does not fall. A vole drinks from it, working its narrow tongue so fast that the drop wears a little white dimple; the vole goes away with wet whiskers and the drop is still there, full to the lip. The same abundance obtains in the pockets. Newts hunt under the mats, beetles row belly-up in the clear lanes around the roots, and small dun moths hang by the dozen under every curled frond. There is food down to the last fleck of skin a gnat leaves on the water. Yet away from those five places the eye can travel over yards of clean rock without finding so much as a stain.

Nothing accumulates. That is what takes the longest to notice. There is loose grit in the groove a mouse runs along, and a particular grain, rather too big for its foot, clicks away each time and is there for the next foot. A fern is just unrolling; the tender hooked end is dense with cinnamon hairs; it will always be possible to come here to see a fern at exactly that desirable stage. This is not because the place goes back and begins again. The mouse grows old. The birds that startle from the sedge are not all the same bird. It is the shelf that is held to a measure, with all the power and expense of that ceaseless turning given over to keeping a few yards of mountain precisely as they are.

At the outer lip the wind is bitter. A few inches farther in, under the alder, there is scarcely a stir. Here the great noise resolves into separate sounds again: nail on stone, water taken up by a tongue, the dry tick of a wing case opening. Then the call from the upper end rings out. For a moment the sedge at the lower end stands perfectly straight, relieved of its small accustomed weight.
