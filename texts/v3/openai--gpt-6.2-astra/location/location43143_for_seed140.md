The hollow is high enough that the rain usually passes underneath it. You could cross it in sixty paces if you could see to walk. It lies just under the crown of the mountain, between two beds of stone that were once close together; water took away the softer bed between them and left a room. Even the break through which you enter has no corners now. To get in, you follow a root along a warm stone lip, sit down where the root ends, and feel about with your heels for the floor.

There is no light, including at the entrance. The passage has turned twice by then. For a while the impression is of being in an absolutely enormous place. There is a considerable wind at ankle height. Water can be heard falling a long way off, though if you put out your hand at the right place it falls into your palm. The ceiling is a single shallow dish, and a sound made anywhere along its rim goes around it and comes back remarkably well preserved. One animal cracking a shell can give the impression of a number of people clearing their throats in succession, each about to speak.

Almost everything alive here grows in a band around the room. The middle is bare. The stone there has worn down to the level of a small sill at the outlet, so that once a year, when the clouds come up over the mountain, an inch of water stands on it. The rest of the year it is dry and a little greasy to the touch. There are three rounded humps underfoot, harder mineral inclusions, spared by something like a thumbnail's breadth over all the time it took to carry a mountain out through a hole.

At the edges you could put your arm into the growth up to the shoulder. Roots come through from the little forest overhead and open out into fans of fine drinking hairs. On these hang fleshy cups with thick, cool rims, some no bigger than the end of a finger, others big enough to wash your face in. A blind shrimp lives in the larger cups. It stands upside down against the skin of the water, combing it. There are mats that live on the iron in the seep, and pale jointed runners that live on the mats, and soft animals so like the runners that the distinction, to the hand, is mostly one of patience: keep still, and one will presently draw itself between your fingers. There is plenty to eat. Very little of it ever needs to cross the floor.

Warmth comes up along one seam in the wall. Someone long ago fitted a pair of ceramic staples across it, a pace apart. They are still working. Lay a wet hand on the lower staple and its fellow higher up the wall becomes wet instead; the hand comes away dry, with the little creases in the skin picked out in salt. There is no pipe between them. Whatever greater use they had is hard to guess from what remains. Their present use is to a colony of minute snails, which have gathered in such numbers around the upper staple that the overflow descends by a hundred separate routes over their backs. This is the water you hear falling. Now and then an old snail lets go and the sound changes for months.

The air from outside tastes of crushed leaves in the afternoon and of cold bare rock before dawn. Those changes reach only a little way into the hollow. Farther in there is the one weather: warm above the waist, cooler about the feet, a drop gathering somewhere on the body and staying there a long time before it runs. The great event is the annual flooding of the floor. The cups brim over; shrimp wash out and go skating into open water. For a few nights the hollow sounds like fat spitting in a pan, thousands of tiny tails striking the surface over the drowned stone. Then the water draws down. The last of it lies in thin crescents behind the three humps, full of creatures small enough to be carried off on the belly of a snail. By the time the rain has gone back below the mountain there is nothing left in the middle but room again.
