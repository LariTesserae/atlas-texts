One of the things that lives there can be seen best by covering the slit with your shoulder. While you are in the way the ceiling goes gray. Move a little and let the light back in. Here and there on the ceiling are pairs of fine black horns, each pair waving slowly from side to side. There is nothing on the ceiling to account for them. They are the shadows of feelers. The animal itself is on the wall, just under a ledge, considerably nearer your eye than the place where you have been looking.

It is a thread cricket. Six of them could shelter under the wing of a moth. Its body is narrow where a cricket's is usually thick, and the great folded thighs of its hind legs lie in grooves along its sides. Looked at end on it has very nearly the section of a grass seed. Its feet, though, are absurdly wide. When it comes to the shallowest lip of stone it stops and feels over the edge with one of these feet, as carefully as if there were a drop of fifty feet beyond. A lizard can send it off in a jump, but left to itself it will take the long way down.

The feelers are longer than the cricket, longer even than a cricket and the space it leaves between itself and the next. It is possible to watch three of the animals standing quite still in a notch and to see on the ceiling above them what looks like a small naked tree in considerable wind. In a little while one of the moving lines is laid across another. Two of the crickets change places. That is about the extent of their traffic at midday.

They are waiting for the beetles. There is very little in a seed for a thread cricket as long as the husk is whole. It cannot work the notch, nor would its mouth take hold of the smooth shell. But when a beetle gets through, a little meal comes out, not enough to be worth the beetle's while to gather from the dust. Even before the two halves of the husk fall apart there are crickets behind it, brushing the stone with their mouths. They get meal on their feelers and draw them through their forefeet, hand over hand, until they are clean again. A beetle will turn and butt one that gets too close. The cricket backs up perhaps the width of a letter on a page and begins again.

Under the lip by a good notch there are crickets so small that what you see at first is the meal going away. A white grain rises, travels a little distance on a wavering course, and goes in under the stone. These are the young ones, still soft across the back, with feelers that curl at the tips when the air gets dry. They spend much of the day in the old shells the beetles have emptied. Pick one up and the weight of the little passenger is beyond discerning. Yet set it down a little out of the perpendicular and in a moment a foot comes out and rights it.

If a seed escapes into the middle, the crickets do not go after it. They come as far as the polished bottom of the notch, which has kept the warmth of the beetle working there. They stand in the parting dust. One reaches a feeler a good way out over the edge as if there might be another inch or two of wall it has somehow overlooked. Nothing touches the feeler. Presently it is drawn in again.

At the wet end of the room they come out farther. There is a hollow there that rarely fills, though its bottom is dark with damp. Here, after the noon light has left the bank, the males let down their wings. These lie so snug on the back that until they move it is difficult to believe the cricket has any. There is a ridge on one and on the other a short file, the two together scarcely big enough to make a roughness your fingertip could feel. The sound they make would not carry across the hollow in open air. The male stands with his feet well apart, his flat belly just clear of the floor, and works his wings. Nothing audible comes of this where you are standing. Put the side of your finger on the rim of the hollow and there is a little burr in the stone.

It goes a long way in the limestone. A female near the drinking flies will stop with a bead of water in her mouth. She sets down the front foot that she has been using to wipe her face. For a while she stands on all six feet. Then she goes along the rim, turning each time the burr grows faint, until she is standing on the other side of the little rise of rock from the male. Their feelers come over the rise well before either has a view of the other. By then it is nearly dark. All that remains of the slit is a thread of grass-colored light, and you have to be very near the stone to see the two pale tips touch, part, and touch a little farther down.
