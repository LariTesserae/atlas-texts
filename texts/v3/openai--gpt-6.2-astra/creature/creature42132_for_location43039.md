A lost bearer is not always dead. At the bends of the old channel there are strips of gravel which no stone has covered for years, yet the feet come down on them, a few hundred at a time, leaving neat pinkish pits in the pale mat. Sometimes a whole passage accumulates there before the weather is right to carry it on. In a thaw you can see the track of a single animal going steadily for the rim, across the traffic of the living ones. The steps are shorter on one side. Far out on the plain there will be an old bearer going about its business with a corresponding limp.

There is a creature at the bends that follows these tracks. It looks at first like a little heap of gray laundry that the water cannot quite get under. Turn it over and there is a mouth the length of a bootlace, laid in pleats, and on either side of it a pad of exceedingly fine hairs. A touch anywhere on the gravel draws the two pads apart. If the touch is repeated at the proper distance, the creature unfolds, first to the length of a foot, then of a whole row of feet. It lays the crease of its mouth along the advancing pits. Each footfall squeezes a little white milk out of the mat. That is what it takes. In a good track it travels for hours without having to work a muscle except to swallow.

The birds know how to make the pads open. One stands at either end of the heap and they tread in turn, very delicately for birds with such big, serviceable feet. Presently a wet frill comes out between them. They take an end apiece and walk off backward. There is none of the usual uproar. Even birds passing overhead keep quiet at the sight of those two intent orange faces drawing apart across the stones. The skin of the frill stretches astonishingly far before it tears. The birds eat the ends. What is left draws together again by evening, though for several days it will open at the fall of a single sleet grain and lie there trembling, with nothing to drink.

In winter the gray creatures go into the holes. If you put your cheek flat to the ice over a deep one you can see them hanging in dozens from the ivory hooks. They are in no danger there. The hook is the only hard part of a long animal which fills the hole rather as a tongue fills a mouth. Along its inner curve there is a groove, and the groove is full of very small copies of the animal, each already possessed of one splendid, useless tooth. At the blow the parent brings up what it has caught. Very little of the catch arrives whole. The gray mouths strain the rags against the groove; the young feed on what seeps through. By spring the inside of the great hook is as clean and lustrous as the handle of a cup.

For a few nights after the ice breaks the young hooks are everywhere. They swim on their backs with their teeth out of the water. A bird coming down among them takes off again at once and sits on a stone lifting first one foot and then the other. Nearly all the young go down the sill. A few get their teeth into a crack and stay, dwindling through the long summer until there seems nothing left of one but a white staple in the rock. In late autumn a foot put down beside the staple comes up smartly. Something has grown below.

Much the shallowest holes are occupied by the oldest of these animals. It takes a long time to understand this, since a shallow hole can have a hook in it no bigger than a pin. The rock has been wearing away from above while the animal has been wearing away from below. In the end there is just room for the two to fit. On a still day you can watch such a hook fish in a spoonful of brine. It rises at the blow and sinks at the knock. Nothing large enough to be seen ever hangs from it.

One of the stone bearers, coming down on such a place, will often stay for the better part of a week. Its neighbors divide around it. Its feet keep up their testing and sorting, but the two immediately over the hollow make a little arch and hold it. Through the clear skin of a foot you can see the white point rising and falling between them. Perhaps the animal feels some minute relief each time the point lifts the weight. Perhaps a hook is simply something one does not step on. When at last the bearer moves, there is a dry spot under its middle where the water has been pressed out. The first trickle in brings a silver fry no longer than an eyelash, lying on its side from the force of the last blow. For once there is something on the hook.
