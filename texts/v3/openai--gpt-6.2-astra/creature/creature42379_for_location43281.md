Most households in the high works own a pot of earth. The pots come up from below in the laps of passengers, since the luggage vans get too hot. A woman may be seen carrying one wrapped in a damp shirt, holding it a little way out from her dress as though the earth itself were hot. What survives best in it is a small onion. You cut the greens with scissors. A child sent to borrow some goes with the scissors and comes back with the blades closed on three or four lengths, the cut ends still wet. There are children of twelve who have done this every week of their lives and are quite surprised, on their first visit below, to see onions growing out of the world.

A great many of the inhabitants came up for a term of work when they were young and can still name the particular thing that kept them a second term. A man in the bearing hall stayed to have a set of teeth finished. They wanted him back twice for the fit; by the second visit he had got to know the woman who ground the little hollows in them and had begun going with her to the late pictures. Their grandchildren live in the eastern wards. Asked why he stayed, he says, quite truthfully, that there was a rough place on the inside of the left canine.

The people born up here have the habit, on entering a strange room, of putting out one foot and letting a little of their weight onto it before they bring the other foot in. It is a movement learned at the joins between buildings. Two rooms may make common use of a door for years, and then one morning there will be half an inch of daylight under it and the floor beyond will take your heel sooner than you expect. The habit is especially marked in waiters. In the older eating houses a waiter can cross three properties with a brimful plate of soup. He looks all the while at the person who ordered it, so that a stranger has the pleasant impression of being singled out in a crowd and brought something which nobody else in the room could possibly have wanted.

There is some difference of feeling about the little animals in the inspection chambers. The pipe people like them. A good wide sweep in the peach film means that at least that much of the pipe has stayed cool and wet, even over a shut period. The tank people would rather they were somewhere else. Once, when a child asked at school to be shown one, it took nearly a week to find a keeper prepared to admit in writing that he had any. The class went down by fours. At first all four children saw nothing but a patch of wet with a hair laid across it. Then the hair bent, and the patch of wet went with it, and there on the tile behind was a little fan of clean white strokes. The last four, who had waited longest and had time to hear a number of accounts, were the only ones who noticed the feet.

It is often supposed below that the people on the rim must be good judges of weather. In fact a curtain tender asked what the day will be like is apt to give the depth of the cloud, which is of very little use to a person deciding whether to take a coat. The tenders know a great deal about what goes on below the white surface. They can feel hail coming up a wire while the sun is shining on the backs of their hands. One of their lesser pleasures is to stand a visitor against the rail on a fine day, put a length of loose wire between his fingers, and let him feel, far down in the brilliant still afternoon, the hard little knocks of quite another afternoon going on underneath.

On the evening when the cup is tried, it is usually a curtain tender who brings the water to wash out the seam. The job could as easily be done with tap water, but a bottle drawn straight from a gutter is cold. People pass it round first and set it briefly against their eyelids. There is no appointed person to try the cup. Whoever has got down on hands and knees to pick the grit out generally does it, while the rest lean over and give contradictory advice about the handle. For several years now the lip has gone in as far as the blue maker’s mark. Each time, when it sticks, there is a small sound of satisfaction from those old enough to remember when it fell well short of the blue.

There is already a difference of opinion among the younger ones as to what should be done when it goes all the way in. Some think it ought to be left there. Others point out that there is a hollow under that part of the square and a perfectly good cup might drop a considerable distance and break. A girl who lives above the former pump yard has made a net to catch it. She has allowed enough cord to lower it nearly to the bottom of the hollow, whose depth she obtained by writing to the survey office. In the meantime the net hangs on the cool side of the kitchen door. It holds the onion scissors, the tram money, two spare shutter pins, and all the other things her father used to spend the first ten minutes of his morning looking for.
