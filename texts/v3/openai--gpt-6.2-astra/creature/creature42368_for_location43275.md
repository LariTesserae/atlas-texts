On the morning after a line of purse flowers has been left out, there are little blue tracks on the frost beside it. They come in sets of four, run for a yard or two, and end at a flower. Sometimes one can find the maker still there. It has gone in headfirst. The two hind feet sticking out of the purse look very much like the green forks by which a flower ordinarily holds its thread.

It is a moth, though for most of its life it has little occasion to fly. Draw one backwards out of a flower and it will open on the palm into four wings the colour of wet slate. Across each, near the body, there is a crease. The wing folds there first and then lengthwise, a considerable business which the moth carries out with two hind feet and a shiver. Folded up, it can get through a slit scarcely wide enough to take the edge of a coin. The blue of its tracks comes from the dust on its belly, which drags when it walks on a level surface. It does better on a ceiling.

There are great numbers of them under the terraces. In the dark they walk from purse to purse along the underside of a root lip, feeling ahead with the two forefeet that made no mark on the frost. What they take is a clear gum from the bottom of the purse. Coming out, they have to shoulder past a yellow pad. One that has fed well has two little epaulettes of pollen; one that has been caught in the open and searched over by the grey bird leaves nothing much but the epaulettes and the wings.

Their young live elsewhere. Peel back a knob of the apricot moss and the inside is full of short passages lined with silk, each with a brown head stopped in it like a cork. A grub eats the old moss from beneath, drawing its meals off the roof in single strands, so that the green tips, or what would be green in any other moss, go on growing overhead. A great old knob, warm enough to take the ache out of a cold knuckle, may have no more substance to it than a worsted cap. The silk holds it up. On the underside of nearly every knob is a tuft of loose ends, all combed in the same direction by the little draught that precedes a charge of water.

The moth comes out in spring with its wings quite straight. It has one flight to make then, from the hollow behind a root to the flowers beneath the next one. How far this is depends on the water. There are mornings when the two places touch. One sees the new moth standing at the end of a silk thread, working its splendid wings for the first time, while the roof it is to spend its life on comes slowly up against its back. On a dry morning the same journey will take it out over a bright chasm of leaves, in full view of the bird.

What the unopened moth feels in its moss is the draught. When that stops it wriggles out. In a usual spring there is always another root taking water somewhere, and a few moths are coming out all down the slope through the morning. After a long freeze the collars let go almost together. Then, just behind the dark bands on the pass, there is a band of quite another sort, low and blue and ragged at its upper edge. From the notch it looks like a little smoke rubbed up by the friction of the leaves. Down in it, kneeling in the hollow with one's head just clear of the moss, one feels the moths go by on either cheek, each no more than a cool tap, and can hear for once what an immense number of soft things it takes to make a sound in that place.
