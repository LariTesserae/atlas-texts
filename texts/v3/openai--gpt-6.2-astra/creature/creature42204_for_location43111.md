The woman who drives the morning cart has a son who can hear the wagtail. So he says. He will put down a spoon, or get out of bed and go barefoot to the back door, and say that the bird is in. His mother has come to think he hears the little tin knock of the depth gauge. It hangs on two screws, one of which has worked loose. She could set it right in the time it takes to turn the cart. She rather likes to see the child go to the door.

He is the youngest person in the hollow. The oldest is the man who oils the comb at the works. There is no great stretch of years between these two in which the hollow has grown old-fashioned. The oiler wears a digital watch with four alarms on it. He buys the straps in threes because the pale dust gets under them and the ends split at the buckle holes. At the second alarm he walks out with his mug and sits on the knot opposite the loading doors. On a raw day he sets his mug in the little cleft there and cups his hands over it. In his father's time the cleft would hold a pipe stem and no more.

Of the twenty people who sleep here, eleven work at least part of the day with felt. At table, without thinking, they will pick a crumb up between finger and thumb and roll it to see whether it binds. One woman does this even with salt. It drives her wife to distraction: come to the end of supper and there are all those hard little white peas on the cloth. They have the house with the three-sided bay. When the two of them began moving it out, they meant to make a place to stand a sewing machine in good light. Long before there was room for a machine, the wife who sewed had gone off sewing. She draws there now, chiefly things she would like to have made in metal. There is a drawing of a hinge that will open flat both ways, and another of a handsome pair of tongs for lifting a hot pudding basin out of a pot. The tongs exist. On Sundays you can eat the pudding.

There are creatures in the houses which belong to none of the households. A little dun moth stands on a wall with its wings fitted so neatly to the grain that the surest way to find one is to look for the break in the pattern made by its feet. The boy has counted thirty on the wall behind his bed. When one of them flies he can seldom find it again, although he has seen it settle. There are flat grey lice under the enamel signs. There is a fat glossy thing, seldom seen entire, which eats the paste off the carter's labels. It leaves the paper loose and wonderfully clean. Her brother thinks it would be worth money to the people who take old books apart. He has been going to catch one for six years.

The people are not particularly light on their feet, though from the rim one might imagine a race of soft walkers. In the first house on the cross-grain street lives a man whose tread is so heavy that the milk jug shifts along its ledge. He is the one they send for when a floor has begun to sound hollow. He goes over it in his socks, slowly, with his head to one side. Once he found a pocket under the public scale big enough to take a winter's worth of coal. It had a dry lining of its own and a single white root laid along the bottom. They put the pan back and shifted the weights a little to the left. For years after that the children would weigh one foot and then the other, hoping for a difference.

Ask any two of them where the wood is alive and they will give you different answers. The oiler says only the thickness of a sixpence at any surface exposed to air. His sister, who has had to ease a hoop out of a bedroom wall from the inside, says he is welcome to try that theory on her bedroom. No one supposes the hollow takes much interest in them. It will close up the hole for a stove pipe as readily as it will close a split made by frost, and choke a family in bed for want of a half hour's work in September. They keep a red-handled scraper on a hook by every stove.

Still, there are small accommodations for which it is hard to assign the credit. The carter's left hand lacks its last two fingers. In climbing to her seat she takes the rail well down from the place where her father took it, turning her wrist so that the heel of the hand gets a purchase underneath. There is a shelf coming there now, hardly more than the edge on a well-used cake of soap. Her son puts his whole small hand on it when he rides beside her. Up at the bend, until the cart comes round, he is tall enough to see into the cistern. Some mornings the gauge is perfectly still and there is the bird anyway, up to its ankles in water, giving nothing away.
