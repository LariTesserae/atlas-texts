If there is already a lodger in the house, you generally find out by taking off a glove. What felt like a little ridge of displaced grit at the head of the sleeping hollow rolls up against the warmth of your wrist. From the middle of the roll comes a foot, which feels about with considerable care and takes hold of a hair on your arm. Then a second foot takes hold of the same hair. You can draw the animal out to nearly the length of your forearm before the far end of it consents to let go of the floor.

The survey people called these animals hems. Laid out flat, one looks very much like the worn binding off the edge of a blanket. The upper surface has the color of whatever stone the hem has been living on; indeed most of the color comes off on a damp finger, leaving a smooth skin of a rather lovely plum blue. Underneath there are two rows of feet and, between the rows, a white seam. There is no particular head end. A hem making good time to the west can begin making equally good time to the east without any of the intermediate business that occupies a centipede. It does, however, have a persistent preference for going one way rather than the other. Put a spot of chalk on the leading end and come back a year later; the odds are quite good that this is still the end that leads.

For a long time the sleeping hollows were thought to be their nests. Nearly every occupied house has a hem in the hollow by morning, and a good many have two or three, pressed edge to edge with the seams down. But lay a bedroll at the other side of the room and sleep there for several nights and the hems will come over. What they want is the moisture you leave behind. In the early light you can see the white seam parting, a little at a time, like a long mouth trying not to spill a drink. By the time the sun reaches the floor the stone under them is quite dry. In an empty house they do the same thing in a hairline crack near one of the doors. A house can look the same at evening and morning for a hundred years and still, somewhere in the course of each night, squeeze out a drop of water.

A hem has no dealings with the snail. It does have dealings with the fan. If you follow one out of a door in daylight it is likely to disappoint you: it goes perhaps two yards, slips under the lip of a stone you could scarcely get a knife under, and stays there. Lift the stone and there will be the hem with one tender blue edge tucked among the ribs of a fan. The few that have been kept under glass will go without food for years, but take away the fan stones and their blue fades in a matter of days. Nor does a fan cut off its growth where the animal lies. It puts out, along that edge only, a row of short, unusually stout ribs, with just enough space between one rib and the next to admit a foot. Nobody has seen what happens when the fit is a good one. You have to lift the stone to look, and that puts an end to it.

It is the young hems you occasionally meet a long way from any house. They are no longer than an eyelash. Each has a single polished scale on its back, quite out of proportion to the rest of it, and in still weather it lies under that scale with its feet drawn up. Walk out after a silver front with the sun low behind you and you may see one of the scales still going. It gets up, falls over, lies still a moment and gets up again. The little animal is jumping, taking from each stroke of the ground enough to be going on with after the stroke has passed. A run of twenty or thirty jumps may carry it the length of your shadow. Then it has to wait for weather again. Some of those journeys must be very long. Near the western lip, where there is nothing whatever ahead, you find the little scales collected in the last shallow groove just as you find husks along the high-water mark of a pond.

The old ones seldom leave their houses for more than a day. There was one for many seasons in the house on the garnet belt, recognizable by a division near the middle where the two halves had grown together a little out of true. It used to come up onto the neck rest before a slip, while the floor still felt quite steady. A woman wintering there got into the habit of putting one finger down for it. The hem would set its first few feet on her finger and wait, sometimes for minutes. When the lift came, the rest of it would let go. For that brief interval when the shelf had no use for the weight of either of them she could feel the animal hanging from her finger by its own choice, without the least pull. Then the weight came back and it poured down over her knuckle into the hollow again.

Late that winter the house failed. Both walls were still standing in the spring, but there was a step across the floor where there had never been a step before, and one door would no longer open. The woman looked for the hem on a number of visits and did not find it. What she did find, in the angle of the new step, was a line of minute polished scales. They were there in the morning, and there again when she looked at noon. In the afternoon a little weather came through from the green-black country. One after another the scales went up onto the higher piece of floor. It took most of the afternoon for them all to go the height of a thumb.
