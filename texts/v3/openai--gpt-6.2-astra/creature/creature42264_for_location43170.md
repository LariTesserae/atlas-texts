There is a small animal that comes out from under the white tiles when the washer lies down. First there are two threads, one of which feels round the rim while the other goes through the hole. The rest of the animal comes out rather suddenly. It has a flat grey back and six pairs of feet, the last pair long enough to keep hold of the edge of the tile while the first pair lift the washer. It eats what the washer has come down on. A little of almost everything in the street comes to the square sooner or later, and though the sweep carries it all away again, there is generally something pressed underneath: a spore, the egg of a ribbon worm, a few grains of the meal that comes out of the old woodwork. When the animal has eaten it lets the washer down quite gently. There is time to do this sort of thing properly between strokes of the mill.

There are a great many of these animals. The one on the square is unusually easy to see. Elsewhere their backs take the colour of the fine deposit in which they have lately lain, and in any case they spend very little of their lives with the whole of themselves outside something. The thumb-deep hollows under the ledges are full of them. If one were to lift a pinch of the grey sand with a spoon, the sand would run off the spoon in several directions, leaving perhaps three grains in the bowl.

The young have no back plate to speak of. They are pink, and live at first in the reddish felt. A female parts it with the two long feet and backs in until only her threads are left outside. For a day or so the threads go on examining things, tying themselves round a stem and untying themselves again; then she pulls them in too. Some weeks later there is a patch of felt from which a quantity of pink feet protrude whenever the plate begins to rise. The feet set up a little favourable current through the nursery. At this age the animals are interested chiefly in whatever the mill is doing. A spoon held in front of them will get no attention at all, but put the handle against the iron and every foot in the patch will turn toward it.

They keep something of this preference as adults. There are parts of Mill Street with good feeding and admirable ledges which none of them will occupy, because the pipes there have been cut off. At Number 4, on the other hand, they will put up with an indifferent diet and a considerable draught for the sake of the two brass clips on the mat. The clips go through the landing into a girder which rests at its far end on the casing of the driving chamber. A dozen animals at a time hang underneath the mat with a foot on the brass. They change places occasionally. There is no special advantage, so far as anyone can tell, to be had by feeling a pump stroke at such close quarters.

It was the people on the tender who found out that the animals travelled. For years they had supposed that the grey ones lived low down, that the honey-coloured ones lived on the brick, and that the nearly transparent ones were a kind particular to the green glass collars. Then for a season there was a bad bearing on the delivery line. The man who went down to mark the place with yellow chalk came up with a little grey creature on the chalk, and put it back on the nearest chimney. Three days later, through the inspection glass at the tank, he saw a line of animals coming up the delivery pipe on the outside, every one with a yellow mark across its back. He went down again. His chalk mark was across a narrow place, and as long as the bearing knocked the animals kept coming to examine it, squeezing under the sill there and picking up the yellow. They were of all three colours and several shades between. Some from the honey brick had been down in the felt so recently that when they turned sideways they showed a quite lovely red nap along the edges.

The bearing was replaced. For a while the animals continued to go there, then fewer went, then the window over the pipe filled up with weed again. The tender people still call them the chalks.

One thing the chalks do which has nothing to do with the mill is turn over for the eel. It happens a little in advance of its arrival. From the gap opposite Number 4 one can see a pale wave travel along the underside of the seam, each animal letting go with one side of its body and hanging by the other. Their bellies are white. The eel puts its narrow head into the weed and goes along wiping first one cheek and then the other against the combs on their hind feet. It comes out with the little leeches gone from the folds of its gills. The chalks stay where they are to eat, holding on with whichever feet are free, and this is why the round windows persist for a time in the seam. The weed has been gathered up in quite a number of small fists.

There is one very old chalk behind the ear of the painted woman. The lamp was taken out for cleaning eleven years ago and it was there then, recognisable by a back plate which has healed crooked and carries a single waxy disk like a green button. It lives between the enamel shade and the wall. At low water its shadow can be seen from a boat, enlarged to about the size of a hand, slowly going round the curve of the shade. It has gone round so often that the brick there is clean. When the tender takes water and the level in the pale tank falls, the load on the last stage alters; the lamp flares just perceptibly. The old chalk stops. For the length of time it takes a person on deck to undo a coupling and put the cap back on, it sits in the light with both threads laid along the wire. Then the tender moves off, the shadow resumes its passage behind the woman's ear, and down on the white tiles the small grey one, which has been holding quite still with a foot through the washer, takes it out.
