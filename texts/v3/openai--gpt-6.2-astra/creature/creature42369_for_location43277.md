The mechanic calls them the tenants. He includes the beads in this. He will say there are three tenants on the bottom step when what is there is one animal going about its business and two bright points with nothing under them. If he must put a foot on a step he puts it down heel first and rolls the sole forward, which gives anything underneath time to go. He does this on dry stairs too, on Thursdays, until about dinner.

A tenant seen through a watchmaker’s glass has a more finished look than one would expect. There is a little binding of yellow all round its edge. The joints of the walking legs have this binding too, so that when a leg is bent double the two edges fit and there seems to be a single piece. The forelegs are quite different. Open, they are rather like the hands of a person feeling for the loose end on a roll of tape. It is with these that the animal takes hold of the skin of the water. First one hand goes up and makes a dimple; then the other. The hind end floats free and comes round very slowly until the creature is on its back. Its bead is now underneath it. One would think this a considerable inconvenience. In fact it rests its hind feet on the bead from time to time to give them a rub.

The very small ones have no beads. For the first few days they go about on the backs of their elders, sometimes two or three on the same back, all with their feet in the narrow margin between the bead and the yellow binding. When the old one turns over they turn over too. It is possible to watch this several times and miss the moment at which a young one lets go. There is just a little passenger the less, and down on the fan, a hand’s breadth from the place where one would look for it, a new tenant standing with all six legs wide apart. It stands like that most of a morning. A clear point gathers between its shoulders. By afternoon it can pass for a grown one seen at a distance.

An old, unoccupied bead is a good place to watch a young tenant make a mistake. Coming up to one for the first time it will often go to the wrong side and find itself facing the animals coming the other way. There is no great disturbance. The first animal it meets folds its forelegs. So does the next. Presently there is a line of them six or seven inches long, all with their forelegs put away, waiting. The young one feels along the line until it gets to the bead again. Then everybody goes on. This can take as long as the interval between two cars overhead. No tenant has yet been seen to go round the obstruction, though there is room on either side for a boot.

The pink on their feet wears off unevenly. On the flat paving it lasts quite well, the feet being set down squarely and lifted straight up. It is at the places where the animals have to turn that most of the color comes away. Consequently the path out to the pump, looked at closely, is a succession of little pink commas with long gray intervals. The water is too deep there for the noon light to show the animals themselves. For a few minutes one can see where each of them has had to put a foot out to steady itself.

In summer the mechanic eats his bread on the disconnected pump at the far end. A crumb fallen in the water is of no interest to the tenants. They go on scraping stone until the shadow of his hand falls among them; then a whole patch lifts, hangs a moment on edge, and settles again a little farther off. Lately he has taken to holding his hand still. After a while they come back into its shadow. On a fine Thursday in June he sat there until the sun had gone past the boss and there was no longer a hand to be seen on the floor. The tenants were arranged by then in five short files and one longer one along the edge of his cuff. They kept that order for several minutes in the ordinary brown light, each attending to its inch of stone.
