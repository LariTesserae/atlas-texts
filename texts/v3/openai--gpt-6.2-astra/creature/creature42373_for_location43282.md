What lives there is usually taken for a length of old packing. There is packing enough in the room to make the mistake a reasonable one. The difference is easiest to see at a loose end. A strand of hemp spends all day brushing the same bit of brick and is the worse for it. This thing puts its end down, tries the brick a little farther on, and brings the rest of itself up to the place it has chosen.

It is seldom more than two fingers wide. Underneath, where nothing scours it, it has the fine nap of a peach. The nap holds to the minute pits in the iron, a very little hold in each pit. A foot of the stuff could be peeled off with a fingernail if one began at the edge; pulled lengthways it would strip the rust off a pipe. There are places where it has done so. The three bright lines just below the east return are not drill marks.

For most of a shift it lies between the two lowest pipes, with a narrow lip turned out into the faster water. The lip is divided into little aprons. On the face of each is a track, hardly deep enough to cast a shadow, down which the fine matter in the water travels against the wash. A tuft of wool too big for the track goes over. The smaller things close up in single file as they go down it. At the inner end there is a brownish swelling which becomes rather plainer towards the close of the morning, especially on a day when the blanket works are rinsing. The aprons nearest the stone throat are sometimes a handsome blue. The colour does not go far in. A torn place shows milk-white, as it does on the aprons that have never had any colour at all.

It is possible to follow one edge of the creature a considerable way round the room without finding the other. It divides at a hanger, runs up the shank on either side and joins again behind the nut. On the oldest oak plug it spreads into a patch the size of a watch glass. The shrimp can walk clean across this without disturbing it. Once, in casting its shell, a shrimp hooked three of its old feet into the nap. For the rest of that shift the empty shrimp rode upright along the lower pipe, travelling so slowly that the only way to be sure it had moved was to count the threads still showing on a brass union ahead of it.

At ten past two the aprons shut. They do this while the loose hemp beside them is still blowing the old way. A tightening passes along the fold from the north end, where a piece of it lies flat to the bricks immediately over the main. By the time the first of the ceiling water comes down, there are only smooth narrow edges offered to it. Then the edge on the oak plug lets go.

For a moment there is a fair view of the underside. It comes off in a widening loop, white at first, then almost clear as the loop takes the strain. A second length pays out from behind the hanger. Between them they make a loose bag in the middle of the room, large enough to put a man's coat in. Nothing about its appearance on the pipes would lead one to expect so much of it. The hot water fills it on one side and turns it through itself; the watch-glass patch, folded now to the breadth of a bootlace, goes skimming across the basalt. When it comes to the thumb hollow it catches at the bottom. The bag empties. Length after length comes down to that one patch and feeds away from it along the floor, laying its nap to the stone. By the time the thread on the bolt has straightened out again there is practically nothing in the middle.

It does not always get the whole of itself across. Short pieces are left in the lee of the caps, each with an apron or two, each laying down its little bright track in the rust. Some are taken up again at the next change. Others stay where they are and grow. There is a piece on the blind side of the oldest plug which the wash cannot reach in either direction. It has grown thick there, in close grey pleats, with a clean white growing edge facing the wood. A fitter drawing that plug would bring up something very like a damp carnation on the end of it. Left alone, it opens only far enough to admit the forelegs of the shrimp.
