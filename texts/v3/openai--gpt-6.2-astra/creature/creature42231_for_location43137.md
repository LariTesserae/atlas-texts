The first one you find is likely to be a young one. It has gone up the last standing wheel tooth in the dark, on the side with the number, and got as far as the overhang. All its feet are on the wall. It can go on up a vertical face so long as it can see a little more face above it, but it has never made the turn underneath an overhang and it does not know how to come down backward. There it stays until the number grows warm in the morning. Then it lets go, all at once, and comes down flat with a sound like a hand dropped palm first on a table. It is not much bigger than the hand.

The grown ones have a way of getting down. They loosen the back half of the body and let it swing, keeping the forefeet where they are; for a moment the whole animal hangs like a badly pasted notice. Then the hind feet take hold below the head and the forefeet come away. A succession of these undignified folds will get one down the highest piece of old iron in the basin.

On the level floor they are beautiful walkers. They have eight feet, not in four pairs but in two groups of four, one group well forward and one well aft of the soft, rather long middle. What travels between the two sets of feet is a minute difference of height. The front ones stand on a shutter and the back ones on the last shutter but one, and the belly hangs just clear of the one between. When a turn begins under either set of feet the animal feels it in the stretch of its belly before the shutter has sunk the thickness of a hair. It steps over. Several will walk in close order along the very edge of the white country, stepping now to the left and now to the right; the brilliance goes on passing through their ranks and none of them go down with it.

They go there to eat. On a face newly brought up from below there is sometimes a skin so fine that the only sign of it is a small imperfection in the reflection of the sky. It grows on the bottom face, in those long intervals of blue light underground. In the open air it dries, draws together into a brown comma, and blows away. The walkers take it wet. A feeding walker puts down a lip as broad as the front of its body and walks forward a little way inside its own skin, so to speak, the lip remaining stuck where it was and the rest of the head advancing over it. Then the lip comes free with the film rolled neatly up inside. On a good morning an individual will leave a succession of clear oblongs behind it for half a mile. Dust settles differently on the licked places. Long after the animal has gone, a faint ladder can be seen going out over the white.

A walker that has fed well is the color of weak tea, except for two black lines down its sides. Through the middle you can see the brown rolls going slowly round. Through an unfed one you can read the arrow on the tooth. The black lines are its young. What looks at first like a continuous ruling breaks up, toward the end of the hot season, into a row of little dashes, each with a wider space round it day by day. They take their shape from the way the parent walks. The front of a dash clings tight when the forebody goes forward; the back clings when the hindbody comes up. Before they have feet you can see them letting go at alternate ends.

An animal in this condition quits the moving boundary, though there is still good eating there, and makes for one of the crescents of forked growth. Sometimes it has to go a long way. The soft middle gets thinner on the journey and the dashes more distinct. It finds a place among the stalks already visited by the grazer, where there are standing forks with one cup gone, and goes slowly in and out among them. A dash catches on the broken lip of a cup. The parent does not stop or jerk away. It goes on at just such a pace that the little one peels off in one piece, back feet, belly, forefeet, and hangs doubled over the lip. In a well-grazed stand a walker can come out the other side quite clear.

For a few days the young eat the dark bead left in each broken fork. It keeps trying to climb the missing branch and coming back, a very small meal served repeatedly to a very small mouth. By the time it is gone the feet have hardened enough for the floor. This is when you find them on the wheel tooth, or at the tops of spent stalks, or ranged along a shaft with their clear noses over the edge. They go up anything. In the evening the thread of air over a buried axle will take a young one off the top of a stalk and sail it, legs spread, an astonishing distance. Most come down on the slope. A few go clear over the lip.

There are walkers in the cool country on the other side of the world. They are dark there, and rather short between the feet. For a long time it was thought that the warm air must carry the basin young all that way. But if you lie by a shaft when the slant sun is coming up in it, and let your eyes grow used to that other morning, you can see small oblong clearings in the film on the upright floors. Far down, beyond the hair rods, a little procession is making its way across a wall. One has reached an edge. The long middle loosens; the hind feet swing out into the blue.
