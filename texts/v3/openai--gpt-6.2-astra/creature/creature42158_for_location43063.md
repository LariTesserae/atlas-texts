The plovers were entered in the station book as wagtails for nearly a month. There was a space on the form for birds and somebody had already written WAGTAIL in it, so that a person seeing two small birds on the flats had only to go indoors and alter the figure. It was the woman who watered the geraniums who first remarked that the wagtail had got a short tail and a wife. She brought her field glasses on the next shift. There were two little ringed plovers, a cock and a hen, and also, when she came to look along the warm seam, the wagtail.

The plovers do not go much on the grass. They like the place a sheet of water has just left. There are minute brown creatures there which lie perfectly still until the foot of a plover goes down beside them and begins to tremble. The bird holds its head steady throughout, looking rather as if there were something wrong with the foot which it did not want to acknowledge. Then it picks something up and runs eighteen inches and does it again. Seen end-on through the glasses it has a white waistcoat, a black neckband, and a narrow yellow rim to each eye, of a much better finish than anything on the tramway.

For a while the cock tried to make a nest against the ridge. He would settle his breast among the porcelain saddles and kick out behind him, sending pieces quite a surprising distance downhill. He made five or six little dishes this way. The hen examined them all. A saddle is a light, hollow object, excellent for exposing a large wet surface to a current of air, and very poor stuff for sitting on. If she put her weight to one side, a dozen saddles would turn over together and the side of the dish would go out. By the time she got up she was usually several inches from where she had sat down.

They settled in the end by the new cut, on a tongue of gray floor between its two blue branches. Grit had lodged there in the lee of the bolt, coarse upstream and quite fine at the tip of the tongue. The cock scraped in the coarse part. The hen brought up little flakes of the blue from the fine part and laid them, with other things, round the edge. She laid four eggs, all pointing inwards, which remained invisible to the pump-house people until one afternoon the fitter put her hand on the bolt to steady herself getting across. Something warm went off almost between her fingers. She was so taken aback that she put her boot in the water after all.

The tramway acquired a notice asking people not to go down to see the birds. This had the useful effect of letting everybody know there were birds, and since the ridge afforded a perfectly good view, most people stayed on it. From there one could see a changeover on the eggs. The bird coming off would walk away in a perfectly ordinary fashion, stopping to feed once or twice, while the other one walked in by a different route. For a few seconds the eggs would show, if one knew the particular bolt. Then there would be a small settling motion like that of a person getting comfortable in a theatre, and nothing whatever to see for another hour.

What they made of the clocks is not known. They took no notice of the blue flash. They knew a tug, however, considerably before its rain came down. The sitting bird would close its eyes to slits and draw its head in, and presently the little blows would begin to land on its back, hard enough to part the feathers and show the dark underneath. A tow gave it some difficulty. After the tug had gone over it would shake itself, open its eyes and put its head out, only to have to go through the whole business again for a load of empty drums.

Three of the young hatched during the night shift and the fourth some time after breakfast. They had long legs of nearly the finished size, with a small amount of sandy fluff carried high up on them. On their first day a joint between floor panels was a considerable undertaking. A chick would come up to it at a run, stop with both feet together, and look down into the black line. Its mother, already on the other panel, would put a foot back across. This generally did the trick. By the third day they went over the joints without noticing. They could all get under the hen at once, but there was no room for their legs, and in a quiet spell on the flats she might be seen resting on as many as ten.

They range a good way from the bolt now. In the station book they occupy a line of their own, with six in the space for number, though six is seldom the number anybody sees. What people mostly see is one chick on a great pale panel, running for all it is worth towards a point indistinguishable from any other. It gets there and puts down its queer, oversized foot. Something moves in the skin of water. Even from the ridge one can see the little dip of satisfaction with which it takes it.
