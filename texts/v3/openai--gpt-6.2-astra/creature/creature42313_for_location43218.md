The animals on the blue peg belong to one household. The two at the ends are full grown; the other seven are of three different sizes. From a distance they suggest a row of little grey pears hung up to ripen. Close to, one can see the toes working. A blue survey peg is planed quite smooth, and every so often one of the smaller animals loses its purchase and goes down the row, hand over hand across the bellies of its relations, until it finds a place where the grain has been raised by the weather.

They are called tackers. They can fly, though they seldom go any distance on the wing. What looks like a rather ill-fitting waistcoat opens out between the first and last pair of legs, leaving the middle pair free. With these a tacker catches hold of the underside of a leaf as it sails past. Its hindquarters swing up; there is a small sound like a purse snapping shut, and the animal is at home again. On a level surface it is in difficulties. A man finding one on the station platform will usually lend it the edge of his newspaper. It takes hold and can then be posted, as it were, under the nearest bench.

There are tackers under the benches of the old districts that have never seen a growing leaf. They eat the minute white creatures that live in dust, and are valued in particular by the keepers of the clothing stores. Their country cousins have a different occupation. At the raw edge of the floor there is a great deal of loose fibre, as fine as the stuff that hangs from the break in a bean. The tackers comb this out with the hooks on their wrists. Some of it they chew. Most of it they carry away in great streaming bundles, which makes them look, on the short flight home, like very small comets going the wrong way.

Home is a pocket on the underside of the leaf. It is a communal construction, surprisingly commodious, with a lip sufficiently stiff that a young one may sit on it and look out. There the young learn to use their eyes separately. One eye attends to the family business, the unpicking and laying down of fibre, while the other watches the immense dim space below. A young tacker watching with both eyes is apt to get the end of its own tail incorporated in the work. For several days afterward it must go about with a pennant.

It was a tacker pocket that first took Ina Venn under the floor. She was nine and wanted one for a doll's bed. The leaf had drawn back in an unusually cold week, leaving the pocket empty and just within reach of a long spoon. She got the pocket, lost the spoon, and went after it on her stomach. On the other side she could hear, with extraordinary distinctness, her father at breakfast above her, tapping the shell of his egg. She called to him. He put his ear to the floor; she put her mouth to a vein. They were able to discuss the spoon without raising their voices. It was not until she came out that he took fright and gave her a shaking.

Ina is now the person the planners send under with the lamp. She is sixty-two, narrow through the shoulders, broad through the hips, and has the habit, tiresome in company, of laying a hand flat on a table whenever anyone sets something down on it. She likes to know what a thing is resting on. In the new pleats she walks with one foot a little ahead of the other, feeling for the two distinct motions of a lap. If the upper floor goes down under the forward heel while the rear heel rises, the two leaves are still independent. If both heels go down, they have taken somewhere nearby. She can generally find the join within the length of a kitchen.

Of the people who go about with her, the most necessary is an apprentice from the works who stays on top. This is not considered a good assignment. One must follow a slowly moving spot on a drawing, carrying a lunch basket which may not be set down, since the weight of it overhead can be quite alarming to a person in a close place. At intervals Ina strikes the leaf with the butt of her lamp. The apprentice makes a blue dot and writes down what she says. Along the two miles of open rind the works men can be seen leaning from their windows to enjoy the spectacle of a large, well-nourished boy walking at the pace of a minute hand and carrying somebody else's sandwiches.

The present apprentice, Dav, likes it. A long time passes between taps. He has seen the grazers turn over from the middle outward, and knows now that the first one to turn is not necessarily the first to see the glider. Often it is a grazer which has been knocked sideways by a frightened neighbour and has simply failed to get up. He has seen a motorman slow an empty tram to keep its shadow over a woman walking home in a bad patch of noon sun. Once, waiting for Ina to get round an obstruction, he watched the shadow of a bird go across the shaft light, pause, and go backward. He thinks it was a bird on a wire swinging in the wind. There is nobody he can ask who would know for certain.

In the gold-seamed room the bench is already polished at one end. A woman from the plate press comes there between shifts to undo her hair, which has to be worn tight under her cap. Two girls from the bakery use the other end to practise part-singing. The low ceiling gives them back the lower part very handsomely, so that each is anxious to sing it. Sometimes there is a third visitor, a rust glider with one bad membrane, which has found a way in along a pipe. It creeps the length of the gold seam licking the little salt scales where they stand proud. Seen from beneath, with its tail laid straight behind it, it might be the single dark hand of an exceptionally leisurely clock. The girls have got used to it. The woman from the plate press has not, and waits outside until someone has looked.

The proposed school has taken up a good deal of Ina's time. From above, the site looks excellent. From below, there is a difficulty: an old rib crosses the new at a slant, and in another twenty years it is likely to lift one end of the schoolroom nearly a yard. There have been meetings about moving the peg. The schoolmistress, who will be retired by then, thinks a slope would be perfectly satisfactory for seating and would give the children at the back a view of the board. The man responsible for school furniture points out what will happen to a dropped pencil. Dav, not being asked, has drawn in the margin of his plan a long shallow pencil trough with an opening at the teacher's end. Ina has told him to leave it in when he makes the fair copy.

Meanwhile the blue peg moves west a little every day. The tackers go with it. Their old pocket is by now a good six feet out of reach, and they have begun a new one directly below the mark for the schoolroom door. Lying there with her lamp off, waiting for her eyes to take up the green light that comes through the leaf, Ina can hear their wrists at work. After a while comes a second sound, much slower and farther off: Dav upstairs, shifting the basket from one hand to the other. She knocks to let him know he can eat his half.
