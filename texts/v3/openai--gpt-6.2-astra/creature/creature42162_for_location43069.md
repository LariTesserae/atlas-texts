A child coming home from the reed beds carries a bundle longer than the child is tall. From the parapet this gives the walk a curious appearance. The reeds go on steadily at the height of a grown person’s head while the two brown legs beneath them take an extra step now and then to get round a patch of water. At the last turn the bundle shifts from one shoulder to the other. Whoever is waiting in the court sees the pale cut ends come round the corner first.

Distance makes these people look exceedingly deliberate. Close to, they are as apt as anyone to put down a thing they will have to go back for. There is a boy in one of the upper courts who has made the whole journey to the gardens carrying a head pad and no basket. His grandmother saw him go, but could not make him understand at that distance what it was he lacked. He lifted the pad to show her that he had it. She sat down again to her mending. It would do no harm, she thought, for him to make one return journey on which he was free to look about him.

The pad is among the first things a child makes for its own use. It is a braid of reeds sewn round on itself, with the hard ends worked under, a difficult bit of sewing since nearly all the discomfort of carrying a weight on the head can be traced to one end left sticking up. Later the same hands learn the wide loose stitch for a roof and the close stitch for a sleeping mat. You can tell where someone likes to sleep by looking at a mat that has been rolled up all day: one part of the roll is a little smaller and shinier than the rest. In damp weather the rolls stand on end against the common kitchen wall, and the warmth brings out a smell of the beds on a cutting day, when yards at a time of the ordinarily shaded water are open to the sun.

A great many people who appear from a distance to be sitting idle are waiting for something to dry. The lime worker waits with a basket turned bottom up beside her. If she fills it damp, she will have a white crust between the strands that breaks off into the next load, which may be food. While she waits she pares the callus at the side of her heel with a flake of the local stone. There is much more individual care taken of feet than of faces. An old man may have only a misty notion of his appearance since his beard went white and still be able to give an exact account of a troublesome place under his fourth toe. There are people especially good at putting a new sole under an old sandal without altering the set it has taken from its owner. Their best customers are the carriers who could not walk comfortably in a pair that were perfectly alike.

For food they have the gardens in the deep pockets, and a meal made from the thick joints of a water plant that grows farther down the shelves than the roofing reed. The joints have to be grated wet. At a kitchen wall in the morning there may be three women sitting with bowls between their knees, working away with little backward strokes on rough slabs, and a fourth person whose entire employment for the moment is to keep a baby from reaching into the paste. The cakes have very little taste hot and quite a good one cold. A carrier takes two folded about a handful of sharp leaves. A child will eat all the leaves at the first court and arrive at the gardens unable to face the cakes.

One of the water keepers is a woman whose fingers no longer close well enough to hold a grater. She walks the courts with a strip of roof reed in her hand. She remembers when a certain end house had eight people in it and the next one was shut up, and must remind herself that it is the next kitchen now that needs the greater share for washing. Before moving anything she lays the strip on the surface above the slotted stone. It will sometimes go a surprising distance sideways to find the mouth of a slot. On those occasions she feels under the lip and generally brings out a soft black wad of fern. There is an agreeable little restoration of sound along the slab. A person indoors, hearing it, sets aside a shirt or a pot lid to take out to the wash.

Under the lip there are also threads as green as bottle glass, held out straight in the current. On the occasions when a basin is emptied, these fall against the stone in such a thin skin that one could take the green for a property of the stone itself. The woman is careful not to scrape them away. Her reason, if asked, is simply that they do no harm there. She has been known to spend much of a morning on her knees getting a tree seedling out of a drinking slot with its two leaves and its long naked root intact, and then to cut down a tree thicker than her wrist that very afternoon because its roots were lifting a paving block. The seedling went into a pocket below her kitchen. Nobody expects every such pocket to become a grove, but all the groves began with something comparably unlikely.

The houses looking out on the treetops are much sought after by young people and less so by their elders. In the flowering season it is possible to lie on a mat in one of those houses and see nothing at all through the doorway but yellow. It is also possible to spend half a day rinsing yellow dust from a dark piece of cloth and have to begin again as soon as it is spread out. Two sisters who live there have rigged a screen of old thatch to stand over their washing. Their father helped to put it up; from under its edge he could see the doorway of the house he used to occupy, very far away, across two fields of bright water. He had not realized until then how many years of his life he had been looking in this direction.

Toward evening someone generally moves a little way up one of the streets to meet a late carrier. It is of no particular use to call out. Long before a voice could carry, the person with the load will have recognized the way the other one stands, one foot on the sloping edge of the pavement, a hand shading the eyes after the sun has ceased to be troublesome. The child with the reeds knows quite well who has come. At a hundred paces there is still time to decide against giving up the bundle. At ten paces the shoulder underneath it has begun to ache past bearing. The two stop, the bundle remains for a moment at the same height, and then it travels on again, a little higher now, with the child beside it drawing a long piece of split reed through both hands, feeling for the smooth side on which to begin a braid.
