In the eastern of the two rush beds there is a water rail. For most of the day it is represented by a single stem that leans out over a clear place, stays there a little while, and comes upright. Now and then one of the bird counters, looking for something half a mile farther off, gets the whole bird in the bottom of his glass. It is a surprisingly handsome thing to have been effecting so little: a long red bill, a blue-grey cheek, bars of black and cream on the folded sides and, behind, a scrap of white which it jerks up as it goes under cover.

Seen coming straight on it is hardly any bird at all. The two halves of a clump of rushes need only give a little to let it through. Its feet are another matter. Each of the three forward toes goes a considerable distance on its own account. In the bed it will stand with one toe on a bent stem, one in water and the third on the small hard collar of mud round a root. It can reach down from this uncertain footing and put its bill in the mud right up to its face. A worm comes up stretched almost straight, with a little button of mud on the free end. The rail draws the worm through its bill several times and the button falls off.

The rail has been in the basin through at least one rise of the river. At the height of it there was nowhere in the eastern bed to stand except on the rushes themselves. The watchman, making his round along the wall, saw it swimming in a lane of brown water that led apparently nowhere; on his way back he found it in the same lane going the other way, with something pale and jointed in its bill. For a week after the water went down there were little shelves of rubbish caught high in the stems, mostly broken reed and the long empty cases off the river trees. The rail took to walking on these. There are places now where it still goes up and down a little bridge although the mud below would carry it perfectly well.

Nothing in its behaviour suggests that it ever takes in much of the basin at once. A fly settles on a blade near its eye and is gone; a shrimp gives a kick under an inch of cloudy water and the bird turns its entire body to attend to the spot. It works a margin of mud until the mud gives under one foot, whereupon it works back along the same margin. Yet the bird that emerges in the evening on the side toward the other bed knows to a nicety where to put its feet. It goes out along the submerged back with short quick steps, not directly toward the rushes opposite but toward a bit of stick lying well to the left of them. At the stick it makes its turn. A man in waders trying the shorter way between the beds went in over one boot top.

In the other bed there is sometimes an answer to it. On an evening when the middle engine was down for a bearing, the men working under the open square of roof heard a noise like a young pig in difficulties, followed by three quite mild separate grunts. One of them went to the apron to look. There was the usual quantity of sky and mud, the heron well out on the flats, and a slight agitation at either end of the crossing; nothing that could reasonably have made such a noise. Once the engine was going again they heard no more of it.

Late in the season the counters saw a party cross while the heron was still fishing on the seaward side of the join. First came the red bill and the narrow blue front, then four little creatures in black down, all feet and hurry. At the stick the first three swung left with the old bird. The fourth stopped to peck at something and by the time it looked up the others were inside. It stood quite still. The top of the stick, which in spring had shown above the water by the breadth of two fingers, was just about level with its breast. After a moment a red bill came out of the bed, low down this time, at the height of the little bird's head. The black thing ran to it over water so shallow that it left a row of separate dark stars behind it.
