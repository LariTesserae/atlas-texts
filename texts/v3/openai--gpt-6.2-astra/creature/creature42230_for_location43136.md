There is a keeper of the well, though you would not give it the name on a Thursday. Then it looks like a comma written on the mud with the point of a pin. For most of the afternoon the smaller beetle stands over it. The beetle is after the damp that comes up in the two dents made by its forefeet; it pays no attention to the comma, and once has put a foot squarely on it. When the foot comes up the mark is still there, a little deeper and with a grain of red in its tail.

On Sunday the water gets in at the tail end first. The comma lengthens, opens down one side and puts out a wheel of fine hairs. There are really two wheels. You can tell this when a scrap of mite skin, going round very fast on the left, passes between them and goes round just as fast the other way. The keeper is a little bag of clear flesh with a hard brown mill in it. The mill works away whether anything comes down to it or not. At the far end of the bag are two toes by which it holds on to the mud. It can take both wheels in at once, put its head to the ground and bring its toes up to meet it, and in this way it walks quite respectably for something that has spent three days as a mark.

The first water is poor. The keeper walks out along the drowned blade of grass almost to the tip before it finds much worth milling. Here the foot of a woodlouse goes past, so close that the hairs on one wheel lie flat in its wake. A little later the foot comes back the other way. There is a white fleck caught in the last joint now. This comes free by the grass and proves to have two toes of its own. For a while the two keepers work side by side, one with a red grain still showing through it and one quite clear. The water between their heads is scoured so clean you could count the pits in the blade. On either side it is thick with dinner. Presently the clear one takes a step sideways and the arrangement suits them better.

When the glass comes down there is not much danger of being crushed. A blade of grass is a substantial prop. The trouble is the water going out: in a moment the tip is on dry land, though the grass is still bowed with the weight of a drop halfway down. The clear keeper goes with the flood. The one with the red grain is left hanging by its toes on the underside of the tip. It feels along the grass with its head. Each time it puts its head down a silver waist draws out behind it, finer and finer until its toes let go and the whole waist runs forward over its back. It has water enough to walk in if it takes it along.

By two it has reached the drop. There is something brown in it which in the morning was inside the keeper, beside the mill. It is an egg, rather large to have got out of so small a parent. The keeper feeds on the low side of the drop, where the specks collect; the egg lies on the high side against a nick in the grass. In the white light coming up off the grit you can see a notch at either end of it. One notch stays put. The other, if you keep quite still and let the shadow go another brick's width up the wall, moves round.
