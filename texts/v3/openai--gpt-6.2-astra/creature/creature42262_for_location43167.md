The farmer’s dog is of two minds about the salt water. In the lane it will go in up to its chin to get hold of a stick; at the edge of the hayfield it stops and smells the grass before it sets a foot down. It has been afloat once. The piece that went out with it was quite large and grounded almost at once, and the farmer walked out by way of the west path and lifted the dog off by the scruff. It remembers the voyage better than the rescue. Even in summer, a long way inland, it goes very carefully over a place where a cow has torn the sod.

Its chief business is with the cattle. There are little black cows here with a good deal of hair in their ears. In the short daylight they can be hard to pick out against the alder, but once they start feeding you can hear them a field away: three or four pulls at the grass and a wet slap as the tongue comes home. They like the turf that has had salt water over it. Left to themselves they will work out to the last dry inch, all facing the same way, until it looks as if there were another fence across the meadow. The dog goes down the water side of them. It knows every cow by the distance it must keep from her heels.

There is one brindled heifer that goes across the bay twice a week. She belongs to a boy on the far side, whose mother has the byre for her but not the keep; the farmer lets her into the aftermath with the black cows. The boy fetches her home when there is weather coming. At the porch he takes the bell off her neck and puts it in his pocket. There is nothing in the regulations about bells. It is simply that, the first time he took her across, the tongue fell out of it halfway, struck the path once, and went into the water, and for the rest of the crossing she kept trying to turn round to see what had fallen. She will go ahead of him now with the halter loose, putting her feet down with the particular hollow knock that horn makes out there. In fog people hear her passing their upstairs windows and know the wind is due to get up.

Under the bench in the crooked porch lives a wren. More exactly, that is where it lives by day. There is a space where the brickwork has parted from the wood, very rich in spiders. It goes in at one end brown and narrow and comes out at the other end so puffed up with cold that it has to squeeze. At night it sleeps with seven or eight others in the pocket of an oilskin hanging in the farmer’s shed. The oilskin belonged to her father. Nobody wears it because one sleeve has gone board-stiff, and nobody moves it because of the birds. On a still night, if the shed door is opened softly, there is a little dry seething sound from the pocket. Well before light they are gone. One can spend a lifetime here and never see two wrens together.

The farmer is up about the same time. She has one hand that will no longer close properly, so she warms it round a mug before she does the catches on the byre. When she was a girl her father sent her out in the boat to lift a little string of creels. She could find the first with no buoy showing: you put the kitchen chimney straight over the cleft in the headland, then rowed till the cleft opened enough to let a thread of sky through beside the chimney. The chimney is down now. The stone that held the end of the string lies in her field with its iron staple still in it, and every year when the cutter comes round to that place she gets down and does a yard by hand. Good clean hay grows through the staple. She could have the stone out in an afternoon with the tractor, but there are few things left of which she knows the exact position in the dark.

Near that stone the field mice have a town of their own. The old runs lie on three or four levels in the moss, the lowest full of clear water, the highest roofed with dead bent grass and just wide enough for a mouse with its cheeks full. They live well between floods. There are seeds in the hay and little white things under the bark that comes ashore, and in a cold spell it is possible to gnaw right up inside a turnip and plug the hole behind you. What kills them is a small rise after a bigger one. They have moved the young, gone back for the seed, made everything snug in a place that was dry at full water yesterday. The fox is waiting before the first mouse puts its nose out. In a thaw you can see where it sat, a neat pair of forefeet with a brush mark beside them, and the little stitches of tracks coming up to it from half a dozen directions.

The dog will not tolerate the fox in daylight, though at night they sometimes clear a gate between them. This happens when the people with the lamps have gone in and an eddy works a fresh wad of wrack into the bars. There are often things alive in it. The fox pulls from the meadow and the dog from the lane, each believing the other has hold of something worth having, until the whole mass comes through and the gate swings free. Once it was a skate’s egg case, empty and lighter than a shaving. The farmer found it on the mat in the morning beside the dog, which had kept one paw on it all night.

She generally goes down the meadow after breakfast to see what has been left. The dog comes too, but not to heel. It runs wide circles round her, farther out each time, until it finds the mark of the highest wash. Along this it moves slowly with its head down. Here is a bit of green paint off a boat; here a frog quite stiff with cold, which shuts one eye when the warm nose touches it. At the corner by the spring there is a whole armful of alder twigs with the red catkins already long on them. The farmer picks these up. They will open in a jug in the kitchen, and for a week there will be yellow dust on the sill where she sets down her mug to look across the water.
