A dish at rest has no front that one can discover. The white lip goes all the way round. Touch it anywhere and it withdraws at that point alone; an inch to either side of the dent the grazers go on feeding. A travelling dish is another matter. One edge lifts just clear of the stone, and under it there is a row of small blunt feet, pale at the ends from wear. This is the front for as long as the animal needs one. On meeting a neighbour it need not turn. It puts that edge down and lifts another.

The part that makes a poor show in the light is the belly. In a dish freshly lifted there are two creases running across it, one inside the other, very finely puckered. The inner crease fits over the seam. The outer one opens here and there to the air, and at each opening the puckers are stained yellow. It is from these that the meal works its way up onto the lip. A grazer puts the thin end of its head into a stained pucker and holds very still. Presently a plug comes out entire, a lovely little cast of the hollow it has filled. Six or seven heads get together over one of these. In a minute there is nothing left of it but a yellow smudge on the backs of their heads.

A grazer that has eaten its fill can scarcely get its feet to the ground. It walks on the lower curve of its belly, pushing alternately with the legs on either side, and is in considerable danger at the join between two dishes. Here the hunters lie with the long hairs of their fringes spread across the join. Under the weight of a grazer a hair bends very gradually. Nothing happens until the grazer takes its weight off again. Then the hair springs up and rolls the beast inwards, often with such gentleness that its feet continue walking in the air. The mouth of the hunter is underneath. For the last small business of getting its meal round the edge and under, it must raise itself, and it is then that most of its own lodgers change places.

Some of these are long enough to clasp two hairs at once and go hand over hand along them. The round kind cannot do this. They wait at the roots, and when the edge goes up they drop onto the dish and make for the next hunter, which may be a journey of half a day. Between the two there is a good deal of exposed earthen-coloured ground. A pale speck going slowly across it, now lost in a roughness of the shell and now emerging, may be the only creature in sight that knows quite where it is going.

The sleepers on the roof are easiest to see just after the young dishes have gone sliding into shelter. A few sleepers let go then, opening out as they fall something like the two halves of a husk. They land very lightly in the pockets behind the walls. There they walk among the young, touching each overturned belly once with the face. What they take is the fine skin which comes loose at the joints of the little folded legs. Very often a sleeper has hold of a piece of skin at the very moment the dish gets its footing. The dish walks away clean, and the sleeper sits back rather abruptly on its hooks, holding a nearly perfect pair of stockings. These it eats from the toes up. Before the low current dies it is back on the roof with its face warm under somebody else's belly.

There are dishes in the wall flutes so old that the lip has worn through in places. A thin new lip has formed inside the first. Grazers prefer the old one, for the notches in it are deep enough to shelter a whole grazer when the night air comes in. From below, what one sees is a broken white ring with a sound white ring inside it, and in the gap between the two a row of small, well-fed bottoms. One such dish has been sideways on the lowest of the laid stones for a very long time. Its belly covers the joint. Every evening a few of the young come to rest against the underside of its rim, and every evening, under their quite inconsiderable weight, the old dish slips a little. There is a crescent of clean stone above it, about the breadth of a fingernail. The rest of the distance it has come is already occupied.
