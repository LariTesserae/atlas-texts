Under the runner's flake there is a little more room than the runner needs. Its paw goes in at the pink end, sweeps once, and comes out white to the wrist. The snails it misses are mostly at the other end, near the place where the flake is still joined to the bed. There is something among them about the thickness of a snail but as long as the last joint of a finger. While the stone is up it keeps perfectly still. A little after the stone comes down it begins to travel, with a motion like that of a loose thread being drawn between the pages of a book.

It is a salamander. The feet are so small in proportion to the spread of the ribs that they are easily overlooked. On top it is the gray of a wet thumbprint; underneath, where no one ordinarily sees it, it is a clear apricot color, fading to white in the throat. Its eyes would pass for two grains of the black stuff that gathers in the corners of a bedding plane. All the same it can see. Sweep a hand between it and the narrow line of daylight and it draws its head in; hold the hand there a while and the head comes slowly out again.

It feeds chiefly on the little white jointed things that follow the snails. There are scores of these on a fresh track, taking something from the slime too fine to be seen. The salamander lays its chin across the track and waits. Every so often there is a small swallowing movement, scarcely sufficient to lift its chin off the stone. When the track begins to dry there is often a whole row of snails, a finger's breadth away, traveling in the opposite direction on their way back into the wet. The salamander has only to turn round.

There are a great many of them in the shelf. A man might spend a summer looking for them under loose stones and conclude there were a dozen. The stones a man can lift are generally dry underneath, or wet for an hour after a cloud has come well over the edge. What the salamander inhabits is the thickness of a wet seam. In one place this gives it a strip no wider than its body, with a wall of perfectly sound limestone on one side and on the other a drop into a roaring black pipe. In another the seam opens out under half an acre of stone. There are low places with water standing in them and dry rises that divide one feeding ground from the next. The whole difference in elevation would hardly be felt by a hand passed over a table.

At the rim, where a bed has broken off short, one can sometimes see into such a country from the side. The best light is the light thrown up by the cloud. Kneeling at the edge in full sun one sees nothing. Let the shadow of one's head fall across the stone, and in the silver light from below there will be a throat working, or the lovely little print of a foot held up against the roof. The young can walk there upside down. The old ones are rather too heavy; they go along the bottom with their shoulders brushing the ceiling.

Once in a while, in the lee of the high back, a runner gets more than its paw under its stone. It hunches its shoulder and holds the flake up for some seconds, looking for a particularly good snail it has felt go by. Then the light goes much farther in than usual. What looked like a patch of coarse salt at the back of the opening resolves into eight or nine eggs, each on its own short stalk, hanging clear of the water on the floor. The gray length curled beneath them is a female. She has been there long enough for the snails to have worn a clean way round her. At the noise of the flake dropping she does not stir. In the renewed dark she puts up the end of her nose and passes it gently along the underside of an egg, taking off a drop.
