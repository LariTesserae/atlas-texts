A person from the town, met on a road in the valleys, is apt to stand too close when asking a question. At home two people who mean to talk hook their heels under the same rail and bring their faces close enough to read each other’s mouths. It is hard to give this up for the sake of a man selling onions, especially if he has a beard. Otherwise there is little by which to know them. The white in the creases of their knuckles goes away after a few days. Their climbing slippers are much sought after below, and plenty of people wear them who have never seen the mountain.

They do not breathe the water. The first thing a baby learns is that somebody will always be there when it comes up. There is a little dipping place in the floor of a house, made by passing a cord from one side of the shelf to the other. The baby sits astride a wrist, grips the cord, and goes under as far as it likes. Sometimes this is no farther than the lower lip. There are old people who remember with perfect clarity what their mother’s thumb looked like from underneath, magnified in the water, with a little loose skin beside the nail. They cannot remember the room.

Later there is the journey to the next house. Somebody goes ahead and somebody comes behind, and a good deal of trouble is taken to have something worth arriving for: a hot nodule split and filled with salt, or a small cousin forbidden on any account to laugh. It is generally the cousin who is remembered. The children become good swimmers in the course of going to see one another. They are not particularly interested in swimming. Asked how far she can go on one breath, a girl will say, “To my uncle’s, if he’s home,” and consider it a fairly silly question. If he is out, she must allow enough breath to get to the house beyond.

There is one bell on every street that has no shelf. Anybody may put a head up in it. Here one finds a woman with her hair streaming down over her eyes waiting for a pain in her leg to pass, or three boys having an argument which began quite a way back in the beans. The inside of the bell is ribbed, so that heads at different levels do not knock together. In some of them the clay was pinched into shape by a potter who made places for noses too. A great many people have tried putting their noses into these hollows. Very few admit it.

The people who go down for willow are a little different. They spend nights in the open and have the valley habit of looking up before undertaking a piece of work. On the outward journey they come over the lip in wet clothes and have to stop a short way down to dress, the wind on the far side being quite terribly cold. They have made a dressing place behind a boulder. A boy going down for the first time is told in detail about this boulder, about the notch in it where he will find the wool wraps, and how he must keep a hand on his slippers lest they slide. Nobody thinks to tell him much about the sky. There is usually a delay there that has nothing to do with dressing.

It is these carriers who bring home the other life of the town. A willow bundle shaken out in a work bell will yield beetles, a spotted slug, once a wren which had held on tight through the whole afternoon’s climb and had to be taken out again in a dinner pot. The little flat brown spiders that live above the sleeping shelves came up that way. They make no webs, but sidle along the meeting of wall and roof, feeling ahead with two long legs. People like to have one. Where a spider goes the ceiling is still dry. If it has spent a whole day on the awl rack, somebody ought to look into the air supply.

Much of the visiting is done by old people. They have the time for it, and some business that can be carried in the hand is generally saved for them: a gauge for the cane bore, a twist of dye, a sole cut for the left foot which must be taken to the owner before a right is cut to match. Their routes get shorter. Nobody remarks upon this. Someone who once took a sole clear round the headland now takes it three doors, where she finds a friend quite willing to take it the next four. A pair of slippers can spend a very sociable week in the making.

One of these women has lived in three houses, each almost directly above the last. She has the ordinary trouble of old age in the town, which is that when she is thinking about something else she turns off at the wrong level. Her daughter goes after her and finds her hanging by one hand from the lintel of the middle house. There is nothing the matter. She has just been trying to put her key away. It is not a door key but the small toothed piece of bone that opens her workbox, and its place used to be a notch cut under that lintel, out of reach of children. Her hand has remembered where to put it long after she has stopped keeping it there.

The daughter feels in the notch herself. It is almost closed. There is a gritty little hollow left, into which the end of her smallest finger fits, and a soft rim that comes away on her skin. She has passed this house a thousand times without knowing about the notch. On the way back up she keeps turning her finger against her thumb. At their own door her mother gives the water a slap, although it is her house and there is nobody inside, and waits, as she always does, for the rings to reach the wall.
