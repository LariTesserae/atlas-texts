The nearest keeper is the one that used to mind the landing. Its scraper is still there, laid flat at the foot of the stair. The keeper put it down before grafting, with the handle a little way out from the lowest riser so that a fold could get under it. Rind has grown over the handle since. The grit on the blade is clean, having had nothing to do.

It is difficult to say where the keeper ends on the floor. On the side away from the stair it thins into a skin, and a little meal goes under the skin at every knock and does not come out again. Toward the stair it is still quite thick. There is a pleat there with a smooth inner face, the color of the sill that it left. This face has never had grit set in it. It is the part that used to be put down on a neighbor's sill when the neighbor was at work and there was no occasion to make the house shake by setting down a tool. A keeper could feel the pleat of another beside its own. There used to be room on some of the sills for four.

What has reached the landing keeper is not the weight of the crumb. There is no strand leading up the stair to bring word of anything so small. But when the crumb fell, it broke. A chip went down three treads and lodged in the angle of the fourth. Something finer came all the way down. It lies along the clean grit in a pale line, and one end of the line touches the pleat. The keeper is turning toward the taste of ceiling. Most of the meal that comes through the house is old floor, quite bland by the time it has been stored and shaken out and divided. The ceiling is a little bitter. The keepers used to eat it in the eastern ward when the houses there had upper rooms.

Of those who lived upstairs, one is now the middle prong of the fork. It had the trade of taking in a load from above. The other two prongs grow stiffer where a crumb bears on them; this one gives, making a bed in which the crumb loses its bounce. It can still do this for a load much larger than any that passes through the fork. For a long time after the last upper room came down it used to make the bed at every blow. Now it waits for the touch. This saves a little oil, not much at one blow, but the middle prong is appreciably thicker than its fellows.

In their moving days these two keepers worked a landing between them. One went down a step and braced its foot against the riser. The other eased a load onto the smooth back of the foot, then came down to take its turn below. There was very little scraping to be done on a stair. What was wanted was a pair of soft places that could be moved, so that meal could be brought down whole instead of arriving as a powder that got into every joint in the house. The seven sharp edges are in part the work of those two, who kept anything hard from striking them.

The fold at the foot of the stair cannot reach even the first tread. As it turns, it lays bare a strip of floor on which the old marks can be felt very clearly, a short drag, a narrow dent, a short drag. Oil begins to fill the dents. It will be some time before this much of the keeper can be lifted again. What the fold can do, and at last does, is get its thin end under the exposed lip of the blade. It does not lift the scraper. It grows a little thicker there, until the scraper slopes.

At the next knock the crumb stays on the landing. The chip comes out of the angle of the fourth step, goes down the remaining three, and strikes the blade. There is a small loss of powder. The rest takes the slope very nicely and comes to a stop against the middle prong of the fork. The prong gives. For the first time since the graft took, there is bitter meal lying whole in the bed of it.

Up on the landing there is enough for this to happen a great many times. Down below, the keeper leaves its fold where it is. Along the blade, just above the grit, a fine ridge of living rind begins to form on the side away from the fork.
