The little swimming animal in the third pocket is a water shrew. If you watch the line move through the liverwort you will expect something longer. It has a narrow black head and holds its tail so stiffly behind it in a turn that the line goes on opening in the old direction after the head has gone off at a right angle. It comes out under an alder root. For a moment it is all silver. Then the air goes out of its fur and there it is, black and white, with a caddis case between its teeth. It sets one foot on the case and draws out the tenant like a hand out of a glove.

It eats a great deal. It is probably the most expensive thing the shelf keeps. At certain hours you can follow its supper in the wheels. A shrew has been along the bottom turning over stones; in the midge there passes, wheel by wheel down the wet hall, a brief hesitation, very conspicuous against all that easy motion. Outside, a pale side of stone goes dark again. The shrew does not wait to see this. It has a place in the green sleeve of the alder where three young lie with their pink feet pressed to one another's bellies, and when it goes in to them the whole sleeve squeaks.

The young are not held to any measure. One is already big enough to come as far as the mouth of the hole and bite experimentally at the rain of fine brown threads that hang there. It has a little white nick in one ear. You can pick it out some weeks later, fishing on its own account in the first pocket; later still you might see it, quite by chance, on a night of low cloud, going up the back wall. There is a seam there just wide enough for its feet. It goes up into country where a stone turned over will lie pale for years.

It is rather difficult, on this account, to say which things live here. The mice certainly do. There is an old male with one hind foot drawn up against his belly who takes the bare stretch in three runs, sitting down twice to wash as though that had been his intention in coming out. The particular grain clicks for him three times instead of four. But the dun moths, which give the place so much of its inhabited look, are nearly all away by dark. They rise beside the rock in a loose brown column, seeking something in the upper air, and at daybreak a column comes down. One has a torn wing with a blue scale at the tear. It has come down on three successive mornings. Of the others there is no telling.

There is something in the works as well. Towards the end of the hall, where the wheels look no bigger than the pierced heads of pins, a light occasionally fails to shine on the water beneath it. Something has passed between. Once, in a midge that had got its feet stuck in the juice of a bruised leaf and so afforded a long steady view, it was possible to see the obstruction come nearer. It went on four feet along the rim of the bath, stopping where each root came through the ceiling. At one it put up a forefoot. The bead came down round the foot and for a second magnified the spread toes, the fine web stretched between them, and a little lump on one toe exactly like the seed of a grass. Then the wheel took the bead. The creature sat back on its haunches to lick its foot. Whatever its business was among the wheels, it had been drinking.

There are likely to be a great many such things in there. There is room. And a warm bearing, a root end, the minute flying things that assemble wherever a lamp shines on water: these would furnish a living without any creature having to know what the works were for. Perhaps there are runs polished on the far side of the baths by feet that have never touched the mountain. From in there what could be known of the shelf? A load coming on; a slight easing; once in a long while the enormous disturbance of a root drawing up out of its hole when an alder dies.

Meanwhile the bird at the lower end has acquired a follower. A young grey bird sits a stem away, thin still, with a little pale fluff standing out above either eye. At the call it lets itself down after the plump bird but does not yet know the trick of coming up with its feet open. It strikes the sedge breast first and there is a scramble in which both birds go below the lip. For several breaths nothing shows. Then the old bird comes up, and some way farther along, where there has not hitherto been a bird, the young one catches hold of a red fern stalk. It is too supple for the purpose. It bows steadily until the bird is hanging almost upside down, looking, with the greatest attention, at what rests on top of a leaf.
