Under the first inch of sedge there is a bird's foot, neatly divided into three brown toes. It stays there while a party of little waders goes by so close that one of them puts a foot on it. Then the toes curl about a root and the bird draws itself another few inches along under the bank. It is a kind of rail, not much bigger in the body than the waders, though it has legs enough for a much larger bird. Seen end on it is surprisingly narrow. It can go in between two stones where a duck's head will scarcely go.

There is generally a pair at each of the longer springs. A good way of finding them is to wait for the falcon. When everything else goes up, there will be two little wakes travelling in the opposite direction, towards the place the flock has just left. The rails come out from under the sedge and go over the bottom while it is still stirred up by the feet of the other birds. They have none of the quick stitching movement of the waders. One foot goes very slowly down; then the head is laid almost on its side and the bill goes in beside the foot. What comes up is often a blackened bit of stalk. The rail takes it by one end and draws it through the bill. A pink grub stays in the bill and the stalk goes sailing off quite clean.

Much of what looks like plumage on the bird at first is mud. There is a yellow tide mark across its breast where it leans out over the ochre water, and its belly trails little beads which roll off entire when it shakes. Under this it is a sober slate colour with a very pretty scattering of white dashes on the flanks. The dashes are what one sees of it in a hole. For some time they keep their places in the dark; then they revolve together and the bird comes out head first from a space in which it seemed quite impossible for it to turn round.

Behind one such hole the old skin of the lava stands up in a blister. It has cracked along the top and a little daylight gets in there, enough to show a quantity of dead sedge caught across the bottom. The sedge has been put there, blade by blade, with the cut ends all pointing inwards. It makes a springy floor well clear of the trickle underneath. Three young birds live on it. They are black except for a patch the colour of a ripe rowan berry on the top of each head. A parent arriving in the near dark has only to touch one of these spots with its bill; the spot tilts back and there is a mouth immediately below it. The other two crane up so hard that now and then they pitch over backwards on the yielding floor.

The warm strip on which this family depends has already shifted once. A yard or so out on the bare ground there is a second blister with the same litter of cut grass in it, quite dry, and a little frozen white feather stuck to the roof. Between it and the occupied hole are no tracks at all. On the hard flow the rails leave nothing. It is only when there has been a fall of powdery snow in the night that one can see the extent of their travelling. There will be a line of prints going straight out over what looks like perfectly hopeless country, disappearing at a split in the rock and beginning again, much farther on, at another split. At one point the prints break into two fans of delicate slashes with a clean space between. Here, for about the breadth of a kitchen table, the bird has used its wings.

When the sleet comes down the parents get very busy. For that hour there are things to eat on stone which has had nothing on it to eat for months. The two birds work steadily uphill against the sheet of water, intercepting whatever rolls towards them. Presently one finds the loose flake rocking. It stands on the lower edge and goes down with it; a fine dark paste wells up at the other end, full of little white wrigglers. By the time the stone turns right over both birds have their bills full. They run home separately through the silver water, with their heads held high to keep the load dry.

Late in the evening, when even the nearest pool has lost its light, the rail has a call. It is a small double tap with a considerable interval before the next two, easily missed among the noises of the spring. But the knock under the rock goes wandering away down the crack, and the whistle keeps to its one thin note. The taps come always from the mouth of the blister. After a little there is a reply, much finer, three or four taps all tumbling over one another. A bird which has been standing out in the cold with a last billful of food steps in.
