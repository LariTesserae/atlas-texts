On the undersides of the silver leaves there are little grazed windows. They let pinpoints of daylight down into the cups. If you turn a leaf over you may find, at the edge of a window, what looks like a drop of milk with a grain of soot in it. The grain shifts to the side toward your hand. Presently the milk pulls itself into two short horns and begins to travel, leaving a perfectly clear track. It has a mouth as fine as the nick a razor makes in paper. It takes off the silver and leaves the green tissue alive.

Most of these grazers never leave the leaf on which they hatch. In the brief warmth when the dark faces turn, they work their way out to the rim; in the cold they come back to the pocket where the blade joins the stalk. An old leaf is lace at its edges and quite opaque at the base. There are creatures below which know the difference. All afternoon they lie on their backs under the youngest, least perforated leaves, where a body the color of cream is nearly invisible. You can put your face within a foot of one and still be examining the leaf.

It is about the length of a glove, with six pads turned up to the leaf and a fine, untidy beard hanging down. The beard is the only part of it that moves much. A hair curls until its tip catches hold of the next hair; another does the same; a little running hem forms in the air and is let down again. It is taking the measure of the space beneath it. From time to time in among the hairs you see the small bright ends of teeth.

At dusk, as the blade begins to cant, the animal rolls with it. For a moment there is an unmistakable face looking sideways out of the grass, broad across the eyes, astonishingly narrow at the jaw. Then the back pair of pads comes loose and it hangs by four; the middle pair comes loose and it hangs by two. By now the beard has found the moss. Down it goes headfirst and without a sound. All over the hillside this is happening. By the time there is light enough between the stems to see a fair way, the leaves are empty.

Below, these animals go in twos and threes along the ochre roots, which are just wide enough for their pads. They cock the beard to one side to keep it out of the water. They eat the milk grazers, but only in passing, scooping them out of the low pockets of the leaves. What brings them down at night are the much larger, soft, orange grubs that live inside the dead length of a rush. You sometimes find a rush opened from joint to joint and the wet pith combed neatly to either side. An adult will do this for a long time after it has taken all the grubs out. Its young come along behind it, too small to slit a stalk, picking up what is left in the combings. They have little stubbly chins, dark with juice.

There is a difficulty about having young here. Nothing capable of holding a nest dry for a week can be counted on to hold it dry for an hour. A tussock which stands well clear at noon can go under from a fall of hail in another watershed, without so much as a change in the sky overhead. In certain places, however, one can see a little round hummock with water standing all about it, and on its crown, instead of the usual rushes, a close crop of pale, much bitten roots. This is where the young go before daylight. The hummock has a lid. It is a piece of living turf, complete with leaves and cups, and when lifted it lets out a breath that smells of warm pith. Underneath there is a bowl scarcely deeper than a cupped hand.

What is remarkable about the bowl is how level it keeps. An adult returning to it brings, as often as food, a piece of root. It draws this through the beard several times. Some pieces it drops at once. Others it takes below the lid, and if you have the patience to watch for a whole cold morning, you may see one of these come poking out through the side of the hummock, pushed from within, until its raw end touches a root in the surrounding turf. When the turf sinks, the bowl hardly stirs. Somewhere there is a place taking the dip that would have come to the bowl.

There are failures. After a particularly wet night you find families sitting out in the silver leaves in full daylight, the adults with their beards laid over the young. A root has died, or a distant bog is full and can take no more. They are wretched in the light. The young keep trying to get under one another. Yet even here one of the adults is usually busy, letting its beard trail across the stalks as they sway, feeling for a motion small enough, and contrary enough, to be of use. It will leave the others and follow a stalk down. In a little while you see its white rump going backward over the moss, the six pads working very hard, a length of wet cable coming up behind it.

The finest time to see them is when the plateau takes on the dew. In the momentary lift above the leaves, look not at the prospect but close by, along the tops of the folds. Here and there a pale chin has been thrust up through the moss. The animals are lying in their bowls with only their beards outside, spread flat in little fans. The dew catches in them. For a few breaths you can see exactly what each one feels: a shiver running in from the left, a hollow opening at the center, then the long slow stroke of the rising ground passing through the wet hairs one after another. One chin withdraws, and then another. The last is generally a young one. Its beard is hardly long enough to part the moss, but it puts it out into the enormous evening and leaves it there until the light goes.
