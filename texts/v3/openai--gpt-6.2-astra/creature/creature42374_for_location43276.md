The children who swim to the post office have a particular use for the caps their mothers make of oiled silk. They take them off on the steps and put the letters inside, put the caps on again and swim across with their hair quite wet and the letters quite dry. On the other side is a low brass mirror, put there for people to comb themselves in before going on duty at the counter. A child coming up out of the water can see just the top of its head in the glass and can tell, from the shape of the cap, whether it has remembered to bring the letters.

There is a family of small brown animals living under those steps. One sees their tracks much oftener than the animals: four meticulous wet flowers on a dry tread, then four on the next tread, which is so far away that it looks as if the creature that made them must have legs like a compass. In fact it runs the whole distance on the stone under the overhang; only to get round the end must it come up into the light. Its legs are rather short. It has the face of an elderly mouse and a tail flattened from side to side, with a nick in it in nearly every grown specimen from backing into the cracked places where it sleeps.

The postmistress has one for a neighbor. Late in the day, when she takes the brass tray out of the letter scale and goes down to rinse the dust out of it, it is usually sitting on the lowest dry step with a shellfish in its hands. The difficulty for the animal is not to get the shell open. It has a tooth for that. The difficulty is to insert the tooth without getting a mouthful of hot, bitter water from inside the shell. It braces the shell hinge downward against the edge of the step and gives it a little sideways jog. Two drops run over the edge. Then it eats. Some evenings there will be a young one beside it, jogging away at a pebble. The postmistress waits for them to finish. She is one of those people whose private life is thought to be rather empty because the part of it that gives her most pleasure takes less than a minute and is of no conceivable interest in the telling.

Her brother works in the fourth wheel room. He can identify, by a slight pull and recovery in the running of the shaft, the moment the big clay roller is put on in the shed above the exchange. He cannot hear this, being nearly deaf. He puts the ferrule of his stick on the end stone of the bearing and leans his chin on the handle. Asked what it is like he says it is like having somebody else chew your food. He does not use a stick for walking and is forever leaving it where he last listened. There are sticks with his initials burned into them in all six wheel rooms, two in the exchange, and one in the little room off the barber’s where you can have a bath while your collar is being ironed.

In the fourth room there are also some colorless flies. The grubs live in the damp fuzz of the belt guards and feed on something too small to be seen. The adults hatch with wings like the skins on boiled milk and are carried up in the draft of the shaft well. At the top they acquire, for perhaps twenty minutes, beautiful blue bodies. This is the dust that comes off the dry tiles as the girls smooth the edges with a twist of straw. The first water the flies touch takes the blue right off them. If you sit on the bench outside the smoothing shed in the late afternoon you can watch a blue fly descend to the surface of a pan, and a white fly rise from the very same spot, leaving a blue comma on the water. The flat fish come up under these marks and turn their small, unprofitable mouths to them.

The tile smoothers wear their hair tied in cloths, not out of any great regard for their hair but because a loose hair drawn through blue dust and then across a cheek will leave a fine blue cut which everybody longs to wipe for you with a damp thumb. At the end of a shift they go out through the back of the shed, down three steps and into a pool up to their shoulders. There are pegs there for their clothes. Owing to the way the rock has settled, the pool is deep at both ends and shallow in the middle. One of the smoothers, who is expecting a baby, likes to sit on this submerged rise with the others swimming slowly round her. From the packing room all that can be seen through the slot where the belt comes in is the upper part of her face, quite steady, and from time to time another face going by. The boy with the felt squares has known her all his life. It took him several days to realize she was sitting down. He had been wondering how she had come to grow so tall.

There are a few old men in town who still go down below the cloud. They take the wheel road, which is a stair except where a section of it has slipped, and at those places is a ladder. They go to cut the long red rushes from which the shellfish baskets are made. A rush drawn through the hand one way is smooth; the other way it rasps. A good basket has the smooth side uppermost on every strip, so that when the woman who is skimming tips it a little, all the little ones go sliding back together. A badly made basket must be picked clean by hand. The men know each other’s work at a glance, even in a basket black with age, and an old quarrel about the price of rushes can be resumed at supper over the rim of an object that has been in three families.

Down there, they say, the pink-footed birds are not particularly white. In the brown water at the foot of the gorge they are the color a spoon looks in a cup of tea. They dive from the ledges and stay down a surprising time, coming up tail first, as though whatever they had gone under for were still being argued over. Nobody on the shelf sees this part of a bird’s life unless he takes the trouble to go down. Up here their great business appears to be standing in a row getting dry.

One of the rush cutters once brought a granddaughter down with him. She was an accomplished swimmer in the town water and had never seen so much as a leaf on a tree. What astonished her was neither the trees nor the diving birds but the shade of her grandfather walking beside her, now on one side of him and now on the other, broken into a hundred pieces by leaves. At home a shadow has the firm edge of something cut out with shears. She kept putting her foot on bits of him. He cut his rushes, tied them in two bundles and gave her the shorter one, and they came back up into the clear blaze on the shelf just as the shift was changing. For weeks afterward, whenever anyone asked how she had liked the lower country, she took them under the traveling belt between the sheds. She would wait for the frayed place to come round and make them look at those five or six fine moving shadows on the stone. “There,” she would say, though by then the place had gone in through the slot and there was only the one straight shadow again.
