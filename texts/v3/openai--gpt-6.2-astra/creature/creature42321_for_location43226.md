Under the third terrace there is a hem of grey stuff that looks as if it ought to come away on the thumb. It follows the rock for twelve yards, disappears behind one of the fallen blocks, and comes out on the other side much narrower. In the narrow part it is possible to see what the stuff is made of. There are little purses, thin enough for the grit inside them to show through, all hanging with their mouths down. The largest would just take a shirt button. A thread runs through their upper ends. What looks like a fold in a single purse is generally another purse hanging empty against it.

They are easiest to see at the enamel dipper. A strand has grown down the handle into the black water, where it is a clean pink, like the inside of a lip. Over the chipped part of the rim it divides to either side of the rust, and there are six purses in a row on the white enamel. Lift the dipper clear of the floor and for a while they keep their shape. Then the bottom falls out of each of them, or seems to. Six little tongues unroll. The furthest one, which has nothing under it but air, goes on unrolling until it is nearly as long as a match. Put the dipper down and the tongues come in. It is some time before the last one has gathered up all its slack. Along its length, close enough together to look like a stitched seam, are pairs of dark grains. These disappear last.

There is a difference between the grains they keep and the grains they let go. On a piece of slate slipped under the hem overnight there will be a sprinkling of perfectly clean sand. In the purses the grains are furry. The fur is the same bloom that comes on a fig, though down here it grows a dirty yellow and smells, when wet, of the paper that pears used to come wrapped in. Each tongue lies out on the rock taking this dust on its underside. A tremor of the right strength will make the tongue curl at the edges; a succession of tremors rolls it up with whatever is on it. Once inside, the sand is worked slowly from one side of the purse to the other. You can follow a black grain for a whole morning. By the time it drops it has been licked all over.

What is harder to settle is where one of the creatures ends. The thread is hollow. At dawn, when the water comes down the stems of the broad leaves, a clear swelling travels along it, stopping a little at every purse. At the far end of the twelve yards, in a place the leaves do not reach, the empty purses plump out too. There is a branch of the thread that goes up into the brickwork of the works and nowhere bears anything like a mouth. Where the mortar has fallen away it can be seen going back and forth in the thickness of the wall. In one course it is as thick as a little finger. Press it there and water stands in beads along the hem outside.

For most of the year there is very little to distinguish one day of this life from another. In the week of the wavering note the purses at the gravel end of the thread do a curious thing. The intervals of quiet are just long enough for a tongue to go out a little way before the next shudder takes it in. Each time it goes out rather further. Some find a stone small enough to get underneath. The tongue doubles back upon itself, the lips of the purse come down over it, and in a day or so the stone is wearing a tight grey cap. For a while there is a thread stretching back to the terrace, so fine that it can be seen only by getting the dark of the ash door behind it. Then there is none.

Most of the caps turn up again within a few feet, dry and light, with a little sand stuck to the lining. But at the far side of the bed, where the gravel pushes in under the northerly shade, there are several stones with the caps still on them. They have brought up against an old grass cord there. From the foremost cap a thread runs along the cord to the place where a leaf rests its apricot edge against the ceiling. Hanging from it is one purse, as yet quite clear. It gets a drop of water every morning. In the late bar of sun its shadow on the rock is large enough to show the tongue going in with its first two grains.
