One of the drops under the slate keeps its place when all the drops on either side of it have fallen. There is a second drop growing on the end of this one. It lengthens, takes away most of the shine, and falls. What is left is a little brown hat with the crown pushed over sideways. Two horns come out from under the brim and feel the place where the water has been.

It is a limpet, of the small, thin-shelled kind that live in fresh water. A sedge seed would make a respectable load for its back. The brown of the shell is mostly dirt, which has lodged in the lines of growth; at the brim, where the shell is new, it is clear enough to show the edge of the foot working underneath. The foot has the shape and something of the translucence of a grain of cooked rice pressed flat. Just now the whole weight of the animal hangs from it. When a bubble breaks at the door the horns shorten to two knobs, but there is no movement of the foot. It could hold up a good deal more than the hat.

Its country is a stretch of the old underside about as long as the last joint of a thumb. There is food here, though to an eye looking up through water there is no difference between this slate and the three sharp new edges. The difference shows where the limpet has been eating. A translucent brownish bloom comes away in little sweeps, leaving the stone blue. With the head well out, the animal can clear quite a broad patch on either hand before the foot has to bring the shell along. It is like seeing someone wash a floor on their knees. The part immediately in front is done with great thoroughness; there is a little awkward shift of the whole establishment; another part comes within reach.

On the flood there comes a considerable change in the look of the limpet. For a moment, as the water rises, there are two of it, crown to crown, with a narrowing thread of light between. Then the water takes the shell and it ceases to be a bright object at all. It is a brown inequality in the roof, one of a hundred. This is when it puts its head farthest out. The stickleback passes close underneath, trailing the shadow of its fins over the stone, and takes a shrimp out of the peat with a jerk that brings several fibres away too. One of these catches on the limpet. For the better part of high water it goes about with a long, loose streamer of sedge, vastly more conspicuous than itself.

There are others. Over by the root, where the slate has a shallow scoop in it, a clear, flattened packet clings so closely to the roof that its rim can scarcely be found. Inside are half a dozen very small limpets, each in its own round allowance of jelly. In the largest one the brown has just begun at the crown. Given a light behind it and plenty of time, one could see it turn, taking pains over a journey that brings it round to precisely where it was. One of the rounds is empty. Its occupant is on the slate a little way off, grazing already on a patch which the large limpet left unfinished. Its entire shell fits into one of the blue sweeps.

On the ebb the sedge fibre floats free and goes out through the moss. The large limpet has reached a seam. Water draining from farther back in the bank comes along this seam for hours, not enough to run continuously, but enough to gather against the shell, go round it on both sides, and join again at the low point of the brim. Each drop that falls from here has had the shape of the animal pressed into it for a while. In the long spells between drops the head is drawn in and the foot stays spread. The little one is still out on the level slate. A film too thin to make a hanging drop anywhere is deep water round its brim. There is a good hour’s feeding left in it yet.
