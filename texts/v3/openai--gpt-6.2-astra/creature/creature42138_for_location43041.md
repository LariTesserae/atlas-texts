When a child is old enough to go out alone, somebody in the house makes it a hat with a pink crown. A square of old linen is enough. It is dipped in what is left in the kettle after the currants have been put up. From a sill at one end of a street a father can pick out his child among the barrows at the other. There is generally a moment of alarm in the course of the morning when he sees the hat going up into the turnips. The gardeners take children up with them. A child's hand will go through the mesh to turn back a root that has come out into the light.

The people themselves are much of the colour of the streets, having the habit of making a coat last so long that the dye goes out of it altogether. They are broad across the hips and rather slight above, with long, remarkably steady arms. In old age a little frill of fine hair comes up on the rims of their ears. It is considered becoming. There is a likeness of a former keeper of the jars cut in the dull face of a shell, in which the cutter has found room for scarcely more than the nose and the two ears. Everybody knows whose great-aunt it is.

The present keeper is her grandson. He makes the round with a pocketful of wool ends and a girl who is learning the work. Where the tail going to a garden has dried out, he gets her to lay the back of a finger against it and feel her way along to the damp. Sometimes it is only a knot drawn too tight. Sometimes a householder has put a private thread into the public tail and is raising something very lush in a bucket indoors. The keeper is supposed to take such a thread away. He likes first to be shown the bucket. In this manner he once saw a single blue flower, the size of a washbasin, growing in the dark behind a bed. He still tries to describe the blue when he has had a little currant wine. There is nothing in the ward that he can point to.

As a rule they know a great deal about one another. If a couple have quarrelled, it is known in three streets by the fact that the two of them have separately taken the same coat to be mended. If a woman is going to have a baby, the neighbours begin leaving things on the sill long before she has said a word: a strip of soft rush lining for a basket, a little pot with one handle, a shirt cut open all the way down the back so that there need be no business of getting a new head through a hole. There is sometimes a mistake about a baby. Then the things stay on the sill for a decent interval and are taken away, and for a while everybody is rather formal when buying milk.

The quiet of the place is no great indication of sweetness of temper. Two sisters on the stair have not spoken for eleven years. They live on opposite sides of a tread and take their airing on the same seat, one at either end, while the lizard lies before them with the loose skin of its throat working in and out. They have each made a cushion exactly half the length of the seat. When one is ill the other brings both cushions out, since a seat once given up is very hard to get again.

Towards evening a good many people sit with their feet in the street. The children are sent round to return what has been borrowed during the day. They go by the feet, for it is dark quite early inside the houses, and what one sees in a doorway is a pair of ankles, the hem of an apron and, farther up, the occasional catch of light on a needle. A child with a seed measure can go all the way to the right door, put the measure into the right hand and come away with a bit of supper without having seen a face. The hats by then are in a row on the currant pegs that have not yet grown over. After rain the old hats colour the new wood a faint pink. There are lengths of the rail where a knife, slipped quite shallowly under the bark, would turn up that colour still.

There is one man who would very much like to live beyond the tip of the branch. He has cut the pieces for a house and fitted them together many times on his kitchen floor. It would have a round window to the flats, with a shutter which could be let down on two cords to make a table. On fine afternoons he takes the shutter out there and lays it across his knees. People stop to admire it. One will set down a workbox on it for a moment to look through the silks; another, finding a level surface, will bring out a cup and sit a little while. He generally has to stay longer than he meant to. His daughter comes to fetch him when the light begins to go. From the bottom of the stair she can see her father quite distinctly against the salt, sitting among his neighbours at the window of the house that is not there.
