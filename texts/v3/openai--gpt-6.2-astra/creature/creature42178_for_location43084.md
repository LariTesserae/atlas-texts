There is an eel in the pump house sump. When the motor is running she lies in a length of broken drain at the bottom, with her head well back from the broken end. The drain is glazed inside and just wide enough to admit her. There is a whole one beside it, but that one has both ends open. It is no good to her when the pump starts. A loose mussel shell goes knocking through it from end to end.

She comes out after the motor stops. There is grit on the floor of the sump, and where she has been lying you can see for a moment the fine clear edge of one of her fins against the grit. Once she is moving there is very little to see. What generally gives her away is a shrimp getting abruptly out of her path. It jumps backwards in three distinct jumps and lands facing the place she has just left.

The sump communicates with the street by a grating under the doorstep. The two middle bars are gone. She gets through there while there is still water against the stone and works her way along the curb on the market side. The curb makes for good hunting. Little things that could scatter in any direction on the open mud come up against the stone and have to go one way or the other. She can follow a crab along it almost at her leisure, feeling ahead in the angle with her chin. A crab that has lately shed its shell will fold up small enough to fit in her mouth. A hard one she usually lets go.

She is not confined to the street. By the time the valve lamps come on she has the run of a good part of two wards. There is a scullery floor she crosses most nights, diagonally from the door to the corner where the sink stood. It is still tiled. One tile is loose and she can lift it with her nose; there are sometimes half a dozen shrimps underneath. They come out all round the edges, so that even after considerable practice she seldom gets more than one. Further along there is a pan wall with a breach in it. She will put her head through the breach but she does not go in. The water coming out along the bottom is heavy brine. A few inches higher up it is quite fit to swim in, but there is nothing up there she wants.

The man asleep in the lorry has seen her. Last winter he had to clear the grating of a twist of plastic packing tape. He got down on his belly on the step with a hook, and while he was teasing the tape off the iron there was a movement inside his sleeve. She had gone between his wrist and his loose cuff in her hurry to get past him. He remembers the weight of her better than the feel of her skin. For a second there was something like a full bottle hanging in the crook of his elbow. Then she was through and he had a wet shirt up to the armpit. He has had a look for her several times since, generally from above, which is not much use. Seen through brown water against brown stone, even quite a large eel can take a long time to resolve itself into anything other than a crack.

There are nights when she doesn't come back to the sump. Once, after a long spell of wind from the sea, water stood over the street for three days. She went out as far as the shell fan at the end of the wall. The current had sorted the shells there, laying the large cupped halves down together in the slack behind the bend. Small fish had settled in the cups. All along that bank she had only to push a shell over to put up something to eat. When the wind dropped she came back by an altogether different way, up a ditch behind the settling pans and across the market square. The last few yards were so shallow that the ridge of her back showed. A sandpiper stood aside for it and went on feeding almost before the tail had passed.

Tonight there is plenty of water. The loose tile comes up easily. She misses the shrimps and stays a while over the floor, working her lower jaw where a grain of mortar has caught. Overhead the nearest valve lamp throws a small patch of light on the rain. Down here the only effect is to make the white tiles a little easier to distinguish from the black ones. When she moves off, the loose one settles crooked, with a gap underneath. The shrimps begin to go back in.
