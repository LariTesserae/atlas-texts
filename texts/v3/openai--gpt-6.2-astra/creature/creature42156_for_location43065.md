At either exchange there is a person who knows the teeth. Pins go into a common box and lenses into pockets of folded leaf, but a tooth has to go back to its owner. It is not enough to find someone with a gap. There is the angle at which the two teeth on either side have leaned in, and there is often a little channel worn on the inner face by the tongue. The woman at the western exchange can put a tooth into the right hand without interrupting what she is saying. She has lived at that end of town all her life. The reach of her acquaintance at the other end is astonishing.

The people are most easily seen when asleep. Awake and out on the slopes they have much the color of the ground, and the long loose clothes they favor begin to pick up a grain almost as soon as they are worn. But in sleep the ears fall open. They are pleated, pale on the inside, and large enough in an old person to rest against the collarbones. On fine mornings there will be half a dozen such pale pairs under a roof, turned this way and that as the sleepers follow small disturbances in the street. A mother can sleep quite comfortably through a procession of laden carts and be up for a child easing the door shut with both hands.

Much of the business of childhood consists of getting one's ears under control. They spring open at an interesting noise, which makes it difficult to listen secretly. There is a game in which a child stands facing away from the others while someone behind slowly rubs the nap of the floor the wrong way. The object is to guess who it is without opening an ear. Children who have spent time looking after a sick person are said to be very good at this. They have learned the differences between feet.

There are people up before the light inspecting the bridge houses. They go in pairs, one inside, one out, with their bare heels on opposite ends of the same floor member. The outside one lifts a heel and lets it fall. If all is well the one indoors feels it twice, once quite distinctly and once like a little roll of meal shifting in a sack. A house that gives only the first answer has grown fast to the leaf below. This can happen in one night, especially at the warm end of an oven. It is quite pleasant for the occupants at first; the rooms are quieter. Then a door starts sticking, and some evening a family in the next ward sits down to a meal that has split beautifully along the top and is powder clear through. The inspectors spend a good deal of their time on their stomachs with a thin wedge, persuading houses to let go.

The youngest member of the western pair keeps a deer. This is not usual. Deer will come indoors readily enough but seldom have occasion to come twice to the same door. Hers has a nick across the pad of one front knee, where it knelt on a lens below the exchange. For several evenings she fed it seam animals from a crease in her apron so that it would stay off the knee. It now comes looking for her even when she is at work. From beneath a house she sees the four little commas appear beside her tools. If she puts a hand out, the deer lays its jaw along her wrist. Its jaw is surprisingly heavy. There are two sets of muscles in it, one to shut the mouth and one to hold the head steady against the shaking of the ground. Up close one can see small gray legs combed into the fur of its chin.

What the people do with their dead varies from ward to ward. In the west a body is laid in a shallow cut and the corner of the leaf worked back into place with grease. Someone stays to see that it takes. For a few days afterward that patch gives a double tread, as though a person were walking rather awkwardly along with whoever passes. In the east, where the leaf has a tendency to curl back no matter how carefully it is laid, the cut is made under a roof. There are quite ordinary households with a considerable length of their floor given over to this use. The living do not keep off it. Indeed a visitor who edges carefully around it to reach a chair is likely to be taken for someone dissatisfied with the chair.

Neither exchange will take a body, though every few years someone comes to ask. No one knows what would count as a new button.

Late in the year there are evenings when the streets scarcely carry at all. The coat remains at the western end; a loaf opens just wide enough to put a fingernail in. On such evenings people who have long since finished supper go on sitting with their shoes touching. A small sound comes up through the floor that at first seems to be the friction of one sole on another. It is the birds moving about below. They have come back to their places by touch and found the fibers slack, and they keep letting go with one foot and reaching farther up, trying for a better purchase. In some of the houses it is possible to follow a single bird for the length of a room.

The woman at the western exchange has one under her bench. She knows it by a third small tap after the two grips; one of its claws has grown round so far that it catches and has to be pulled free. Sometimes while she is sorting she feels it come up immediately under her heel. She moves her foot a little to one side. There is no reason to suppose the bird can feel her there, with all those leaves between them. Still, there is plenty of room under the bench.
