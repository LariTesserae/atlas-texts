One of the commonest inhabitants is a dun-coloured animal with a snub nose and a tail flattened on two sides. You can put your hand down among six or eight of them without being aware of it. They lie along the little shelves of rind just below the walking surface of a street. The tail hangs over, the narrow edge outward. What looks like a line of stitches along the street, a rather poor piece of sewing with several stitches missing, is a company of these animals asleep. The missing stitches are where two have drawn their tails up, or one has got up and gone away.

Even by the wet rim, where one can see them entire, it is difficult to get an idea of their size. One will arrive looking as small as a cricket and put its nose into a bead of water. It drinks and drinks. Its flank comes unpleated, its legs straighten beneath it, and presently a substantial little person is standing there on four pink soles, washing its whiskers in what is left. It can get most of itself flat again in an instant. Only the water in it has to be got through a narrow place by degrees. After the evening drinking there will often be one delayed at the entrance to a shelf, its head and shoulders comfortably in bed and its hind feet still at work out in the street.

They feed on the white cuffs the grubs push out of their bores. A cuff comes out very slowly and is quite tough until the grub has finished with it. The dun animals have learned to wait for the small double knock that means the grub has turned round inside. Since there may be a considerable difference between when this happens and when the knock reaches the street, a good deal of their day is spent attending at the wrong holes. They do not appear to mind. They sit with their tails out flat behind them and their chins lifted, looking remarkably like things waiting to be shaved. If a cuff drops elsewhere, the nearest animal takes it. There is never a race.

Their young are black at birth, except for the entire tail, which is pink and nearly transparent. For the first few days the mother carries them between her flank and the wall. You see her going by with what appears to be a bit of red fringe caught under one arm. Later they are left in the sleeping shelves. The rind begins to grow around a black youngster almost at once, and the mother shifts it a little every time she passes, pushing with her nose until it rolls over. In a deserted shelf one sometimes finds the beginnings of a dozen rooms, all for the same child.

There is another creature down by the water which is visible chiefly by the trouble it gives a fly. It is a little clear raft no wider than the fly's foot is long. Across its upper surface grow three stiff black hairs. Underneath there are a great many legs, kept curled up in the dry hours; when the basins brim together the legs come down and the raft picks its way across the submerged grain. The flies like to stand with one foot resting on it. After a time it walks away with the foot. The fly leans farther and farther over, occupied with some private washing or with watching a mote go past, until it has either to take a step or fall on its face.

No one who has seen this several times can go on thinking of the bottom square as empty. There are rafts in the fine cracks all over it. When the floor is dry the three hairs lie down in a fork of the grain and the clear body fits the crack like a drop of varnish. In rain they come out by the hundred. Most set off uphill. They will not get very far before the water sinks, but the journey back takes place at leisure, under the surface, in the thin wet interval between one layer of leaf and the next. In strong slanting light a few can be seen there, upside down, walking on the underside of the square. Above a raft and almost touching it a lizard may stand, with all the thickness of a soap bubble between its horny sole and the company of busy little feet.

Of the animal that occupied the round rooms there is rather less to be said than the rooms seem to promise. Seven hollows need not mean seven young, or six young and a parent, or even seven of anything the same size. At one doorway the last dissatisfied scrape comes from quite close to the threshold. At the next it comes from well inside. The blue moths pay no attention to either. But a dun animal going that way with its mouth full will sometimes stop, flatten itself politely against the high side of the turn, and wait until there has been time for somebody to pass. Then it goes on with its white cuff. Whether it knows whom it has made room for, or has merely lived on a narrow street all its life, is hard to tell.
