The girl who keeps the visitors’ book at the landing has seen the bird twice. The first time she saw nothing but a quick interruption of the blue. The second time the interruption had a notch in it. She has drawn the notch in the margin, very small, since the book belongs to the town. Her business is to take the names of strangers who go down to the landing and cross them out when they come back up. There is only one stair. On most days nobody comes whom she doesn't know, and she is free to pursue her other interest, which is the private life of the copper mice.

Some of what is generally believed about the mice is her doing. It was she who found that a nest with its ends turned in need not currently have a mouse in it. An old mouse living by itself will keep as many as five such nests in order, moving to whichever is warmest. A nest with young in it has one end left sticking stiffly out. She watched a mother coming home in the dark put her whiskers against this end and follow it in. In the visitors’ book opposite a day in February when there were no visitors there is a drawing of the arrangement, with the dimensions given in widths of her front tooth.

She lives with the youngest of the three sisters. This is her grandmother. The other two are also called Grandmother, except when all three are present and something has to be delivered into a particular pair of hands. Then the girl can distinguish among them by giving the article a little turn as she holds it out. The eldest likes a handle, the middle one a flat surface, and the youngest will take anything by the business end. She eats pears this way when pears come up, holding the wet cut face in her fingers and taking bites out of the dry back. All three women have a shallow groove across the ball of the left thumb. A strand of copper lying in the groove can be drawn through a piece of silk in the right hand without the wire itself being touched. A roughness that wouldn't catch in a naked fingerprint will pluck a thread of the silk. The women can find a fault halfway through an evening's conversation and go on talking with the loose thread held in their teeth.

The girl hasn't got the groove. Her special competence is in letting go. Given a cuff on the back of her hand she can lower the hand to a collar, bring the skin of one knuckle level with the inner lip, and let the animal run off without frightening it flat. It is quite difficult to do this with a creature whose notion of safety is to get into the smallest available difference between two surfaces. Usually some urging is necessary. People blow on cuffs. The girl's cuffs walk off under the impression that the town has been put back where it belongs.

She was useful to the surveyor last winter. At the foot of one arm of the Y a pencil date had appeared in an unlikely position, well beyond the lighted end of the line. He went down on the following evening with a square of white card to cut off the furnace light a little at a time. The line stopped where it had always stopped. The date was indisputably in his hand. He had begun to consider the possibility that the rock on which he had written had moved entire, with two houses and a section of the washhouse drain on it, when the girl asked him to look underneath. Directly below the date, in a warm ripple of solder, was a mouse's nest. It contained a narrow curl of pale green stone, pencil side out. What he had been reading was the back half of a November date fitted very neatly against the front half of one in March.

They didn't disturb the nest. The surveyor now finishes every date with a little stroke running out onto the surrounding stone. The girl visits that mouse particularly often. She would like to see how it carries a thing which can't be bent.

In the eleven minutes in August she goes up to the highest street with a sheet of the spoiled white enamel the sisters use for sorting screws. The people standing there have generally come to feel the sun on their closed lids. Some take off their jackets and stand with their backs to it; an extraordinary sight, all those shirts, much patched and seldom ordinarily visible, with the washing marks of the still in blue along the hems. The girl stands at the rail and tips her sheet. Down goes a patch of sun, from one cold sill to the next, losing its square shape a little at each remove. A woman on the seventh street holds up her new baby in it. Farther down, a tender puts her hand into it palm first, then back first, like somebody testing bathwater. The girl cannot get it as far as the landing. The angle is wrong no matter how she stands.

This year, just before the sun went, a small shadow ran across the enamel. It had the notch. She looked up in time to see, quite distinctly, one of the copper mice clinging to the upper wire of the lift. Its hind feet were together and its forefeet wide apart; the notch in the shadow was the space between its chin and its bent foreleg. It went hand over hand along the wire into the ordinary sky and disappeared behind the lip of stone.

She has not altered the drawing in the book. She knows what crossed the sun in August. The other two crossed when there was no sun, and she sees no particular reason why everything that lives above the highest street should have to be a mouse.
