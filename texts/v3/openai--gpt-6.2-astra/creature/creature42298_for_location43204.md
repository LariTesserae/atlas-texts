On a peak that has lately come out of the sea there are sometimes two yellows. One is the underside of the weed, which shows wherever the wind has got a purchase on a strap and turned it over. The other is in the bare stone well below the weed line. It consists of a number of narrow rims, about the colour of butter, following the edges of a crack. They are soft enough to take the print of a raindrop. By nightfall the weed is black and the rims have drawn in, leaving no sign except that the two sides of the crack are a little cleaner than the rest of the mountain.

Most of the animal is a long way inside. It has the shape of the space available to it. A slab broken from the face of a cliff may expose the impression of something like a pear tree in winter, with a thick short trunk and an extravagance of fine growth above it; six feet away another slab will bear the impression of a square of coarse net. There is no difference of kind. In one place the stone split under a single heavy strain and in the other it cooled unevenly. The animal grew out to the limits of the split. Where two cracks come within a hair of meeting it may have half its body almost touching the other half, and the journey from one part to the other, by way of living tissue, may take a day.

It does not bore. At the very tip of each branch there is a bead of clear fluid containing a little blunt head. The head advances into an opening if there is one. Against sound rock it simply goes on nosing for a while and presently draws back. These unsuccessful ends are numerous. On the broken face they appear as rows of pinpricks, like the holes someone makes in a piece of cloth while trying to find a lost stitch.

What it lives on comes from the plain. Down in the warm rock, water that has never seen daylight works slowly up through beds of dark metal. It comes up quite clear. Where it meets the cold water draining in from the sea it deposits a reddish milk, a few spoonfuls in the course of a year in any one fissure. There is a skin of minute grey growth on the side where the two waters meet. The yellow animal keeps the skin cropped so close that for a long time after the animal dies the crack appears to be bleeding: the grey growth, left to itself, holds the red stuff as fast as it is deposited, and comes welling out in a rusty velvet roll.

The butter-coloured rims are lips, though not for eating. They admit sea water. In ordinary weather there is not much to be seen of them even on a submerged peak. They stand a little proud of the stone on the sheltered side, where they are safe from being peeled back by the current. When the two sets of waves come together overhead the lips shut. A charge of cold water has been taken in. It travels down through the occupied cracks in a series of squeezes, loosens the skin from the warm side, and is let out again lower down, almost fresh, with a faint smell of iron. Much of the grating sound attributed to the mountain is indeed the mountain. There is, however, a small soft interruption near the end of the note. It occurs just when one expects to hear the stone ring clear. That is the closing of several thousand lips.

On the older peaks an animal may have its highest openings in the green water and its lowest ones a mile below, in the rain. A drop sent in at the top does not fall anything like a mile. It passes through flesh the thickness of a thumbnail into the next channel, and what is pushed out at the far end is water that went in perhaps last winter. In a dry spell this makes a peculiar little weather at the foot of a mountain. There is a ticking in full sunshine. Each drop falls from a different height, but they all strike within a pace or two of the cliff, and there, in a country almost without gravel, one finds a narrow bed of it. The pieces are lilac-grey on top and red underneath. They are rounded on one face only.

For many seasons nothing much happens to the animal except an extension here and a retreat there. Then the range heaves. This is a much more serious matter than the departure of the sea. It is possible to remain alive on the water already held in the rock for a considerable time; it is not possible to remain in two pieces of a mountain that have parted company. In the instant of the heave the long body pulls apart at its narrowest places. The open ends curl in upon themselves. Some of the portions are scarcely larger than grains of boiled rice. Each has a little warm water, a little cold water and a length of the grey skin tucked inside it. For a time it is a complete household.

Most such households stay where they are. A few, if the split widens toward daylight, are washed out. It is these that one might conceivably see away from the stone. They are not swimmers. In water the curled ends open just enough to let out two transparent films, unequal in size and set somewhat awry, so that the little packet turns over very slowly as it goes. With the plain behind it the packet is quite invisible. With sky behind it there is a thin brown mark that vanishes at every turn. A whole mountainside may be going by in this fashion without giving the water the least appearance of being inhabited.

It is of no particular use to come against the weed. What the packet requires is a raw face with water issuing from it, and this is most likely to be found on the very mountain from which it has just been shaken loose. In the slack behind a new shoulder a few packets make the turn and come gently back against the rock. There is no hurry about the attachment. One film lays itself down; the other goes on waving for an hour or so. A blunt clear head comes out and tries the stone.

On the plain, meanwhile, below some summit left high and dry by the shift, the last of the stored water is coming through. The ticking grows infrequent. Long after the weed up there has lost even its black colour and blown away, a single drop gathers on the sunny face of the cliff. It hangs all morning over its little bed of gravel. There is just enough iron in it to leave, when it falls and dries, a red ring on one stone.
