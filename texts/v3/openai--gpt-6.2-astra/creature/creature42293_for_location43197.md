There is a girl in the second house from the bakehouse who can carry a jar from the cistern to her door without steadying it with either hand. She does not do this when anybody is watching. It is not spilling the water she minds, but the sight of grown people holding their hands out a little way from their sides, ready to catch something which they are much too far away to catch. When she carries the jar for use she keeps a hand on it like anybody else.

Her father weighs wool. At the close of business he takes the pans off the balance and hangs them face to face on a nail. A little way down the valley there are people who know him only as a voice calling out a weight. He has a high voice which goes a long way, and a way of waiting until the carter has finished whatever he is saying to the donkey, so that he need only call once. At home he is rather deaf on the side on which his daughter likes to sit. She can talk a long while on that side of him and come round to the other when she has got to what she wants to say.

The woman with the sieve lives above them. The girl has had the same view of her for most of her life: two forearms on a sill, with a little loose skin hanging down from the elbows. It is odd to come up the outer steps and see the rest of her sitting there in the sun, her heels tucked under the rung of a stool. She mends sieves for three or four households. The owners can tell their own by the colour of the willow, except for one man who has to put his round his head. If it slips as far as his ears it belongs to his brother.

There are things living here that have no occasion to come out into the square. Behind one of the whitewashed walls a gecko spends the daylight hours in the run left by a shutter bolt which has been moved the width of a finger. Sometimes, when a cart goes by close to the house, a little white dust comes down on its back. It comes out grey in the evening and goes in white in the morning after running along the wall. In the wool room, under the board on which the bales are rolled, there is a nest lined with short blue threads. Nobody in the upper town spins blue. Something small has gone some distance for those threads, or else they came here tied round a parcel, and what was a considerable journey for the blue was only a trip across the floor for the mouse.

The girl is often sent to clear the elder spout. What stops it is generally a little pad of grass roots, quite clean, with one long root hanging from it like the string of a purse. She likes pulling the elder free and feeling, through the sole of her foot on the shelf, the difference when the water goes back to running down the rock. One morning, with the spout out, she put her ear to the groove. There was a sound much louder than a drop, a rubbing and letting go, repeated. She called her father over. He put his good ear down and listened for some time. Then the woman in the sun laid her sieve aside to reach for a fresh length of willow, and the sound in the mountain stopped.

Since then she has tried the rock in other places. In one place she can hear the turning of the balance, which is quite inaudible to anybody standing outside the wool room. In another, if somebody is coming up the inner steps, she hears the foot being dragged up after the foot that takes the weight. She knows her father that way before he comes into view. There is a patch as broad as a door where she cannot hear anything at all. It is the best place to lean in hot weather. The rock takes the heat out of one cheek and leaves the other to feel how warm the day is.

In the evening there is very little left in the square to show how many have been there. The stool goes up the steps upside down on the woman's head, with the finished sieve caught between its legs. The donkey is led down to a yard where it stands with its lower lip in its drinking water for a while after it has done drinking. The girl brings a last jar home. By then the round of old cloth she wears on her shoulder is damp through and has taken the print of the jar in a close red circle on her skin. She hangs the cloth on the peg beside her father's coat. Sometimes he takes it out with him in the morning by mistake, caught on a horn button at the back of his neck, and from four different doors along that short stretch of street somebody steps out to set him right.
