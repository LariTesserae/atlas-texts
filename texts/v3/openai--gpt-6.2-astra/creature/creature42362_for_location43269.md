At the corner below the grain store the stone is polished about as far up as an ant can reach. Higher up it is still quite rough. A worker coming backwards with a seed puts her hind feet on the polished part, loses her purchase, and for a little while walks very fast without getting anywhere. Another worker takes hold of the far end. Between them they get the seed broadside across the passage. There is room for an unladen ant to go under it, and a number do, touching the two carriers in passing. By the time the seed is in the store it has changed ends three times. Its awn is still out in the light.

The workers look much alike when they come singly through a stripe of sun. Seen together there are considerable differences. Some have heads nearly the width of the passage. There are little ones so lightly coloured that the dark seed in the jaws goes by first and the ant materializes behind it. One of the broad-headed workers has lost the last joints of a hind leg. She does quite well on bare rock, less well on chaff, where the stump goes through between the shingles. She spends much of the afternoon just inside the store, opening seeds. The pieces go down to the young in other mouths.

On the warm shelf the young lie in a shallow heap, small ones in the hollows between large ones. Down here the roof is low enough for a worker to brace her back against it and lift a larva considerably heavier than herself. As the stripe of warmth travels inward through the rock, the heap travels with it, a larva at a time. In a pocket behind the shelf there is a single ant with a deep chest and a long shining abdomen. The two little scars on either side of her chest are all that is left of the wings on which she came here. She could not get through the turn into the grain store now. An egg comes from her folded almost double; a worker straightens it with her mouth and lays it against a dozen others, to which it sticks.

There are inhabitants that can go all their lives without coming to the ants’ notice. One of the pale snail shells at the back still has a tenant, though not the one that made it. A little many-legged grey thing lies curled in the last turn with its plates overlapping. In the dry months it fits so closely that a hair put into the opening would hardly go in beside it. When cloud presses into the grass, and the root foot darkens where it comes out of the bank, the plates ease apart. Two jointed feelers come round the turn. By morning there are minute paired tracks on the damp dust and a place on a dead leaf where the soft surface has been grazed away, leaving the veins standing up quite clean.

The beetles on the tip are harder to overlook. Turn up a husk and what seemed to be its black underside divides into four or five glossy backs, each going a different way. One beetle has the habit, on meeting a worker, of drawing its legs in tight. The worker picks it up, carries it to the lip of the tip and drops it. It lies still among the seed coats for a while. Presently a leg comes out and gets hold of a husk, the other legs follow, and the beetle climbs back up into the rich stuff. There is a particularly good pocket under the red pebble. An ant can put her head in there but cannot open her jaws.

Toward evening the traffic thins on the exposed stone and thickens on the creeper. The scale insects look like blisters of the stem itself, even to the fine dusty bloom on them. A worker taps one for some time without result. At the next there is a clear bead already standing, so large that for an instant the pale joint of the creeper shows upside down in it. She drinks until the bead comes loose. On the way in she stops in the throat of the entrance, mouth to mouth with a worker bound uphill. Neither of them has to go the whole distance from the leaf to the shelf.

After the light has gone there is still a little silver to be seen at the mouth. It is the underside of the grass, turning in the wind. The long-legged spider is out on it, feeling from blade to blade for the thread it left there yesterday. Farther down the face the lizard has wedged itself belly to the warm stone, its chin resting on its forefeet. Almost against its cheek, on the other side of a thickness of rock no greater than a seed, a cricket begins to sing. The lizard opens one eye. In the town a loose shingle lifts a little at one end and falls, lifts and falls. A worker comes up from underneath and lays a grain of grit on it.
