There are dippers under the pear booth. For years people thought it was the same dipper, a dark little bird with a white front like a man who has taken off his waistcoat to do a dirty job. Then a child at the floor window saw three white fronts in a row. The birds had their toes hooked over a ledge so slight that, seen from above, they appeared to be standing on the palms of their own reflections.

They feed on little transparent flies that spend most of their lives in the cold water under the gardens. A fly comes out of a wet joint in the stone, unfolds a quite extravagant length of wing, and is taken by a dipper before its wings have properly set. On warm days there are more flies than the birds can eat. They blow across the hole in skeins; on the far side people take them out of the dye with a spoon. A son of one of the dyers keeps a book of them, their wings laid flat between slips of onion skin. In the vat they are all much alike. In his book there are wings like the ribs of a fan and wings like a pear leaf after the flesh has rotted out of it. He would like to know what they look like before they come out of the water. His mother has offered him the use of the small yellow bowl, but not the good lens, which she needs to see whether the blue has taken in the seams.

The dippers used to nest in a drain mouth. Now the ward crew fixes a shallow box beneath the booth for them. This is on account of Ena Voss, who cleared drains for thirty-one years and who once came down on a chisel and felt, through the pad of her thumb, something give that was not stone. She took out the whole bend of the pipe with the nest in it. For the rest of that summer it stood on two pegs outside her kitchen. One egg had a little round dent in it. It hatched a day later than the others, by which time Ena had worn the finish off the sill with her elbows.

Ena is old now. She lives almost opposite her younger sister. They can see into one another’s kitchens, which has been at different times a great comfort and a considerable nuisance. The sister lost a husband in the winter, not to the hole but to an inflammation following a bad tooth. For weeks afterward Ena made the walk every morning with a covered dish. Sometimes, setting out, she could see her sister already at the table with her spoon in her hand, and it was hard then to take the turning that led away from her and go past the rope maker’s, past the public oven, past all those doors behind which people were getting perfectly adequate breakfasts of their own. In the end she moved in with her sister. Now they look across at Ena’s empty kitchen and discuss whether that is damp coming through below the sill or merely the shadow of the box.

There are people who like the walk. A great deal of courting gets done on it. You may take someone a distance that would seem quite forward anywhere else and still be able to point to her father on his doorstep. There is a particular pace for going home together from the baths, slow enough to let the hair dry, and a particular pace for the last hundred yards, when it has dried and neither person can think of any further reason to be slow. The potter’s son has lately taken to washing his hair on the same evening as the boy from the dye yard. He comes home with small damp paper packets of wings which his mother finds in the morning among the shavings on the floor. She puts them on his end of the table and weights them with a lump of clay.

Very young children have to be taught what is far away. A baby reaches from the washhouse roof for a red sleeve across the shaft and cries when no one will give it to her. A little later she learns to call across, an almost soundless effort involving the whole chest and a prodigious opening of the mouth. The person opposite, if he sees, will open his mouth in reply. By school age the children can carry on quite involved exchanges in this fashion, though each side comes away with a different account of what was said. Adults mostly use their hands. To ask *Are you coming round?* one finger makes the curl of the street; to say *I’ve only just got back* one shows the bottoms of both shoes, a gesture best attempted sitting down.

From outside the town the people are sometimes accused of keeping nothing in repair. A visitor sees the bridge ends, the tiled roofs in their odd colors, the stair disappearing into ferns, and thinks how handsome the place must once have been. It is difficult to show him the things that are kept in repair. A clear passage the width of two fingers, fifty feet in under a house, does not look like much from the street. Neither does the clean bit of light under a door that scraped the flags last week. In Ena’s time the most admired hand on the crew was a woman who could get the lining out of a pipe whole, draw it toward her a little at a time on a hooked wire, and lay on the grass a pale crooked thing four feet long, faithfully reproducing every joint the plumber had made a hundred years before. She broke it up like anybody else for tiles, but first she would put her eye to one end. If the pipe was straight enough she could see her friends at the other end, reduced to an eye or a moving patch of cheek. They waited their turns to look.

Ena goes to the booth most afternoons. Getting down on the floor is still quite easy; it is getting up that requires another person, and she prefers to wait for a child, because a child will let her pull as hard as she needs to without saying anything encouraging. From the window she can see the top of the nest box and, below it, the blade on which the young birds stand when they first come out. It is broader now than when she put the box there. This year one of the young stayed on it a long time after the others had gone, stepping sideways to and fro, bending its legs and straightening them, looking not at the depth under it but at the tiny moving things in the wet. Ena watched until her sister appeared across the way with an apron spread between her hands, their old signal for something hot just out of the oven. When she looked down again the ledge was empty. There was a print on it, three fine strokes in the soft buff stone, already filling with clear water.
