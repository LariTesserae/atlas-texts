The woman with the spanner is Nell Hobb, a fitter on the early turn. She has taken off her cap. The drops that come off the little tree are appreciably colder than the rain, and there is a spot on the crown of her head, just where her hair begins to go two ways, on which she likes to feel them fall. She has no business at the tree. From the stair one tends to credit people crossing that great expanse of floor with a singleness of purpose which they do not, on acquaintance, turn out to possess.

What she has in her cap is a brass wheel off a door. A boy is waiting for it in his shirtsleeves at the fourth house on the left. He could perfectly well come and fetch it, but he has been told to stay where he is and hold the door up. He is doing this with his hip, eating a slice of something folded over and following Nell with his eyes.

In the fourth house they have to take the bottom wheels off the door every six months and slip a thinner pair on. The lintel is coming down. At the fifth house, which shares a wall with the fourth, the trouble is quite the reverse; there is a space over the door through which a grey flier, with its wings shut, can walk into the kitchen. One did, on the morning of a wedding, and spent the greater part of the festivities asleep in the deep outside pocket of the bridegroom. There is a photograph in which the two little feet can be seen hooked over the piping. People looking at it for the first time generally take them for a sprig of fern.

There are thirty-one people in the court. There used to be considerably more. The empty houses are kept open, not from any expectation of tenants but because an unused hollow, if left entirely to itself, grows shut. Every Sunday somebody goes round and draws the doors and sweeps out the pale flour that has sifted from the ceilings. In one of the houses the furniture is still in place. The wood has grown round the legs of a table and lifted it until a seated person would have his chin just level with his plate. This is the house the children like best. They serve one another meals standing on chairs and conduct the conversation in the loud, exhausted voices of their parents at the end of a shift.

It is the children who have discovered that the drinking animals, of which nothing is ordinarily visible except the heads, have feet. There is a loose brass strip at the edge of one wheel track. If you lift it during the first minute of rain you can see into the side of the nap. Each little beast stands on the shoulders of the one beneath, three or four deep, and the bottom one has all its feet braced against the wood. Only the top one drinks. When it has had enough it folds at the knees and goes down through the others like a man climbing down inside a ladder. There is a good deal of jostling to be next. Once a girl lay with her cheek to the pavement through an entire shower, keeping account of a particular animal with a white foot. It came up twice. This fact has not found its way into the little printed account of the town which is for sale beside the weighbridge. The girl, who set some store by it, wrote it in in pencil in eleven copies before she was caught.

The ivory animals in the walls are much less known, although everybody hears them all day. There are people who can sleep through the combing engine starting up but will wake at once if the high note at the head of the bed breaks off. Nell's father was one of these. In the last year of his life, when he could no longer get his boots on, he used to send her out at inconvenient hours to listen to some particular sill. Nine times out of ten it was only a stream of animals turning a corner. The tenth time, in a house two doors down, she found a weep of clear water behind a child's pillow. A coupling had worked loose in the wood. The animals had gone round the wet place, leaving a patch of perfect quiet about the size and shape of a hand laid over a mouth.

There are fewer mites lost in the combing than one might suppose. At the bottom of the return chute a man named Vale sits with a piece of flannel over his knees, picking out those that have got their legs tangled in the fibre. He does this with a pin blunted in a candle flame. It is not his paid employment, which is to count the pads and tie them in scores. He has time between scores. A freed mite will stand for a moment on the ball of his thumb, furiously polishing its face, and then make off up the cuff of his coat in precisely the direction in which the bark lies, though there are three walls and the engine between. At dinner time he hangs the coat on a peg by the intake and goes home in his waistcoat. His wife can tell from the state of his sleeves whether he has remembered.

By now Nell has got the wheel into its fork and told the boy to ease off. The door runs the length of its slot under the push of one finger. They both try it several times for the pleasure of the thing. From inside comes the smell of onions softening in a pan, and the boy is pulled in backwards by a hand which also takes away the end of his bread.

Nell stays a little to wipe the filings out of the runner. High up, a single flier has missed its place on the steam pipe. It goes sidling along the row, putting its narrow face into one crease after another and being gently but quite firmly shoved out. At last two of the grey things move apart without waking. It gets one shoulder in, then its head, then by a very determined wriggle the rest of itself. The row closes. Only its feet are left out in the cool.
