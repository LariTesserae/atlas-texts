There is something under the bright foot of the staple. In the ordinary light it looks like a flaw in the setting: the silver does not quite meet the black grit, and in the narrow space between them there is a row of pale folds. When the light comes up from zero you can see grit through the folds. You can also see, lodged between the second and third of them, a chip of blue shale. The chip is what first made it possible to tell that the folds were alive. For three successive returns of the light it was in a different place, though always between the same two folds.

The creature holds to the staple by one edge. The rest lies loose, like the skin that forms on a drop of oil, except that it has a hem of some substance heavy enough to draw it downhill. It has lain in this attitude so long that the hem has made a shallow bay in the grit. At the deepest point of the bay it doubles back under itself and comes nearly to the foot of the staple again. Here, well below the band of cold, is the softest part. A grain rolling in there will make an impression which takes years to fill out. The blue chip entered sharp end first. Its shape can still be seen a little way behind it.

What the creature lives on is a small irregularity in the giving back of height. When the corundum rolls down, the near slope loses a little height before the far mountain receives it. The staple, being all of a piece, settles at once. The grit settles grain by grain. For the time between the ticks there is a pull on the creature, its fast edge going down with the silver while its hem is still held up. One of the folds opens. By the second tick it has closed again, but not upon quite the same part of the skin. In this way a fullness travels slowly from the staple towards the hem. The chip travels with it. Nothing passes through the creature from end to end in less than a few hundred winters, and most disturbances die out on the way. This one has come almost halfway.

There may have been others. Under a plate of shale on the near slope are several curved depressions, one inside another, very like the bay. But they might equally well have been made by a shaving of brass curling up as it cooled. There are no trails between them and the staple. Nothing here has ever needed to get from one place to another.

Most of the time the folds are too slack to cast a shadow. Then comes one of those afternoons when the white head sends the light uphill. For a minute you see the whole underside, including a portion tucked into the square corner of the staple where you would not have thought there was room for anything but silver. It is finer there, and furled in the opposite direction from the rest. The first time you see it you will probably take it for a young one. It may be. Or this may be the way the creature keeps a little of itself free of the weight of its hem. In any case, when the corundum comes to rest and the pull goes through the skin, that little furl tightens instead of opening. Long after the answer has come from the other mountain, it is still holding on.
