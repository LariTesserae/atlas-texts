In the black part of the grove there are little people. You can spend a considerable time looking into it before you see one. There are figs darkening on the low wood, and figs with the bloom rubbed off them, and then one of the rubbed places opens an eye. By the time you have made out the rest of the face the person to whom it belongs has usually made up his mind about you. He either goes on with his dinner or comes down to have a look at your feet.

They are about the height of children who have lately learned to walk, but there is nothing of the same uncertainty in their knees. An old man coming down a fig root will stop halfway down with one foot against the wall and the other hanging loose, and conduct a visit in that position. They wear an apron of the thinnest split rush behind rather than in front, for sitting on hot surfaces. The aprons make a pleasant whisking sound when a party of them goes by. The eyes are brown, with a little clear space below the iris, as if the owner were looking up even when looking straight ahead. Their hands are small even for them. This is naturally the first thing anybody at the landing wanted to know. No, the thumbs do not fit. There is as much room again in one of the marks as the broadest of their thumbs will fill.

They have their own opinion of the marks. They think they are the prints of knees.

It is a mistake to describe them as living in the grove. They go there for the cool and to bring the children to stool, being extremely particular about the water in the trays. Their houses are on the yellow tiling in the full light of day. What at the landing is called a tile is called by them a roof. There is no contradiction in this. If you lift a tile you find a tile; but if, instead of lifting it, you go to the side of it where the lily runnel makes one of its sharp doubles, you will see a dark slot a little above water level. Presently somebody will come out sideways, shake a tablecloth into the runnel and withdraw. At certain hours the whole apparently useless length of the water is lively with these little domestic falls of crumbs. This accounts for the fishes having one eye out.

Inside, the ceilings are so low that the little people themselves sit down to comb their hair. They are fond of long rooms. A family will have a room as long as a ropewalk, perfectly clean, with a fine red line painted shoulder-high on either wall. The furniture is disposed at intervals along it according to the time of day it is wanted. At the light end there are a washbasin and a stone for rubbing up the yellow stuff they put on sore places; a little way on, the breakfast mat; then the loom or the block for cutting leather; then a great deal of quite empty room in which the family passes the heat of the day, moving by insensible degrees away from the light. At the far end there are hammocks, and beyond the hammocks a small bright door giving on another bend of the same runnel. In the morning they walk home outside. A child who has fallen asleep over his breakfast is said to have gone the long way round.

They have less use than we have for words denoting possession. They have plenty of words for asking leave. A woman about to take a fig from a bough on which somebody else is sitting will go through quite a ceremonial exchange with him, though he has no more title to the fig than to the branch under him. What she is asking is whether he is looking at it. It is very bad manners to oblige another person to watch the place where a thing has been. At dinner, when a good dish goes round, everybody looks politely down at his apron while his neighbour helps himself. Then they all look up and admire the dish again. Their children find this difficult. Nothing is more enticing to a child than a space with something due in it.

There is a game played by two little girls at the lily, one standing on either side of the stalk. One girl catches a frog and holds it, quite gently, to her cheek. The other girl watches the green place on the stalk. They stay like that until something so interesting happens elsewhere that the watcher turns her head. Of course the girl with the frog does her best to hasten this. She is allowed to tell lies. There is a fish walking on the tiles, she says. Your aunt is standing behind you with all her teeth out. Sometimes they are there an unconscionable time. Once the woman at the landing saw the girl with the frog begin to cry quietly from fatigue, and the other, without moving her eyes, put a hand across the stalk to be held. It is a serious game. One of the things it teaches is how long it is possible to keep a place for something one loves.

For they do die. This surprises visitors, who have generally got hold of the wrong end of the business with the figs. The dead are washed at the light end of the house and taken out by the bright door. A little slope of tile goes down to the water there, and on this the dead person is laid, with the heels just wet. The family sits along the opposite bank. Nobody obliges them to turn their heads. Friends come with fans and food; at night the folds of the fans are full of little dull lamps. It may be morning, or the morning after, before the last of the family has looked away. When they look back there is only the slope of yellow tile, with two wet places on it where the heels were. They go home then. It is considered kind to go with them and sit for a while in the long room, wherever they have got to in their day.

There is one old woman among them who goes up to the landing pretty regularly. She is interested in the basket man, or rather in his waste. He trims the ends of his rushes with a sweep of the knife against his knee, and she gathers the little curls that fall. These she moistens in her mouth and presses together to make whistles. They are not very good whistles. One will give a note so high that only the bats attend to it; another makes a damp puff and unrolls. She takes as much pleasure in the failures as in those that sound. She has a notion that before the country was laid out there must have been a time when people made things which had never been there before, and that the sound of a whistle which goes wrong cannot very well have been there before. She has explained this to the basket man a good many times. He gives her his attention, looking down courteously at his knee while she takes the curls.

In the late afternoon she walks back across the empty tiling, trying them one by one. From the bench you cannot hear her, but you can follow her progress very well. A thin reddish seam opens along the edge of a fan leaf, folds itself shut, and a little farther on another opens. Under the rims a succession of bright eyes comes level with the water. Finally, at a turn of the runnel where nothing whatever appeared to be, a door opens and a great many small people put their heads out to hear what she has made.
