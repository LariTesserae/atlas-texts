The woman at number eight knows the fox by its feet. From the chair where she shells the currants she can see under the switch cabinet, but not behind it. Some afternoons there are two black feet showing, laid one over the other as neatly as the spare pair of tongs beside her stove. If a fly settles on one, the toes spread. She has tried to show this to people. By the time she has got them to sit down, which is necessary if they are to see anything at all, there is just the fly, walking about on the stone.

She is often credited with feeding the fox. In fact it is hard to say who feeds anybody in the yard. A boy at one of the doors throws out the scab from the top of his rice pudding; it catches on a butterbur leaf and stays there all afternoon. By evening the underside is furred with little brown slugs. A blackbird comes along the leaf stalk, which sinks under its weight, takes a slug and tips the rest of the arrangement onto the ground. Next morning the boy can see plainly that his pudding has been eaten, and puts out a bit of sausage.

There are things that live in a much smaller yard. In the pocket of mortar beside the saw cut, each bee has a particular hole to come back to. There may be three of them hanging in front of the same six inches of wall, bright with dust below the belly, making those little sideways corrections that look from a distance like impatience. One goes in; another lands a thumb’s breadth away and walks into a hole that was quite invisible till she put her head in it. At the foot of the wall a fine powder accumulates. Children have drawn doors in it with matchsticks, complete with knockers and numbers. The bees pay no attention to these improvements.

A man who works in the cold rooms takes his break on the iron steps. He sits halfway up, in the strip of shade cast by Bell Street, and eats an orange with a small folding knife. Down there the fans shake the contents of his shirt pocket against his chest. He can tell without looking up when the slower bank cuts in. Once, on a Sunday, he came down to fetch a jacket he had left at work and heard a quite extraordinary ripping noise. It was a young blackbird standing on a leaf, trying to pull a snail off it. He stood a little longer with his hand on the rail. From one of the kitchens came the knock of an egg against the rim of a bowl, and the blackbird went under.

The people at the back doors have the habit of visitors to a sickroom of looking in even when there is nothing in particular to be done. A woman comes out with a colander still tucked into her elbow. A child in one sock opens the door just wide enough for his face. They look at the white strip the sun has reached, or at the currants, or at a wet print on the stone beside the pipe. They do not see the same things. For nearly a week there was supposed to be a second fox, a very pale one, until the man from the cold rooms saw the usual animal come out from under the steps and shake the limestone dust off its back.

Nobody has rubbed out the red survey mark. It is too high to reach without bringing out a chair, and afterwards there would still be a pink place. In summer the woman at eight brings her chair out for a different purpose. She sets it beside the currant bush and picks what she can reach sitting down, dropping the good fruit into a pan and the split fruit between her shoes. Presently there is a blackbird there. It comes so close that she can see a currant pip stuck to the side of its bill. Behind her the fans labour up to their deep note and fall away; in front of her the bird puts one foot on the toe of her slipper to reach a berry that has rolled into the fold of her skirt. She goes on picking on the other side of the bush.
