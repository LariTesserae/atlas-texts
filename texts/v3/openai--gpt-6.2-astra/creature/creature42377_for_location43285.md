After the mouse has gone over, the tortoise stays where it is. A little dust has come off on the mouse's belly. There is a clean streak across two of the plates of the shell, the color of horn, which shows how pale the rest has got. Presently one of the forelegs comes out from under the rim, then the head. The tortoise takes a mouthful of purslane without otherwise altering its position.

It is not a large animal. A good deal of what looks like its bulk is the clearance under the shell; when it gets up on all four feet there is room for daylight under it, and a beetle, overtaken in one of the lanes, can let the whole thing go by overhead. It feeds a little on either hand as it walks. There is water in the thick leaves, enough that a bead stands for a moment on the cut end of a stalk. Sometimes it misses a leaf in among the grass stems and has to back up to get at it. It backs with precisely the same gravity with which it goes ahead.

There are places on the plain where the grass grows in little dark green fans. Most of these are places where a tortoise has lain up through the heat, several days running, in the lee of the same mound. The soil is richer there. A few of the purslane seeds go through the animal entire and come up together beyond the reach of the parent stem. It would be possible to follow the travels of a tortoise by these small gardens if there were not so many tortoises, and if some of the gardens did not long outlast their makers.

The mouse, meanwhile, has got to the end of the lane. It sits up and draws a grass head down, working from the tip toward the stem with such small rapid motions that the head seems to be passing through its paws under its own power. The husks drop straight down between its feet. When it lets go, the bare stem stands higher than its neighbors. A patch of these thin uprights is a better indication of where to look for a mouse than a hole is. Holes change tenants. Half the holes that look right for a mouse have a lizard in them, its chin fitting the sill, its eyes open well after the light in which it could have seen anything has gone.

By then the tortoise has found a place for the night. It pushes its face into the loose skirt of a tussock until the dry blades part over the top of its head. The feet stay out a little longer. At the touch of a beetle one hind foot draws in, slowly enough for the beetle to walk off it onto the earth. Underneath, quite close to the belly plate, a file of termites is taking the dead stems away from the foot of the tussock. All night they work at the bed without waking the sleeper. In the morning, when the tortoise walks out, a little of the bed goes with it, caught in the folds above its heels, and a little has gone down into the dark.
