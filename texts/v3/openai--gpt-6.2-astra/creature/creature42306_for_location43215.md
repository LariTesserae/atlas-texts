The feeler comes out clean except for a little cuff of dust at the elbow. What looks, on the inward stroke, like a single dark joint is a pair of forefeet hooked together. The animal advances them edgewise and brings them home flat. Underneath, they carry two fringes of hair so fine that a smoke-grey speck will sometimes break loose from the load and turn out to be walking. The beans pay no attention to the feet going out. It is when the elbow straightens that they take a step back.

Only the feet and a little of the head will fit under the copper. The rest of the animal lies in a shallow fault that runs away from the road at an angle, so that anyone looking straight up the swept strip sees right past it. A milk-white thing, no longer than three beans laid end to end; wide in front, and behind that hardly anything but folds. When it draws its feet in, a pair of short legs at its mouth begin to work over the fringes. It eats some of the catch. The rest goes along under its belly in a steady procession of little parcels, each carried by something nearly the colour of the stone.

These are its young. They have the hooked feet from the first, but no fringes yet. Two together can just manage a husk. One backs, one pushes, and when they come to an obstruction both push, sometimes for a considerable time. Toward the rear of the fault, where the old stone rises to meet the sill, there is a bank made of what they have brought. It is a loose affair. In a good side light you can see black passages through it and, here and there, the blunt shining ends of bean legs. The legs move. Most of what gets carried up there is alive.

On the first weighing after a long interval the use of the bank becomes plain. The wick bracket gives its note; the young drop what they are doing and get six feet into the nearest thing that will hold them. The white animal uncouples its forefeet. One goes down each side of the bank. For the short time the tread is falling, the whole structure swells toward the road, arrested and held together by those two hairy props. The wind goes through it. Out on the quartzite little balls of graphite race one another to the screw; in the bank the meal stirs, the husks lift and settle, and nobody is carried so much as the length of a leg. At the double knock the forefeet come away. For some moments afterward small animals back out into the light, grooming themselves. Among them are nearly always a few beans, their grey backs powdered white with scraps of cotton. Their mothers are still at the wick.

There is a way of seeing the white animal entire. In cold weather the wire contracts enough to lift one shoulder of the bend off the rock. Then, once in a great while, an empty bean shell comes bowling down the road and sticks fast between the two. The feeler tries it from several directions. Presently the white head follows; the forefeet part company and get a purchase on opposite lips of the shell. As the animal heaves, all the folds behind its head draw out, pink in the creases, and for an instant it is a handsome long creature with a waist and a tail. The young, taken by surprise, are left standing in two exact rows along the fault, each with its parcel raised to meet a belly that is no longer there.

The shell comes free all at once. Usually it is lost to the west. Once one lodged on the back of a passing bean, open side down, and rode almost to the wick before the bean went under a low place and scraped it off. By then the white animal was back in its fault. The little carriers had closed up the gap. There was a mark across the road where the rim had dragged, and for the rest of the afternoon the feet came out a little farther on that side, down into the scratch and up the other lip, taking nothing at all on some of the strokes.
