The first earthworm anyone saw in the ward was going up a wall. It had come out of a joint between the bricks in the laundry cellar, a long pink worm with a clay-colored tip. By the time the woman folding sheets saw it, it had got as high as the hot-water tap. She took it down on the back of a washing ticket. There was a discussion about where to put it. The blue clay had set so hard that the worm lay on top of it making and unmaking a letter. In the end she carried it upstairs to her box of parsley.

This was Nella, who lived on the landing level with the school cloakroom. She had the only parsley that did much good. She grew it in soil from her sister’s garden on the mainland, and every caller who stayed to supper was sent home with some, twisted up in the damp corner of a napkin. Soon after she put the worm in the box she began finding others in it. She would take one out for any child who asked, provided the child had first run cold water over its hands. The children liked the instant when the worm, which had hung between her fingers like a piece of wet string, got its belly down on a palm and began to pull.

Her grandson Emil was rather old for this. He wanted to know how it had got to the tap in the first place. The joint was above the level the clay had risen to, and on the other side of the wall was a cupboard full of clean overalls. He went into the cupboard with a torch. There was a worm there too, dried to a brown thread on the shelf. Behind it he found a hole no larger than the hole a drawing pin leaves in plaster. When he put his eye up to it he could smell turned earth very distinctly. He could see nothing. There was a pair of overalls on his head.

For some months he spent an inconvenient amount of time in the laundry. People learned not to put a basket down on a patch of floor without first checking that it hadn’t got Emil on it. He had discovered that on days when the clay was coming in, there were sounds from it. With your ear flat on the floor you could hear a tearing, at long intervals, like the sound of lettuce being divided for a salad. Nella heard it. The woman who looked after the boiler heard it and said it was the boiler. She had the advantage of knowing what a surprising variety of sounds the boiler made, even when quite cold and in pieces for its summer cleaning.

One Thursday, in a place where Emil had been listening, a piece of the clay lifted. It stayed up for much of the afternoon, a little roof with a dark eave. At ten past four a broad white foot came out, the toes separately visible, and swept a quantity of earth back under the roof. Emil got down beside it. There was a nose, quite bare at the end, and behind it a face so well covered in grey fur that it seemed to have neither eyes nor mouth. It was only for a second that he saw the whole mole. It rested its chin on the tile. The fur on its head had been brushed the wrong way by the edge of the clay.

Emil fetched Nella. Whenever he told this afterward people took it to mean he had gone running upstairs shouting for her. In fact he had gone as far as the door and stood there until she happened to look in his direction. He was afraid of putting a foot down hard enough to drive the mole away. Nella came in wiping a spoon on her apron, and the two of them had a good long look at the bit of floor where it had been.

There are moles under all three courts now. Their runs show up particularly well in frost, long branching lines along which the paving dries first. A few residents take an interest in them. One man used to go out before breakfast with a piece of chalk and the back of his rent book and make a map of the lines. His chief discovery was that the warmest one, and the one that branched most handsomely, was a pipe that supplied his own bath. A mole does poorly in the fresh blue clay. It needs the brown layer, shot with little white roots, that is arriving somewhat unevenly underneath. Nobody has yet seen the top of anything that grows from these roots. In places they are as thick as a wrist.

Nella no longer has the parsley box. One wet spring the wood gave at both ends and the contents went down into the court in a single oblong piece. For a while you could see precisely where it had fallen. Then some of the parsley died and some seeded, and there were separate plants in places she could never have reached to water them. There is a particularly fine one at the foot of the lowest door. It has to be held back with a shoe when a parcel comes in on the trolley.

The woman who delivers the parcels is Emil’s daughter. She knows which of the old people like to come down for themselves and which will insist they like to but would really be glad of a hand. Occasionally she brings her father something from the mainland that will interest him, a sack of bulbs somebody found under a shed, a piece of drain with the print of a fern fired into it. He still goes down to the laundry, though the floor is too far away for him to get his ear to it. On Thursdays he changes his sheets. He sits by the machine and watches the level in the sight glass, the brief white surge of a pillowcase against it. Once, while he was sitting there, a mole came out from behind the boiler and crossed the open floor. It went at a fair clip, rolling a little on the outsides of its front feet. There was time enough to see where it was going and tip the laundry basket out of its way.
