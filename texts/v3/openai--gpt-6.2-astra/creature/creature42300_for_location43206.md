There are small brown frogs in the yard. A good place to see one is at the end of the third walk, where a palm has grown up through a bench and forced two of the slats apart. If you stand on the walk you see what looks like the tip of a dead leaf caught in the fork. From the floor below you can see two little forefeet tucked under a chin. The throat goes in and out, with occasionally quite a long wait between one movement and the next. At the jar of a foot on the brick the chin comes down on the wood, and from neither side is there much to see.

They do not go about on the floor if they can help it. There is a way from the tilted bench to the overhead rack entirely by way of the palms, and along the rack itself a frog can travel the length of a house under cover of the projecting pot rims. Each toe ends in a pale disc. On glass the discs spread out, so that a frog seen from the street, if there were ever occasion to look down through a clean pane at exactly the right time, would show chiefly as a collection of small round dots, with a patch of cream belly a little way behind them. On the fronds the grip is different. The toes curl round the ribs. A frond can be let go by a man who has been holding it out of his way and swing back through a considerable arc without dislodging a frog no bigger than the last joint of his thumb.

Most of their business takes place after the wired squares overhead have gone grey. There are gnats then on the undersides of the shelves and very small white creatures that jump when a scale of bark is disturbed. A frog will sit for a quarter of an hour looking along the edge of a slat on which nothing whatever appears to be moving. Then it takes one abrupt step forward and shuts its eyes to swallow. It resumes its watch from the new position. Over the course of a night it may work its way round three sides of a pot like this. In the morning there is a clean place in the green dust where its belly has rested, already losing its outline under the drip.

The attendant first came across them when there was trouble with the return pipe and both pumps had to be stopped. He had been down there half an hour before it occurred to him that the little double tick he kept hearing could not, after all, be coming from a bearing. There was one quite close to his ear, in a rolled banana leaf, and another a long way off. He called the man who was working at the tank to come and listen. By the time the man had wiped his hands and come up the steps the near one had stopped; the far one went on, but the man said that was water, and there was nothing in particular the attendant could say to the contrary. Since then he has seen quite a number. He takes hold of the frond on the stairs rather lower down than he used to.

Their eggs are seldom seen. They lay them out of the water, in the little damp chambers that remain when the lower part of a leaf stalk comes loose from the stem but has not yet fallen. In one such chamber there may be half a dozen clear beads and a frog pressed in beside them, its back fitting the green curve rather closely. The young come out with legs already, astonishingly complete except for their size. For the first few days the space between two slats is a serious break in the country. They go the long way round, following the wood to the iron frame and the frame back to the next strip of wood. Later they take the gap in a hop. There are usually several sizes to be found on the tilted bench, where one end is now so far down in the leaf mould that the smallest can walk aboard.

On wet afternoons the hollow noise behind the wall brings them out a little early. While there is still light under the glass they begin to appear at the mouths of the rolled leaves, one blunt snout and then another, facing out into the walks. For once it is possible to get some idea of how many there are. The attendant, standing quite still beside a stopcock, once counted eleven without turning his head. Then a drop lying in the trough of a banana leaf ran down to the point and fell. The leaf rose on being relieved of the weight, uncovering, along the rib of the leaf beneath it, four more.
