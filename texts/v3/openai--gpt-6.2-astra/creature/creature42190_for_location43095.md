The woman at the wool table lives directly behind it. Her front door is in the lane, but when she goes home for her dinner she lifts the canvas at the back of the shop and steps over a sill into her kitchen. Her name is Ada. She has worked at the table so long that she can tell the buttons from the two faces of a mattress apart by touch. The ones from underneath generally have a little clean pit in the middle where the thread has sheltered them; the ones from the sleeping side have been rubbed quite smooth. She puts them all through the partition just the same. It is a distinction for which she has never found a use.

Her son Len sleeps in the room nearest the washhouse. He is eleven and spends a good part of the day aboveground. When he comes back from school his hair smells different from hers. On wet days he will put his head down for her to smell before he takes off his satchel. The smell goes very quickly. Rain beads brought down on the shoulders of a coat will sometimes make it as far as the hook, but not much farther.

Len was the one who found out that the fig had roots outside its pot. During the last repair to the main, the plumber asked him to shift the basin the breadth of a tile. It wouldn't go. He got down with his cheek against the warm floor and saw a skin-colored cord running out of the hole in the bottom and along the joint between two tiles. Farther on it separated into threads so fine that he lost them against the mortar. The plumber had his new elbow in by then. Rather than tear the root they hung a short length of chain from the pipe. In the mornings the water gathers at the lowest link and falls into the old place. The chain is brass except for this last link, which is green.

There is some disagreement about who planted the fig. Three people remember the man who first set the basin there, a washman with one stiff leg, but setting a basin under a leak and planting a tree are different things. His daughter, who still comes down on Thursdays to have her sheets dried, says that if he had meant to grow a fig he would at least have given it earth. It has never borne a fig. It once put out a leaf so large that the washhouse women marked its spread on the wall in blue pencil. The marks are there still, well above the present crown. You might suppose, from the attention paid it, that everyone in the yard was fond of the tree. The man who sweeps has to get the blue lint out from under all seven leaves with his fingers and would gladly see it go.

On most days there are more people working in Copper Yard than living in it. A customer coming down with a ticket in her hand might take them for a single family. They look after one another's kettles and keep up conversations through holes no bigger than a hand. Yet Ada has never seen the front room of the man who works the other side of her partition. For sixteen years she has passed him buttons. She knows the white crescent of an old cut across the ball of his thumb and the rather elegant way he takes a handful, letting the buttons run into the cup of his fingers before he closes them. If they meet up in the street they generally say good morning and walk on.

The people who stay when the iron leaves come down have several ways of passing the hot interval before the shaft begins to draw. Two sisters from the end dwelling mend stockings, one with her back to her door lamp and one facing it; they change chairs when the one facing it can no longer see. Len plays a game with an old brass cupboard check, sending it rolling up the lane on its edge. The trick is to give it just enough turn to bring it round the leg of Ada's chair and back to his hand. Nobody minds the noise until it falls flat. Then, from somewhere behind a door, somebody says, “Len.”

Ada mostly sits still. All day she has had white wool folding toward her, more of it arriving as fast as her hands can clear the table. For a little while after work, if she looks at a brick or a square of paving, it appears to move slowly away from her. She waits for this to wear off. Presently Len comes to stand between her knees and she picks the little dags of wool from his school jersey, where they have got to him by way of her apron. The first cool air is scarcely a breeze. It reaches the ankles before the face. One sister pushes a stocking down to feel it against her bare leg, and the other, a minute later, does the same.
