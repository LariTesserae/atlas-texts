The woman with the gutter pole keeps a saucer under the short leg of her kitchen dresser. In the saucer is a little pink grit, which she tips into the fire of a Sunday when she blacks the range. It comes out of the leg. A creature lives in there which has been eating the dresser since her mother was a girl. It is welcome to the wood as long as it leaves enough round the outside to hold up the plates. She has a wedge ready in the knife drawer against the day it does not.

Her name is Linnet. She does the high windows of the laundry as well as the gutters, and knows rather more of what goes on above the level of a man’s head than most. The black birds, for instance, close up altogether differently from other birds when they come in to roost. They hang by their feet under the eaves and take in all that width of wing a fold at a time, till there is a tidy row of objects about the size and colour of mussels above the laundry door. Linnet has had one in her hand. It was chiefly feet. The toes went on opening after she thought they had quite finished, and the last joint of each was as fine as the end of a darning thread. She set it on the window glass. It went up three inches, slipped back two, and went up three more, with the air of somebody who has been promised the whole afternoon for the job.

When the fans are forming in the ponds, the birds go out singly to eat. What they take off the fans is not the mineral but a nearly colourless fly coming out of its wet clothes. On some evenings the empty clothes blow into town in such quantities that a person who has been out for a walk must have the back of his collar brushed before he can sit down to tea. They are very handsome under a glass. The school has a slide of one, mounted by an old boy who went down to the river country and made a good living there testing milk.

It is a mark of having been born up here that one speaks of going down, without further particulars, much as elsewhere one speaks of going abroad. Linnet has a sister down. She sends up pears with paper twisted round their stalks and asks for news of the little beasts that come into the washhouse. She remembers them as white. They are in fact the grey of a stocking sole, and much the same shape until they stand up. Then they have four short legs and a head with a blunt nose made for getting under things. A lift of that nose will turn back a square foot of moss. They go along underneath, buttoning the moss up again behind them with their backs. In the washhouse they make for the pile of damp flannel, which has the right give to it but is otherwise a disappointment. Linnet can usually lift a whole one out on a soap shovel. Its passengers fall off on the way to the door: minute yellow beetles, each of which sets off at once in a different direction across the tiles.

She once took the trouble to follow one of the beasts through the hedge, thinking it might be the maker of the hoofprint. Where it crossed a bare place it left five dots and the mark of a heel like the palm of a very small hand. She was rather pleased. There would have been something flat about finding the owner of that foot in a pile of washing.

The people who tend the chains keep a different account of the country. They have little to do with the garden creatures; a shift on the lip begins before the first windows are unshuttered and ends when the town is quite shut in again. They know a long green grub that hangs in place of a link, holding the link above in its mouth and the one below between its hind feet. It gives the chain the slack it needs to hang straight. As the grub fattens, the chain gets a little longer. There is a boy on each gang whose job it is to raise the lowest loops clear of the channel, and he will tell you, if asked, that it is the grubs and not the iron that stretch. There have been hot arguments over this in the shed. No one has yet been willing to keep a chain free of grubs for a season to settle it. It is trying work to get one to let go, and the water must be had in the meantime.

The boy on Linnet’s stretch is her sister’s eldest, sent up for his chest. He has grown wide through the shoulders and remains a poor colour. Going home from work he likes to lie with one ear on the school stone while there is still a warmth in it from the day. The note that can be heard at the windows is not, he says, what you hear with your head down. There is a great deal of ordinary noise in there. You can hear a pail put down in somebody’s yard, and after a while the same pail a long way off, though much enlarged, as if a man at a distance had set down a tank. There is also a going about, too slow to be footsteps and with intervals in it long enough to get up and have your supper. He has marked the places where he hears it best in soft pencil on the sandpit side of the rib. The infants rub the marks out with their thumbs. They believe he is keeping count of the teeth.

In winter, when there is less cloud and the moss draws up at the edges, Linnet goes round at dusk with a small can, wetting it down where it crosses the lane. She does not water the whole patch. She keeps a covered way between her yard and the hedge of the empty house opposite, a distance of perhaps eleven paces, which holds for two days after wetting. Quite a little traffic passes that way. At the near end the moss rises, a travelling fullness runs through it, and then it settles flat, with a faint working of the red tips as they fit themselves back among one another. Sometimes two such disturbances meet. There is a pause; one backs the whole length of the crossing; both go on. She can see it very well from her kitchen while she is getting the plates down. She has never been able to tell, in all the years she has watched, which has the right of way.
