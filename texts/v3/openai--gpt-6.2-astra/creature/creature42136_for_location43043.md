The animal under the nursery spends a good deal of its life as a crease in the underside of a fern. There are half a dozen creases on some of the larger fronds. They run beside the ribs and are a little plumper at the end nearest the stalk. If the frond is lifted they stay where they are. It is only by lifting one that has begun to go yellow that you see the difference between the green of the leaf, which is leaving it, and the green of the crease, which has a faint brown dotted line down its middle.

It is a slug with a remarkably thin shell. The shell covers about a third of its back and has something of the appearance of a blister in varnish. Through it you can see a heart going quite fast in an animal otherwise at rest. The brown dots are in the gut. It eats the fine felt that grows on the stalks just where they enter the earth, and its excursions out along the fronds are not in search of food. On a warm dry day upstairs, when watering has been going on in the nursery, little beads break out at the notches of the leaves below. A slug will lie with its mouth at one of these notches and take the bead as it forms. There is no occasion for it to go down to the clay to drink.

The young ones are quite white. A dozen of them together, drawn in tight under a flake of bark in the potting soil, look like the cut end of a little piece of household flex, the wires just showing in the sheath. They spread out as they grow. In a well-established bundle there are animals all the way from the neck, where it is positively cold on a winter morning, to the last loose twist of root below the foliage. They do not occur on the side facing the main. Even on the narrow stocking, where the difference between one side and the other is the breadth of two fingers, this holds good.

Once in a while one comes down. It comes with a leaf, and the leaf comes down the usual way, sailing a little out to one side and then slipping back the other way, not turning over. For some time after it lands there is nothing to see. Then at one of the notches a pair of short horns come out over the edge, followed by another pair so small that the four together might be the frayed end of a stitch. The slug goes the length of the leaf underneath. At the stalk, where there ought to be the familiar rising way into the dark, there is a clean break and an inch or so of air between the end of the stalk and the ground.

Those that come down near the wide stocking have a fair chance. The clay there is cool for a considerable distance out from the ruff. A slug goes across it with the shell held well up, and leaves behind it a track which, in a light held low, looks like a mark made with a rubber on dull paper. Some tracks run in rather grand curves, double back, intersect themselves, and finish under the very leaf they started from. Others go neatly in under the ruff. By the time the clay is dry there is no telling which is which.

There is another little settlement on the detached steps. It is in the joint between the bottom step and the next one up, at the back, where a handful of earth has lodged and a single fern has managed to take. The fern has only three fronds and never gets much bigger. In spring you can sit on the top step, reach down without getting up, and turn the newest frond over on the tip of a finger. The whole colony will be there, six or seven clear shells on a leaf hardly wider than a watch strap. Put it back and there is the considerable hollow again, the long brown pipe and the brick house at the far end of it, and nothing stirring anywhere.
