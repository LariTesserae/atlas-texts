The crab on the new island has hold of it. One of its claws is sunk nearly to the hinge in the pink, and at intervals it lifts the claw, brings up a piece about the size of a bean, and eats it. Each time it does this the lizards shut their eyes again. They are attending to the pillar. The island is of no consequence to a lizard.

To a potter it is the better part of a winter’s work come up in the wrong place. She knows it by the pale blue crock sticking out of one end. In the wet months a woman broke that crock and laid the pieces over a bed of fine clay to keep the shrimp from walking on it. Something has got under the bed and lifted the whole of it, shell gravel, crock and all. It is proceeding across a pan of quiet water at about the rate a tongue goes across a sore tooth. The crab has found a pocket of grubs in it and is having a very good evening.

The thing underneath is called a sole, though it has no bones. A small one will fit exactly under a wet hand, and there is a temptation to close the hand on it which almost everybody yields to once. The whole of its lower surface takes hold. You bring your hand back with nothing on it and five little disks of skin missing, white at first, then filling up red. The soles in the water jars are removed with a twist of wool. They take hold of the wool and you can wind them up in it without doing any damage to either party.

A sole begins life swimming. There are nights when a lamp set on the rim of a spring shows hundreds of little pairs of oars going round the reflection of the flame, and nothing visible between the oars. By morning there will be a clear spot underneath each one on the grey skin of the spring. Its oars have stuck and spread into lips. It is from this time forward rather a different animal at either end. The front feels its way about with two loose corners which can go quite independently, one investigating a stone while the other turns up and tastes the water. The back lies still until the front has found something worth moving for. Then a gather goes along it, like the gather a woman puts into a length of stuff too wide for her loom to finish. An arm’s length of sole passes under its own back edge. You can be watching closely and miss the movement altogether.

The larger ones take their gardens with them. There is a crumbly deposit from the spring water on the upper skin; things lodge there. The little shell openers do well in it, and the shrimp leave their cast coats, and presently there is enough of a bottom for grubs. A sole which has spent a year or two pressed up under a ledge may bring the ledge away. This is how the potter lost her clay. There was no mark where it had gone, only a clean long shelf with a peculiar polish on the underside.

It is possible to live down here a long time without knowing about the soles. The woman at the last kiln lived here from her first tooth to her first grey hair before she saw one turned over. She had gone out after a run to collect her drying racks. A rack was still travelling, very slowly, uphill. When she tried to pull it free it tipped the animal off a lip of rock. There was a sound like the last of a bath going out through a narrow pipe. What she saw underneath was a yellow-white field of mouths, each shut tight on a different little possession: a pebble, a thread, a crab’s empty wrist, a fragment of red jar with the glaze still bright. As she watched, the mouths let go in succession and turned their wet rims toward the ground. She put her shoulder to the rack and helped it over. For years afterward she would turn up her sound ear to listen whenever she crossed what appeared to be an unusually clean bit of floor.

By now the knock has come a second time. The woman takes the cakes of shrimp off the low end of the drying rack. Her boy is on the sleeping platform, getting the thornwood up beside the blankets, a job he can do in his sleep and has done in something very like it. They leave a cheap saucer on the bottom step. In the morning the height of the salt mark on the saucer will tell them whether it is worth going to look for the little kiln furniture they have left out to cool. Beyond the knob of rock they can already hear the buttons spilling.

Out on the pan the sole has stopped. Both its loose corners have risen out of the water. They hang there, as thin as the skin in a cracked egg, turned toward the coming run. The crab digs out another bean. Then the front of water arrives, the corners buckle under, and for a moment the island stands up steeply enough to show the blue crock entire. The crab goes off backward with the grub still in its claw.

In the morning the pan is dry again. The boy has the luck to find his mother’s clay bed not twenty paces from the kiln, drawn up whole on a slope of shell. He fetches her to see before he touches it. Together they lift the pieces of the crock and cut what they need in heavy square cakes. The underside of the last cake has two fine curled impressions in it where the animal felt its way out in the dark. The boy puts the cake aside for the bottoms of drinking jars. He likes there to be something to see when he gets to the end of the water.
