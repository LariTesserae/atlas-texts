At the foot of the square island a bank vole lives in one of the cracks that has not quite closed. There are two ways in, one through the heather on top of the fallen slice and one at floor level under a curl of birch bark. The upper one is the one to watch. First a spray of heather tips a little, although the sprays on either side of it are still. Then for a while there is a small face among the bells. It is shorter than a mouse’s face, full across the cheeks. A drop hanging from a bell wets the fur between the eyes and stays there when the face goes away.

The crack goes back about the length of a man’s arm. At its blind end the two walls are held apart by a stone that must have been in the peat before the cutting. Here the vole has brought shreds of dry sedge. From outside it looks a poor choice of bedding. The sheaths are so hard that if you wade through a patch of them in your shirtsleeves they bring up fine white scratches on the arms. The vole takes the inside of a sheath. It holds the butt down with its forefeet and works backwards, stripping out a ribbon about the width and colour of a shaving off a pencil. A good many journeys have gone into lining a space in which it fits with its nose against its flank.

It has no occasion to cross the open floor. Most of what it wants is on the fallen slices. A berry on the oldest slice has been eaten from below, so that seen from the stair it would still be a whole red berry, if anyone could see a berry from the stair. Underneath there is only skin and a little damp yellow pulp. There are tooth marks in the bark of the birch roots and a place where the vole comes to wash, a low fork in a heather stem polished on the near side only. To get from one of these places to the next it keeps its back in contact with something nearly all the way. A space which looks from above like a hand’s breadth of uninterrupted moss has a passage through it; the reddish fur goes in at one end and for a moment three of the gold moss heads move in succession. 

Once in the afternoon a weasel comes off the middle terrace. It is a small one, still brown along the spine, and where the ground is broken it makes considerable use of its length, putting its forefeet up on a ledge and looking well over it before the hind feet leave the ledge below. In the cutter track it goes much faster. There is nothing hurried about the vole at first. It is sitting up with a seed between its paws. Then the paws are empty and there is a seed lying on the moss with a pale nick in it. The weasel examines the bark at the lower entrance. Its head goes under as far as the ears, comes out powdered black, goes under again. All the while, about a yard higher up, a single heather spray is tipped aside and held there. The compressor houses make exactly the sound they have been making since morning.

By the time the maintenance man comes back to the stair the weasel is out on the floor, going in and out of the ear-deep track the hare uses. The vole has not gone back for the seed. It has come down instead to the fresh face of the island, where a fringe of fine roots hangs free of the cut. It bites one off, gathers the loose end in against its chest and draws the rest toward it, paw over paw, until the last inch comes whipping up off the peat. For several seconds in the failing light there is quite a conspicuous bit of pale stitching travelling along the dark join between two slices. Then the root catches on a twig. The vole comes back out, takes hold of it nearer the middle and gets it all indoors.
