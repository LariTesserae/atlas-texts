At the point where the frond touches there is generally a notch in the green, deep enough to admit the head of a mite. The mite is equipped to make the notch itself. It has a pair of little shears which work sideways. It cuts through the skin of the frond and drinks, getting rather darker about the middle as it does so. At the end of a long dry spell there may be half a dozen at the same notch, the later arrivals standing with their forefeet on the backs of the others. They are very civil about moving up. On the underside of the frond, visible through the green if one lies down, is the single gray hook of the animal that holds it against the stone.

One can spend a summer in the hollow without seeing much more of this animal than the hook. It is the color of a fern stalk on one side and the color of the floor on the other. It lies on its side. What appears to be a slight thickening of the lowest stalk is its abdomen; its head, which is soft as the end of a soaked match, it keeps tucked well back in the cool of the bed. There are four legs on which it gets about, and the fifth, much longer, ends in the hook. The leg has an elbow that goes over center like the stay on a folding table. Once set, it will hold a frond down for days at no expense to the animal. A dead one sometimes keeps a frond down until the joint rots.

It takes every fourth or fifth mite on the way back. There is no great pounce. A little pink mouth travels along the side of the stalk, closes on the mite, and goes back the way it came. The ones that get past it leave full of fern juice and presently lay their eggs among the roots of the glossy thicket. Where there is no hook to hold a frond down there are very few mites; where there are no mites the hooked animals drag the fronds down just the same. In some years the stone is spotted red all around the bed and in some years one sees a frond held down from April into June with nothing walking on it but a succession of shadows.

It is the young that are difficult to account for. There are none in the fern bed. If the stone is warm enough the adults lay about a dozen eggs apiece, each in its own small envelope of gray silk, and they do not lay them where they live. A gravid female is the only one likely to be seen crossing the floor. She goes at night, drawing the long leg along behind her with an occasional slight catch at one of the almonds. In cold moonlight the abdomen looks quite green. She makes for the plant with the cupped leaves.

Some of the brown waterlines in those cups will come away on a wet finger. Others are fastened all round with silk. Close up these prove to be a row of cases made of brown grit, with a little round head plugging one end of each case. What the female does is to hang her envelopes a little below the last waterline. They can hang dry a long time. Once wetted they split down the seam and let out something like an eyelash, which swims. For a whole shower, and perhaps two days after, each full leaf has its own company of these lashes, flicking end over end through a broth of pollen and mineral dust. As the water goes they work their way around its diminishing edge, laying silk and picking up grit, until at last they are plastered against the wall of the cup in their cases. A brown line therefore preserves the level of the water on the day the young were old enough to stop swimming, which is not necessarily the level to which the rain filled the leaf. There is one leaf with four lines in it. In the topmost line all the heads are still in place.

They need to be wet a second time to get out. This time what comes out is a pale, bustling little creature with perfectly good walking legs and a fifth so long and fine that the wind can bowl it along by that leg alone. Their first concern seems to be to get under something. The ones in the lower leaves scramble over the rims and drop straight down into the damp about the stem. Those in the high leaves have a considerable expanse of stone to cross. A black lizard can pick up twenty in a minute, going among them with its nose down, quite deaf for the time being to the commotion underfoot.

There is an odd sight to be had after rain in the loose corner of the rectangle. The little walkers reach it while it is still dry. In that corner alone there are ferns small enough for one of them to deal with. A walker climbs a tip, sets the hook and lets its weight bring the tip down. Then the delayed rain begins. The fern folds; the elbow, not yet hard enough to lock, gives way, and fern and animal go flat together under a drop as big as a cobblestone would be to a man. By the time the shrimp come up the gutter the water has gone clear enough to see a dozen of these pale elbows sticking up. When the weight comes off, the tips open slowly, bringing the young up with them. One or two are missing. A shrimp will have a go at anything soft.

By evening the new little paths into the fern are already in use. It is possible to watch a mite walk over a hooked leg still so transparent that the red of its belly shows through onto the stone. The young one has its mouth stretched out ready, but a mite that has had a proper drink is nearly twice the width of that mouth. It has to let it go. For the first week or so it lives on what oozes from the notch after the mite has taken its shears away.
