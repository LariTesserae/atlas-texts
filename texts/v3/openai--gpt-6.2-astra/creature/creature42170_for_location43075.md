When the cloud lifts, the herd generally turns out to be much smaller than it sounded. There are five or six animals standing well apart, with a great deal of unbitten country between them. The space is on account of the wood. The weight of two grown cows on the same buried span will set water rising round their ankles. They dislike this, not so much for the wet as for the inconvenience of having to pull each foot out with a separate little gulp. Where the going is doubtful they spread out. A calf has very little of this difficulty, but goes in its mother's prints all the same. There is a time in its first summer when it has grown too long to stand comfortably in four of them and is not quite long enough to skip one. You can pick out these calves a great way off by their peculiar indignant hop.

The beasts have handsome mouths. The upper lip is divided and the two halves can work at different things. A cow will hold down a spray with one half while with the other she runs the stalk clean of leaves; the freed stalk flies up and sometimes strikes her smartly under the eye. Her eyes close in anticipation. This gives her much of the time the expression of a creature tasting something quite exquisite. The colour called dun at a distance is, close to, a fine peppering of dark and light hairs, with a stripe of almost white hair running down the back and disappearing between the two halves of the tail. In cloud, a following animal can see this stripe after it has lost sight of everything else.

There are small dependants of the herd that seldom see a whole beast. A dun hoof, coming down over a waterway, drives a plug of cold water ahead of it, with scraps of pith and the minute jointed animals that eat the pith. Under a well-used path the blunt-headed fish stand facing this delivery. They get it first from one side and then the other according to the direction the herd is taking. If the procession halts, the fish go on facing the wrong way for some minutes. Look down a hole between the roots and you may see an assembly of pale lower lips, each with a drop of air shining on it, waiting for a cow to finish scratching herself.

Deeper in the wood are creatures to which the whole affair must come as a change in the pressure of the dark. Very little is known about these. A bough that has rolled over in the night exposes on its underside long cream-coloured cases so thin that the folded legs of the occupants show through. Before the exposed wood has lost its gloss the cases are empty. There are also the makers of a sound like two wet pebbles knocked together, which ceases if you put your ear to the ground and begins again, almost under your chin, when you stand up. The newts are easier to account for. They come out in a warm rain and lie with just their blunt foreheads in the weather, the rest of each newt extending back into its tree. Some of these heads are as wide as a man's hand. Nobody seeing the small newts in the pools would think there was so much newt to come.

Of the people who made the rings there is one fairly intimate thing to be learned. On the inner face of a few of the arches is a cluster of little knobs, polished on top, in just such a position that a person getting up from the floor will take hold of one. At first they seem to have been grown for the purpose. But the knobs are of different ages, and the youngest are too small for anything but a child's fingers. It seems likelier that a family, getting up in much the same place for a long time, kept holding back the tender shoots with their hands, and that the tree, thwarted there, went on making fresh attempts a hand's breadth to the right or left. The shelf may have had a similar beginning. There need not have been a day on which somebody decided to make a shelf. There may only have been a desirable shoot, and something that wanted putting down.

The rail who has taken possession of it is a little brown hen, tall when alarmed and hardly taller than an egg when composed. She has added a sill of rush stems to the shelf, for the tree leans slightly towards the door. There are four eggs. When she leaves them she goes some distance along the wall before stepping out, and on returning she comes in by way of the roof. This is a sound precaution against the newts, which will patiently follow the smear left by a purple foot but lose interest where the smear goes straight up. The hen herself has no such scruples about a trail. Let one of the great heads appear in the rain and she will run along the line of its buried body, striking down through the mould wherever a movement promises a meal. It is the little white wood-shrimps disturbed by the newt that she is after. The two can feed in this fashion practically touching.

At night the herd lies down on the dry paths, head to tail, and the rails walk about on their backs. Loose hair comes out readily in the beak. At the occupied ring there is a tuft of it caught on the doorpost, exactly at the height of a returning hen with her mouth full. Inside, the eggs lie in a warm lining of it, as finely speckled as the beasts themselves. One end of a forgotten shelf is going on out into the marsh; the other, under the small sleeping breast, is still in daily use.
