The woman takes the wool in through a window. She has to turn sideways to get the bundle past the jamb. There is a worn place on her right hip from doing this, a patch in the skirt sewn over an older patch, and the stone at that side of the window is as smooth as a crock. On the inside the sill is a good deal too high. She puts a foot down on a stool. When she was small there was a second stool on top of it, with the legs cut short so it wouldn't tip.

There are five of them in these rooms. The smallest is asleep, not in a cradle but in a drawer on the floor; the drawer goes back into the chest when there are people to supper. The two big girls are out somewhere on the stairs. Her father sits in the light of the other window picking burrs out of yesterday's wool. The sheep come through at the upper end of the city twice a year. They bring in their fleeces the seed of a little yellow flower which nobody here has seen growing. He keeps some of the burrs in a saucer. Every spring he tries them in a box of earth, and every spring something comes up which turns out to be the russet grass.

He used to set steps. It was a good living; there was always somebody wanting a stair taken up or brought round a corner, and he liked working on a house with people in it. They would put a cup of something on the top step for him. In this kitchen you can still see, under the table, the head of the stair that went down to the room where his wife used to knead the bread. He has the long wooden board across his knees now to keep the burrs out of his trousers. One end of it is pale from flour that got worked into a split. His daughter, who does all her kneading in a bowl, has never got the quantity quite right for that board. She remembers the dough covering it like a clean pillowcase stretched tight.

The two girls have gone down to feed a fish. It is a small dun fish living in what was a cupboard in the bread room. They discovered it by letting crumbs fall through a hole left where an iron pipe was pulled out. For a long time all they saw was the crumbs going briskly sideways out of the light. Now if they lie quite still on the stones they can see the fish come out backwards, which is the only way it has of getting out of the cupboard without going through the patch of sunlight. Its tail is almost clear. They have tried at various times to tell their grandfather how big the fish is. He holds up his fingers a little farther apart each time and it takes them both hands to get his fingers back where they ought to be.

There are other creatures in the house that no one feeds on purpose. At night, on the underside of the landing, little snails with flat shells travel out along the damp and turn back at the dry stone. By lamplight their tracks look like the fine scratches on a pewter dish. A grey wagtail sleeps in the socket where the hinge of the front door used to be. Early in the morning it comes upstairs on foot, taking insects from the edge of each tread. It is generally at the kitchens by the time somebody opens a shutter. Then it flies off as if it had been caught doing something foolish. The old man, who is up before anyone else, sometimes sits with his hand on the latch until it has finished the last step.

Most of the people the girls know live within reach of the same stairs. Far across the city there are children of precisely their age who have never seen a fleece brought in. They know instead the men who cut rushes in the wide water and the woman who can bottom a chair so neatly that you can draw a stocking over the seat without catching a thread. Once she came up to do six chairs for the bathhouse. She stayed the night on this landing, in the next kitchen, and in the morning the girls showed her the fish. What she liked was the hole. At home, she told them, people had to go out of doors and get wet to look into the water. She lay down with her good black coat on and looked for so long they had to ask her to move over.

It is the older girl who notices the water coming up this evening. Not by the fish; the cupboard has gone dark. There is a little skin of chaff and old spiderweb, dry all winter in the pipe hole, and it has floated up nearly level with her eye. She puts a finger in and gets it out entire. The sisters carry it up to show their grandfather, but on the way one of them breathes on it and there is nothing much left to show. The older one goes back for another look. The younger stands by his knee with a wet finger.

“Up to there,” she says.

He feels the finger gravely to see how much of it is cold. Then he tells her where on the stairs to set the milk for the night. She is big enough to carry the small crock, if somebody will take off the lid and bring it separately so it doesn't slide about and frighten her. Her mother does that. Down they go in the remaining light, the child holding the crock well away from her stomach, taking the outside of the turn where there is room for all of her foot.
