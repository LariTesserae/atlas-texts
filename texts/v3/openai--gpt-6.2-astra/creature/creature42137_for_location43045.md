There are snails in the dark line halfway up the shelf. Most are no bigger than a rush seed. A few have grown large enough that one can make out a pale turn of shell against the black, but even these are flat, and when the water draws back they go in after it a surprisingly long way. Part of what looks like the shadow of the seam is a film of green kept cropped so close by their mouths that there is no filament long enough to stir in the drip. Under the bush a snail has come down almost to the grit. The toad's forefoot is set beside it, the four soft-ended fingers spread to take the slight forward slope.

The toad has a place to go when this dries. Behind the woody heel of the bush there is room in the soil for just its folded shape. It goes in backward, working its hind feet by turns, and what remains visible for a time is a face quite level with the ground, with a bit of pink grit sticking to one eyelid. But it will not go in tonight. Winged things have been alighting all afternoon on the hot stone overhead. With the cooling of the air some will creep under the lip, and a wing brushing the dark above the toad is enough to bring it up on all four feet.

The little frogs out in the rushes are much less solitary. One of them, calling, has worked himself so far out on a leaning stem that every inflation of his throat brings his belly into contact with the water. For a moment there are two circles widening beneath him, one from each heel. A frog a hand's breadth away keeps silent through six or seven of these calls and then puts in his little double knock. There are females there too, difficult to see even when they move. One climbs down a stem headfirst, turns, and goes the last inch into the water backward, leaving nothing to swing behind her but the brown husk of a fly.

Among the rush roots there are young fish so clear that what startles at a shadow seems to be a scattering of eyes in pairs. The bigger pale fish at the trickle have a little color on them in the evening, a yellow line along the hinge of the gill. One takes a midge from beneath, with scarcely a dimple, and returns to precisely its former station. In a fortnight there may be no water between that hollow and the rushes. For the present a fish can go the whole distance under cover of the ruffle on the surface, though it must turn on its side at one place to get over the stone.

Farther out, where there seems least to live on, a sandpiper has worked a patch of mud no larger than the spread of a hand. Not all the holes there are its own. The worms keep openings hardly wider than hairs, with a bead of wet mud at each mouth, and where the bird has missed, the bead slowly forms again. Long after the bird has gone to stand in the last of the light, the strip of shore it searched is changing in this minute way. By dark the wind has dropped. Water slips back over the punctures, lifting a loose skin of salt, and a little gray shrimp comes out of the flooded mud and swims, quite steadily, across the print of the bird's foot.
