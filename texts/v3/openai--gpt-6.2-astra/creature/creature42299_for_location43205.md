One of the blunt-faced swimmers has a white place on her chin. Most of the old ones have it. The skin there is quite sound, but the color has been rubbed out of it by the little forward shove with which she finishes a tuck. In the young this movement can be seen entire. A young one brings up an end of runner much too long for it, gets a leaf raised on the bridge of its nose, puts the end under, and then, having nothing left to hold on by, goes backwards a foot or two with the runner still in its mouth. In the old one there is scarcely a pause in the motion of swimming. The leaf lifts, the white chin goes by, and something which was loose is fast.

She is about the length of a hand, though in repose she takes up considerably less room. The tail folds along one side. Her two lower fins are soft at the tips and turn outwards, so that she can lie on them without touching the bed with her belly. There is room in that space for a slow exchange of water over the gills, even when she has gone well in under a leaf. Seen from above, she is brown, with the faint marks of three branching veins across her back. Seen coming towards one through the water, she is mostly mouth. At either corner of it is a little hanging tag, very sensitive to a draught. These tags go in first when she examines a seam. A seam which looks shut may have a perfectly good room behind it.

She has had several rooms. The first ones were small and opened to the east, since she could then depend on the current to bring her something to eat. That was before she could turn a grazer over. Now she picks one whose shell has grown a little awry and gets a purchase where the high side clears the felt. The grazer holds on. For some time the two of them remain still, the swimmer a little oblique in the water, with one fin going to keep her so. Then there is a soft pop, remarkably audible among so many small grinding sounds. The grazer's pale foot goes wandering over the roof of her mouth. Presently an empty shell wavers down and settles, cup upwards, to collect the brown dust.

In the room she has now, the draught comes from below. One runner has taken root too far down, and in thickening has parted two of the old pressed leaves. At intervals a seed comes up the crack. She knows of its approach some time before it arrives, having heard the changes in its rattle at a bend, at a place where the roof is soft, and at a hollow which gives back two knocks for one. She does not eat the seeds. The young do, while their jaws are too tender for shell; they scrape the green off them and leave the hard middle to go on its way. There are usually a few young in her room. They come in clear and go out with a distinct green ball inside each of them. She admits them until they are big enough to make the ceiling stir when they turn. After that she puts her head in the door and stays there. An excluded youngster will lie against her forehead for hours with its fins working, as though this were only a rather firm piece of water to get through.

There is a particular difficulty in taking the very small ones to the light. They have no color yet by which they can follow one another. In setting out from the room each takes hold, very lightly, of the turned tip of a fin in front of it. The old swimmer leads them along a seam, keeping low. At a certain place a root stands in the way, though the seam undoubtedly goes on under it. Here she goes up a little, and the whole string rises after her. The silver fish in the lee divide to let her through and close again, and sometimes the last of the young lets go of its fin and finds itself among hundreds of bright sides, all very near, all moving the wrong way. By the time it gets clear the others are gone. It lies down with its face to the felt. In a country so full of sound there is very little chance of hearing so small an animal. Yet when the old one brings the string back she goes round the foot of the root, quite out of her way, and the little fish lying there feels something soft turn out against its mouth.

In the court they let go. What the light does for them is easiest to see when one turns broadside. For a while there is only the shadow of the green meal on the bottom. Then a narrow shadow joins it, faint at first, running nearly to the tail. The young ones are making their first color. On some it takes the form of a fork; on others there are already the beginnings of the branching marks that will match the leaves where they settle. The old swimmer waits outside the pin of light. Every so often an eye appears in it, pale gold, of an astonishing size beside the eyes of the young, and withdraws. There is no other sign she is there.

Much later her room will have a floor too thick for seeds to come through. She will go on putting her cheek to the place. Indeed, for a long time the seeds will still be audible, running a little deeper each season, until the double knock at the hollow is the only part she knows. By then there will be a nest on top of hers and she will have another somewhere towards the west, in newer work. The animals there have to keep their tails unfolded. The felt lifts in great easy swells under them, and where it drops again there is the curious sensation, felt first in the tags at the mouth, of the water failing to come down quite as fast as the bed.

Almost to the last leaf there will be places for her. What looks from the bank like a single green welt is, at the scale of her head, full of pockets of still water, each with its under surface to be tried, its loose end. There are grazers out there with their shells worn thin on the upstream side. There are even a few young, green-bellied, holding on well back from the light. Only at the very edge does the work cease. A runner tucked there comes straight again; there is nothing beyond it to take the other end. She may try it two or three times before she leaves it. The runner lies down pointing west. A little way in from it, under the first leaf that will stay raised, her two soft fins turn out and the worn chin comes to rest.
