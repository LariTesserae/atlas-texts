When the afternoon exchange is over, and the birds have tucked their ginger toes away again, a mouse comes out of the back of the ledge. For a while there is only a nose going up and down in the mouth of the seam. Then a bird lifts itself to turn an egg, and by the time the egg is settled there is a small grey back showing between two dishes. The mouse goes on in this fashion. What cover there is keeps changing, and she makes good use of it.

She is grey only along the spine. The rest of her is the fawn color of the dust, except for a white place under the chin, usually quite wet. She has ears like the two halves of a split pea. One has a nick in it, the work of a bird. The birds will have a go at anything that comes up over the rim of a dish, though they put up quite well with a mouse going by at floor level. Sometimes a sitting bird feels her pass and raises its wing a little on that side, without interrupting its answer to its mate.

She eats things the birds have dropped. An insect picked out of cloud is apt to be all leg and wing, and a chick, in its hurry to get the whole affair down, will quite often kick it out from under its own beak. It is lost to the chick then. The mouse gets a surprising number of very fine flies this way, some still walking about with their wings stuck together. She takes a fly in her forefeet and sits back to eat it, beginning at the head. The wings collect wherever she has found it comfortable to sit. Under a flake of stone at the broad end of the lip there is a pinch of them, clear and dry and faintly blue, which trembles bodily when a bird runs overhead.

At the hollows she goes more carefully. The white mineral makes excellent going when dry and very poor going indeed an inch farther on. She drinks lying down, with her hind feet well back in the dust. Her chin trails in the water. There is a cricket in the low place of the collar, holding a drowned gnat against the stone with one foot. The mouse could reach it from where she lies, but she would have to put a foot on the wet slope to do it. She drinks on her own side. A drop falls, and for a moment mouse and cricket are both gone; before the rings have died against the sides they are back very much as they were.

Behind the flake where the wings are, the rock has split across the grain. The split is too narrow for a bird to get its head into and widens handsomely a little way in. Here the mouse has four young ones in a felt of breast down. Much of it is charcoal at the tips, quite white underneath. A loose feather is a difficult thing to take from among the dishes without seeming to take part of a bird, and she has collected it chiefly along the back wall, where a little draught rolls it up of its own accord. The young have just got their eyes open. When she comes in, all four put their noses up, and four minute black mouths appear in what had looked like a single piece of rather inferior fur.

One of them has begun to come as far as the entrance of the split. It can hear its mother eating on the other side of the flake. It puts one foot out on the open floor, where the stone is pleasantly warm, and presently the other. A few inches away is the bird whose tail hangs out in the weather. From down here the tail looks quite solid, a little charcoal shelf with a pale line along each board. A breath of air comes up the face of the mountain and lifts all the boards apart. There is an astonishing brightness between them. The little mouse sits back hard on its haunches. By the time it leans forward for another look, the feathers have closed, and its mother, coming round the end of the flake with her chin wet, has filled up the rest of the view.
