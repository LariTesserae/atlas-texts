There are some animals on the blue fibre which are only to be distinguished from it when the draught stops. They lie with their backs to the twist and their feet deep in the wool. At the approach of the pink darkness the little fan shuts up; the end of the fibre hangs down, and what appeared to be a number of short loose hairs on it get up and walk towards the floor. They are not blue. Each has a polished hollow along its back which takes its colour from whatever is underneath. One that has got as far as the solder looks like a fault in the solder, a fine crescent thrown up at the end of a score.

They go down for the salt. While the fibre is still, its tip just touches the top of a bead. The first animal lets itself down head foremost until the water fits about its neck. Behind the head, on either side, it has a little white pad; these swell in the water and presently meet under its chin. It backs up the fibre with the appearance of having put on a clean collar. The whole business takes rather less time than it takes a man to decide whether the cold he feels is the cold of something he is actually touching. Usually there is time for three or four to drink. The others go back empty. There are long periods during which scarcely a fingertip comes up to the seven, but the pink darkness is not the only thing that checks the draught. A cloud coming level with the gantry does it too. Then they may drink for an afternoon.

The pads are for the young. Farther down the twist, where the wool is tight and will admit no light between its strands, there are rooms each the shape of an orange pip, with half a dozen eggs fastened to the pile. The animal goes round the room pressing first one cheek and then the other against the eggs. A newly laid egg is dull all over, like a grain of starch. One that has been well attended to has a window in it. It is possible, by picking the twist a little open with a bristle, to see six windows in the blue dark, and in each one a foot moving slowly as if to clear a larger place to look through. The wool springs back without injury. Very likely the rooms were there in the cuff.

Some of the young, on coming out, take the wrong turning along the fibre and arrive at its buried end. This lies under the overhang of the largest solder lobe, in a part of the country which has no daylight at all. They do perfectly well there. Their collars fill at every pass of the comb, when a little more water is squeezed up under the lobe. But there is nothing for them to stand on that does not presently need to be stood on the other way up. As the water rises they have to turn over and take hold of its lower face; as it falls they set their feet on the nickel again. An animal taken from this place and put on a level bit of the sill will go over on its back at quite regular intervals. For a while it continues to do so even on a slip of glass carried down to the engine room. One could find the speed of the upper condenser there without looking at the dial.

These inhabitants of the buried end are handed their food. The mouths that keep the fibre clean pass along anything too big to swallow, and most things get smaller on the journey. Once a scale from a moth’s wing went in at the blue fan. It was an amber-coloured scale with a notched end and some very beautiful fluting. For a considerable time it travelled lengthwise down the wool, showing first here and then a little farther on, like a light being carried past the windows of a train. What came out under the solder was one narrow rib, still amber, which two of the blind-end animals took between them. They turned over with it when the comb passed. They did not appear to eat it. For several days afterwards there was a gold line in the dark that went flat and stood on edge and went flat again.

At intervals the plate has to be taken off. It is laid face down on a folded rag on the gantry while the fitter oils the bearing, and for perhaps two minutes the whole population is in the open air. Nothing flies out. The red reeds are gone before the first screw is loose. The mites draw their wet feet up under them, carrying enough water to make the back of the plate shine. On the rag the seven is dark and perfectly legible; inside the exposed country, for once, there is more day than anybody knows what to do with. The wool animals all look the same, a little spoony, the colour of the sky. Then the plate is fitted again. The wet feet go down, a pull goes round from the washer, the loose end of the fibre lifts clear of the bead. What the fitter feels through the screwdriver, and waits for before giving the screw its last half turn, is the slight even vibration that tells him he has the plate seated properly. By then the fan is open.
