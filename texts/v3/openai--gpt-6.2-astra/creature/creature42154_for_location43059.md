In the grocers’ quarter there is an old woman who can tell, by the weight of a coin striking her awning, whether it is worth getting the steps out. She is occasionally wrong. A snail shell full of damp earth comes down almost exactly like a silver six. She keeps one in the till and will demonstrate this to anyone who doubts her.

She lives behind the shop with a grandson and, for part of the year, a long-finger. It comes in through the fanlight when the leaves outside have grown level with the sill. It is a much smaller animal than it appears to be in the gardens. On a leaf it stands on the spread tips of all four feet, with its scanty coat hanging down between them; on the kitchen table it folds up to the size of a pear. Its fingers have little pads of pale blue skin, cool and adhesive enough to lift a sheet of writing paper by one corner. The grandson has several times gone to school with only the top half of his arithmetic.

What it wants in the kitchen is the underside of the sink. Small transparent snails collect there along the sweating joint of the waste pipe. Having cleared them off, the animal generally stays to sleep in a string bag. The woman has learned to set a second bag inside the first. Otherwise a foot comes through, then the head, then the rest of the sleeper, without its ever consenting to wake up.

It was a long-finger that discovered the other business of the crossings. Or rather it was a long-finger that brought the matter into a house where somebody was prepared to notice it. This was in the days when a threshold was still a slab on trestles, and customers took their hats off to go through. A woman sanding the enamel letters on a slab felt something pulling at the hem of her skirt. There sat one of the garden animals with a rosy little fish in its hands. It kept turning the fish over in the reasonable expectation of finding a snail on the back.

There are fish in the thickness of the plain. They live in what remains of the bay water, in cells and small blind galleries in the lime. A well may pass within a hand’s breadth of such a gallery without drawing a drop from it. Ordinarily there is barely enough water above a fish’s back to float its fin. The fin lies flat to one side like the parting of a child’s hair. But when a load goes through a crossing, the water goes before it in a running bulge, lifting clear of the floor of one gallery and filling the next to its roof. For a second or so a fish has room to turn round. Sometimes, following the bulge too far, it comes out under the municipal tiles.

No one has seen them feed. Those that have been picked up are very old, to judge by the layers in the little stones of their ears. They are a deep pink just after a crossing and lose their colour rapidly in a saucer. Each has two perfectly good eyes on the same side of its head, not the upper side, as one might expect, but the side it keeps pressed to the rock. There is a boy in the grocers’ quarter who thinks they look into the next gallery. His grandmother, asked what she thinks they see there, says, ‘Another fish looking back, I should hope.’

On market mornings he sits with his heels against the four loose tiles and a preserving jar between his knees. He has never caught a fish. What comes out is usually a thimbleful of warm brine, a curl of weed fine as an eyelash, or a bubble which keeps its shape on the dry tile rather longer than seems proper. Once there was a minute, quite furious crab. He carried it home and gave it the thick end of a stick to hold while he considered where to put it. It is in the habit of the family now, when a difficulty arises, to give the person concerned the thick end of a stick.

At noon he leaves the jar in the shop and goes up to the school yard with his horn. The net overhead has caught a good many things besides balls. There is a striped stocking in it, three swallow nests and a melon which landed as a seed and has since had to be furnished with a sling. His music mistress stands at the far end of the yard. In the interval of quiet she can hear quite well what he is doing with his lip. The seventh note gives him trouble because he is listening for it in advance, and what he hears in advance is the sound of getting it wrong yesterday.

Lately something in the wall has begun to answer him. It is not an echo. It comes a little too soon on the way up the scale and a little too late on the way down, and on days when he has left the horn at home it makes, at noon, a soft preliminary blurt of its own. The mistress has put her ear to the wall. So has the grandmother. There is a shell showing through the plaster there, no bigger than the nail of a thumb, with the old bay mud still packed into the fluting at its lip.

They have not reported it to the thresholds office. A partner might be anywhere, under a stair thirty miles off, or in the dark behind a sink. For the present the boy stands a pace back from the wall and plays six notes. Then he waits, horn lowered, while whatever lives at the other end has a go at the seventh.
