Under the plank there is a little roof that goes to and fro. It goes upstream very slowly, along one margin of the ditch; coming down it keeps to the middle and makes much better time. It is made of chips of the white vein, two slopes of them meeting along a ridge. The chips overlap so neatly that the first man to notice it thought it was a bit of scale come off the pipe. He put out a finger to turn it over. The roof sat down hard on the rock, and he had the curious sense, for the rest of his visit, of having knocked at a door where the person inside was keeping quiet.

There is a snail under it. A thin brown snail, almost all foot, with a clear lip that goes a little way ahead of it, folding and unfolding against the stone. The white chips are stuck to its shell. They were no bigger than sugar grains when it picked them up, but the warm water lays down a little more white on them each year. The oldest ones have grown together. At the rear there is a place where three came off at once, and there you can see the original shell, the color and shine of a wet apple pip.

It lives by taking off the green that grows on the warm face. Generally the snail goes no higher than the plank. Above that there is a stretch where the film of water is too thin to cover its foot, and to cross this costs it most of a morning. But in a wet week it will go up as far as the vein. A man with his face close to the wall once watched it clean out a crystal socket. The lip went in first, then the head, until the little roof stood on end. When it backed out, the socket filled again, this time quite clear, and the snail went on to the next. By the following Sunday there were seven bright dents in a row. For some weeks afterwards you could tell from the shed door where it had been.

There are small ones too. Their shells have as yet only a few white specks, and their whole bodies are so nearly the color of the water that what you see is three or four grains travelling in company. They stay in the moss on the downstream end of the plank. If a thread of it is lifted, the grains draw together and remain still, even after the thread has been put back. A soft brown track leads from there into the pool, down the underside of the drowned trunk. It stops at the lowest of the pale rings. Below that the water is cold.

Once the works were stopped for a day. A fitter came up to pack the bearing of the wheel, and ate his dinner sitting on the sill with the window out on his knees. In the absence of the running note he could hear a number of rather unlikely things: the little slap made by a leaf turning over in the pool, the water sucking air through the moss at the end of the plank. Towards evening he heard a dry tick, repeated at long intervals, quite close to his boot. The snail was coming down the wall. Each time it drew its foot together, the back edge of the shell tipped and one of the white chips touched the rock.

He put the pane back with a new strip of felt. Then he waited until the snail had got across the hand's breadth of bare wood before he used the plank himself. It was dark enough by then that he had to feel for the first rung going down. Below his hand the iron was beginning to tremble again. Up on the shelf the water went on rising out of the clean sockets, and under the moss, where a little of the day's light still came through green, the snail put its lip to the soft edge of a saw mark and worked its way along it to the end.
