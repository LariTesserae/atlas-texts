As the drain comes in there is a white tile showing, the curved kind that used to go along the bottom of a wall. Most of it is buried. The part you can see looks very like the inside of a spoon. An eel lies under it with its chin on the sand. At first all you see is a piece of brown lip, not quite touching the other lip, and two little tubes sticking out above it. One of the tubes has a grain of sand on the end. The sand gives it a finish like the head of a match.

It is too big an eel to have much of itself under one tile. If it goes back an inch, which it does at the stroke of an oar on the kerb, a little trouble passes through the stones as far away as the next door number. A prawn comes shooting backwards out of a joint; a flake of white glaze rocks once and settles. You would give a good deal to see that length of eel clear of the wall. Even the attendant, who has been there eleven years, has seen the two ends of it together only once. It was going down the yellow steps. He thought for a moment that he had left the hose running and that the hose was taking itself down to the water with a series of neat, quite deliberate bends.

It leaves the fish at the pipe pretty much alone. They are looking the right way for food and the wrong way for an eel, but to get at them it would have to come over a piece of bare sand where the bird has an equally good chance at either. It goes along inside the fill instead. The little crabs can get a surprisingly long way in there. You see a pair of claws come out round a stone, folded close like the spectacles a man carries in his top pocket; then the eel's mouth comes out round the other side of the stone and the claws go in again. For some minutes after that there is a fine flour in the water. The fingerlings wheel into it, find nothing worth having, and take up their old places.

There is a second way out under Number 8, through a hole that once took the end of a banister. The attendant discovered it sitting on the steps eating a pear. He dropped one of the pips. Before it had come to rest on the submerged tread, a head came out of the yellow paint and took it. Almost at once the pip came out again, shucked quite clean of the bit of fruit that had been sticking to it. He tried with another pip the next afternoon and in the end had to pick it off the step himself. There was something about having it lie there in full view of the hole that made him ashamed to leave it.

Once in a while, when both motors are off for an inspection, the water covers the pencil mark and makes another street of the kerb. Then the eel comes out. It keeps to the inch or so of new water, its back quite dry except for a thin travelling shine. There are things to be had there that cannot be had twice: woodlice coming apart like pepper under the lick of the water, a soft white crab inside the hard brown likeness it is trying to get out of. The heron walks abreast of it on the tray. Seen from the steps they seem well matched for length, the bird taking one long, finical pace to every three or four thrusts of the eel's head. Neither hurries. There is concrete between them all the way to the bars.

When the pump starts, the kerb drains off so quickly that a leaf lies down almost where it floated. Sometimes the eel is left on the wrong side of a pier. It has no difficulty. It turns on its side, puts a loop of itself over the edge and lets the weight draw the rest after. The last few inches hang on long enough to show a small fault in the tail, a nick with an old milk-white rim. That is what the attendant saw going down the steps eleven years ago. He has never been tempted to give it a name. When a new man comes to take a shift he tells him about the loose tread, and which lamp to tap if it goes out, and, if they are letting the water up, to mind where he puts his boots.
