There is a boy in the western quarter who has seen the back of his own head. He saw it by the third step one afternoon while his mother was feeling among the fans for an empty nest. There was a pale place behind his ear where the hair had never grown in properly. He put his hand up to it and so did the boy on the step. For several days afterward he was very particular about having that place washed. His mother had expected questions of quite another order and had stayed up late thinking how to answer them.

His mother makes latches. She has a left thumb so broad at the tip that people sometimes ask if there is a second nail coming on it. There is not. The thumb is useful for holding a little length of horn over steam and turning up the end; the resulting hook fits very agreeably under a finger. She made all the latches in the western quarter, including the ones on the insides of the doors. Now and then, late at night, somebody trying to get out of his back room lifts all the inside latches a little. They drop again with a sound like a handful of seed thrown gently against a wall. Most people sleep through it. The latch maker wakes and lies pleased in the dark. These are the first fittings of hers that have taken.

The boy would like a white climber. What he actually has, in a shallow box under his bed, is one of the brown animals that carry their young. He chose it because it had fourteen scraps on its back and another lying crosswise over the fourteen. He reckoned on having sixteen animals by morning, counting the mother. In the event the young went out through the handles of the box at dusk and the mother stayed. It is the least interesting of the household animals to watch, but the most satisfactory to hold. Laid across the eyes it admits just enough light to turn the whole afternoon brown. When it wants to get down it puts out one cool pad on the cheek and waits. The boy, who has been known to keep a visitor at the door for the time it takes him to finish an entire griddle cake, always lets it down at once.

His great-aunt is one of the two sisters with the short path. They do not get on especially well. One likes the skin left on a dish of meal and the other beats it in, and it is as much in order to demonstrate the merits of their respective methods as out of kindness that the dishes pass back and forth. When they were girls the walk took long enough for either of them to have a change of heart. There was a stone about halfway along on which the elder used to sit until she could think of something civil to say. It is still there, under the two thresholds, and once when her sister was ill the elder took up a sill board, sat down on the stone with her knitting and stayed the better part of the night. They were both quite comfortable. In the morning it took three people to get her out.

Of all the inhabitants it is the little ones in the curb nests that are least often seen, even by the latch maker, who goes looking for their cast skins. The skins make excellent washers. A new one is a silvery cup with a hole neatly in the middle and a rim soft enough to turn back over the head of a pin. Judging by these, one might expect the creature itself to be something like a limpet. In fact it has a narrow face and six elbows and sleeps bent round until its mouth rests on its own warm belly. It sheds the whole of its coat without waking. For a while afterward it is tender and nearly clear. The gold meal of the fans can be seen going down its throat, stopping, going down a little farther.

The woman with the green blades has had some success at moving these creatures. She waits for a day warm enough to soften the crease of a blade, folds the blade across itself and slips the cut end under a nest. The little animal, disturbed in its sleep, reaches round for its belly and gets hold of the folded green edge instead. Once it has a good grip she can carry it pretty much wherever she likes. There are four now in the joints of the west gate. One lives just above the broad foot of the post and has grown decidedly lopsided, though it eats well. She had hoped to have places ready for all the nests by the time the chisel next came out. It is taking longer than she thought. The small clear ones cannot be moved at all.

It was the boy who asked why the notch had to be very big. He had been turning the chisel over on the supper table while the grown people talked; there was a nick near one corner of its edge, and when he pressed the edge into a bit of cake it left a line with a crumb standing up in it. Could they not, he wanted to know, do something the size of the crumb? His mother took the chisel away and finished cutting the cake with it. But early the following afternoon she went down with him to look.

There were the fans with their meal on them and the white fists going away and away. There too, where nobody had thought to look, on the underside of the lip of the curb, was a little pit in the clay. The woman who had made the curb must have worn a ring and turned the stone in against her palm to work. In all that length her hand had slipped just once far enough down to leave its mark beneath the lip. The boy could get the very tip of his smallest finger into the pit. There was a root in it, one thread, rather stout, with a rub of gold on the end.

The latch maker fetched her horn parings and spent the afternoon making a cap for the pit. She gave it a hook like the hook on a door, but smaller, and a washer of shed skin. Before they fitted it the boy teased out the root and laid it between two of the parings, which were curled enough alike to keep it for a day or so. Nobody supposes that a bit of horn will keep the curb apart forever. For the present there is one place that has to be opened to be felt, and a particular person whose finger fits it, and who is let out of the house each evening after supper to go and see how it is getting on. Sometimes he goes the long way, past his great-aunt’s, with the brown animal riding on his shoulder. Coming home he takes the three steps at a run. His mother knows his latch out of all the latches by the weight with which it falls.
