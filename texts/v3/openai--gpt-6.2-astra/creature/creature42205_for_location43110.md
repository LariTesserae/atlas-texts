Something has eaten the snails on the inside of the lip. The keeper can tell by putting her fingers into the water and feeling for the shells. A bird takes the whole snail away to a stone to break it. These shells are still in place, held by the last little skirt of whatever it is that sticks a snail to a rock. Each has a hole in the same place, a little behind the mouth. She leaves them. There is a child in the third house who likes to thread them on a grass stem, and it would take a good deal of work with a needle to do it so neatly.

The animal that makes the holes is called an underleaf. There is usually one in sight of anybody gathering snails, though it takes shutting one eye, and sometimes moving one's head nearly into the water, to see it. It lives on the ceiling of the overhang. The part presented to the world below is slate blue, with a scattering of white flecks. Seen from a little way off against an afternoon sky it might be a bit of sky itself. The part against the stone is the yellow of split peas. It has six feet like wet thumbs and a body about the length of the keeper's hand. A narrow jaw comes out of a fold at one end. A still narrower one comes out of that.

For a long time the keepers thought it went to bed there and did its feeding in the reeds. Certainly there are underleaves in the reeds. In the first light they can be seen going round and round a stalk at the waterline, the stalk swaying very slightly more each time the animal comes round to the downhill side. But these are the young ones. A young underleaf will go a considerable distance for a meal. The old ones on the lip let the meals come over to them. There is a crop of weed as fine as hair just under the overhang, in the last place where the warm water clings to the stone before falling away. The snails go a long way upside down to get at it.

Once, when a keeper was laid up with a bad knee, she spent parts of nineteen afternoons lying on her stomach watching one feed. She had a folded cane mat to lie on, and kept a little tally in charcoal on its edge. On the best afternoon it ate four snails. Most of the time it did nothing that she could see. Clouds went by behind it. Once there was a great upward travelling of seed floss, out of some dry country far below, and two or three seeds stuck to its back. At the end of the nineteen days she knew it well enough to say what it was doing with its feet. Five held it to the rock. The sixth went feeling, not very far, in the water upstream. When a snail touched that foot the underleaf put it down flat on top of the snail. There was a wait of such a length that at first she had taken the two events to have nothing to do with one another. Then it brought its head round.

Nobody knew what became of them in the season when the reeds turned at the tips and the lip ran nearly dry. There would be perhaps a dozen to be seen there on one visit and on the next there would be none. They had never been seen crossing the liverwort. The keeper with the bad knee found one by accident, quite high up on the ridge, when she went to put her mat away. She had left it rolled up against a leg of the bell. The underleaf was inside, pressed into the space between the last two turns, and it had the clean, almost lilac color of a stone newly got out of clay. There were young ones under it. She unrolled the mat no farther.

After that, a loose roll of split cane could be found against most of the bells in the lean-water months. It did not always have the desired result. One keeper got a nest of green birds in hers and had to go all summer without the use of a perfectly good window blind. Another got grubs. But in the third house the child woke one night to a noise like rain on the floor, and found a quantity of underleaves, none bigger than a bean, dropping one by one from the mat his mother had brought inside to mend. They stuck where they fell for a moment. Then they lifted themselves up and went along in a close file, the first one trying a number of directions and the others faithfully making all its unnecessary turns. They went through a chink under the door. He got a light in time to see the last of them take hold of the underside of the doorstep and disappear. In the morning he eased the step up. There was nothing there but two woodlice and a damp groove in the black rock, running off under the house toward the basin.

It seems there is a fair amount of country of that sort. Beneath a lawn that will bear a person's weight there are low passages carrying an inch or two of water; there are pools with lids of stone over them; there are the undersides of all the islands. A keeper driving a post for a bean frame will sometimes go through the ground quite suddenly and bring the post up with a yellow smear on it, and shift the frame a foot to the left. The hole in the reeds is merely a place where this other country happens to have a window.

The child from the third house is grown now. He keeps the bell on the far side of the summit, where the beetles are going out into the gravel. On his sill is a length of darkened grass with seven white shells on it. Small visitors are allowed to handle it. They nearly always ask the same thing: whether the animals under the lip ever lose their hold. He has no answer to that, but he has lain there often enough to know what happens when a foot comes loose. The underleaf does not snatch at the stone. It lets that part of itself hang out into the pouring water, soft and rather longer than one would have supposed, while the other five feet keep their places. In a while the loose foot curls up at the end. It touches the rock here and there, lightly at first, as a person in the dark might feel along a shelf for a familiar cup. Then it settles, over a snail perhaps, or on a little patch of weed in the full warmth of the mountain, with the red river turning in the light a long way under its back.
