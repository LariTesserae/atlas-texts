On a dry shelf a shellback can go nearly a day without taking its foot out. There is a little room to spare under the shell. The fans go on beating in there, slowly enough that a red-legged fly will sometimes walk into the opening, turn about on the damp horn, and come out with its belly wet before the next stroke. The new shell at the rim is thin. Even in an old animal the passage of the fly is visible there as a moving fleck of pepper.

The first shellback to move need not be the one nearest the water. A gram or two of stone has settled somewhere in the depth of the shelf; what came out yesterday at a wide black crack is coming out today through a hairline. One of the animals well back in the ranks puts its foot down on wet grit. It fetches its shell a little way forward, waits for the weight to come onto the foot, and does it again. Where it has been, a print darkens behind it, no longer than a grain of oats. The animal below it finds the print. In this way a file sets off, usually before there is a bead of water big enough to see.

A shellback is an awkward shape for getting round the lip of a stone. Its front half has to let go and feel about underneath while its shell sticks out over the drop. Here the young have an advantage. One can go round on a curve of stone no greater than the curve of its own clear back, without ever quite losing shelter. The big ones hang in the open. Now and then a hunter comes along the lip taking them as easily as fruit. It eats the foot first and licks the hollow clean. Three or four empty shells, all still wet and set concave side up, are a good indication of where its head has been. The rest of the hunter may be almost anywhere. It is a long animal, with fur that parts into separate little brushes when it bends.

In a year of scant water the hunters have their young well down in the steps, on beds that never quite dry out. The young are born with the soft wedge of the chin already much in evidence; they keep trying it on things. A mother lying on her side with two or three of them at the teat is forever having her foreleg prized up and set down again. Later she brings them shells with a little meat left deep inside. On such a nursery shelf there may be hundreds of shells pushed one into another, like cups put away after a meal. Sedge takes particularly well in them. Its first root makes the circuit of the inner wall several times before finding a way out, and years afterward, under a quite considerable tuft, there is a tight white curl at the top of the root.

The white bird, too, comes down to these beds. It stands a little aside from the place where the young hunters play. At that distance the old hunter knows it perfectly well by sight and will take food past it without undue haste. What the young have to learn is the reach of the neck. The wings hang nearly to the stone; above them there seems to be only a small, rather inattentive head. When the bird strikes there is suddenly a great deal of bird between the head and the wings. Sometimes it misses. For a moment its bill lies flat on the ledge and its eye, yellow with a square black centre, is on a level with the shellbacks. Then the neck goes away into the feathers again. A youngster that has once seen this will give even a shed white feather a wide berth.

By the time the pink begins to show on the upper beds, most of the ledges below are in shade. The flies gather in the clean shells to spend the night. There is room for five or six if they fold their long red legs, and a lip of horn fine enough to hold a film of water against the morning. In a wind the occupied shells travel a little. They turn on the rounded point of the back, lodge against a ridge, and stay there until another gust takes them. At first light the flies come out singly and stand a while beside the shell. On all that extent of stone, they have been carried perhaps a hand’s breadth, from the dry side of a crack to the wet.
