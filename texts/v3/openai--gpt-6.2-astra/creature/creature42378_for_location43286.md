There is a keeper in the city, although the office was abolished before the last families left. It lives under the pivot of the catch basin, in the part of the bearing that never quite cools. When the bowl is level the keeper is a dull half-ring with a greenish cast, easily taken for an old repair. One end of it has worn a polished seat in the platinum. The other end is free. If the bowl were lifted out by daylight there would be nothing to prevent the keeper falling into the socket, and considerable trouble getting it out again.

It is not one of the works. The seat is how this can be established. In a bearing of that age a groove ought to be deepest at the bottom. This one goes all the way around the pin, though for part of the way it is scarcely more than a breath on the metal. The keeper has been getting up in the night.

It waits until the basin tips. There is no advantage to getting up earlier: the first water is scalding, and the edge of the bowl comes down hard enough to cut a thing of its thickness in two. When the edge has struck, a little cool water wells back along the pin. The free end unrolls into it. For a while the keeper resembles a shaving coming off a lathe. Then a second edge separates from the first and stays on the metal while the first goes on. It moves by opening the space between them and closing it again. There is no part that remains the head.

On most nights it goes no farther than the underside of the basin. There is a copper washer there which the old plans specify shall be replaced every seventh year. It has lasted much longer than that, owing to a fault of installation: the man who put it in pinched a fold into it, and only the two crests of the fold have been exposed to wear. In the trough between them the green comes up very tender. The keeper passes over this and comes away a little thicker in the middle. It leaves all the water.

On a good cold night it can cross the market. Its passage over the black flakes is quite fast for something without a fixed front end. Each flake lifts at one corner, as though somebody were looking underneath for a lost article, and settles in its bed again. At the steps there is a pause. The keeper belongs, by the disposition of its weight, to the basin district. To enter the district of the drinking niches it must put an edge over the fourth riser and grow heavy on that side. For a moment the two districts each have hold of half of it. It was at this point that a child used to be able to pick one up, by offering it the edge of a platinum fare-piece. A child running late for school could arrive with a keeper in his pocket and a green line right across his thigh. The stain came off in the bath. Getting the creature to let go of the fare was another matter.

There was once a keeper under nearly every warm fitting. That is where the office got its name. The human keeper made a round after the water had gone, touching each bearing with a fine brush. If the brush came back green he could pass on. If it came back clean he took the fitting apart. In the last years the post was held by a woman from the upper street who could do the whole round without changing her vertical; she went down the public steps on her hands. She was judicious about what really needed taking apart. There are nuts under the baths with the original tool marks still on them.

At the farthest drinking niche the keeper has a difficulty. The clear knob coming out of the pinhole is salt water with a skin tough enough to bear it. Sometimes it gets on top and finds that there is no other side to get down on. Its weight turns the knob slowly in its collar of salt. So far it has always been saved by the heat: the knob draws in, the keeper touches the rim, and it can open itself a hair's breadth onto solid ground. The result of these excursions is a small break in the oldest crescent. Each time the water runs, a little salt goes into the break; each time the keeper comes home that way, a little comes out. It is the only drinking niche in the city that is not eventually going to close.

By the time the flue clicks it is back in the bearing. It has to get its thickness exactly right. Too much left in the middle and the bowl will hang tilted tomorrow; too little at the free end and the returning weight will nip it fast. All day it grows dry along with the hinge. In the afternoon, when the tooth rocks, daylight sometimes reaches the pin and shows, in the green of the half-ring, one clear fleck the color of the distant atmosphere. This is a chip from the market. It has been all the way to the last niche and back, and up and down both sides of the steps, without ever moving far enough inside the keeper to work free.
