The first one I saw was hanging from the underside of a cream flake in the wall. It was the colour of the wet stone except for a white seam along its back. The seam opened a little at the stair end. I had hold of the flake, using it to steady myself while I took off a shoe, and felt something very soft feel the pad of my thumb and withdraw. There was a dark oval on my thumb afterwards, about the size of a lentil. It smelled like the water in the bottom of a flower pot.

Once you have seen one you can find the others. They hang wherever two of the hairline channels meet. The white seam is the meeting of two shells, so thin that when the sun comes down the stair you can see through both of them and make out a little folded fan inside. At intervals the animal opens the shells, unpacks the fan and wipes the stone with it. It takes the faint brown growth that comes before the moss. On a dry flake it can take the better part of an afternoon over a piece of ground that would fit under a shirt button. In a running channel all it has to do is hold the fan out. The drops go through and something stays.

There were animals like these in the street before it went down. The woman who had the key to the tram shelter remembers them inside the cistern. Every winter she shut off the supply and went round the inside with a piece of sacking, lifting them off by the handful. She used to put them in the plane tree grate. She had supposed they were a sort of seed.

They do spend part of their lives in the grate. There is a hollow under it on the side away from the stair, where a root as thick as a wrist has lifted the iron off its bed. If you put a looking glass down there on a bright day you can see hundreds of the white seams, one above another like the edges of the paper in a book that has got wet. Those are the little ones. They keep their hinges against the root and feel about in the shallow water with their fans. As they get bigger they work their way out along the undersides of the bars. At the end of a bar there is a bad few inches to cross, gritty and ordinarily quite dry. Most of them wait for a filling.

I went down in a shower to see them go. I thought they would float off when the water reached them. In fact they hold on very hard. The bits of leaf and the sweet wrappers went past first, then the long grass rose and swung clear of the grate. All along the iron the shells were open. From each there came a foot, not soft and blunt like the part that had touched my thumb, but narrow, tipped with a dark little nail. They reached down together and took hold of the crossing.

It was a good while before I understood why they waited for the arrow. They had only to let go of the grate and stay where they were. The tile did the travelling for them.

For a few minutes I could still pick out particular ones: one with a chipped lip, one with something yellow stuck to its shell. Then the intervals between them became streets in themselves. They were so evenly spaced that I thought of the woman in the shelter going along with her sack and planting them. In all that water each had its patch of white clay to itself. There was a blush on the clay, hardly a colour at first, the same brown stuff they wiped off the wall. Their fans made pale half circles in it. I watched the one with the chipped lip finish a sweep, shift its nail a little way, and begin another. Above us the trams were running again. A man came halfway down the stair to shake out his umbrella, stood a moment to get his breath and went back up. Out on the crossing there was still food enough for days.

They come home very big. You can tell when the distance is going back by the sound against the kerb, a light knocking quite distinct from the note of the brass mouth. They arrive with their shells full of flesh, the two lips no longer able to meet, and for a while lie on their sides opening and shutting their fans in the thinning water. By morning the kerb is clear. Some get into the grass. Some climb the wall; the little nail fits the cut in the stone exactly. On a quiet afternoon you can hear a grain come loose under one and tick down from ledge to ledge.

Yesterday the chipped one was within reach again, just above the line the water had left. Its seam had closed. Below it on the stone was a trail of perfectly clean ovals, each overlapping the last by the width of a white lip. The trail went up through the new green points and stopped under a flake where the light could scarcely get at it. I put my finger there. After a time the fan came out, passed once across the whorls of my skin, and went on with the wall.
