By morning there are pinholes in all the new beaches but one. The beach under the shrimp stays smooth. She can reach its whole extent without leaving her saddle, and if anything stirs there she puts down one of her long glass hands and rakes the place over. The eggs under her belly are far enough along to have eyes. When she turns, it is like looking into a green grape and seeing it full of people at windows.

The creature that makes the pinholes is sometimes to be seen on the underside of the float. It has six pairs of legs and a back like a clipping from a fingernail. Wedged between the last pair of legs is a much smaller creature of exactly the same shape. For a long time these look like a mother and her young. But the small one is always facing the other way. When the two come to a place where the float touches the wall and there is no room to turn, the little one gets its feet down and walks off with the big one. They can spend a whole delivery going up a porcelain flake no larger than the one they have just come down.

On the beaches the little one digs. The big one stands over it, rocking from foot to foot as the sand comes out against its belly. Presently both are underground except for the pale hump of the big one's back. The hump has a hole at either end. A thread of water goes in at one and comes out at the other, clear of everything that was green or gold in it. If the shrimp puts her hand down now she gets only the grit the little one has passed up and fitted, grain by grain, into the wet seam around the hump. By evening it takes the full pull of the return water to fetch the two of them loose. They go up together like a button off a shirt.

There is a fish in the deepest of the three bights that eats these pairs. It will take nothing on the bottom. One can be digging away in full sight, with the little silver shovelfuls coming up almost against the fish's lip, and it shows no interest. Let the pair rise a hair's breadth and the mouth is there. The fish is short and thick, the brown of copper where a drop has dried on it. One of its eyes looks out into the bowl and the other has grown over with skin. It goes about its bight always in the same direction, keeping the good eye to seaward. There is a polished place on the braid where it takes a tight corner on its blind cheek.

Once, in a sway of the post, the bead came down far enough to touch that corner. For a moment there was air on the polished copper. The fish put its mouth against it and made the smallest possible smacking noise. A single black beetle let go. Seen free of the bead it was a surprisingly awkward animal, all its legs spread wide to catch hold of a surface that was no longer there. The fish backed halfway out of the bight to let it pass. It was still going down when a shrimp caught it and began, rather tenderly, to clean the air from between its legs.

Most of what happens takes longer to see. There is a tube worm by the window whose wall contains a complete turn of blue glass. One blue grain in a course, sometimes two, then a long run of white; the blue works round from the side against the window to the side toward the light and comes into view again higher up. There is no blue anywhere on the floor now. High above the tube, in the lip of the daylight pipe, is a chip out of a ring of blue enamel. For as long as the grains from that chip were coming past, the worm was building in a slow screw, turning its head a little farther each day to set the first stone after the water quieted. It has grown nearly twice that height in plain white. Its red comb closes at a shadow, even the shadow of an eyelash on the window, and the whole soft length of it goes down past the blue days into the dark.

Just after the bell there is usually a minute in which nothing crosses the glass. Tonight something does. One green egg has come off the shrimp and is rolling on the porcelain, too heavy to rise with the dust. It stops against the worm's wall. The two dots inside it shift together to one end; then there are legs outside, a tail still inside, a considerable interval of kicking. When the return valve opens the empty skin goes streaming through the ratchet. The shrimp itself stays by the tube, so clear that the blue grain behind its head shows between its eyes. It is trying its fans. Each time it opens them a little wider it comes a little farther off the ground, until the water takes it the last inch to the hanging wood.
