The girl who finds the rim in the morning is taking a lame greyhound for his walk. His bad leg is the near hind one. He can go very fast on the other three, but at that speed there is no accounting for where he will stop, so she has him on the short lead. She ties him to one of the recumbent lampposts and turns its bulb a little in its socket. The light comes on and shines across the grit at the height of her boot buttons. By it she can see what has been going on under the ice.

There are little yellow cases, each with two grains of black grit fastened to it, working their way round the inside of the rim. In the ordinary way a person sees the cases dead and empty, stuck like burnt rice to the underside of a lifted plate. Here there must be sixty. They go the same way round. Where one has got wedged behind a flake of stone there is a considerable tailback, although the entire column, including the flake, could be covered with a shilling. The girl puts her cheek almost on the ice. A small fan of legs comes out of the leading case, feels the flake all over, and goes in again. There is a scratch along her jaw from the zip of her coat. The dog, seeing her lie down, lies down too.

Her name is Kit. She works afternoons at the track, rubbing chalk into the white numbers on the traps and taking the dogs that have had their dinner out separately from the ones that haven't. This one is called Pocket. He belongs to a woman who sets type for the evening paper, and in his day he has won her enough money for a good copper wash boiler with a tap. Kit has the use of him in the mornings. She has found that on the new ground there are things he can see provided they're moving. A twist of white paper went by them once and he followed it with his whole long face until it caught on a thorn, at which he lost it, though it hung there as big as a handkerchief.

She unties him and takes him to fetch Mr Vale. He was a pointsman until the two lines he was responsible for drew apart beyond the reach of any points yet made. Now he keeps the bulbs clean at the terminus. In his hut are a paraffin ring, a chair on blocks, and a collection of spectacles which have been handed in on the trams. It is understood that a person can come in and try them, since it is quite possible to go on losing one's own spectacles and finding a pair better suited to one's eyes. Kit chooses two pairs with thick lenses. Mr Vale comes along with his tea.

By then it is daylight. They stack the spectacles, holding a pair apiece, and find the cases again. The flake is lying flat. On top of it a creature no longer than Kit's smallest eyelash is backing out of its case. First comes a yellow folded bit, then a clear folded bit, then yards, it seems, of exceedingly fine black thread. Mr Vale takes a sip of his tea without moving the spectacles. Along the path to the day shift people step round them and the dog.

What has come out is a fly. It goes along the under surface of the ice with all its thread streaming behind it, looking for a way out into the air. At the centre of the saucer there is none. Towards the rim the ice has lifted a little and there is a space about as deep as the edge on a penny. Here the fly turns itself dry in an instant. Its wings, which were the clear folded bit, open against the ice with a minute tick quite audible through the spectacles. Pocket's ears go up.

All that morning the flies come out. They settle thickest on the black iron lamppost, which by ten has the warmth of a windowsill. By noon there are so many pairs joined tail to tail that the post looks as if it has been dusted with little brass staples. Kit would like to stay to see what happens next. But there are the numbers to do, and Pocket's owner is coming off the night shift and can't sleep until she has heard him drink from the enamel bowl on the kitchen flags. Kit puts the spectacles in her pocket by mistake and runs.

For several days after, passengers at the terminus get down on one knee to look in the rim. There isn't much to see. The ice has gone, there is a slurry of empty cases at the bottom, and somebody has left a teaspoon there. But all along the tram line, wet or dry, the lampposts are in use. A fly that has rested an hour on one can reach the next. At night the females sit on the glass till the current goes off, and then they let themselves fall. A few fall into fresh breaks. Most fall on old stone and are taken by the little brown spiders that have come out from the bolt holes. At the last upright lamp, where the rails turn east for the carriage works, the stone has opened right across the pavement of a covered stop. There is a bench there at just the height for Pocket to get his chin on Kit's knee. In June, while they are waiting for the car, she feels his chin go slowly sideways, and looks down in time to see a single yellow case crossing the toe of her shoe.
