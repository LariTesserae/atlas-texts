Of the three beads, it is the one nearest the entrance that a second visitor is likely to remember as having been in a different place. In a room so difficult to get about in, one can stand on the wrong side of a dish without knowing it. There is, however, a way of making sure. Run a fingertip around the bead. On the side towards the entrance the lip of its hollow is sharp enough to catch the skin. On the far side the lip is gone and there is a shallow polished runnel in the floor of the dish. The bead is travelling towards you. It takes a good while to establish where you have come in.

There is something living in the pavement. It has the use of the beads but they are no more a part of it than the little peak of grit which, in one or two places, it also has the use of. Its own substance is mostly in the bedding of the tiles. On a still day, if there were one, it would be possible to prise up a paver and see it: a dry silver-grey stuff, like the bloom on a plum, with a darker grain in it all running one way. Wherever two pavers touch, the grain of the stuff stands up in loops. The loops go flat under pressure and a few of them catch in the grain opposite. When the pressure is taken off they draw it a little way after them. Miles of such small, imperfect fastenings have been made under the saddle of the pass.

It does not extend equally far in all directions. Out beyond the eastern wall there is a sheltered course of tiles which a person could lift and carry away without troubling it in the least. On the west, on the other hand, it goes nearly to the place where the mules put their heads down for the last of the climb. A loaded mule makes four dark places in it, widely separated. For some time after the mule has gone these places continue to close up and loosen in turn. The gait travels a little further under the pavement than the mule goes on foot. Then it is lost among the other gaits. This is about as near as the thing comes to seeing the traffic on the pass.

A person entering the room is a considerably more distinct affair. There is the toe set down and twisted, the long pressure on the outer edge of the other foot, the hand let go of one pier and not yet put out to the next. Few animals stop in the middle of a pace. It is the hanging fire of the weight that interests it. The vibration in a loaded paver differs very slightly from that in a free one, and through all the castings about and changes of foot it keeps trying to bring the two into accord. It can do this quite neatly for a man who sits down. After an hour or so under the seated man there will be scarcely a loop laid the same way as when he arrived. He will have been comfortable, without finding anything soft.

Long ago it used to lose the arrangement each time a man got up. Now a little of it is put by. It is for this purpose that the beads are moved. At the bottom of the dish the bloom is very thick, and it can work a bead uphill, though not so fast as a growing nail would push it. Under the steady weight of the three beads the work of accommodating a weight elsewhere can go on in miniature. Two of the hollows are deep. One belongs to a burden set down for the night, long since shouldered and taken away. From the shape of the other, which has two equally worn ends and a smooth little rise between, one would say that somebody had once stayed here rocking a child. There is no reason why it should have been a child. A man nursing a sore foot does much the same thing. In either case what has been kept is the rocking.

The finger and thumb in the clay are of no interest to it. They had already let go when it began. Its oldest acquaintance is with a fault under the western wall, where a wedge was bedded a little crooked and used to chatter in a high wind. It grew there first, working into the loose fit, and the wedge has long been quiet. Even now, when all the piers are sounding full and nothing on the whole saddle needs attending to, there is a slow thickening and thinning of the grain at that spot, of just the width that would have steadied the wedge. It is one of the things it does to itself, like a sleeping man rubbing his tongue along the place where a tooth has been.

If you come back the following summer there will be no use looking in the dish for a likeness of your foot. Your visit was too short for that. But turn sideways in the gap and set your weight for a moment on the foot still outside. In the sole of the other, not quite down yet, there comes a most peculiar ease, as of finding a stair in the dark at precisely the height one hoped for. The note has not changed. Under the first paver the little grey loops have got ahead of you this time.
