The woman who weighs the pears lives one floor down from them, so that her ceiling in spring is the last place in the house to get warm. Her name is Ada Vale. There is a crack over her washstand which she used to fill every year until she found out what caused it. At the other end of the crack, quite a distance away under the street, is the foot of a pear tree. When the gardeners take the weight of fruit off its wires in September, the two edges of the crack come neatly together again. She has drawn a twig across it in green pencil, with a pear hanging from the twig on either side.

She is a small woman with a considerable reach. At the scales she can catch a pear rolling off the far side of the table without interrupting the tilt of the pan. The first year a child works with her she pays him for the pears he bruises as well as the sound ones, and makes him take the bruised ones home. There is a boy three doors along who became a quite accomplished pastry cook during his first year with Ada. He is nineteen now and sells little square pies at the western ticket office. He sends her one occasionally in a tin with a false bottom, under which she is to put his clean cuffs. His mother has soap but no place out of the soot to dry anything white.

The people on the seven viaducts do a great many errands of this sort for one another. There are things one household can do easily which are next to impossible for its neighbour. In the Vales’ house bread will keep for a week in a tin; across the street it goes furry in two days, but there is a cupboard there in which a violin can hang all winter without coming unglued. The man who has the violin calls for it on dance nights. He stands in the cupboard owner’s kitchen to tune it, and if they have company he has to play the company something before he can get away. Ada’s part of the arrangement is that she keeps the rosin. It melts in his pocket at work.

Ada herself came up in a goods lift at the age of eleven, with a little sister and a box of combs which their father had made below and which, in his estimation, were what was wanting on the heights. He had made them out of horn. There were great beauties among them, streaked with honey colour and black, with the thicker end teeth carved into birds’ heads. Unfortunately most of the women above the cloud wore their hair cut short because of the machinery. For some time the three lived in the comb box, as Ada puts it, though she means of course that they lived on the proceeds of the box. Her father did much better when he began to cut the same horn into pear labels. A few are still in use. Scrape away the name of the variety on an old label and there appears under it, very fine and rather cross, the eye of a bird.

Her sister went back down. This is more usual than strangers suppose. A place where you can watch the weather coming for fifty miles is not necessarily the place in which you wish to wake up every morning. Her sister liked a street that ended in a field, liked to go out without first deciding at what level the thing she wanted might be. She has a shop below which sells, among other things, pears. She and Ada correspond on the backs of the blue weight slips. Often enough there is a pear at her sister’s end of the journey which Ada remembers at hers: one with a wire mark on it, or two grown together at the neck. Once the message came back, *Your ugly one was lovely inside.*

In her sixty-third year Ada takes on a girl who cannot get down the ladders. On a stair the girl is perfectly good, even on the iron stair over the engine shaft, through the steps of which one can see a little lamp four hundred feet below. It is the operation of turning round at the head of the ladder that she cannot perform. She gets one foot over the square of wind and stays there, while the pigeons slip in and out below her and the small hairs at the back of her neck blow up straight. The other pickers have shown her how to do it so many times that by now she cannot even approach a lid without somebody coming over to show her how to do it again.

Ada puts her to weighing instead. She has a very good eye. By winter the girl can fill a half-stone order to within the weight of the brass scoop, and by the following autumn she has discovered that the scales are out on windy days. It is not the wind on the pans, as Ada at first supposes. It is the awning post working loose in its socket and lifting, by the merest shade, one of the tiles on which the table stands. They shift the table the breadth of a hand. That night Ada sends down a slip to her sister: *You have been getting the east wind at pear prices these thirty years.* The slip comes back with a drawing of a little bag tied up with string and bulging violently to one side.

On fine evenings the girl and Ada sit a while after they have covered the scales. There is always one pear which will not keep till morning. They divide it with the weighing knife, which has no point, and eat it looking west. At that hour the ticket office is too bright to be seen; the hawk on its roof might be a flaw in the eye. Nearer at hand, almost within reach, the pigeons are settling on the warm bars beneath the street. A pigeon turning in to land there shows for an instant, in the last light from above, the extraordinary pink of its feet.

The first time the girl sees this she goes to the open lid and lies down flat on her belly for a better look. Ada goes on eating. After a little while an arm comes up out of the square and lays half a pear carefully on the paving, cut side uppermost. Then the arm goes down again. There is a faint stir among the pigeons. Ada sits where she is, keeping the flies off both pieces of pear, until the girl comes back to tell her what it is like under the orchard.
