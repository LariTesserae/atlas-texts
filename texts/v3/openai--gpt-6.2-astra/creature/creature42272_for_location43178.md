There is a stoat living under the square of turf. It does not often come up through it. There is a better door behind the lowest stone of the shelter, where it can put its head out in the shade and have a look along the whole green strip. First the nose comes out, wet and divided by a fine line; then the rather widely set ears. It can stay at this stage so long that one begins to allow for a little brown face in the shape of the stone. When it does come out there is considerably more of it than one was expecting.

It is a female, small enough to go wherever the voles go. One ear has a nick in it, still furred on both edges. She has three young down there in a chamber that used to belong to a vole, lined with dry willow leaves and feathers, including one long swift feather which will not bend to the shape of the chamber. It sticks out of the door. She has pulled it in a good many times.

In the morning she works the wall from the far end inward. Her course can be read for a while in the frost: two feet together on a campion mat, a brush mark where her belly went over a willow, then nothing for a yard or more. In those places she is underneath. The moss lifts quite gently and subsides behind her. Once a vole goes out onto the pale stone and sits there in the open, its sides going like a little pair of bellows. The stoat passes within the length of a hand of it, following with great exactitude the passage it has just left.

The young are at the age of putting their teeth to things. At noon, when the stone inside the shelter is warm, one of them comes out backward with a piece of turf in its mouth. It has taken hold of a root which goes much farther than it knows. Its small pink heels dig in; the sod rises; the other two, who have been asleep beneath it, are let out into the light. For a little while there are stoats all over the old shelter. One gets its head through the space under the horn peg and finds there is nothing on the other side to put a foot on. It hangs there, draped over the horn at the armpits, looking down with interest. The mother, coming back along the wall with a vole, makes a short noise hardly louder than a grass stem snapped between the nails. All three get down the hole with such readiness that the swift feather, drawn after them a little way, wags in the door.

She herself does not go down at once. There is a drop gathering at the end of the wood. She stands with her forefeet on the cairn and takes it against the side of her mouth before it falls. Then she sits up on the warm flake, washing the wet place with one paw. Out beyond her the falcon is still there. Its head turns; hers turns too. For those few seconds each has an excellent view of the other, down to the nick in the ear, down to the fine dark shaft of each feather on the breast. The falcon lets the wind take it a little higher. The stoat finishes the side of her face.

Long after the shelf has gone into shade there is still a trace of the day's warmth under the turf. The three young sleep wound across one another with no particular regard for which head belongs to which length of back. Their mother lies between them and the stone, her chin on the sill of the little door. In the last light her white throat is easier to see than her face. Then that goes too. If one were sitting inside the old curve of stones at that hour, with a hand laid flat on the soft square, one might feel, far down, a single brief drumming as one of the sleepers scratches an ear.
