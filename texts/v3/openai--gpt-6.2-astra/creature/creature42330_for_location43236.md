At the blunt end of the middle ridge there is a place where the cream plates appear to breathe. For a space about as long as your hand the edges rise together, disclosing a fine cinnamon-colored nap, and settle again without a grain of dust slipping into the seam. What looks like a chip of the upper plate has two eyes in it. They are set far apart, with an edge of stone between, so that one has a view along the ridge and the other looks out into the yellow. If you stand quite still the animal will presently lift its head. The underside of its chin is a surprising tender pink, crosshatched from lying so long on the same piece of ground.

There are numbers of these little sleepers along the ridge. They have six short legs, a blunt tail with a notch near the end, and overlapping shields on the back which they do not grow for themselves. A young one presses its sticky back against the roof of the crack it was born in and comes away wearing a flake. As it broadens it takes on others. In a large old sleeper the foremost shield has sometimes worked loose enough to rock when the animal chews. This accounts for a sound heard in these parts at long intervals, a light confidential knocking, quite distinct from the tick of the beetle.

They eat the apricot fans. The fan has to be taken from below; touched on its upper face it folds into a hard little comb which even the sleeper's broad grinding teeth cannot manage. The animal lies on its back to feed, bracing four feet against the roof and holding the fan spread with the other two. You might find one like this apparently dead, with its mouth full. Look along the next dozen yards of the ridge and there will be another in much the same attitude, though the fan in its grasp may be quite a different size. At the tip of the ridge a third, smaller than either, is busy chewing. It is chewing for all three. You can feel the work going on in the throat of the one you first found, and watch the fan diminish from margin to stalk, although its jaws never stir.

They are not three appearances of one sleeper. The smallest has a hind toe missing, and the others have all of theirs. Nor do they share the taste. A fan that has grown across one of the bottle-green leaves takes on a bitter edge. The one that gets it draws its lips back and screws up both eyes while the little fellow at the ridge tip goes on with its comfortable, slightly sideways chew. It is the work alone that is in common. On ground as favorable as this a sleeper can lend the use of a set of muscles to several others and still have the use of them itself.

The young have to learn what work to take. In a crack near the southern end of the thicket there is a litter so new that each has just the one shield, a translucent scale through which the fur looks like a wisp of smoke. Their mother is out on the level, walking. At every third step one of the young in the crack gives a vigorous kick and rolls a little farther onto its side. Another is making walking movements with its mouth. Only the last and least of them has found the long muscles either side of its spine that correspond to the ones its mother is using. It lies in the dust describing a beautiful shallow S, and every time she puts a foot down its little tail straightens with pleasure. By the time the mother comes back they will have gone some distance with her, though she will have to nose them out of the crack before any of them sets foot on the open ground.

They do not all stay on the ridges. Out toward the east, where the reeds are in the act of turning, you can follow a track of paired scratches which begins at the foot of a stem and ends at the foot of the next. Each scratch has a curled shaving of cream at its forward end. There is nothing at the head of the track. A little way up the second stem, however, the leaves sag, and if you part them carefully you will find an old sleeper lodged in the fork. It has climbed there by its own efforts. Its belly fur is worn almost to the skin, and the shields it acquired as a youngster are buried under so many later flakes that its back is high and narrow like the spine of a mussel. It has no use for fans now. Both its forefeet are clasped lightly about the stem, in just the attitude of the root hairs far below, and that suffices.

What it has come up for is the air. Down on the plates it went about largely by touch, with its nostrils full of mineral dust and a prospect, from either eye, of some six inches of good detail before the stems shut off the view. Here the rising blades have parted in front of its face. There is a lane of brightness all the way to the far rim. Across its whiskers lies the first warm current that ever passed this way, still lifting the same ranks of leaves, smelling faintly of the red shrubs it has yet to reach. Now and then the sleeper turns its head and offers the other cheek to it. Nothing else in all that reach of reeds is doing anything quite so quickly.
