The first person you find is some distance from the laughter. It is a woman sitting on the ground with one foot in her lap, picking something out of her heel. What kept you from seeing her from the house is the color of her coat. The gray birds have a patch under each wing where the feathers are dun instead of gray, and her coat is made of those patches, sewn with the dun side out. There is a green gleam in the crook of her elbow where a seam has turned. She has good broad brown feet. The thing in her heel is one of the white husks. She has got it by a corner between her nails and is giving it her whole attention.

You would take her for a solitary woman but for the way her hair is parted behind. Nobody could make so fine and straight a parting on the back of her own head. There are seven little plaits on either side. At the end of each is a knot of yellow grass, and where there was not quite enough grass to finish the work, a knot of rather dirty blue thread.

On the other side of the rise there is a considerable length of the dressing of her hair. It was done by a man with a bad shoulder. He has to rest the arm at intervals on the top of her head, which makes her laugh. You can hear this if you put your head in between them, though it feels an imposition to do so. The child sat on the ground beside them teasing the grass into strips. It was the child who supplied the bit of thread, at some cost to the hem of a shirt.

There are not very many people. One gets a wrong impression of their numbers from the family at the white channel. There appear to be enough of them to lift a house. They are lifting a house, in fact, but there are only four: the woman with the plaits, the man, and two rather elderly sisters, so much alike that it takes a little study to separate the course of one from that of the other. One has the first joint missing from a finger. Where the two reach under the same slab you see that the other sister, too, has kept that finger clear. She has been making allowances for her sister's hand for a long time.

They have moved the house on account of a grazer. It is not that the beast has touched it. There is room for a person to pass sideways between the corner of the house and the great dun flank. But a little farther down the channel there is a foot, the one you never get a good look at out on the pavement. It is an astonishingly delicate affair, four toes in a fan with a pad like the underside of a mushroom. A chip off the corner slab has got well into the pad. The people have taken down the roof, levered up the slab, and turned its good smooth face to the channel. There is no undoing the hurt on the foot. It is there to put your finger on. A little farther on there is the place where one of the sisters took the chip out, and the minute dark well it left, and in the end a sound pad bearing down on fine gravel. The slab is smooth for the next beast.

The child has not helped with this business. There is a certain age here, as elsewhere, at which a person asked to fetch a short stick can be gone most of an afternoon. The going can be followed from the house. There is the back of the small head with its parting considerably less straight than the woman's, and a view of one ear through a rent in the dun hood. There is a crouched child investigating the underside of a bird. There is a child who has found a perfectly good short stick and is using it to draw toes on a stone. Then there are the heel marks.

You might dislike to follow them on account of the drop. From the sill they certainly seem to go straight out into the notch. It is only quite close to the rim that you see what the child was running to. The old river went over here. It cut a lip in the white rock, and under the lip there is a shelf some six feet wide, warm from the sun on its underside and out of the wind. It has its own small population of the mitten-shaped animals. Seen from below, against the light, they are a beautiful clear amber. Their eyes come up through little holes in the lip. This is why they have stalks.

A very old person lives on the shelf. There is a mat, a cup of horn, a heap of clean husks to put under the mat when the rock gets cold. There are the white heads of the same old person all along the shelf, some bowed over work, some tilted to get the sun into the hollows about the eyes. What the old person makes is soles for children's shoes. The tender side of the grazer hide goes in and the hard side out. At one end of the shelf a pair hardly bigger than the bowls of two spoons lies finished on the mat. At the other end are the soles that fit those deep heel marks in the gravel.

Here the child has come down the last bit sitting, with the stick tight in one fist. The old person has a hand up to steady a bare ankle. There is a little pocket of talk. It is not talk about the shoes. The child has made a discovery concerning the moon and has come a long way to get somebody to look at it from exactly the right spot. In order to see it properly the old person has had to lie almost flat, with the white head supported in the child's lap. The stick does to point with. From anywhere else on the shelf it is an ordinary moon in a blue sky. From here it is caught between two black eyes on stalks, and makes a face.
