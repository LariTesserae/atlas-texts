The last crescent has a tenant. When the bead of water stands up between the curbs, a notch appears in it near one end and travels slowly to the other. A little water passes into the notch. A little passes back, clouded now, and the rest of the bead stays clear. Below the curbs something has turned over in its bed. For a while one can hear it breathing, a sound like the last of a drink being taken through closed teeth.

There are others under the walks. Most of them lie up close to the surface, where the chalk is soft. One uncovered by a slip of the east bank lay with its broad side toward the air. It was about the length of a bench, pale brown, much pouched and folded, with a row of small hard soles along its underside. There were grains of black silt in the creases between the soles. The exposed side began to dry. After a time one of the folds opened and let out a hand, which went feeling rather absently over the dry place, much as a sleeper will feel for the edge of a cover. It drew a little of the loose bank down over itself. Nothing more could be seen of it except the tips of two fingers. Those took quite a long time to let go of the earth.

Their burrows have no entrances of any size. They can put a hand through a pore that hardly admits a drop; it comes through a finger at a time, and on the far side spreads out, smooth and full, with the faint hollows of nails. In the rooms of the low houses the hands come out at shoulder height. They pass over the oval places on the walls. Sometimes, in a favorable arrangement of the houses, another hand is already there. The two lie palm to palm until the wall begins to move away. There is a little slack in each wrist, enough for a parting to take appreciably longer than one would suppose.

It is difficult to say how many hands belong to one sleeper. Under a short row of houses there might be a dozen animals, or one very old one lying doubled and redoubled in the warm chalk. Injury in one place is sometimes felt a long way off. A roof fell in the bath quarter, driving a piece of eave deep into the ground, and all that ebb a hand kept coming out beside a bench on the eastern walk. It would close hard on the edge of the seat and presently ease its hold. There was a print left there, including the print of a little sixth finger set low on the heel of the hand. One can still fit a fingertip into it.

At the beginning of the rise they move. The soles loosen in order down the length of the animal, letting off a fine grit which can be felt underfoot on the walk. A body that had lain scarcely longer than a bench now extends the length of the walk between one turning and the next. The folds come out of it. Along its newly uncovered flanks are rows of shallow cups, each with something nestled in it, olive-sized, soft, already creased across the middle. These are the young. For the whole of the ebb they have lain pressed between fold and fold, in a spoonful of water each. Now the water seeps away and they lie exposed to the broad warmth of the stone. Their soles are still pink. They work them against the bottoms of the cups, and all down the side of the parent the chalk takes on a faint stipple like rain on a sill.

Most stay where they are. A few of the larger ones ease themselves out and make off through the saturated ground. In the bare mile east of the baths there are long low welts in the silt, generally ending at the foundations of a vanished neighborhood. It is the old warmth they go by. Ground that has had a house on it cools differently from open ground, even after the house has been gone a long while. The young one will lie there for several tides, trying out its hands. Now and then a house comes back.

The oldest live under the baths. Parts of them hang through the ceilings of the lower chambers, too heavy to be drawn back into the stone: a blunt grey lobe here, a long purse of striped skin there, polished underneath by the water. At full tide one of these purses fills and swings down against a ledge. In a neighboring bath another swings. The sound is low but distinct, and between the knocks there is time for all the little soles beneath the city to cease their working. For once the ground is perfectly still. Then the skin comes off the water in a single piece and begins its slow journey toward the drains.

In the few clear minutes afterward it is possible, from the north of the square, to see something sitting on the ninth step. It is a small one, old enough to have come some distance on its own, not old enough to know what to do in a place with so little stone over it. Its hands are laid quite neatly in its lap. The light reaches it there, the deep brown light, and under that unaccustomed touch the whole pleated length of its back puckers. It could go down. Instead it puts one hand out on the step above it, palm up, and leaves it there until the water darkens.
