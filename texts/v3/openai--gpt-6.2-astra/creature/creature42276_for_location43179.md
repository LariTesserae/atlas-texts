A child brought up on the eastern flats drinks very neatly. She can kneel in her mittens, pick the little platter of ice off a cup with her teeth, and empty the cup without putting any weight on its lip. She sets the ice back. There is a feather of new ice underneath by morning, where the two wet edges have met, and sometimes, after the next fall of water, the cup is full again without the lid ever having been disturbed. A stranger goes down the row breaking all the lids with his thumb. It is one of the ways you can tell that a family has come in from a country farther down.

The people have rather ordinary faces. One expects, after hearing about the shelves, to find some peculiarity of the eyes or the feet. What is peculiar is to see two of them resting together. They sit some distance apart, facing the same way, and the one who talks does not look to see what effect the story is having. This is not reserve. A person in a good temper may go on for an hour describing a mitten that did not fit. They like to have the same stretch of floor before them. If the grain begins to move against the grain beyond, neither will have to interrupt the other to explain what has happened.

They make a considerable number of things in pairs. A scoop newly hollowed is laid bowl to bowl against an older scoop and the two are held up to the light; daylight showing at the join means there is more work to do. One stays on the peg and one goes in the coat. There are pairs of seed packets, pairs of combs. Even a very young person who has made a poor little figure with a grazer's six legs all on one side will generally try to make another just as poor. This is partly on account of losing houses. But there is also a pleasure, peculiar to a place where so few things have permanent neighbors, in laying two things together again. A woman once stepped into a shelter near the third pond and took out her scoop to dip some meltwater from the bedding trench. The scoop on the peg fitted hers. She had been a girl when her father made them; she had children with her now who were older than she had been. Nothing else about the shelter was familiar. The peg had grown thick enough to close the hole in the handle, and she had to use the scoop where it hung, lifting the children one after another to drink.

It is the children who know most about the grazers. There are two sorts of tracks commonly mistaken for one another. A sleepy grazer sets down its feet in the shallow spots left by the feet ahead, so that its trail is two dotted lines. A thirsty one runs rather wide and leaves all six marks clear. Often the trail that appears to be going straight to water is the trail of something going straight away from it to sleep. Children will lie with a sleeve across a cup, leaving no more than a finger's breadth open, and wait to feel six small tugs at the wool. The creature drinks under cover if it can. Its chin is divided into two pale pads with a dark seam between. While it drinks the seam travels up as far as the eyes, like a cut opening, and shuts again. Its back stays quite dry. Handling one is discouraged, not because the animal minds particularly but because the warmth of a hand wakes the minute red flies folded in the fur along its spine. Once awake, these will live in a cuff for weeks.

In a house where the flies have got well established, there is a red line low on the wall, at just the height to which the sleepers warm the air. Some of the old empty houses have three or four such lines, the highest well out of reach. The wall has grown. There are people who can tell from the distance between the lines how long the house stood empty between one family and the next. These people are often asked to settle other questions about a shelter too: whether a slit beside the sleeping place was made for a baby's sling or for drying strips of sweet edge, whether the hollow in the threshold was worn by a stone being dragged indoors. They are willing enough to come and look. Most of what they say is, “It might have been.”

A stone does leave a recognizable mark, however. Even wrapped in bedding it dents the floor crosswise to the grain. The old people who carry one have generally a flat place high on one shoulder, and a habit, on rising without their bundle, of putting out a hand as if the ground had tilted. Very little stone is actually struck against anything. A mallet with its head intact is used chiefly for listening. Laid flat, it gives the rubbing of the shelves back through the wooden haft with extraordinary clarity. A good listener can distinguish the far shelf passing from the near shelf beginning to go. Asked which way a shelf will move, the listener takes the mallet up and puts it carefully away. There is nothing in the sound to tell that.

For all this attention underfoot, they are great watchers of the ponds. Children keep an eye on the little one while a parent has both hands occupied; they call out when the comma goes by. There is a certain satisfaction in getting a row of stitches done between one passage and the next. Not every interruption of the light is a fish. In summer a growth fine as hair comes up through the pond mud and moves in the water under the ice. Seen from the flats it resembles a great many fish keeping perfectly together. As soon as one sees this one puts away any work that requires counting.

It is quite possible to meet a grown man who has never seen the ponds except from underneath. Such a man will ask very particular questions of anyone who has come through a break from the sunward side. What color is a fish there? Does it cast a little shadow of its own on the mud, besides the great one it sends down here? People who have seen one are apt to disappoint him. They have been busy on their knees, scooping water into everything they own. One girl, coming thirsty off the underside at the season of open water, drank a fish into her mouth and let it go again. All she could tell her brothers afterward was that it had struck the back of her teeth with its tail. This impressed them more than a description of its color would have done. Long afterward, whenever a shelf brought the three green lights into view again, one of those brothers, by then a man with a flat shoulder and red in both his cuffs, would put his tongue against the back of his teeth.
