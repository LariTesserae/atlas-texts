The youngest crickets are white. There is generally one to be found in the hollow on the underside of the fig branch, where the wood has grown over two sides of an old brass foot and left the third side bare. The hollow is about the size of a vest pocket. The little white fellow will be standing there quite still, with his old skin standing beside him. The skin is coppery and has eyes and looks much the more capable of the two. By the next knock there may be a mite walking in and out of its mouth.

It is difficult to say what so many crickets eat until one has seen an onion split. The outer scale opens, usually on the side toward the warmth, and a drop of perfectly clear juice comes out and sits in the opening. A cricket will back down the stalk, turn round on the bulb, and drink hanging head downward over a drop of several thousand feet. The gardener who first saw this thought it was laying eggs. She came back with a shaving mirror on a stick. In the mirror the cricket was right side up, with the mountain upside down behind it, and she could see its little jaws going crosswise through the drop.

She still carries the mirror. Once a year she uses it to look for a snail on the underside of the hoop, considerably farther round than she can reach from the hatch. It is easy enough to find the place. One of the fitters, when the horn was put together, gave a screw a last turn with too small a screwdriver and raised a burr. From that burr hangs a thread of wool with three knots in it. They are natural knots, made by the turning of the thread in the exhaust. The snail is usually at the second knot.

It is not a remarkable snail except in its choice of a habitation. All its kind get down into the wet wool sometimes, and occasionally one will follow a loose fiber a little way into the air. This one lives there. At the bottom of the thread a bit of brown stuff has collected, scarcely enough to dirty a fingernail, out of which two green blades grow at an angle to each other. When the iron sweats, the whole arrangement gains so much weight that it hangs straight down. In dry weather it stands out nearly level with the hoop. There are consequently two quite different views in the mirror. In one the snail has the whole white horn for a background and is almost impossible to see. In the other it hangs against the sky, and the light goes through its shell and shows the dark crescent of the last meal it ate.

The gardener made a drawing of it one year in the book in which the shifts below record the condition of the joints. She meant to show where the loose wool was. But she put in the blades, because they showed which way the thread was hanging, and the snail, because she had never made a drawing so small before and wanted to see if she could. A man on nights recognized the blades as the first two leaves of one of the sweet onions. His father had grown them in a window. He asked her the following year how big the onion was getting.

That was a good while ago. The onion has never got any bigger. There are still the same two blades, one or the other of which is occasionally yellow at the tip. The man on nights has a daughter working on the inspection stairs now. The daughter has looked through the mirror too. It was she who noticed that there is sometimes a much smaller shell on the far side of the knot, and that the large snail in a drawing made eleven years ago winds the other way from the large snail there now.

The gardeners no longer put a date beside the snail. They put a date beside the drawing. The distinction matters down below, where the ordinary life of a piece of packing can be looked up in a table, and where someone will eventually have to decide what to do about this one.

In the meantime there is a small provision made for it. Each spring, when they clear the approach to the hatch, they take the cuttings away in a bag instead of brushing them over the edge. A shaving of fig bark would take the thread with it as cleanly as a knife. It is an odd sight from the stairs below: a grown woman on her knees, picking up crumbs in a place where, for a hundred miles in any direction, there is absolutely nothing to sweep.
