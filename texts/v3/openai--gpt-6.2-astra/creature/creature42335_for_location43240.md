The tracks in the blue strips are generally taken for the prints of a two-toed foot. Each is a pair of little commas turned in toward one another. Where the crust is very thin there is sometimes a third mark between them, a pinhole going down much farther than either toe. This is made by a hair on the animal’s breast. It walks so low that it could hardly carry anything under its arm, though the length of a stride gives the impression of a creature standing well up off the ground.

One can see a whole one by lying on a slate cap and looking down the north face just before noon. There is a delay while the eye sorts out what belongs to the animal. The blue goes clean through its feet. What first appears is a little brown object about the size of a walnut, making good time across the frost with no visible means of support. This is the stomach, full of food. A little ahead of it are two black eyes, and a long way behind it the end of a tail catches the light. The rest comes clear as it passes over dark stone: a narrow level back, four enormously long legs bent out sideways like those of a pond skater, and, hanging from the breast, the single stiff hair. The feet on each side come down in the same place, the hind foot settling into the print of the forefoot so neatly that it does not even chip the lip of frost thrown up around the toes.

It is an eater of things that have fallen asleep in the cold. There is quite a traffic into the shadows, though very little of it goes all the way across. A bird sailing between backs occasionally comes down short. It runs a few yards, dragging the webs between its legs, and presently sits down as if it had meant to be there. Red mites blown off the cliff collect like cayenne in the hollows of the frost. The small clear animal goes about picking these up. When it comes to a bird it puts both forefeet on one of the webs and eats a narrow scallop out of the edge. Generally the bird wakes up. It is a common sight on the backs of the grinders, a furious little bird trying to shoulder its way into the wet meal, with a row of perfectly round bites letting the sunshine through its folded skin.

There are places in each strip where the clear animals stand for a long time doing nothing of the kind. They pick a spot well away from the pillar, settle on all fours, and let the hair down through the frost until it touches stone. The hair has a thick foot inside the breast, set in a little purse of fluid. Through it they can feel the grinders. A pillar half a mile high has a slow beat in it and a young one a quick beat, and even two pillars of precisely the same height can be told apart by the occasional catch in the teeth of some old beast that has fed on one side of its mouth for a century. The clear animals will go on setting their hair to a familiar spot long after a pillar has lost its colony and fallen silent. They lift it, move a foot or two and try again.

The use of this sense becomes apparent late in the day. The shadows then are several miles long, very slender at the tips, and swing across the country much faster than a running animal can follow. There comes a moment when the shadow of one pillar lays itself across the foot of the next. Along this brief junction the animals pass, not at a hurry but in their ordinary long, punctilious walk. If the junction is a little late in forming they wait within a toe’s breadth of the sun. They know where the next shadow will be by the sound of what stands at the head of it. An animal with an injured breast hair is apt to walk confidently out into the glare. The feet, almost all surface and very little substance, curl up first. More than one of the loose bright objects that lie around a colony melting holes in the frost is a whole small skeleton folded up inside its feet.

In one respect the young have an easier time. For the first part of their lives they are brown all over, with short legs and a hide like a seed coat. They can bear quite a bit of sun. The mother leaves them in the open at the end of winter, generally on a warm pebble already in use by a young grinder. They lie tucked up around its mouth, taking the bits that come loose between its teeth. For a few weeks the little grinder has the appearance of a plum wearing a rather untidy brown beard. The young pull away from it one at a time to turn over in the warmth; at night they pack themselves back into place, and the grinder, which would otherwise have to stop work at sundown, goes on chewing for an hour or so with its face wrapped up in them.

It is in this situation that they get their length. The legs grow folded under the coat. By the time a youngster is ready to leave, the skin of its belly has four long ridges in it and it can no longer get close enough to the stone to feed. It waits for the shadow to come over the pebble. Then the coat splits along the back and a creature of quite astonishing dimensions steps out, putting down one long wet foot after another, carefully, as if the ground were a great distance below. Its first act is to lower its breast hair. For weeks it has known only the loud, irregular chipping of a single pair of new teeth. Now through the wet skin of its feet, and much more distinctly through the hair, it has the whole country at once.

The empty coats stay where they are. They are light and tough, and every evening a little moisture gathers on the inner wall of each and runs down to the pebble. On a good pebble, one that has brought up many broods, there may be a collar of these husks a hand deep. The grinder grows into it. Long after it has put out its second rank of plates and got a tree shoot established in its belly, a fringe of brown can still be seen below its mouth. In the dark strips north of the pillars the clear animals sometimes live long enough to cross the sound of such a grinder again. There is then a curious interruption in the tracks. The commas come close together. Between them is a pinhole, and then another, and sometimes for the space of several minutes the frost is pierced over and over in the same place, while the shadow slips steadily away to the east.
