The first one you see is halfway up a dry wall, doing nothing to save itself. The wall has begun to come down. A little brown back sticks out of a crack, with a pale fringe on either side of it, and as the crack narrows the fringe disappears. You might suppose it had been drawn in, the way a snail draws in its horns. Then the back comes off in your hand. It is a lid, no thicker than the skin on boiled milk. The owner has gone on down through the wall, leaving its old coat to take the squeeze.

There are dozens of the lids. Where they have weathered, you can see daylight through the places that covered the eyes. Two clear windows look up and two look sideways, one at either selvage; there is no place from which the animal could have looked straight ahead. Nor, when you see a live one again, does it have much of an ahead. It comes broadside out from under a sill, a soft oblong the length of your thumb, with six pairs of feet disposed along its long edges. Each foot has a pad and, tucked up off the ground, a little bunch of fingers. On good going it walks on the pads. At a break in the surface the fingers come down, feel under the lip, and take a grip. It can pour all of itself after those fingers. You see its two upper eyes tilt toward one another and go down separately through a slit that would hardly admit the edge of its discarded coat.

They do not eat the walls, though for a long time it looks as if they do. All along the pink fork they have their mouths to the cuts. One goes over a place another has just left and finds quite as much there to please it. If you draw the lips of a cut apart a little, the attraction becomes plain. Beads of clear sap stand on it, each with a cuff of gray meal about its middle. It is the meal they take. A fed one has a thin gray cord running its whole width, visible through the belly when a roof turns it up to the light. Behind the row of feeding adults come little ones, no bigger than apple pips, whose fingers are not yet strong enough to pull the cuts open. They slip a foot under an adult and wait. Presently a pad lifts and the little one puts its whole face to the place the fingers are holding apart.

In dry weather several generations will keep to a single court. They have a use for one another even when there is nothing to eat. Down in the coolest rooms, where two flanges are almost in contact, you find twenty or thirty laid edge to edge. At the entrance the outermost animal has a free row of feet. The next one has hold of its inner row, pad to pad, and so on into the dark. A settling of the house runs the length of the company as a quite visible shiver. But after that has passed there goes a slower movement the other way. One foot lets go and takes hold again; the foot beside it does the same. At the mouth of the room the last brown animal gets up, turns its uncovered edge inward, and begins the laborious business of passing over everybody else to the back. A white one comes out. Its new coat is so soft that the impressions of the others’ fingers stay in it awhile. It lies there in the draught until it is brown.

Rain breaks up these households. Not at the first drop: at the first hollow knock of unlike eaves. The nearest animals come out onto the underside of the sill. There is an instant in which you can see their sideways eyes at work. The eyes turned toward you are dull; the far pair shine green with a roof that is not overhead. A few go across. Most put out just the fingers of one foot. From the other eave, where to your knowledge there has been no room for so much as a gnat, another bunch of fingers opens to meet them.

It is worth watching a pair that have found one another in this way. They take a surprisingly long time about it. Around them the wet roofs are pitching and banging. The fingers pass between one another and draw back, then pass between again, feeling down to the soft place where they join the pad. Whatever news a foot can give, they appear to require all of it. At last one animal turns belly up. The other settles over it. For a little while they have one outline and eight eyes looking in four directions; then they part, each going backward onto its own wall. Sometimes the drop between the eaves gives out before they have finished. There is no tearing in two. Both are left on whichever sill had the better purchase, an eastern animal with its almost transparent coat lying curled up for an hour in the unfamiliar heat of a western court.

Later in the season you can tell the wet streets of a month ago by the nurseries. The eggs are laid in the cast coats. A slit that has closed on a coat makes a particularly good lodging: the old back holds a pocket open behind it while the narrow mouth keeps out most of what comes scraping down the wall. Through each of the four windows you may catch the movement of a foot, and be surprised, when the coat lifts, to find not one creature under it but a score. At first they hold on to the inner surface even when it is quite free of the wall. They go about under it together, a brown lid shuffling up a stair, hesitating at the edge of a tread while all those little fingers investigate the drop. One by one they let go. For a day or two the smallest may be seen carrying the whole roof of its childhood on its back, propped up so high on one side that the sun goes clean under it.
