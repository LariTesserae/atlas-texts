The woman leaves the mat folded in half. She puts one knee on it and feels along the hem until she finds the seed that has worked its way out of its pocket. There are six of these pockets, one at each corner and one halfway down each long side. Her daughter, who made the mat, filled them with the hard black seeds of a tree that grows on the plain. They are good weights, but they work through a loose stitch like a tongue through a gap in the teeth. The woman takes a length of hair from behind her ear and sews the seed back in. By then the pull is the other way. The mat opens almost tenderly against her knees.

She is gathering the yellow gourds. They are ripe when the little dry collar at the stalk comes away with the fruit; if the collar stays on the vine the flesh will draw the inside of your mouth up small. She tips them out of her skirt onto the mat and rolls them home. Most people here have something meant for one purpose that serves for half a dozen. The mat is also her door. In the morning she likes to lie a while looking at the light that comes through the place where her daughter's fingers began to get the hang of the weave.

There are fewer people on the crown than the number of little stone roofs would lead you to suppose. A person may keep three lying-down places, and a child has a right to sleep at any pan it has helped to clean. The woman's youngest grandson has taken this quite literally. He goes about with a loop of soft root on his wrist, hoping to come upon somebody lifting a lid. He has beds as far away as he can see. So far he has slept in two, both within reach of his mother's hand.

He knows the red-legged lizards better than any of the grown people do. They live in the creeping orchard and lay their eggs on the bare rock under it, where the old leaves have made a felt. When a branch puts down roots, a new little space is walled off; sometimes you can lift a branch and find a room no bigger than the palm of your hand with a lizard and a single pink egg in it. He always lets the branch down very slowly. A lizard surprised in the open will go up on its toes and show two spots of such a violent blue that for a while afterward there are yellow spots wherever you look. It took him some time to discover that these last belonged to him and not to the world.

The people do very little calling out. A wave is of no great use either, with all that bright movement in the leaves. To get the attention of somebody a long way off, you stand on bare stone and hold one arm crooked over your head, making a hole for the sky to show through. You can do this with a baby on your hip or a pan under your other arm. When the other person makes the answering hole, you know you have been seen. What you want is another matter. The woman's sister once stood like that on a rise until well past noon. Two households came over to help her. She had found a gourd that looked exactly like their father, right down to the fold beneath his chin, and couldn't bear to cut it before they saw.

The white birds are surprisingly shabby close to. There is a rubbed place on the breast where the feathers part and show the gray of the skin. All day they have been going in and out of slots in the face of the crown, edging past their nearly grown young, which are too big for the nest and still insist on being fed there. What the old birds bring them is a pale, soft-bodied fly from the shaded face, not much of a meal one by one. A bird holds several dozen in the loose skin under its bill. From below you might see one come to rest on a ledge and look absently up at the sky while a young one takes its whole face in its mouth.

At evening a few of the young follow their parents up. It is these that miss the lift. They go bowling along the stone, running on their big dangling feet, and fetch up in the fern with an expression the boy knows well from having been carried farther than he wished by someone holding each of his hands. The old ones circle back. They do not feed a bird once it is up on the crown, however piteously it opens its beak. There are things in the gourd flowers that come out at dusk, fat enough to make an audible tick when the beak shuts on them. Presently the young bird shuts its mouth and begins to look about.

When the woman reaches her shelter the boy is up on its roof, watching one of these birds work along the green. There is just enough sound left in the stone to come through the mat when she leans it against the doorway. She cuts a gourd in half and with her thumbnail loosens the seeds. In a little while he will slide down backward and hold out his hand for his half without turning his head. For now she leaves him where he is. The bird has come to a break in the ferns. It steps out onto the open pink rock, sees its shadow go along with it, and steps back in.
