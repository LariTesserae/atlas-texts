The boy who waits for the blue cloth thinks of the court as having two sorts of inhabitants, those who have to be taken home at night and those who are allowed to stay. He would give a good deal to be one of the second sort. He wants to see the toad come out from under the footrest. He has seen it go in hundreds of times. It seems a great injustice that a toad of that size can get out again without anybody seeing.

His mother washes for a woman who sells cheese. The blue cloth goes over the cheeses on market days, and has a little dark spot where a very good cheese once leaked oil through all the layers in which it was wrapped. The boy knows which is his end by the spot. He has been promoted from holding a corner to holding the whole short side. There is a still smaller child who would like the corner and stands about with her fingers pinched together in readiness, rather to his annoyance.

The woman at the trough has a name, Sena, though hardly anybody in the court uses it. There isn't much call to use names where you can't hear them. She knows who wants water by the way a paddle is laid down. Flat means that the woman is going to stretch her back or see to a child; laid across the lip of the basin it means she is ready. Anybody can pull the peg. The knack is in putting it back against the weight of the water. Sena lets the bigger children pull it, and closes her hand over theirs to put it back. Up in the street there are people going about with grandchildren of their own who still remember the surprising weight that came on their hands when they thought the interesting part was over.

Not everyone at the benches takes washing home. One woman comes down with nothing in her basket but her dinner and takes whatever work there is too much of. She has a long white scar on her shin from a bench that used to stand the other way round. There are two sisters who wash at opposite ends of the court. They have to talk to one another at home and are glad of the distance. There is a young father who brings the baby's squares before the ovens are thoroughly hot. He does them rather slowly, examining each one against a roof hole, because if he goes back too early his wife will get up, and he has been advised that the best thing for her is sleep. Nobody offers to help him hurry any more.

Of all the creatures entitled to stay, the pigeons are the least attached to the place. Give them a fine afternoon and the whole roof is empty except for whoever is on the eggs. The sitter has to endure a great many faces coming level with hers at the turn of the stair. Most are familiar. The cheese woman's washer has a loose tooth she pushes into place with her tongue just there, before taking her basket off her hip to go round the turn. The boy used to put one eye very close to the jar handle until the pigeon struck him on the eyebrow with her wing. They look at one another now from a civil distance.

The black beetles, on the other hand, have hardly any occasion to go beyond the trough. There is always a meal to be had where a dinner basket has stood, and it is never quite dry under the peg. Sometimes one comes out powdered white from an investigation of a bread crust, and for a few steps leaves exquisite black footprints on its own back as it tries to clean itself. The little girl who wants the corner will watch this as long as it goes on. She has not yet reached the age of wanting small things to do something else.

In the hour after the washing, when a person on the landing can hear wings being drawn quite softly through the passages between the sheets, the boy gets his wish more often than he knows. His mother eases the blue cloth down. He gets his two fistfuls of selvedge, walks backward, and lifts his arms high. For a moment there is a low blue ceiling over the footrest, with light coming through the thin places and a round dark moon where the cheese oil spread. Under it the toad works one forefoot clear, then the other, and comes out in plenty of time to see the family leave.
