The little brown dippers are easiest to see in winter, when there is a white shelf along either side of a run and the bird has to cross the snow to get from one stretch of open water to the next. In summer a person can follow one quite a way without seeing it. There will be a bubble the size of a currant going downstream, and then a wet print with three toes pointed up the bank. The bird itself is under a lip of stone getting at the little cases the caddis larvae make out of grit.

One pair has a nest under the east edge of a school. This is a good situation for a dipper. There is a brace with just room on top of it for a globe of moss, and the whole weight of the school above it to keep the rain off. The children can see into the mouth of the nest from the paving at the end of their cloakroom. A piece of carpet has been put down there for their knees. Early in the season there is very little to see except the yellow corner of a bill. Later, four heads fill the opening, and there is some discussion as to what has become of the mother, who could not possibly fit in there with them.

When the pair began to build, the water ran right under the brace. By the time there were young, it ran behind the bakery, a flight of nearly half a mile each way. The male went back to the old run for several days. He would light on a certain knob and go through two or three of the customary knee bends, looking down at a bed of perfectly dry pebbles. Then he would fly off behind the bakery. There is a mark on the knob like a dab of white paint, renewed often enough that the children use it to find the place.

The woman who gets the school ready in the morning sees a good deal more of these birds than the children do. She comes on the first car, with the people who have to put the bread in. It is part of her work to walk to the four edges of the school raft and try the little flights of steps that let themselves down onto the rock. A step sometimes hangs free, or comes down with one corner in a pool. She blocks that exit until someone from the district can come and shift it. On cold mornings a dipper will be singing under the steps in the dark. It has a long, rather headlong song for such a compact bird. She waits until it has finished before she tries the tread.

Her father kept sheep below the cloud, and for her first few years up here she missed the smell of the ground. A warm afternoon would come, the windows of the cloakroom would be opened, and there would be no answering warmth of earth or crushed stems, only a smell like the inside of an empty cup. She knows where to go for it now. Beyond the district laundry is a hollow where the geese have rested for as long as anyone has kept records. The turf there has a little spring in it. If she puts her heel down hard, brown water comes up round her shoe, and for a yard or so downwind it might be an evening in the lambing fields.

In June one of the children found a young dipper in the cloakroom. It had flown in through the open exit and settled among the toes of the boots, where its speckled breast looked very much like a wet woollen sock. The woman shut the inner door, propped the outer one wide, and had the children come round by the kitchen. From time to time she looked in through the pane. For nearly an hour the bird stayed beside a pair of red boots with silver buckles. Then it was gone. That afternoon she saw it on the white-marked knob. Its tail was barely long enough to lift. It bent its knees, looked down for a while at the dry stones, and set off in the direction of the sound of water.
