There is an eel in the room. On a low tide you may see six inches of it in the dish nearest the stair and another inch in the fourth dish along. The two bits are much the same color as the mortar when the wet is going out of it. If you put a finger near the smaller bit, the larger one goes away.

Its head is broad for an eel. A pale seam runs from the corner of its mouth to a place behind the eye where a black crab once got hold of it. The seam shows best when the head is coming toward you. The rest of the time the most conspicuous thing about the animal is the little clear fin behind its gill. This will go on fanning after the eel has settled to the bottom of a dish. It keeps a bit of shrimp shell turning over and over, the same pink bit, as long as there is water enough.

Under the floor there is a second set of hollows. The mortar was dearer than the dishes, which were seconds from a pottery, and the men used only as much as they needed to bed the rims. A little way in from each rim there is room for a finger, then room for a wrist, then the belly of the dish comes down again. In these places the eel spends the greater part of its time. It has been longer than a dish for some years now. To lie at ease it must dispose of itself in two hollows, with the part that joins them flattened considerably. There is one passage in which it can do this facing either way. It has polished the pottery there as smooth on the unglazed side as on the side people see.

It comes out late on the ebb. By then the fish near the stair has accounted for most of the easy pickings, but the eel is after a different thing. In the dish that drains under its neighbor there is a moment when everything too light to keep its footing comes to the crack together. The eel lies below with its nose a little short of the opening. A crab going through will spread all its legs and catch. A shrimp goes through lengthwise, very neatly. Often the eel has no more to do than open its mouth. Occasionally a shrimp comes down on its feet on the flat of the eel's head and stands there feeling about with its whiskers. The eel has no arrangement for dealing with this. It waits for the shrimp to get off.

The woman who brings the bucket knows the eel chiefly by the feel of it. As a girl she came down in summer with her skirts tucked up and a little brother on her hip; something passed between her bare ankles, warm, she would have said, though it could hardly have been warmer than the water. Her brother felt it too, by the grip she took on him. He is the one who brings the bucket now when the stair is icy. Both speak of it as the same eel. There is no good way of knowing. It may be that any eel living under the dishes would learn to come out by that crack, and any woman carrying a child would stand just there, with a sound dish under either foot.

This morning, while the bucket fills, the eel has its head in the open by the patch of yellow. A very small white crab is busy at the pale seam behind its eye. There is something lodged there, a grain perhaps. The crab gets its two claws on it and leans back until only the hindmost points of its legs are still touching the glaze. For a moment the skin of the eel comes up in a little tent. Then the grain gives. The crab sits down hard in the brook. The eel remains quite still as the water thins around its jaw, and the crab, having turned the grain about and found nothing in it, goes back to the same place to see what else there is.
