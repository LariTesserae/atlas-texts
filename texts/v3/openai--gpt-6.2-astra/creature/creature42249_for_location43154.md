The grey birds have a nursery. One would not look for it away from the herd, since even a dead browser will carry its birds for a day or two. They hang in the long belly hair and go on picking out the salt. But in the evening, when two strips of country draw level, a bird will sometimes leave the warmth of a living animal for the bare end of a strip on which there is nothing to eat. It runs out to the last inch of glass, puts its head down, and goes over.

There are blisters under the lip with holes in them. The holes are the work of a little flat animal, yellow as a pear pip, which rasps its way out of the glass as the glass cools. A fresh hole is too small to admit much more than the bill of a bird. This is sufficient. The young bird does not go in through the hole. It is laid there, while the blister is still open at the back and no thicker than the skin on hot milk. By the time it hatches there may be an arm’s length of sound green stone between it and the place where its mother stood to lay.

From below, with the cloud down and a good wash of light on the cliff, one can see several nurseries at once. In one blister there is an egg, in the next a damp bird with its great feet drawn up beside its head, in the next what looks like a full-grown grey bird preserved in a bottle. At feeding time the bottle grows a thin red tongue. The old bird brings up fruit pulp and a few of the pale mites from its throat. For a little while after a meal the young one is visible down to the last quill, warm against the glass, and then it takes its feet off the wall and subsides into a ball of down.

It is not the teeth of the wheels that interest it. A tooth may pass so close as to bend the floor under the egg without disturbing the bird inside. What it attends to, almost from hatching, is the little yellow animal. This has a row of hard knobs along its back. It turns about night and day in the hole it has made, eating the syrup that sets there and wearing away a little more glass with every turn. The young bird puts its bill to the inner end of the hole and feels the knobs go by. Long after the hole is wide enough for the bird to come out, it stays where it is.

The first thing to leave is a foot. It is an ugly mauve object, ludicrously large in the clear air. It opens and shuts a few times. If there is nothing to catch hold of, back it goes. Sometimes a blister travels to the extreme horn of the country with that one foot fishing out of it at intervals, and travels all the way back. The opportunity comes when a herd lies down. Long belly hair hangs over the cliff then, within reach of the toes. The young bird catches hold with both feet and pulls. Its head comes through quite suddenly, with a little ruff of yellow crumbs about the neck. For the first few days it rides facing inward and takes no part in the easy traffic under the animal. It has spent its life with a wall at its back; the feel of air there makes it sick.

One can see why a bird should want a browser. It is less plain what use a browser has for quite so many birds. Certainly a good deal of salt and dead skin is removed, but a solitary animal without birds has a finer coat than any in the herd. The difference is more noticeable when the herd lies down. All along a hundred yards of ridge the grey heads come out of the hair and the grey bills are laid sideways on the stone. Now and then a young bird will go on picking at a fly until an old one reaches over and holds its head down for it. They lie so for perhaps twenty breaths. Then some of the heads come up and some stay down. Where they stay down the long hair begins to shiver. The browser feels it. First the feet close hard on the ridge; then the animal rises, often with a whole fringe of birds still stretched between its belly and the ground.

A watcher once marked a place a herd had left in this manner by driving a white twig into the root mat. Nothing happened to the spot. It bore two good crops of pears. The white twig put forth a spray of rather inferior green leaves. The herd returned, went down on its bellies, got up and left again. It was the next ridge over that went into the cloud, taking the storm that lived there with it. For a week afterward there was pink lightning deep below the grove, so far down that one could take a pear in both hands and turn it quite round between the flash and the faint illumination of its underside.

What the birds felt, in all likelihood, was the beginning of the fall. What troubled the browsers was losing the way back. There are animals on strips no longer than their own bodies that have been there for years. They eat when a crown goes by. In dry weather the birds bring them water, not much at a time, in the loose skin under the tongue. Their calves learn to stand with all four feet almost in a line. When at last such a strip runs in alongside the mainland, the old animals are reluctant to step off. They stand smelling the acres of good warm glass as a beast in another country might stand smelling the sea.

The little rasper also comes out in the end. After the young bird is gone it works its way into the empty blister and eats the syrup off the inner wall. This leaves the blister beautifully clear. On a still evening an entire cliff may be set with small, unaccountably bright views: a fern bent over black mud, three silver fish nose to tail, the blue of clear air above a long slope of grass. A rasper crossing such a view seems a very large animal indeed. Its yellow back goes laboring up the slope; its shadow falls across a mile of sunny country. Then it reaches a little syrup in the corner, curls itself comfortably into the drop, and the light goes out.
