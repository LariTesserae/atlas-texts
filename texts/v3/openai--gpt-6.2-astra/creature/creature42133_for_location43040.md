The florist at the terminus keeps the same hours as the tram. She sells a great many buttonholes and very few bouquets. A bouquet is an awkward thing to bring home on a crowded car, and on the walk from the stop to the houses there is nothing to break the wind. Her best customer is the shunter. Every Saturday he buys whatever has a stem long enough to go in the little glass tube he wears behind his lapel. The tube is meant for a sample of boiler water. He has still to remember to take the flower out before he goes to work on Monday. Sometimes, along the whole length of the cabbage train, there is a smell of stocks.

His daughter looks after the goats on the north face. From the town they appear to be very much of a size, but seen from close by they range from a buck as heavy as a kitchen range to a late kid which she brings back in the pocket of her coat. The kid likes travelling backwards. It keeps its chin on the flap and watches the ground come away from under her boots. There is good grazing on the oldest ledges, where the wind has been emptying itself for a hundred years of everything it picked up in the valleys: husks, thistledown, a surprising quantity of fine yellow sand. On the newer ledges the goats will lick the rock for the sake of a salt that comes to the surface for a day or two and is gone by the next rain. They know the taste of a new ledge but not its strength. This is chiefly what the girl is there for. She has a long crook with a fork in it exactly the width of a goat's ankle.

In wet weather she takes the herd home by the laundry. The women come out at the sound of the bells and let the kids suck the warm ends of their fingers. The blue snails are a nuisance to these women, though nobody much minds the frogs. A snail can get right inside the rolled hem of a good tablecloth and go through the wash unharmed; it is under the iron that the damage is done. There is a woman solely employed in holding the damp cloth up to a bright pane and going round its four edges with her thumb. The snails show as little dark commas. She puts them in a jelly jar, with a piece of loose knitting over the mouth to give them something to do on the way home. Her windowsill is nowhere near a heating pipe. Nevertheless she has made quite a good garden there by the simple expedient of saving the hot water from her eggs.

There is a family in the east ward who have been listening to the same hare for nine years. They know it by a slight drag in the second beat. The others give two neat knocks, as if to draw attention to a mistake on a bill; this one gives a knock and a scuff. At first it lived below the scullery. As the chambers filled in from beneath it moved up a little and sideways, until now it is more or less behind the bread bin. The youngest boy, who has the bedroom above, can hear it pulling grass through the narrow entrance to its chamber, a long squeak followed by a click as the seed head comes through. He has seen any number of hares on the boulevard but would like very much to see this one. His idea is that it has one slipper on.

Mice live even farther out than the hares. The first sign of mice on a new shelf is generally an owl sitting in midair. With good glasses a bit of stick can be seen under the owl, and under the stick, if there is any moisture about, a faint oval of condensation. It takes a certain thickness of mountain to give back the light. By the time a comfortable plum color has developed, the mice have usually had several litters and the owl has worn the bark off its perch. Nobody knows how the stick gets there. The municipal surveyors, who are required to account for everything of the nature of timber on a site, put it down as driftwood. They are thoughtful men and have left a blank on the form for the name of the river.

One of these men comes out to the terminus on Thursdays to measure the piece beyond the car floor. He takes his lunch in the florist's upstairs room and is careful not to say whether it will be a street or a bit of common ground. There are people already saving up for a shop there. But he will tell you what is living in the puddle. At the moment there are three red threads that coil up tight at the passage of a shadow, and a beetle small enough to hang upside down from the skin of the water without making a dimple visible from above. The magpie comes back to drink and has learned to wait after putting its beak in. It takes a little while for water to run into the impression left by the beak. On the other side of the glass the surveyor waits too, a spoonful of cabbage halfway to his mouth.

On the most recent Thursday there was something else. One of the tufts had produced a flower, a poor pale affair on a bent stalk, all its petals facing the ticket hall. Towards evening a moth came out of the florist's shop. It had been asleep all day on the brown paper in which she wraps the stocks; she had cut round it twice. It flew down the empty car, rising a little to clear each seat back, and out through the doors on the other side. For a moment they lost it against the snow of the far mountain. Then the bent stalk went down under its weight and came up again somewhat short of where it had been. The driver put off ringing his bell. With the current out and nobody boarding, he could hear quite distinctly the moth's wings striking the grass.
