The woman who lives behind the pay office can tell the three rows of houses apart by their doors. In the front row a door opens over the floor with room to spare. In the middle row it brushes the flags in a quarter circle, and in the back row you have to put your knee to one of the lower panels and lift a little as you push. She sews for people in all three rows. Since her fingers have begun to stiffen she has given up buttonholes, but she will still turn a collar so well that a man will wear through the second side without ever having found where she put the stitches.

Her name is Enna. She used to work at the dry end, counting sheets into stacks. There are two hollow places above her collarbones in which the fine brown dust used to collect. At home she would wet the corner of a towel and wipe first one and then the other, as regularly as anybody else wipes their glasses. She still counts things that pass her in fairly quick succession. A party of ducks can give her quite an uncomfortable feeling if some of them go behind a boat.

A boy comes to her house after school. He is her daughter’s youngest, a long boy with a beautiful singing voice which he will use to imitate the whistle for low steam but for very little else. He likes the notion of working on a barge. What he likes about it, as far as Enna can discover, is the little round window in the sleeping place, through which you might see at night a single house going by. He has been over every barge on the landing and knows which windows open and which have been painted shut. He doesn’t like the smell of the sleeping places. That is something he expects to get over.

They have a small disagreement about the creatures at the back step. Down in the groove of the old sill live some little brown frogs, hardly longer than the last joint of Enna’s thumb. In daylight there is nothing to see of them. If you part the mint at supper time you may find a pair of eyes level with the clear water; then the water gives a kick and is muddy. The boy maintains that the frogs come up the drain. Enna thinks they live under the stone. Once, on a perfectly dry morning, she lifted the earthenware saucer from under her geranium and found a frog flattened against the bottom of it, one small damp hand spread on the red clay. This seems to her to have some bearing on the question, though the boy says he doesn’t see what.

In summer she leaves the back door open while she sews. People come up that way to see her, cutting between the office wall and the herb bed; it saves the whole length of the first lane. A bargeman with a split in the seat of his good trousers comes in carrying them over his arm in a newspaper. The girl who minds the cutter brings nothing, just sits for ten minutes in the low chair. She is a big, cheerful girl out of doors. In Enna’s house she likes to sit without speaking, her knees well apart, her two strong hands hanging down between them. It takes a little while for her eyes to stop following the motion of the needle. When she gets up she generally takes a leaf of mint and folds it into the back of her mouth.

Enna knows these people better in some respects than their families do, and in others hardly at all. She knows the bargeman has put on an inch since winter and that he carries something with four sharp corners in his left pocket. She had no idea, until she saw them leaving together one Sunday, that he and the cutter girl were father and daughter. Once seen side by side they had exactly the same walk, putting down their heels rather carefully and then letting the rest of the foot fall all at once.

On pay evenings she sets another chair outside the office for the people who would rather not sit on the slant. Usually the boy gets there first and sits on it backward, with his chin on the rail. From there he can question the boatmen as they come out. He wants to know where they tie up, what they do with their boots when they turn in, whether the lamp swings or is made fast. Enna has noticed that the part of the voyage which interests him begins later and later. Lately all his questions have been about lying in the dark.

One evening the bargeman stops by the boy’s chair and tells him that if he comes down now he can see for himself. They aren’t going anywhere. There is half an hour before the light goes. Enna watches them go down to the landing, the man still carrying his trousers in their parcel and the boy taking care to walk at the man’s pace. She puts her work away. She can hear a frog quite close to her, making a noise like a thumbnail drawn across the teeth of a comb.

When the boy comes back his hair is standing up at one side from where he has been lying on the bunk. He has very little to report. From the pillow the round window shows nothing but the underside of the landing, green boards with water trembling along their edges. But if you put your head at the other end, where your feet are supposed to go, you can see the bench, and the office wall, and Enna’s lighted doorway with the mint growing up across the bottom. He had lain there a good while, he says, in case she came out.
