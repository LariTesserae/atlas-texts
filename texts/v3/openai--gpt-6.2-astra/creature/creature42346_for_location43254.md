There is a creature in the old forks that people generally meet one foot at a time. A bare sole comes down on something warm; there is a moment's pressure in return, quite deliberate, and the thing goes away. A child will stand on the spot for a long time afterward, first on one foot and then on the other. It very seldom happens twice.

It is called a seam-runner. The woman who tends the crossing has seen a whole one, in the face of a piece of turf that turned over. It was about the length of her forearm, brown on top, cream underneath, with small damp ears folded forward over the openings. What she had taken for a very wide body proved to be a narrow body lying on its side with its legs stretched out. The older layers of felt come apart more readily than any one of them tears. The runner lives in the parting. Its fur will brush either way, and when something stops it ahead it goes backward at scarcely any loss of speed. In the open, on the turned turf, it had a good deal of trouble turning over onto its feet. She put a corner of her apron over it. Under that little weight it righted itself and was gone.

Much of the food up here comes down through the leaves in the hour after the birds pass. There are wings, of course, which are hardly worth a runner's while, but there are also singers with a leg gone, still working the pegs on their bellies, and grubs shaken loose from the ends of holes. The white mice get the best of these unless something holds them back. A runner will lie along the underside of a much-used mouse road and put one forefoot up through it. It has a long bare palm. The first mouse to come down the road finds that palm applied gently to its face and backs into the mouse behind. While the business is being looked into, the runner works its way along beneath them, taking the grubs out of the floor. One sometimes sees a dozen little white heads emerge in daylight for no apparent reason, all looking exceedingly put out.

The runners have something to do with the good going at the crossing. They carry the tough outer skins of roots a considerable distance to line the chambers where they have their young. These strips do not lie tidily along the chamber wall. A strip is pulled in until it catches, bitten off, and the end pushed out somewhere else. When the chamber falls in there is a sort of loose darning left behind. The willow wand goes through this with a series of quick soft pulls unmistakably different from the catches of undisturbed felt. The woman leaves such places alone for a season; afterward they will often bear a post where a post would have leaned before.

Her daughter once found a young runner at the bottom of the pothole near the rim. It had come down the raw edge to drink, apparently, and the height it had descended almost without noticing was too much for it going back. It went round and round the pebble. Each time it came to the side toward the turf it rose on its hind feet and spread its forepaws against the stone. You could see the little pink fans of them through the water. The girl put in her hand. It would not climb onto it. Only when she laid her hand flat across the pebble, leaving a dark interval underneath, did it go in there and settle, with its wet cheek against her smallest finger. She lifted it out between her hands.

For some days she had a wish to tell this which came on her particularly in the mornings, when there was no possibility of being heard. By the time the birds had passed there were cups to be sounded, or somebody had brought a pipe all the way from the south fork because of a troublesome low note. When at last she had her mother's ear she found there was very little to tell. Instead she took her mother's hand, made a roof of it against her knee, and slipped two fingers under it. She turned them a little on their sides and let them rest there. Her mother, who remembered the weight of the apron, knew quite well what she meant.
