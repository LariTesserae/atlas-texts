Before the bath closed, the attendant knew most of the people in the street by the way they got into the pool. She could sit with her back to it, mending a towel, and feel the arrivals in the folds of skin behind her ears. The freight clerk went in headfirst and turned over on his back without touching tile. His two boys made such an elaborate business of easing themselves over the rim one toe at a time that she generally felt them coming for a full minute. They had once been put out for jumping, and wished her to understand that they had taken the lesson to heart.

They were long people, rather lightly put together for people who handled so much stone. A grown person could stand on the floor of a house and lay a palm flat on the ceiling without getting off their heels. The legs accounted for very little of that height. Much of it was in the body, which had room on either side of the spine for a pair of breathing chambers extending nearly to the hip. A sleeping person on a foot loop slowly subsided and rose again. The children liked to slip a finger into the slack of the loop at the top of the rise and have it gently caught at the bottom. This was one way of waking a parent without waking the other one.

Their hands looked more serviceable than they were. The fingers were square at the ends, with a dark nail folded over each tip; but across the palm there ran three raised lines as tender as the inside of a lip. A cut straight across those lines could put a joiner out of work for a season. The gloves that turned up sometimes in consignments to the summit always wore out in the wrong places. People cut the palms out of them and wore the backs, to protect the knuckles from the walls.

With the lines on the palm they could feel where a bearing began and, with practice, how much it would take. Making one was another matter. In the freight office there is a practice frame, a wooden square no bigger than a slate, with a plummet tethered to the bottom of it. The pupil had to lay water across the frame and set the plummet on it point down. For a long time it seemed necessary to hold one's breath to do this. Then came the surprising day when somebody asked a question, and the pupil turned and answered, and the plummet was still there. That was about as far as most people got. A mother could set a cup down beside her in midwater while she did up a child's coat. She would not have cared to walk under a landing of her own making.

The last person to live all year at the summit was the younger of the freight clerk's boys. By then he was an old man with one breathing chamber gone stiff, obliged to sleep with a little weight in his left pocket to keep from turning. There were only two carts a year to see in, one in either direction. For several years he kept up the six houses much as if their occupants had gone to the bath. Then a shutter split while he was trying to free it, and he began to distinguish between things that wanted doing and things he merely disliked seeing undone. He went down on the autumn cart with the shutter still split. It is the one with the elbow on it. The split follows the pale stone for a little way and then goes off on its own.

The white animal is no relation of theirs, although from below its feet give a rather touching impression of having fingers. Each sole is in fact a single lip. To take a step it rolls the lip back from one edge, admitting a little water under it, and fastens on again farther along. It can remain attached by any one foot. In this way it cleans the other three, bringing them in turn up to its mouth and drawing the narrow tooth across them. Its head really is too heavy for it. If all four feet come loose together, it goes down face first and must wait until it strikes something. There is very little in the way of a lower jaw, but what there is is thickened underneath, like the toe of a work boot.

In the time of the carts the animals lived chiefly in the wheel notches. There was always a little salt there, renewed by the passage of a load, and the floor of the notch gave a young one two surfaces to catch on when it lost its footing. Now they must go farther out. The bearings keep perfectly well without traffic; it is the food along their edges that grows poor. An animal will sometimes spend a day going the length of a wheel track and come back with its dark little stomach no fuller than before. It has no eyes. When it reaches the end of a track it extends one forefoot and feels about for the continuation, exactly as it does at a gap of a grain's breadth. It cannot tell the difference between a grain's breadth and the open sky except by putting its foot there.

Once each year the autumn cart still stops at the quay. There is no freight for the summit. The driver brings his daughter along, and while he is below inspecting the column at the court, she lies on her stomach at the end of the stone. She has found that if she sets the brass hubcap of the spare wheel down on the lane, and leaves it there while they eat, a fine white crescent forms along its downhill edge. By the time they are ready to go there is usually an animal at the crescent. This last autumn there were two. Her father had the wheel cover off for nearly an hour after he wanted it back. When he came up the stair to fetch it himself he found her with one cheek against the quay, listening to them eat. He lay down on the other side. Each could hear the tooth on their own side quite distinctly and could hardly hear the other one at all.
