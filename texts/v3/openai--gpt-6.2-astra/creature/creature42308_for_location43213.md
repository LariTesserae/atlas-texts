If you put a hand on the middle stone and stayed there a while, a little animal would come and drink from the edge of your palm. It would come from below, where you could not see it without leaning over and putting it in shadow. Then it would be gone. The feel of it would be much like having one of your own fine hairs caught under the heel of your hand and drawn slowly out.

There is a shrew in the hollow. She is rather smaller than the impression one gets of her in motion. A good deal of that impression is nose. It goes on ahead of her over the grit, lifting and laying its two sides against things in quick succession, as if there were a fork in the way at every grain and she had to choose. Her fur is the gray of the underside of the ledge. Along her flanks there is a faint division where the hairs lie one way above and another way below. She can pass through a space that presses this fur quite flat and emerge looking round and dry.

She sleeps behind the squared stone. It was not quite squared at the back. One corner broke away under the tool, and when the stone was fitted into place it roofed over a pocket in the bank. There is dry earth in the upper part of the pocket. A root as fine as sewing thread comes through the ceiling there and holds together a little shelf, on which she has made a bed of the insides of grass blades. She brings them in bent double, which is why a blade sometimes works loose at the entrance and stands for a day or two in a place where no grass could take root.

She knows both sides of the sill. On the water side she works with her hind feet braced and her forefeet in the wet, turning flakes of yellow stone with her nose. There are soft, jointed things beneath them, colorless until she lifts them into the honey light. On the earth side she runs the length of the ledge. A beetle slipping from the gray bed onto the overhang of the yellow one makes a sound too slight to carry across the basin, but she can be there before it has righted itself. Now and then she goes as far as the second tuft of grass. She returns by a different line, passing under the edges of the flat stones wherever the earth has shrunk a little away from them. Seen from above, these journeys would seem to cost her a great deal of unnecessary turning. Down where her eyes are, the floor is full of eaves.

There are young in the pocket now, three of them. Their heads seem heavy for them. They have begun to leave the bed, though not yet the hollow, and when the mother moves them they go in a chain: the first takes a mouthful of fur near her rump, the next takes hold of the first, and the smallest, after some blind nosing at the stone, finds the last. For a moment the sill has something like a thick, badly made cord being pulled along its foot. At the joint it goes to pieces. Each small animal has to cross the damp for itself, lifting its feet high at the unfamiliar coolness, and on the other side they assemble again.

By the time the light leaves the tamarisk they are all behind the stone. Nothing has been displaced that you would notice. One of the white rims has a pinprick in it where a forefoot came down. A grass blade stands with its pale fold uppermost beside the sill. Between one drop and the next, if your ear were against the level top where a cup once stood, you might hear the young turning over in the roofed place underneath.
