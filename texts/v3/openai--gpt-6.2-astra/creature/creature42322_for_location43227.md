There is a small gray beast under the shelf whose tracks have several times been taken for the tracks of two beasts traveling together. The forefeet leave neat little hands in the dust, with a space between the fingers and the ball of the hand. The hind feet leave a pair of long strokes, generally off to one side of the hands and sometimes well ahead of them. On the polished parts of the floor there is of course no mark at all. A set of hands may go up to the edge of the old market and a set of strokes come out of it forty yards away, as though the two creatures had gone into the market to exchange legs.

It is about the length of a glasses case. Most of this is body. There is very little head in front of the ears, and behind there is only a short plump tail, flattened on its underside like a heel. It can stand on this and its great folded hind feet and do quite delicate work with its hands. A favorite food is the seed in the orange flowers. By the time the seed is worth having the flower has shriveled into a tough brown purse. The beast holds the purse by its neck, works a nail into the seam and draws the two sides apart. It eats the seeds and eats also the little white grubs in the seeds that have grubs in them. The emptied purse is often left hanging on the plant. There are bushes in the clefts on which every purse has been opened, down to the height at which a rather short animal standing on its tail can reach.

It is tempting to suppose that it eats the builders. In fact a builder walking over its foot gets much the same treatment as a drop of water: a quick shake, followed, if that has not answered, by an exasperated wipe on the ground. It is after something else when it goes into the streets. There is a little glossy roach which lives very well on the shavings that come loose at the corners where the files turn. When disturbed it runs straight for the dark. The gray beast puts one hand over the nearest window and noses about in the litter with its eyes shut. Presently there is a roach under the hand. A young beast will try this on a flat wall and be kept quite busy covering one window after another; an old one chooses a corner where the bottom stories are bricked up all but for a single hole.

For sleeping quarters it wants a crack in the limestone itself. There is one behind the stately row of owl houses which goes a surprising way back. A thin wash of afternoon light reaches just far enough into it to show a sill of pinkish stone and, on the sill, what looks like a dirty scrap of fleece. The fleece consists of the backs of five or six of the gray beasts. They sleep with their noses in toward one another and the big naked soles of their feet turned out. For a good part of the day even an owl sitting in the mouth of the crack would have some difficulty in knowing what to take hold of.

The young are pink-soled for a long time after they have got their gray coats. They are allowed out on the sill before they are allowed down. One ventures to the edge, puts both hands over, considers the distance, and backs into the heap so abruptly that another youngster is thrust out at the far end. Down below, in the street, the builders go by with pieces of leaf held up on edge. The young follow each piece with a small turn of the head. From the solemnity with which they attend to this procession one would think useful instruction was being given.

They have a way of calling one another which only becomes apparent in the unnatural quiet that follows a cold night, when the crickets are late getting started. It is not a voice. The animal lays the side of its jaw against the floor and brings one hind heel down twice, lightly and very quickly. At a little distance the two taps make a single sound like the breaking of a hair in a comb. A beast receiving the call lays its jaw down too. One can see them doing this on quite noisy days, when the sound in the air is enough to trouble the sight, and there is no doubt from the promptness of the answers that down in the stone it is perfectly quiet.

The old oval is the best place for it. A call made there will bring heads out of cracks well beyond the last standing tower. In the dusk, when the gray of a coat and the gray of the floor are so nearly alike that it is easier to watch the space beside the animal than the animal itself, a whole party will assemble there before setting out for the groves. There is a good deal of touching of noses. One sits up suddenly and washes an ear. Another goes three or four bounds in the direction of the nearer cleft, finds that nobody has come, and returns at a walk. Eventually they are all off, not in a file like the builders but loosely abreast, the youngest on the outside having to make up a great deal of unnecessary distance.

On the far side of the oval there is a low worn step at which the party separates, some going up over it and some taking the longer way round. It is scarcely the height of a thumb. The little beasts that go round are the old ones, whose hocks no longer open readily after a day spent folded in the crack. They know a short way through the first block and are generally out among the fallen leaves, opening purses, before the others arrive.
