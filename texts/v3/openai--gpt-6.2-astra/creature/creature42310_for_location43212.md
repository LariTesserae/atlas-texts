There is a little whiskered animal on the plain which is easiest to find by offering it a cup. Lay the cup on its side on a tolerably dry place, with the handle toward you, and keep a finger through the handle. After a while, often a considerable while, the cup will turn a quarter turn away from the weight of your finger, and then a little way back. An animal has got into it and is trying the roof with its shoulders. Two turns usually mean that it has found the roof satisfactory. It is bad manners to take the cup up after that.

They are called hems. A large one, stretched out, is about the length of the distance between the two bones of the wrist and the first joint of the thumb. There seems to be very little to one except the shoulders. The hind part is soft and tapers away, without any perceptible division, into a tail with a flattened end. But the shoulders are square and hard. A hem can insert itself under a slab of lime that a man would have some difficulty lifting at one end. It turns on its back, gets its forefeet against its breast, and by a series of little shrugs works the slab up on the flat of its skull. What it wants under there are the white egg strings of a kind of shrimp. It eats them as one might eat a length of buttered twine, walking backward over its tail.

Nobody who handles a hem can account for the idea, common in the dry country, that they are the drowned young of some larger animal. They have excellent teeth and a great deal of business of their own. Possibly the idea has come from the habit the females have, while suckling a litter, of taking hold of the little finger of a person who has disturbed the nest. It is an unpleasantly deliberate grip. She does not bite or try to pull the finger in any particular direction. After a moment one understands that she is waiting for one to put the roof back.

There were hems well out on the lime before there was anything there that a land animal could reasonably live on. The first crews, sounding for the foundations of the works, used to come on their runs scored into the undersides of slabs. A run fits the back of a hem exactly, with two fine grooves just ahead of the shoulders where the long whiskers lie folded. Some of the runs ended at the sawn faces of the foundation blocks, and the men who bedded the blocks in cement took the trouble to cut continuations in the cement with their trowels. It cost them very little time. In one place a man made a little vaulted room with the back of his spoon. There have been young in that room every spring since the works opened. There are said to have been young in it before then, but this rests on the recollection of a man who was away at sea at the time, and who admits that he has trouble with that particular spring.

Of the two attendants, the elder has a hem living in the cuff of her spare coat. Not the same hem all the time. A succession of animals has pushed the stitching out of the cuff and made a passage up the sleeve, with a turning place at the elbow. She gets the coat down off its peg when she wants it and taps twice on the elbow. There is a short, disagreeable wait while the occupant makes up its mind, and then it drops out at the wrist into her hand. On very cold days it is allowed to go back as soon as she has the coat on. Feeling it at work between her arm and the lining, one would suppose that it was mending something. Occasionally she finds a shrimp head or a snail's door buttoned neatly into the cuff.

The younger attendant has never got used to them. Above all he dislikes the way a hem will come out from under one's foot when one knows perfectly well one has not yet put one's foot down. It happens principally near the western house, on ground which has been pumped more than once. You feel the animal run across your instep. You check the foot in midair and set it down somewhere else, usually in deeper water. It is a small courtesy, and an easy one to extend once or twice, but on a winter morning, carrying a box of gland packing and a flask which leaks unless held upright, he has had as many as eleven hems out from under the same boot.

He believes them to be one animal. He knows it by a nick in its tail. The elder attendant says that a great many things down there have mouths the right shape to make that nick, and she would like to know under what circumstances he found time to feel eleven tails. On this point they have stopped trying to agree.

In the cold band the hems live quite differently. There are no slabs to get under. They roll up the surface of the clay instead, beginning at a ripple and working inland with their hard little heads until they have lifted a curl about as thick as the lip of a pie. It stays up just long enough. A hem sleeps under it through the warmer part of the tide and leaves by the closed end, which comes down behind it. The attendants often stand among these sleeping places with the cord stretched between them. A foot set incautiously on a roll of clay will bring, not a nip, but a steady upward pressure, surprisingly strong, as if somebody in a lower room were having difficulty opening a hatch.

It was here that the elder attendant found out what had become of her mother's thimble. This was an ordinary cheap thimble with a split up one side, as a consequence of which it gave a little when pushed hard upon and was more comfortable than a better one. Her mother had lost it the year the land came up. Now and then in the course of the next thirty years she had put a finger in her mouth after forcing a needle through something thick and said, without any particular feeling, that she wished she had her thimble.

The attendant was standing in cold water to the knee when a hem came along the cord. They do this sometimes, climbing up one attendant and down the other, for the mere pleasure, so far as anyone can tell, of arriving with dry feet. This one went heavily. She took it off the cord and felt a hard knob at its muzzle. It had got its head into the thimble and was walking about quite competently in it, the split admitting enough of one whisker to go by.

She freed it with her thumbnail. Her first thought, which she is not proud of, was to see whether her mother's name was inside. Her mother had never put her name in anything. There was only the split, and the worn place where a needle went if one was not careful, and a little warmth left by the animal's head. She put it in her pocket. The hem continued along the cord, lighter by a weight which, in proportion to its size, must have been something like a washboiler.

Her mother is still alive. She was pleased to have the thimble back, though not to the degree her daughter had obscurely hoped for. She tried it, said that her finger must have got thinner, and put a twist of wool inside. It is in use most evenings. So far nothing else seems to have come of it.

This is perhaps what is worth knowing about the hems. They take up what shelter they can find, and occasionally a shelter which has gone missing in one place comes to hand in another with a hem already inside it. They do not seem troubled by the journey. An animal turned out of a biscuit box whose maker closed his doors sixty years ago will sit down on the lid to wash, beginning behind the ears and drawing the whiskers forward one at a time between the pads of its forefeet. It will go on doing this while the box passes from hand to hand and people try to remember who it was that used to buy those biscuits, and whether there was an iced one in the middle. By the time it has finished, the tide is generally far enough in to float the lid. Then one must put a hand down for it, or be prepared to lose the lid as well.
