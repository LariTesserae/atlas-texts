On the underside of the lowest shelf there are snails. To see them in dry weather you have to put your cheek almost to the stone and look up. What shows from above is a row of white edges, rather like the chips a chisel leaves when it has been used to square a step. A little lime has come away on the backs of the older ones. Each is shut to the stone by a thin skin, drawn tight across the mouth of the shell. Dust lies as evenly on that skin as on the shelf itself.

After rain the row comes apart. One snail goes up into the ferns. Another takes so long to get round the end of the shelf that the water has stopped running by the time its horns appear on top. There it sets to work on the green film the water leaves behind. Under a fallen frond you can see what it has eaten: a clean road in the green, with close little sweeps from side to side, as if somebody had been down it with a broom too narrow for the job. Where two such roads meet, neither snail makes the smallest allowance for the other. There is a patch of very clean stone and the roads go on.

Not all the shells that move about here have snails in them. A wren has the use of one of the larger ones. She found it mouth upward in the fan at the lower end, with a little water standing in it and something worth eating under the water. Now she turns it over whenever she passes. She has worked it well out of the fan and some distance uphill. In a hard rain it goes back. People looking down from the turf generally take her for the bird that builds on the landing, although she could sit quite comfortably inside the finished nest without so much as putting her head above its edge.

There is a mouse in the stair, too. What was once the back of a riser has opened just far enough to admit her with her ears laid down. At first dark she comes out and washes, drawing each ear forward through her forepaws until it springs free. Then she goes into the fig. She seldom goes down to a fallen fruit. There are enough still on their stalks, and she knows how to eat one without bringing it down. She stands with her hind feet on a twig and her forefeet on the fig, bites through the yellow skin and eats inward, turning her head to get at the pulp on either side of the hole. What is left looks quite sound from the pasture. From below you see a small round window full of stars.

In the stair behind her there are four young mice, already furred, with feet nearly the size of hers. On warm nights they get as far as the crack and sit there smelling what she has brought back on her whiskers. One night the boldest comes out backward, trying to pull a long grass stem after him. The stem is part of the bedding and the bedding holds. For a little while he hangs head down against the old step, attached to his home by his teeth. Then he lets go. It is scarcely the length of a mouse to the next step, but he sits where he has landed, passing his paws over his face with great care. Above him three noses fill the crack.
