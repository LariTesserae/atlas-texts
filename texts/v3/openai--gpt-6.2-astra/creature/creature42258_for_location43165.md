The first one you see is no larger than a flake of the stone. It is resting in the last pool to get the sun. There is a little bar of green across its back, the reflection of the ledge, and what gives it away is that when the sand beside it quivers, the bar bends. A lip has lifted on that side. For a moment you can see the animal's underside, pleated and a very tender pink. Then the lip comes down. Three grains of sand that were nowhere near one another are touching now.

There are a great many in the pool. Some are no bigger than those grains. The old ones have backs the color and grain of boiled eggshell, with a slight peak off to one side, where the first small hard part is still carried on the shell it has grown. They do not graze the stone. Each settles a little to one side of a place where the water comes in, and raises the edge of its shell into the current. Underneath are two fans of hair so fine that what one sees of them is less a fringe than a pinkness dissolving in the water. From time to time one fan curls inward and is drawn slowly across the mouth. While this is being done the other stays out, trembling. There is something of a person eating with one hand and feeling for the next plum with the other about it.

Toward evening the smallest ones leave the pool. They set out when the first threads of water are hardly continuous, coming up against a dry place and waiting for the dark to reach across it. Their shells are still quite clear. Seen from above, each is a minute lens through which the bottom of a groove appears wider and rather beautifully scored. Their going is leisurely, though the water runs fast beside them. They go with one edge down in the groove and the other high on the dry stone, tilted like little carts on a hillside. If two meet, the smaller draws in its foot. The larger passes bodily over it. Afterward there is a long wait before the small one is persuaded to put its foot out again.

It is up at the ends of the grooves that they grow their shells. The pale roots have a nap to them, soft as the inside of a cheek, and between the hairs a yellow meal collects, washed out of the red earth. The young gather it on the rims of their feet. By morning you can see a cloudy crescent on each clear back. All the first wet season they make this journey, taking a little more of the ridge onto their backs each night. They never get farther into the green than the reach of the wet stone. A brown frond lying across a runnel will have a company of them moving beneath its arch and come away, if you lift it, without so much as a pinched place in its lace.

A few arrive each evening at the beads where there are no longer roots. They eat nothing there. They fasten themselves high on the skin of the bead, foot uppermost, shell hanging down into the clear round room of water. In the morning, as the thread of water thins, they come sliding home. Some have a small blunt place in the curve of the shell, a night's growth missed long ago and never quite made up. You can find it with a fingernail on an animal already too wide for any of the grooves.

Those wide ones live by the lower edge. It is worth lying down on the stone, while there is still light enough behind the cloud, and looking under. The dark along the lip is made up of separate backs. They overlap by just so much as to leave each its narrow crescent of running water. Here a shell may be as broad as the bowl of a spoon. The fans are long and hang out into the air, held together by the water passing through them. A fan fills, bows under the weight, and empties onto the back below. In one place you can follow the passage of a little wobble of light down six or seven animals before it steadies out. Below the last, the stone is merely damp.

The shells come loose when the animals die. Most go over. A few at the thin tip are worked upward by the crowd making room along the edge, until one afternoon there comes the small dry report of a shell springing free of its neighbors. It rocks for years on the peak of its infancy. By the time it lies quite flat it is scarcely to be told from a flake parted from the ridge, except that if you turn it over there is a polished spoonful of shade inside, and toward one end, finer than the ridges on a fingertip, the marks of all those evenings going up to the roots.

By then the sun is off the first pool. What looked like a grain standing on end has moved some distance from the place where the water wells up. At the edge of the pool it stops. Out from under it comes a foot almost comically ample for so small a traveler, clear all the way to the tip except for a speck of red meal. It lays this down on the warm incline. There is no water there yet. The foot folds back under the shell, and the whole creature settles in to wait, within the width of a grain of where the water will come.
