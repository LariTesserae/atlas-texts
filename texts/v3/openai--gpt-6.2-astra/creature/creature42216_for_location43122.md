There is a bird on the shelf that is much easier to find in its first summer than at any other time. The young ones sit on the bare knobs with their bills open. From a little distance each looks like a split in the stone showing a surprisingly orange interior. An adult coming in with food will land beside its young, on what appears to be quite smooth rock, and disappear. Its breast is the dry colour of the rock and its back the wet colour. It has only to turn a little to put the division in the right place.

They are called seam birds, though for much of the year there would seem to be little advantage in keeping to a seam. They walk along under the rushes in the low light there, occasionally reaching up to take something off the underside of a crown. What they take is quite small. One can watch a bird swallow and go on walking without ever seeing what it had in its bill. Its feet are large, with a fringe down either side of each toe. In a dry runnel they make a faint brushing noise among the seed scales.

The young on the knobs are not waiting to be taught to fly. They can already fly rather well. They are waiting for someone to show them which way the water will go. There are a great many old courses, very much alike, and in any given afternoon perhaps one in six will fill. An adult goes up onto a stone and looks along the rush crowns. Where the light runs steadily from bead to bead, it pays no particular attention. Where the light breaks off and begins again, it flies down. There is a dip there, sometimes of no more than the thickness of a finger, into which the first drops are already going. The bird walks ahead of them on the dry pebbles. Presently it stops and the water comes round its feet.

For the next quarter of an hour there is food enough for several birds. Little brown hoppers come running out of the pebble bed, some with cuffs of air still shining around their hind legs. A white grub that has spent the better part of a year eating the soft middle out of a birch twig is delivered twig and all. The bird holds one end underfoot and pulls the other end apart with its bill. The black newts go by unmolested. A young bird sometimes picks one up and has a disagreeable time getting it off its tongue; for the rest of that afternoon it is inclined to wipe its bill after quite ordinary meals.

Once the young understand about the broken light there is seldom more than one orange mouth to be seen on a stone. The family goes on meeting, however, at a place which may be a fair way off the seam. A pair generally has the use of a roof in the birches. It is nothing they have built. Under the close leaves the stems fork and fork again, and there is a level at which a bird of just that height can walk about upright in a considerable room. They sleep there in rain. On a still evening, looking across the scrub rather than down into it, you can see a single leaf lifting and settling as somebody underneath gets comfortable.

In spring the hen lays on the floor of this room. The eggs are set well apart, each in its own shallow scrape, and she gathers them under her by walking backwards with short sweeps of her feet. They roll apart again when she leaves. A scrub cat that gets a paw through the roof usually comes away with one egg. It may spend some time fishing in the same hole for the others, while the hen stands a few feet off in the open, looking remarkably like a stone. The young hatch in deep shade. For a day or so they have a little pale thorn on the top of the bill. It makes a ticking against the stems as they follow her out.

There is another place to see the birds, though it requires being below the shelf in the last of the daylight. Late in the season, when an afternoon is too cold to bring a bead to the rushes, they begin going over the lip. For some distance they fall alongside the falling water, drawing away from it where it draws away from the rock. Then their feet come out and they pitch up under the overhang. The yellow moss is full of flies too soft to have done well in the wind above. Here the fringes on the birds’ toes have a use one would hardly guess at, watching them on the pebbles. They spread against the wet moss and take the weight, so that a bird can hang almost upside down without pulling out so much as a thread of the stuff it hangs by.

A few stay under there until spring. There is room to roost on the narrow stairs made by the ends of the lava beds, and shelter behind the water even on days when whole sheets of it are flying upwards. The birds keep their foothold through the worst of it. When the sun gets low enough to shine in between the cliff and the outblown fall, each shakes itself, and for a moment along the face of that enormous dark thickness of country there are little bursts of illuminated rain, all quite separate, at the height of a single seam.
