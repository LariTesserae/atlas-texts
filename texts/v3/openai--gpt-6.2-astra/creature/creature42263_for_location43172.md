Under the longest willow lives a short-tailed vole. From the west lip you would judge it much too big for the cover. It is a plump animal, and where the ground is bare beneath a branch it has to turn its head sideways to get past. But the moss gives a little under each foot, and the grass above it gives a great deal. The leaves rise in succession as it goes by. In a wind you cannot pick out that movement at all. You have a better chance of seeing the vole on a still morning, when one small patch of grass after another loses its beads of water.

Its run goes along the boundary between the grey moss and the green, though that is probably the one feature of the run the vole has never seen. On one side is a dry bank with places to go to ground; on the other are the tender white ends of the grass. It cuts a stem quite low down, draws the stem in past its cheek with both paws and eats along it like someone examining a length of thread. The tough part beyond the first joint it generally leaves. There are little piles of these lengths under the leaves, all lying much the same way because the vole likes to sit with its rump against a particular knob of wood.

What looks from above like a single broad run is two runs, one old, one lately made. Last winter the vole could go over the knob. There was snow caught on the west lip and a long low tongue of it reaching down across the willow. Under that roof the vole went wherever it pleased, including straight over the top of the wood, and wore a groove there in the soft black rind. Now the groove is open to the sky. The new way skirts it, through a stand of grass so close that the vole's passage makes a sound like a thumb drawn over the teeth of a comb. This is audible from about as far away as you can reach with one hand.

There are young in a ball of shredded grass a little uphill of the wetter drinking place. A root as thick as a pencil holds the ball down on one side. On the other it has been built up against the moss, and the moss has gone on growing, so that if you lifted the nest now some of the green points would come away with it. When the mother leaves, the young settle slowly into the depression she has made. One small hind foot, pink so far even to the claws, stays laid across another's neck. When she comes back the whole ball stirs before there is any room in it for her.

The hopper going off makes no difference down here either. The sound comes from all along the sky, with nothing preceding it in the stems and nothing following. A pipit landing scarcely a foot away is another matter. Its weight comes down on a spray of willow, the far end of the spray dips into the run, and the vole stops with a piece of grass still held crosswise in its mouth. For a while bird and vole stand almost one above the other. The bird is looking for the entrance to its own little room in the heath. It takes two steps sideways and slips away; the spray rises; the grass goes in another quarter of an inch.

Towards evening the vole comes out far enough to show the whole of its face at once. There is a drop lodged in the fork of the willow, too far round the bend of the wood for the plover to see it. The vole reaches up to it. For a moment the wet makes two or three whiskers on one side into a single dark point. Then it backs down with the grain of the grass, and the only thing left out in the light is the fork, dark where the water was.
