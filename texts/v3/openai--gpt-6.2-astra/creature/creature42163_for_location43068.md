There is one more thing that looks like a bit of brick. It lies in the thumbprint, in the second turn of the whorl, and has a white place at one end as though it had been nicked by the comb. You may look in on a dozen different days and find the nick in just the same relation to the lines. On the thirteenth there are two nicks. Between them a very fine black feeler goes feeling along the plaster. It finds the edge of the next line and withdraws, and there is one white place again.

The creature has a pair of doors. They are thickest where they meet and rubbed quite thin against the sides of the groove. The brick color is only on the backs of them. What shows white is a little of the bevel of one door which the other does not quite cover. When both are drawn well back there is scarcely anything red to be seen. What comes out is the color of water in which a brush has been washed: first the feelers, which look black only when doubled on themselves, then a blunt head, then so much loose side that it is hard to believe all of it can go in again. It never all comes out. The hind end keeps its purchase somewhere under the runner.

It lives by what leaks in from your side. The bed is a good dry bed except just at the chip, where on certain mornings there is a sweat on the stone. A drop would be a great deal too much. What reaches the whorl is a darkening of one line, which travels round and round because the thumb was lifted clean at the end of the impression and left no smear across it. With the wet comes the minute grey stuff that a finger leaves even on a surface it has rubbed quite bright. The animal lays its cheek, or what looks like its cheek, against the advancing end of the dark line. Seen from underneath, that part would have a mouth in it. Seen from the chip, there is just a regular small hitch in the whole length of the side. After it has eaten, you can still see the line is damp, but it is clean again down to the little pits in the lime.

There are young ones. That is what the considerable length of loose side is for. In the fold of it are a number of discs like the clear part of a blister, each with a dash of pink across it. At first the dashes all lie the same way, as the discs lie, sloped and overlapping. Then one turns crosswise without the disc turning. Once you have seen this you can distinguish quite a company in there, turning about severally while the long side that holds them is in the act of being gathered up and put away. They are carried past your sight into the building. None has ever been seen going out across the pavement.

Perhaps that accounts for the impression the court gives of having been left complete and empty. The part that is inhabited is the thickness of its sill. There are small old pairs of doors there, bleached now, which you will have taken for chips off the larger pair; and farther in, in the curl where the ball of the workman's thumb came down hardest, there is a red roof so broad that the lime line runs out under it and comes out again on the other side. Nothing has opened there for a long time. There is no need for any of them to go as far as the green. They have their furrow of wet, a little more room behind them than you can see, and in front of them, whenever they open, all that light.

If you watch too long you are apt to see the doors draw back even on a dry afternoon. The small head comes well out and waits, the feelers spread flat to either side. It seems a very composed way of looking back at you. Then you raise your cheek from the pillar and see the oval your breath has made on the cool stone, with the point of it just entering the chip.
