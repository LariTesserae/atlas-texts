The woman who has taken in the spoon makes pockets. A pocket in this town is generally worn on the outside, on a tape round the waist, and can be pulled off in a hurry if the wearer has occasion to swim. There are little shallow ones for children, who like to be able to see everything they own at once. The woman herself wears a long narrow one down the front of her left thigh. Into this she puts the spoon, wet end up. Presently there is a dark patch on her skirt just the shape of a pear. She has forgotten the spoon already.

At her feet a rail chick walks out from under the sewing stool. It is a black bird, not much bigger than the end joint of her thumb, with feet that would do for a considerably older bird. It lifts one foot, spreads the toes till they touch the velvet at four widely separated points, and brings the rest of itself along. Its mother has gone through the runnel into the next house. For a while the chick calls in a thin, increasingly astonished voice. The woman lifts the piece of stuff that has fallen over the mouth of the runnel and the chick goes through.

Most houses have something living in the runnel. People sweep towards it, but never into it until they have put a hand in and felt about. A man's hand coming out covered to the wrist with little transparent shrimps was once the subject of a very good likeness cut in the nap on the wall of the bathhouse. It lasted nearly four months. You could tell the man by the way he held his little finger out, for he had broken it in two places as a child. Even after his face had grown in there were people who could tell him by that finger.

The boy comes for the spoon by way of the back runnels. He is too big for this, really. He has to turn his head sideways and go on one elbow, shoving with his bare feet, and he comes up in the woman's house with a red ear and a green beard. She draws the spoon out of her pocket and holds it up.

“Yours?”

He shakes his head. This does not surprise her. Children here will go to immense trouble about an object to which they have no claim at all.

“Whose, then?”

He takes her by the wrist and turns the bowl over. Under it, at the join with the handle, is a gray lump about the size of a currant, with a bright particle of sand in it. The boy has been waiting all morning for the head to come out. She gets up to look in the better light at the door, taking him along still attached to her wrist. As they watch, the gray stuff parts at one end and two little polished hooks come out and fasten on the wood. A greenish neck follows. The woman knows this creature. By this time of year it ought to have wings.

They lay the spoon across the sill with its bowl hanging out. There is quite a collection of things there: a blue glass stopper, the hollow shaft of a feather, a piece of the old stone which, taken away from the shelf, has lost its smell and looks like any chip you might throw at a dog. These things belong to the woman's husband, one of the feet-first divers. When he comes up out of the dark he generally has something in his hand. He never remembers taking hold of it. Once he had the warm hind foot of another diver, who came up laughing so hard she had to be helped out onto the steps.

What the divers chiefly do down there is listen. There are places under the streets where a fold of skin has fallen against another fold and shut the water off. Little by little the water behind such a place gets hot. A diver can hear the fish knocking there, and by going at the fold with a shoulder can generally let them out. They come past the diver's cheek like a handful of spoons fresh from hot washing. After a hard day's work the woman's husband has a pink ear on the side he listens with and a white shoulder on the side he shoves with. He likes to lie in the sleeping recess while his wife works, with his sore shoulder fitted into a cool dent. The dent has taken to forming a little earlier each afternoon, and sometimes, when he is well and still at work, she puts a bowl of butter in it.

Out in the freight court the three carters have found out what the ribs are for. The old woman at the end of Bath Street is moving. She wants her bedroom taken with her. It is quite a small one and has already been cut free on three sides, but the floor is thick and saturated, and there are two grown daughters to go in it, to keep the sides from folding together on their mother. The carters get their fingers under the cut edge. The old woman sits upright in bed with her hair done for the occasion in a roll across her forehead. She has lived within hearing of the bathhouse all her life and would now like to live within hearing of the frogs.

It takes some time to get her round the turn into Dye Street. People come to their half doors; the old woman acknowledges them with small inclinations of her splendid head. The back of the bedroom, dragging a little, brushes the nap the wrong way and leaves a pale lane behind it, up which the rail and her chicks go picking with great satisfaction. All kinds of little white things live down at the base of the nap, out of the ordinary reach of a beak.

By the time the room goes past the sewing woman's door the boy's insect has got its foreparts out. Its head is a poor soft crumpled thing. The rest comes quite suddenly. There is a length of shining back on the spoon and then four wet straps which shorten and widen, taking the light differently every second. The boy squats down so that the insect is on a level with his eyes. Beyond it, huge and slow and apparently borne along on the four bright wings, the old woman rides by in bed.

Nobody sees the insect leave. The boy looks round because one of the daughters has called for a wedge, and when he looks back there is only the little house of grit, split neatly down the back, stuck to the wood with something stronger than glue. He takes the spoon home this time. At the lower end of the street his step is dry again. He sits there scraping with his thumbnail, very gently, to get the house off whole.
