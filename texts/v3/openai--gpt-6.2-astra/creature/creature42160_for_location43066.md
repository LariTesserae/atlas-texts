Before there is room for a beetle at the blind end, there is room for a beetle's feeler. The feeler belongs to one of the brown ones, no bigger than a flax seed, with a nick in the right wing case. It sweeps the new stone until it catches on a needle of glass. The beetle pulls it free, draws it carefully through the bend of a foreleg, and puts it in again. A mite walks past on the underside of the feeler and steps off onto ground that has never had anything walk on it before. It goes about the length of the beetle into the mountain. There is nothing to eat. Presently it comes back.

The brown beetles have quite good wings. One of them occasionally gets a wing out in the crush, a long transparent rag with two rather beautiful folds in it, and has a time getting it put away. They fly down the pass on the few still evenings in summer. Under the flake what matters is the shape of the shell. There is just enough of a slope from the head up onto the shoulders for one beetle to work its way under another without having to dislodge all the rest. A beetle feeding on a fly can have three others on top of it. The uppermost one, its back against the purple ceiling, gets nothing of the fly but the small deliberate movements of the jaws.

The young are a different shape altogether. A grub is narrow, soft except for its handsome chestnut head, and furnished at the tail with two hooks. It lives for a long time in the hollow of some much larger insect. There is one at present inside the thigh of a grasshopper. The grasshopper came over the brow in August with one green wing still beating; the thigh is all that is left of it now, and has been rolled by the movements of the crowd a good way from where it came in. The grub braces its hooks against the ridges on the inside and grazes on what is left of the muscle. From outside, in a quiet hour, the empty-looking leg can be seen to hitch itself along. The grub will have to come out backwards. Its head has grown too broad to turn.

Nearest the light are the arrivals. A little valley fly, still carrying yellow pollen in the bristles of its face, will stand washing its forefeet where the salt jumps round it. It has been cold for so long that the first thing it does on getting warm is the thing it was doing down in the valley. Sometimes one finishes washing, goes out, and flies clear. Others start up toward the light and strike the low lip. The pale spider has two feet on a line there. In the morning, while the mill is running, it lets the line go slack; there is no distinguishing one knock from the next. When the rock is quiet it takes the slack in again. Most of what comes down the line even then is beetle, a heavy brushing contact to which it pays no attention. A fly makes two or three separate little pulls. The spider goes out underneath the line, back down, with scarcely the thickness of a salt grain between its back and the floor.

In winter the flies cease coming. The beetles stay. Farther in than the grasshopper leg, in a socket too small to admit a head, are six eggs stuck separately to the ceiling. They are clear at first. By the time snow lies in the hollows on either side of the brow each has a brown fleck at one end, and the fleck, looked at very closely, is a pair of jaws. The grub uses them first on the skin that contains it. It eats a hole, comes through, and eats the rest of the skin from the outside, holding on to the ceiling by its hooks. Below it the beetles are packed back to back. It can reach them without letting go. For a while it hangs there with its new head resting on a shell that rises a little, passes on, and is replaced by another.
