A length of packing taken up with a hook will generally come away in two pieces. The upper piece has the liverwort on it. The lower one seems to have a clean face, hard and a little pearly, as if the packing had been laid against tile. Leave it face up in a pan of district water. In a little while a puckered place will open near one edge, and out will come a thread with a fork at the end. It feels along the bottom of the pan as far as it will go. By then there will be other threads out. What looked like the marks left by a tiler's comb turn out to be the lines along which the animals come apart.

The fitters call them slips. A full-grown one will cover the nail of a little finger. Its back is set with grains, mostly porcelain, in a skin that gives a little when pressed. The grains come from the hollows. If a slip is kept in a white basin and given nothing but clean water it develops, in growing, a clear margin through which you can see the slow contractions of its foot. Put in a pinch of the blue silt and in a week it will have a blue border. The largest ones have several changes of color in them. There are slips under the northern halls with a fine stripe of pink from a washroom that fell in before the tram was built.

The fork is of some importance to the animal. One tip is nearly always held down against the packing; the other goes questing in the water. Dust sticks to both. From time to time the fork is drawn in and wiped through a little muscular collar, rather as a fitter draws a dirty length of wire through a rag. The collar takes off everything. Edible matter goes down a short, quite visible channel in the foot. A useful grain may spend a day passing round to the back. The rest comes out in small gray twists which retain their shape until touched. A good deal of what has been taken for lettering on the old gaskets is accounted for in this way.

There is a trick to seeing them feed in place. It does no good to put a lamp to the seam when the bellows are at the top of the stroke. The water then is going in, and all that shows is a row of shut collars. Wait for the pressure against your coat to slacken. As the water begins to seep out again, bringing with it the soft matter from behind the packing, the whole row puts out its forks. In the lamplight it looks for an instant as though the seam has sprung a little silver fur. Then the bank takes another breath. A slip in a quiet pan keeps on making these movements for some hours, putting out its fork to meet a current which does not come. After a while the slips in the pan begin to miss one another's time. By morning each is feeding at its own convenience.

They do not go far on the foot. An animal disturbed at the inspection port may take the better part of a shift to get under its rim. Nevertheless they turn up almost at once in a length of new packing. The very small ones travel on the backs of bubbles. They have at that age a fringe of hairs which later wears off, and if a bubble comes shouldering out from under an old gasket half a dozen may go up with it, their feet drawn together and their fringes spread flat against the air. They drift until the bubble touches something. Most of what it touches is wet enamel and of no use to them. On a falling water, though, the bubbles collect against the high side of a seam. A fitter kneeling there to tighten down the strip can get up with a wet knee densely spangled with slips. They are felt before they are seen. They give the cloth a peculiar drag when he passes his hand over it.

There is no particular quarter where the fitters live. Two families have rooms in the wide foot of the fifth stack, and there is a long-established household in a tram car on blocks, but a dry place convenient to work in one year may be on the wrong side of three crossings the next. Their children know a fair amount about slips. They will show you how to put a thumb over the hole in a length of condemned pipe, lift the pipe full, and let the water down by degrees into a saucer. Where the men would look at the quantity of scale in the saucer, the children look up the pipe. Here, well away from the light, there are sometimes slips as big as shirt buttons, with almost transparent backs. A child will carry one home on a wet tile and give it powdered china of some particularly choice pattern. The pattern does not come through, a disappointment in every generation. A willow plate goes on blue.

Once in a great while a bank is taken out of service for a month. The first time this was done in the fifth ward, the fitters expected a smell when the covers came off. There were slips under every joint, shut fast and still wet inside. What the fitters had not expected was the condition of the packing. Everywhere a slip had lain there was a bit left of the original coarse weave. In between, the face had worn quite smooth. You could follow a single animal's progress over the years by a succession of overlapping patches, each a little bigger than the last, ending under the pearly back itself. They put a piece on the windowsill in the tram office. In sideways light the tracks show up very well.

On the new joints the practice is to leave the last turn of the screws until the bank has worked a night. It gives the small slips time to get clear of the lip. There is no entry for this in the maintenance book. A man going off shift lays the short spanner across the long one, and the man coming on knows what is left to do. By then the animals will have worked round to the underside where there is room for the foot and a little play of water even at the closest setting. The screws go home; the spanners go back on their hooks. Down in the joint the first fine grains are already making their way through the clear skin.
