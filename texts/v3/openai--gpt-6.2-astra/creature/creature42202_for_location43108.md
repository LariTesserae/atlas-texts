After the frogs have been at work a little while, there is a movement behind the middle pier. It travels up the narrow channel against the last of the water. A grain of glass tips over and shows a green edge. Then something no longer than a tram ticket comes out on the bank, with a shred of white paper stuck to its back. This is the smaller of the two water shrews. It sits up on its haunches and passes its forefeet over its head several times. The paper comes off on the second pass, but it goes on washing.

There is white on its chin and along its belly, a rather startling laundry white in that place. The rest is dark enough that when it runs on the wet concrete it seems to have gone under the surface. Only the whiskers continue to take the amber light. It goes nearly to the steps. A little frog there draws its feet under it and grows perceptibly taller. But the shrew is occupied with a soft place in the sand. It works its nose in, braces itself, and comes away backward with a red midge grub stretched between its teeth. Where the grub went in there is a hole with a bead of clear water in the bottom.

It is easiest to watch the smaller one. The other works the wall below the steam main, where the light is poor. Sometimes there will be half an hour of small, industrious noises there, as of somebody trying the teeth of a comb one by one with a thumbnail. Then a cricket leg comes turning down to the water. There is a way up by the backs of the pipe brackets which neither a man on the walk nor a frog on the floor would take for a way anywhere. The shrew can get as high as the first cable tray. It comes down headfirst, stopping at the difficult turn with all four feet quite still and its nose going from side to side in the air.

They do not particularly care for one another. When the water is high there is plenty of room for both, including a long shallow cove behind the blue gate accessible through its bottom hinge. At low water they are apt to want the same six inches of bank. One night the fitter, down to see about the float, heard such a thin sustained squealing that he put a drop of oil on the gate hinge. By the time he got his torch out there was nothing to see but two trails of little wet stars going off in opposite directions across the dust on the inspection walk.

The smaller one's sleeping place is in the last of the empty handrail sockets above the landing. A drill broke through into a space behind the wall there. A wisp of yellow grass hangs out of the hole, and underneath it, where the wall is otherwise gray, repeated passages of a damp stomach have raised a small patch of clean pink aggregate. From the landing it can reach either the street or the sump without coming out from under cover. It does go up. In dry weather, very early, the woman who opens the kiosk at the terminus has seen it among the curled leaves at the foot of her shutter. She takes it for a mouse and is careful not to put the milk crate down on it.

But most of its living is got below. The best time is not quite when the frogs come off the steps. It is a minute later, when the water trapped in the sand starts draining back into the channel and brings a quantity of small life with it. The shrew stands in the thread of current with its back feet spread and puts its whole face in. For a while there is only that blunt little rump sticking up and the tail laid along the dark grains. When it lifts its head there are beads at the ends of its whiskers and something in its mouth so small that the act of swallowing is better seen than the meal.

Lately it has taken to crossing between the two fans without swimming. It launches itself off the one tip and lands with its front feet on the other, its belly making a momentary bridge over the running water. On its way home it makes the same crossing with less ceremony, hardly breaking stride. At the rate the sand is coming in, by winter there will be no occasion for the jump. For a time, very likely, it will make it all the same.
