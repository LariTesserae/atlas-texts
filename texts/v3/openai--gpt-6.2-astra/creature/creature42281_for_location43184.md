When the smallest child on the ridge loses a tooth, she wants to put it in one of the pools. Her brother tells her a little white swimmer will get inside it and wear it for a head. She keeps it instead. There is a crack by her bed where she keeps things that ought to be white and are only white when wet: the hinge of a shell, a fish bone like a washerwoman’s pin, a pebble with a soft place in it that comes away on her thumb.

The beds are high up in the shelters. Near the floor there is always a draught. A dog going to sleep there will turn round several times and finally lie down with its nose in the corner, where two draughts meet and seem somehow to cancel. There is only the one dog. It belongs, at mealtimes, to all four houses. It has learned to eat a silver fish from the tail upwards, which is the wrong way, but nobody can get it to change. In the mornings it goes along the ridge to look at the gulls’ eggs. A sitting gull will let it come astonishingly close. It is the bird from the next hollow over, whose business it isn’t, that flies at its head.

The old man who cuts the crescents in the crossing stones has a precise idea of the weight of this dog, having had it on his knees through several winters. His idea of the weight of a stone is rather less good. Two people go out with him to turn one and have to send for two more. They take the dog too. Once the stone is up it puts its nose with intense interest to the place where the stone has been. There are threads there, red and finer than seal hair. As the air gets to them they draw back into holes too small to see. The man has lived on the ridge for seventy years without noticing these. He puts his spectacles on and lies down. The others stand holding up the stone.

Of all the people there, it is his daughter who bothers most about the baskets. She takes them into bed with her when they are new. In a whole morning’s work at the weaving there will be one bad end, a little whisker pointing the wrong way. What a hand may pass over a dozen times the loose skin above a sleeping knee will find at once. Her husband has offered to find these places for her without the trouble of going to bed, but she says he hasn’t the knees for it. The truth is she likes to work in the hour before anybody gets up. You can hear her then, in the dark of the far shelter, drawing a strand out through the stiff weave a notch at a time, like somebody very slowly doing up a boot.

In the water there are creatures that never get as far as a basket. Under the sill of the widest pool live little brown fish with chins like wet velvet. When the tide is running hard they tuck themselves up to the rock by their bellies. A drowned feather will go past them at a tremendous pace, turning over and over, and not a fin on any of them will stir. But in the few minutes when the water stands between one way of going and the other, all the little brown fish let go. They hang under the sill with a space of clear water between their backs and the stone. Sometimes a child lying flat to drink the rain off the lip of the pool sees them there, each fish complete in its shadow, with its two ridiculous curls of whisker. By the time she has fetched anyone the tide has made up its mind.

There is a seal in the lower passages with one eye gone milky. She uses the same rubbing place year after year. It is not much of a place now; she has polished away the very tooth in the rock that first made it worth rubbing on. Still she comes, and the gulls know the look of her. When she heaves herself out they wait a decent distance off until she has finished with one flank and rolled over. Then they walk almost up against her, gathering the loose hair before the wind can take it. On a wet summer morning there will be three kinds of warmth in a hollow on the ridge: what the stone has kept from yesterday, what the gull gives off, and a little of the seal still in the hair.

The children know the one-eyed seal. They know the basket woman by her knees when the fog comes down and they are crouched at the narrow end of a bell. They know the old man by the pause in his walking where he thinks his stick has taken his weight and it hasn’t quite. What they do not know yet is how anybody tells the difference, with an ear against the floor, between deep water on the left and deep water on the right. It seems to them that wherever you put your ear the sound comes from straight underneath.

One morning the girl with the missing tooth goes out with her grandfather to a stone that has fallen. He kneels on one side of it and she on the other. He has a worn red place on his ear from the arm of his spectacles. She can see it quite close, the two of them having put their heads down almost forehead to forehead. There is the hollow knocking she has heard before. He takes her hand and moves it a hand’s breadth along the rock, and has her put her ear there instead. The knocking is still directly underneath. It is smaller, though. She moves again, of her own accord. Smaller still.

They go home with one wet ear apiece. At the door she stops him and puts her finger on the red place. That night, from among the treasures in the crack, she picks the little fish bone, and with it works a tuft of the greased wool round the arm of his spectacles. It makes them sit crooked on his face. For the rest of that season everything the old man looks at has a slight fall towards the sea.
