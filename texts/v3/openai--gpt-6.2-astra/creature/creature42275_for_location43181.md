The people who live on the shelf say that a house is old enough for a birth when there is room for two pairs of feet between the bed and the wall. A new house gives a little at the floor when someone comes in. In an old one a woman can get up and go outside without waking the child laid against her side. From outside, the two houses look much alike. A little child sent home on an errand is told to put a hand in at the door and feel for the particular knee of wood on which all the belts in the family are hung.

Not many houses are occupied at once. There may be six or seven women of a family alive who have given birth in the same one, and a man may be sixty when he first sees the place where he was born. In between, people are much on the move. They go in small parties, carrying less than one would think necessary: a roll of soft hide which serves as a bed and as a vessel for water, a blade, a loop of cord from which things can be hung clear of the ground. A white patch high on the back of a walking person is generally a baby. The white is the inside of a piece of calf shell, pared till it weighs almost nothing, which keeps the sun off the baby's head. Sometimes for miles it is all that can be seen of the party ahead.

There is a small brown animal which goes with them. It has a long tail with a crook at the end, poor eyes, and ears very wide apart, one on either side of its blunt chin. It sleeps with its chin flat down and its forefeet over its eyes. People call it a hem. A house in use will generally have two or three hems in the doorway, so nearly the color of the floor that a stranger sets a foot among them before noticing the row of little feet drawn up out of the way of the light.

At first it seems that these animals have been kept to find water. At evening, while it is still too early for the dark line of a seam to show, a hem will get up, stretch, and set off down a trough at a businesslike trot. Shortly there will be enough water where it has stopped for a whole party to drink. But it does this just as often beside a seam which has pinched shut for good. What it hears is an animal underneath. There is a fat soft grazer with a hard lip which comes up at the time of swelling to bite through the little feeding roots distended with sap. Where many of them feed, the seam is apt to open. The hem has no interest in the water. It stands with its chin down until it can put one narrow foreleg through the check and hook out its supper. If it misses, it spends some time washing the leg, making a show of having put it in the ground for that purpose.

Children learn quite young to tell the sound of the hard lip from the lighter busy sound made by the paired fans. An adult can distinguish the two while walking and talking about something else. There is nothing particularly keen about these people's hearing. Away from the plateau they are rather deaf. What a child learns is where in the body to attend. The sound of a hard lip comes through the heel like the very last instant of a sneeze; a great company of fan mouths, though loud enough to spoil a night's sleep, produces no such sensation. A family walking in file will sometimes stop, turn, and go slantwise off their course without anyone lifting a hand. The smallest child in the file turns too, a little late, with an expression of immense satisfaction.

In their country a conversation need not take place all at once. Something said in a doorway at dawn may receive its answer at a pool that evening. In the hours between, when the courting whine is high, the two people concerned may walk within touch of each other and exchange a good deal of news with their fingers. This is not the same as speaking. The hands do directions beautifully, also numbers, the relations between people, the gait of an absent person, and the conduct of animals. They do poorly with anything for which it matters exactly which word was used. A quarrel involving a quotation can take days. People keep the words very carefully in their heads. It is bad manners to begin at sunset with a version improved in the course of the walk.

They eat the soft grazers, and the kernels that form at long intervals along the descending cords. A kernel is about the size of the last joint of a thumb, hard outside and full of white meal with a faint taste of pears. One can get at the kernels by cutting into the rind, but this is rough on blades; and the next kernel on a cord may lie three old surfaces down. Most are gathered from the walls of houses. In a well-kept house the edge of the seat has a strip of hide let into it. Lift it and one sees a length of cord trained sideways while young, with the kernels standing out all along it like knuckles. A person leaving a house for the last time leaves the strip down. Someone hungry enough will know to look.

For meat in any quantity they depend on the storms. On ordinary days there is simply no getting at enough of the life underfoot to feed many mouths at once. In rain it comes and lays hold of you. People have long aprons of knotted thread which they wear about the lower leg when a storm is near. A great deal climbs up the outside and presently flies away; the grazers push in between the threads, being made to get under things, and stay there. Afterward the aprons are stripped into hollows lined with hide. This has to be done quickly, while the animals are still swollen with water. As they dry they become extraordinarily flat. A neglected apron can empty itself on a surface on which there is not the thickness of a hair of apparent cover. A hem will sit for quite a while looking at the place.

In a good storm there is meat to spare and a reason to stay together. People come to the forked limb. It stands at the meeting of two troughs; in the days when it was alive it must have shaded a considerable pool. The peeled patches are smooth from the taking of handholds. Beneath the lowest, at the reach of a person standing on another's shoulders, is a hole just big enough to put a rolled apron into. There are aprons in it far older than anyone now living. Thread which would be eaten in a night on the ground lasts very well up there in the hot wind.

It is at the limb that a child is most likely to meet an old relation for the first time. There is usually some disappointment on both sides. The child has expected someone recognizable from the gait rendered so often in the fingers of its mother; the old person has been carrying for several seasons a little belt, by now quite hopelessly small. They go off and look for a hem together. Hems are fond of old people, who have less reason than most to move their feet, and one can nearly always be induced to sleep in the crook of a knee. One afternoon spent sitting in this way will generally put things right.

After a few days the parties separate. Under the limb there may be a dark patch for a while, where so many people have been sitting. The little birds come down out of the forks to investigate it. They turn over threads, pick the salt from the rims of the deeper impressions, and quarrel over the odd scrap of white kernel meal; by morning there is nothing left for a bird. Several days later, when nobody is there to see, the checks over the whole patch come out in a fine green nap. The first creature to eat it does so from below.
