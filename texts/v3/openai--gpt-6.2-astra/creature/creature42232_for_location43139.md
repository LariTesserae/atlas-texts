By evening the copper wire has gone behind the middle bolt as far as it will go. One end is buried in the grit; the other sticks out at the height of a crab’s back. The last crab gives it a pull, walks round the bolt, gives it the same pull from the other side, and goes home underneath it. For several days there is an unusual amount of white shell on that part of the beach. A laden crab coming out from under the wire gets through all right, but leaves its load on the wrong side.

The really small crabs have no trouble. They are pink rather than white. A grain of red meal can be seen going the length of one of them, pausing a little in the middle. They live at first in the clear water that stands between the turns of the bolt thread. There is practically no current there, even when red sand is going past overhead in sheets. An old crab climbs the thread each afternoon to crop the weed and comes down green about the mouth. The pink ones pick at its face. It will stand quite still for this, sometimes with a forefoot suspended, until a stroke of the tongue sets all seven or eight youngsters drifting off it backwards. Then it puts its foot down and continues on its way.

There is an animal in the basin that you are much more likely to hear than see. After the door has been open for a while, when your ear has ceased to trouble you with the great noise, you begin to distinguish a second, poor little knock that follows it. This comes from inside the loose washer. The hole through the middle of the washer is lined with overlapping brown lids. Each has a pearly hinge and belongs to a soft, nearly spherical creature whose other end is cemented to the metal. As the washer rolls, each creature in turn gets a little open water above it and puts out a fan. Seen end on, the washer has the appearance of a wheel running over and continually just missing a very fine grey feather.

At a blow of the tongue the lids all shut. Six dozen of them together make about the noise of a fingernail against an egg. The swimmers know that sound particularly well. There is generally a little food pinched off outside each lid, and the smallest swimmers go straight through the hole for it, turning on their sides to pass one another. A big one can no longer get through. It has to wait for the washer to come round and feed off the wall like everybody else. Some of the big ones have a white scar just aft of the eyes, where the fit used to be rather close.

The midges spend very little of their time in the air. A flying midge in there is at the mercy of the tongue. The grown ones walk about on the threads on tall, rather ceremonious legs, testing the brass ahead with a foot before committing any weight to it. Only the new ones fly. They come off the water in the bottom of the nine with their wings stuck together, climb the short slope of the figure, and stand at the ledge where the maker drove the stamp in crooked. As their wings dry they take on a blue lustre, quite bright against the old metal. There is a moment after a stroke when the air is moving out of the basin and up. A midge opening its wings at that moment goes cleanly up to the threads. One that starts a little late lands on the door.

This accounts for the blue dust sometimes found on the ball of your thumb after you have opened the latch. A midge will walk a long way on warm skin. If you hold your hand still in the doorway it will eventually walk off again, though not necessarily into its own basin. There are midges in the door seven stairs below and in the one below that. Those in the lowest door are almost black and have the short legs of creatures accustomed to spending their lives on good rough iron.

By the end of summer the stranded wire is green at the buried end. Grit has banked against it until the crabs can carry their bits of shell over it without a hitch. In the lee of the little bank there is now a pool, no wider than a thumbnail clipping, which stays full between strokes. For a week or two nothing seems to use it. Then, one clear afternoon, there is a brown lid on the copper just under water. It is small enough that the fan it puts out is visible only by the red grains that stop on it. The wire does not turn; the creature can keep its fan out as long as it likes. At the next blow it shuts up smartly all the same.
