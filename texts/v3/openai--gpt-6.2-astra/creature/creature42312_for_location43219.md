A flag on the cool slope may lie for years in one place and then, in the course of a few hours, move the length of itself uphill. It is not the stone that moves. There is an animal here with a back that collects dust. At first the dust sticks to its skin in the ordinary way, wherever the folds are greasy, but the skin grows out over it in little lips, and by the time the animal is full grown there is a single thin plate of grit across its back. Under a finger it feels like the other flags. Only the edge is different. Run a finger along it and somewhere on the uphill side there will be a place that gives a little, warm and soft as the edge of an ear.

Underneath is a pale animal, rather less than half the breadth of its plate. It has a foot with a channel down the middle, and two small heads, or what look like heads, turned away from one another. In fact only one has a mouth. The other is occupied almost entirely by a pair of bladders for water. Both ends can feel their way. When the animal goes downhill the water end generally goes first, though in a bad patch of grit the two will sometimes spend a considerable time trying to go in opposite directions. The plate turns a little on the spot. Then one end or the other gets the better of it.

What it eats lives under much smaller stones: loose white threads that draw nourishment from the cast skins of mites and the droppings of the clear worms. It comes up to a stone edgewise, puts its mouth under, and lifts. There is just room to slip a tongue in. It takes the loose growth, leaving a close white nap, then lets the stone down again. A well-fed one will go a very long time without doing anything else. Mites gather beneath its plate; threads come up around its foot. After a while it has only to shift a little to one side to find its supper where it was lying before.

There is a small brown fly that knows these animals from stones. It knows them even when there has been no movement for months. It walks round the edge, stopping every few steps to put its front feet together. At the soft place it stops for good and backs in out of the wind. Its wings would be of no use on the crest. It has them for the space under the plate, where it hangs upside down and fans a bead of water until the bead grows sticky with what was dissolved in it. This it eats. Several flies working in a row make a bare streak across the damp underside, no wider than a hair. When two such streaks meet there is a dispute conducted with the wings, the flies remaining upside down throughout.

For most of its life the plate-bearer has no occasion to go high enough on the slope to be in danger from a hunter. The danger comes with the fog. In the wet hollows among the crests, along with the eggs of the long-legged things, there are little packets of living skin folded in grit. They have lain there since the last fog. Wet through, each opens into a pale foot with a mouth at one end and a pink knob at the other. It has no plate. Its first meal is the blue skin of the rock; its second, if it can get one, is the soft grit that has come away from the back of an adult. It cannot make the grease to hold its first dust. It must have some from a back already grown.

Hence that slow business of going uphill. Long before a star has gone out, before there is anything one could call damp on the exposed face of a stone, the flies come out from under the plates and walk uphill into the wind. The plate-bearers follow. What the flies feel at that distance is not known. Sometimes they are wrong. They go a little way, wait with their feet together, and come back down. The larger animals settle in new places, leaving oval gardens of white thread exposed on the slope. In the space of an hour there is nothing left of a garden but a few mites wandering in the dust.

When the fog really comes, the plate-bearers arrive late. The great rush of leather grazers is over. There are broad clean swathes across the blue, and in the swathes the little naked feet are gathered wherever a flaw in the rock has saved a grain or two of salt. An adult coming among them draws its water end in under the plate and lets the rim down flat. The young climb onto it. They pass their mouths back and forth across the old grit, taking away so little that a hundred of them leave scarcely a mark. By the time the stars return there are flecks of dark on their backs. They go down separately, each carrying perhaps six grains, perhaps ten. Even so there are hunters that pass them over, feeling only the cold of the grains against the cold of the slope.

The adults stay behind a little longer. In the last of the fog they lift their plates at one edge and let the wind get under. There is a sound then, very slight, of grit running over grit. It is the flies coming home on foot, trying one edge after another until they find a wet one. No plate-bearer starts down with quite the company it took up. Under a stone that has spent half a lifetime beside one fissure there may be a brown fly from the next trough, its feet still powdered with a yellow mineral that does not occur for miles around.
