The black seed has a white side. This is difficult to establish from the pass. Just at the turn, when the ring disappears and there is a black point standing still in the water, a white point sometimes appears a little way off. It looks as if one thing had stopped and sent another on an errand. In fact the creature has rolled over. Its back is black, its belly white, and for the distance it travels belly uppermost it is remarkably well matched to the floor.

It is rather broad at one end and at the other divides into two little blades. There are feet under the broad end, folded so neatly into the breast that a dead one, found dry on the western seat, was kept for years in a box of seeds. The blades do very little to propel it. It goes where the water goes, but not necessarily where it went last time. At a meeting of two sheets it can put one blade down while keeping the other flat, and so turn the breadth of its body into the falling water. This takes it to the floor. To miss the fall it need only shut the two blades together. With these modest arrangements it has the use of nearly the whole country.

It eats the pink off the broken cups. The colour is due to a growth so fine that a splinter picked out with a hair and dried in the sun is white by the time one has found a bit of paper to wrap it in. Down below, the break keeps its colour for half a day. The seed comes alongside a travelling splinter, travelling just as fast, and puts its feet out. The feet are quite disproportionately handsome, amber-coloured, with a fringe on each like the fringe of a poppy petal after rain. They close about the edge. For a while two things go round together instead of one. It is probably as restful a meal as anybody eats on the pass.

The young are white all over. There are generally five or six to be seen in the lee of the pear, but one must look for their shadows. On a bright day these are little grey commas on the floor, all pointing the same way; when the hair swings the commas go off in a body, and come back in a rather different order. The young have not yet got their blades. What they have instead is a single long foot with which they hold on to one another. The first holds on to the pear. From time to time the first lets go and is carried back along the line, taking a fresh hold at the far end. In this way no one of them has to bear the pull of all the others for very long. Occasionally, after a violent swing, there are two short lines riding separately on the same pear. They work their way round it until they touch and make a long one again.

Before the peg was driven in they must have managed otherwise. Even now, over towards the cold end of the eastern seat, a small company can sometimes be made out riding a loose cup. These do not grow so fast. They have the advantage, though, of waking up from time to time in a fresh pasture, whereas those on the pear depend on what comes by. The grown creature brings them nothing. Once in a hundred circuits it knocks against the line and all the little feet shut so hard that the hair gives a perceptible tick against the side of the window. A person eating there can feel it through the heel of a hand.

The finest view of the creature is to be had in the afternoon, when the air collects. It comes up under the silver patch and runs its back along the underside. A film of water remains over the black, which in that light proves to be a very dark green, scored from side to side with fine bright lines where the plates overlap. In the reflection one sees a green animal a great way up among the opposite peaks, going slowly over snow. The actual animal is at the near edge of the patch, almost under one's nose. It is cleaning its feet. One by one the fringes open into the air and are drawn back through the skin of the water, which retains the crumbs of lime. For a moment the patch has a white edging. Then the hair twitches and the edging goes out over the ankles of whoever is sitting there.

After that one can watch till one's neck aches and see nothing but the ring. Towards evening, however, when the last yard has come loose and the light no longer goes through the roof, the burr outside the stone diminishes. The ring draws in under the western seat. There is a shallow place there, just deep enough for a creature with its feet folded, where the lime has been polished dark by the passage of many backs. The seed enters it white side up. As the current fails, the blades, which have been standing a little apart all day, come slowly together. The last water from the window scarcely clears the edge; it runs half an inch down the stone towards the drop and stays there in a bead. Under the seat the feet unfold.
