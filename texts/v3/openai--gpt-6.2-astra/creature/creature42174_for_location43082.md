The gray in the blister wall lives very close to the stone. A little of it can be seen by looking up under the ledge, but the greater part is in the pores, each pore with a lining so thin that a splinter knocked from the wall would still look freshly broken. Only toward the mouth, where several linings run together and turn out over the lip, is there anything to give the stone a different color. Seen very near, the soft-looking powder has a minute pattern of holes in it. The holes belong to the rock.

The sulfurous air comes up the wall from below. Even on a still day the inner face of the blister has a warm side and a warmer side, and there is enough difference to draw a little gas along the ledges. A breath that goes quite undetected at the height of the broken crown may last some time in the recess. The gray darkens first at its lower edge. By the time the upper edge has darkened too, most of the breath is out over the pavement, where it leaves no mark. In the pores a little of what came with it stays behind. Long after the face has gone pale again, the linings go on thickening at the expense of this remainder.

They grow out rather than in. There is a depth, not much greater than the thickness of a flake, below which the air is always spent. Down there the linings end in little blind cuffs. At the surface a fold can grow across a grain of glass, touch the mouth of the next pore and turn down into it. For a long time afterwards there will be two gray dots with black between them; the fold itself is too thin to show. Much of the thumbprint is joined in this fashion. When a grain works loose, the bit of lining on it goes loose too. There are several such grains in the heap of scales on the blister floor. They are as gray as ever, but the breath goes over them too quickly, and the gray on them does not spread.

From one heave to the next there is little change inside the enclosure. The knocks pass through it. Sometimes one brings down a scale which rests at a slant against the wall and makes a new, lower ceiling for part of the patch. The part beneath takes longer to darken and much longer to clear. Along the thin edge of the scale, where a trace of gas is held without being shut in altogether, a narrow gray crescent forms. Thus in the space sheltered by a ledge there comes to be a second, smaller ledge with a growth of its own. A hand could cover the whole arrangement without touching the wall on either side.

There will be no great dispersal when the blister goes. The first tilt may even favor the patch, bringing the warm current more squarely in under its shelf. For a few breaths the uppermost pores, ordinarily the last to receive anything, will take as much as the lower ones. Then the wall will settle on its inner face. Its weight will come to rest on three or four stout scales, leaving just room enough beneath for the powder to keep its shape. In the close heat the last of the gas will be used up. For a while, if the wall could be lifted, there would still be a thumbprint there, with a small crescent beside it.
