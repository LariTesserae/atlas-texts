The first one to come out is generally an old woman with one short foot. She comes out under the third bench on the west side, in water still shallow enough to show the red paving. A little water runs uphill along a joint in the paving, gathers itself against the leg of the bench, and stands up. For a while it is possible to see the joint quite plainly through her shin. Then she puts her weight on the foot and it is as difficult to see through as any other piece of heavily crazed earthenware.

She sits on the bench to do up a sandal. There is no sandal. Her fingers have to go through the business just the same, including the small impatient movement by which she used to get the strap out from under her heel. By the time she is done there are usually two or three others sitting beside her. They give her room for her elbow without interrupting what they are saying. From above, their talk sounds like somebody sorting broken cups.

There have to be connections. A wet tile by itself will not support so much as the smallest child. A whole wet roof will sometimes produce a pair of hands, working back and forth over the same patch, trying it for a wobble. But once the water has joined across the red street, a woman can have the use of the impression of her knee left on a landing halfway up the opposite slope, and of the heel of her hand where she leaned over a jar, and of the particular set of her mouth preserved, for no very good reason, on the underside of a length of drain. The mouth was not pressed into the clay. She held it that way while she smoothed the bore with a wet rag on a stick. It appears that this was enough.

Not all of them come together quite right. There is a tall fellow who has to keep stooping because most of the work he did here he did under a stair. Out in the open, where he can see for miles, it takes him half the evening to unbend. On the other hand there are people who seem to have gained a good deal by it. The little girl who used to follow her father about the kilns has the hands of a woman of fifty. She likes to show the other children how neatly she can snap a bad tile across her knee. Her father, when present, still tries to stop her.

They are not particularly interested in the people who made the place. They are interested in who has come out tonight. There is a man the old woman looks for soon after fixing her sandal. If he is there they go up to a certain landing where the bench is wide enough for the two of them to lie down. If he is not, she plays a game on the pavement with some bits of grit and anybody who cares to lose them to her. On one exceptionally wet evening two of the man came out. She sat between them all night, upright, with a hand on each one's knee. Neither took this arrangement amiss. Only one of them knew her well; the other was quite young and spent much of the time looking over his shoulder at the prodigious extent of the eastern wards, which in his day had ended in a drying shed and a pile of wasters.

They know what the square plugs are for. Sometimes a party will gather round one and the tall man will put both hands to its head. As long as he is holding it they feel the pull of the water going away down a dark bore, though he has not turned the plug and not a drop has gone. It used to go somewhere. People down there used to set out little stools and sit with their vessels between their feet waiting for the first cool breath to come up the pipe. One evening the tall man took his hands away and found he was holding a little stool. He was more astonished than anybody. It had three legs, one of them mended with a winding of cord. The old woman took it from him and sat down on it to see if the winding would hold. It did.

Toward morning the shallowest connections begin to fail. This is why they come down from the landings while there is still plenty of water on the street. Nobody much likes the experience of having a leg left on the other side of a dry stair. They sit in a row with their feet in the last continuous sheet, which is scarcely thicker now than the wet on the blade of a knife. Seen from the rim they might be people waiting for a boat, except that one after another they bend to attend to something at their feet and do not straighten up again.

The stool stayed. It is under the west bench, well back where a person looking down through the water would be unlikely to notice it. On dry days it is the color of the paving. If you put a finger under its seat you can feel, quite sharp and recent, the twist of every strand of the cord.
