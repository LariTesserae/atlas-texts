At one of the oval holes there is usually a child lying on his stomach with one arm out of sight. He is not, as people coming up the path suppose, in difficulties. His mother has sent him for eggs. The hole goes in a little way and then turns along the leaf, and in the turn a brick-red hen has made her nest. She bears the boy a considerable affection. He can tell how many eggs there are by the number of times she lifts one foot and puts it down on his wrist, making room. When there is only one he leaves it. A stranger putting a hand in there would get a cut to the bone.

His family lives under the path. One goes down to them by the notches beside the hole, first facing the leaf and then, at a place polished by the seat of everybody's trousers, turning round. There is a shelf there with a rather better prospect of the weather than one gets above. The house has a roof of its own, although nothing has ever fallen on it but things dropped by people on the path. The roof slopes inward. On market days, when there is a good deal of traffic, the boy's grandmother puts a colander under the spout and sits beside it shelling nuts. Buttons come down, and fruit stones, and the ivory ferrules from the ends of walking sticks. Once a perfectly good glass eye came down, followed almost at once by its owner, who knew the notches and made excellent time.

There are seven people on the shelf, not counting the ones in the wall. These last are small soft animals of such nearly the same green as the inside of a leaf that it is easier to see what one has eaten than to see the animal. They come out at supper for the salt on the bowls. The grandmother gives each a bowl to itself, turning it slowly in her lap until the little tongue has been all the way round. It is a job she could perfectly well leave undone. In her youth she was a tender, and even now she dislikes having her hands at liberty while other people are talking. They begin of themselves to pick things apart.

The boy sleeps next to her. In the middle of the night he feels her sit up and put the flat of her hand against the wall. There is a disturbance that comes along the rim at about this hour, too light to shake anything loose, but enough to set the bowls whispering where they touch. For most of her life she thought it was the gray apes, which get up very early. In fact it is an animal going home.

Few have seen the whole of one. A traveler looking over the lip sees, if he is fortunate, what appears to be the other hand of someone looking over from beneath: four blunt brown fingers, very deliberately taking hold. Farther on another hand takes hold before the first has quite let go. There may be forty or fifty such pairs. The body between them is no thicker than a folded blanket and hangs in easy scallops, so that a young one walking underneath its mother can rest in any of a number of slings. By day they lie one upon another on the underside of the old road with the bells. The flowers have a rank, milky smell which clings to their coats. The grandmother smelled it once on the boy's hair and knew he had been farther along under the shelf than he was allowed to go.

Nobody minds them using the rim. What is troublesome is their practice of carrying a bit of the day's sleeping place away with them. A mother peels off a ribbon of flower stalk, passes it back from hand to hand, and goes about her night's business trailing it behind her. If the stalk comes free, the young have something familiar to sleep on in the new place. Quite often it does not come free. In this manner an old flowering road has been drawn, by lengths too small to notice in any one season, toward the inhabited edge. Already on still evenings the smell of the bells comes into the house. A few pale moths have found their way across, and beat against the lamp while the family eats.

The grandmother has been down to look. There is a knot involved, of course. There nearly always is. It is a poor piece of work, two live ends made fast with a dead one, the whole pulled so tight that a little white juice stands out around it. She could cut the dead part with the nail of her thumb. She has instead asked the boy to count the hands that go by in the night. It gives him something to do, and there is a thing she wants to know.

For six nights he gets different numbers. He falls asleep; two go by abreast; an infant riding upside down takes hold for the pleasure of it and spoils his count. On the seventh there is rain below and clear air above, and the light coming off the rain is enough to see by. He sees that the last animal is an old male with a bare place on his back. He is carrying nothing. Where the others have let a length of stalk fall slack, he gathers it, hand over hand, and lays it along the rim. At the oval hole he stops. The hen makes her small, receptive noise. He reaches in past her, feels about for a while, and goes on empty-handed.

In the morning the boy gets down on his stomach. Beyond the warm eggs, just where the hole turns, his fingers come upon a length of flower stalk doubled neatly back on itself. There are older lengths underneath, some gone quite woody. One has taken root. He brings his grandmother up to feel it, steadying her by the belt while she lies down. Her working hand is too crooked to get round the turn. He has to put his in beside it and bring the live tip to her finger.

They stay like that long enough for two parties of travelers to step over them. It will be years before the stalk is thick enough to take anybody's weight. There is no telling what the distance will be, or what doors might open onto it by then. For the present it is a good dry place to lay. The hen, who has been waiting on the path with her neck feathers up, steps on the grandmother's head to encourage her to move.
