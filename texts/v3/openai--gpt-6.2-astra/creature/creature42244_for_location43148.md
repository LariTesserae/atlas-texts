In the slit between the dead wood and the fig root lives a small brown frog. From the court one can usually see just its chin. There is a pale nick in the lower lip, scarcely a mark until the frog fills its throat and the nick comes and goes like a seed being turned over. The rest of it might be quite large, for all the slit reveals. In fact, when it comes out, it could sit on the fallen fig and there would be room on the fig for another.

Its day begins a little after the rain. While the drops are falling it stays where it is. A drop off the middle fern would strike a considerable portion of its back. When the taps cease, the frog works its forefeet out beside its chin and remains some time in that position, as though the body these feet belong to were still asleep farther in. Then it is on the floor. The hind legs tuck up so neatly that there is nothing about it, at rest, to suggest how it got there.

It feeds at the ragged end of the fig. The flies coming out are easier to take than the flies going in: there is an instant when the head is clear of the split and the wings are still caught in it. The frog sits a little to one side. More than once a fly walks the length of its forearm unmolested, behind the great raised sill of the eye. But let one cross that last purple fold of skin on the fruit and there is a small forward hitch of the whole frog. The fold is bare. Sometimes a thread of pulp sticks to the frog’s mouth, and it has to wipe its face with one hand, an action much slower and more particular than the taking of the fly.

There are other stations. Where the fern tip drips, springtails collect along the line between wet silt and dry. The frog can sit with its belly in the damp and feed on the line as it comes in. Near the termite work it seldom stays. Ants feel under the edges of things there, and the folded ankle of a frog has an edge quite like any other until an ant goes under it. Then there is an abrupt leap, disproportionately long, and the frog has to make its way back from among the fern stems. No track connects these places. Only on very soft ground do the long hind toes leave a pair of fine diverging strokes, with a blank beyond.

By the third dry afternoon the slit is empty too. The frog has worked round behind the wood, where the part touching the bark is black and cool. A minute snail goes over the sill that held the frog’s chin. The fruit settles; the flies find a second way out underneath. When the frog next appears it waits at the old split for a long while. Below it, almost against its foot, a clear wing opens, takes the light, and is gone.
