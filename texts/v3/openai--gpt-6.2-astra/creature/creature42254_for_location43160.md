Something lives under the shell that goes rocking down. It gets to the lower shore before the shell does. For the last part of the fall the two are separate: a white valve still swaying a little, and below it, descending quite straight, a thing the size of a thumbnail with its eight arms gathered to a point. It lands without disturbing the grit. When the shell lands, a little way off, an arm goes out and feels along the lip.

It is a little octopus, nearly the color of the grit just now. Its eyes are good. At the mouth of the valley it can pick out a shrimp standing perfectly still on a wine-colored felt; it will put an arm around the back of the felt before the shrimp has made up its mind which way to go. Here at the crossing the eyes are chiefly useful for telling the roof from the open way to sea. Much of what it knows about the town it knows one sucker at a time. An arm laid on a bank can follow a single whorl of worm tube across six clam shells. It passes lightly over the living fans. There is a little give to the wet lip of a fan, quite different from the give of the sponge beside it, and it is a bad place to leave the tip of an arm.

The fallen valve is worth keeping. There is a room just off the square where a pebble once went round and round until it wore its way out through the bottom. The hole it left is a good fit for the octopus; the room is rather too big. A skate can get the end of its snout in at the upper opening. The octopus takes the valve there by slow stages, walking on some of its arms and carrying the shell upright in the others. When the ray comes through, there is no time to get it the last few inches. The octopus lets it fall flat and goes under it. The push of water drives one edge deep into the sand. Two flatfish go over the shell in the wake of the ray, one after the other, like the shadows of the same leaf.

By the next slack the valve is in place. It does not stop the upper opening, but it makes a low inner roof, with a space above it for the skate to nose about in if it likes. For a while the octopus lies under it doing nothing that can be seen from either hole. Then the end of an arm comes out through the bottom and begins passing grit in. There is one rough place where the shell rocks against the wall. It takes very little grit to steady it.

There are others of its kind in the valley. One has a den among the old lips that face the wrong way. On the flood that octopus can lie well back in the dark and let the empty fan in front of it fill up with things that swim badly. On the ebb it has to go out like anybody else. Another, no bigger across than a goby's eye, lives in the channel down the middle of a dead worm tube. It can turn round in there. By the time it has learned all there is to learn about the two ends of the tube, it will no longer be able to turn round in it.

For several tides the one at the crossing goes on bringing things home: a broken wrist with some of the hairs still moving in the current, half a cockle, a crab so broad that it has to be taken apart outside. Then even the finest food goes by the lower hole untouched. From within the room comes a faint repeated puff. It moves a few grains out across the clay and draws none back. A shrimp stations itself just to one side of the puff, where food keeps coming up past its face without any work of sifting.

Under the valve there are eggs now. They hang from the white hollow in short strings, each egg clear except for a little cloudy knot low down. The octopus can reach them all without moving from her place against the bottom hole. She passes the narrow skirts of her arms between the strings and sends water through them. She does this when the streets outside are bare and when they stand deep in feathers. In time each cloud has two dark points in it. The eyes come early; for a long while there is nothing in the room for them to see.

When the young come out, the water is running toward the mouth. A few go straight up and strike the underside of the valve. Most go out through the pebble hole with the water their mother sends after them. For a moment there are little octopuses all across the slant of the two ways, each complete, each with a little store of food inside it, going backward into the town. One catches hold of a tube and stays. Another arrives on the hairy wrist of a crab, lets go, and is gone before the wrist can turn inward. Farther along there are so many open mouths that it is difficult to follow any one of them for long.

One gets as far as the gray water. It settles on a felt of weed, on the side away from the flow. The felt is much finer than it looked from out in the water. There are spaces between the strands, and down in one of the spaces something is walking, picking up brown crumbs and turning them over. The little octopus stands there on seven arms for a time. The eighth goes down among the strands.
