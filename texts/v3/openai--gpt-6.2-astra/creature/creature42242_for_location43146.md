The first one you are likely to see is no bigger than a wet leaf. It is a vole lying on its side at the back of a chamber, and the rest of it is hidden by its young. There are four of them. The blue of their bellies shows through the fur; one has a willow scale stuck flat between its shoulders. They lie with their noses well in under the long belly fur of the mother. When she gets up, two come a little way with her before they let go.

There are several such chambers. The voles have done very little to them. Where a roof is too low they have taken out some earth, and where it is high they have brought grass in until they can feel the roof again. The grass comes from the old lip of the shelf. In among the heather there are blades which have been bitten through near the ground and drawn backwards under the willows, so that the seed head, going last, has combed a fine line in the mud. A blade will serve first to lie on and afterwards, when it has been broken down to short soft lengths, to stop a draught. At the broken end a whole handful stirs in a hole each time the wind blows against the face of the wall.

Seen clear of the nest, the mother is a rather long, smoke-colored animal with small ears. There is a nick in one ear, only apparent when the light from the snow comes in behind it. She has a way of going down a passage that makes very little disturbance overhead. She puts her chin along one root and her flank along another and goes between them like something being drawn through a ring. Even the wren, which weighs next to nothing, makes the town look inhabited in a way that she does not.

Much of what she eats grows within a body's length of where she lies: the pale bases of sedges, a bud that has opened indoors, bark from the underside of a young branch. There are short lengths of peeled willow in the nest. A young vole will hold one between its forefeet and work at it long after there is anything left on it. For water they have only to press the moss. The little hollow a paw makes fills from the sides and clears almost at once.

They do not all go to sleep when the light goes off the ceiling. It is then that a grown vole comes out from under the grass by the upper seep and stands with its forefeet in the street. Where there was moss to tread on a few minutes earlier there is water up to its white wrists. It tries twice a little farther along, then goes over the arch. Behind it comes a smaller one, which gets as far as the crown of the arch and stops there to wash. For a moment its head is out among the blue leaves in the open air. It has its eyes tight shut and is drawing both paws slowly down over its nose. From the boulder above you could see it quite plainly, if you knew which of the fifty willows to look at.

In winter there is another town above this one. It is made in the thickness of the snow, where the buried ends of the sedges keep narrow shafts open. The voles go up for the seeds. Their breath enlarges the shafts into smooth pockets, and by spring some of the pockets join. When the snow lifts away from the leaves there are bits of grass and little black droppings suspended in the bottom of it, marking a floor that is no longer there. For a day or two a vole coming to the end of a branch will rear up and feel about with its nose at the place where the passage ought to continue.

There were nests in the part that fell. One is still visible in section, a thumb's depth of yellow grass between two layers of dark leaves. Nothing uses it now except a spider that has slung three threads across the opening. The chamber immediately behind it is quite sound. This is where the nick-eared vole goes when the water comes round the back of her bed. She takes the young one at a time, by the loose skin of the neck. The journey is not far, but she has to put each one down to get herself turned at the crossing. Folded up there on the wet moss, a young vole keeps the shape it had in her mouth, feet together, tail tucked in. Then she takes hold again and it is gone. By the time the beads on the roots have frozen hard there are four warm hollows in the dry grass, less than the length of her body from the open face of the mountain.
