The buff on the splinter has a nap. Seen from the low end of the room it looks quite smooth, the colour of the inside of an almond. Seen across the stone, with the light coming past it, it has thousands of little clear ends. A bead sliding under there does not wet a patch all at once. It leans the ends over in rows, and a darker colour travels ahead of it, like the mark a thumb makes going the wrong way on velvet.

There is something at home among the ends. Its back is clear and its belly takes the colour of its last meal, so that in the middle of the pasture the easiest part of it to distinguish is the empty part. A narrow bright fork goes forward, hesitates, and takes a sideways hold. The rest comes up in a tuck. What looked like the two prongs of a fork then prove to be the curled edges of a single foot. Under the pleat where the creature joins its foot there is a mouth. It takes an end of the nap in and draws it through, leaving the fine hair standing, stripped of its soft coat. For a while one can see exactly where it has fed: there is a little combed place in the buff, with a shine quite unlike the raw shine of the crescent the mite left behind.

Two of these grazers could lie abreast in the width of the mite's track. There are seldom two abreast. One may go a long way under the impression that there is only a single one in the room, extraordinarily often to be met at the edge of the splinter. Then a tap shakes the water there. A whole fringe of feet takes hold at once. The backs hang out for a moment in the clear, with a grain of buff meal in the bend of each, before settling separately among the hairs again.

Their young live on the shelf. They have no colour there. They spend most of the day in the drop, their small round backs pressed up against its skin from within. The shelf is rough basalt, and there are hollows in it small enough to keep water from one day to the next; but a young one that has grown too broad for a hollow must go with the drop. In the last minute of the light one can sometimes make out, well above the shelf and apparently standing on nothing at all, a pair of those forked feet. They are feeling along the curve of the water. Each finds the other several times. Neither finds the stone.

When the drop touches down, water goes both ways along the drag marks. Most of it spreads towards the white fans. A little runs back in one of the finer grooves as far as the cool splinter, where the nap draws it in so smartly that the ends lie flat. This is how a young grazer sometimes arrives at the pasture. It comes up against the underside of the stone back first and stays there, held by the thinning water, its foot still feeling about towards the floor. Long after the light has gone it gets turned over. In the morning there will be a buff speck in it, well forward under the pleat, hardly enough colour yet to tell it from the stone.
