For some weeks the best evidence of an inhabitant was a mistake in the laying of the blue glass. The last hole on the white shelf received two beads. For a while they stood one on top of the other, the upper one rocking with each knock of the gate. By morning somebody had taken it down and put it in a niche beside the street. There was no good reason to light the niche. It was shallow and had nothing in it. The bead was there because it had to be put somewhere, which was the first quite homely thing anyone had seen in the sill.

The difficulty in seeing the people is that one looks for something going along between the houses. Very little of a person goes along. Most of one stays distributed through a small amount of building work, rather as the pressure of a thumb stays distributed through the flat side of a plum. What goes out in the morning is a disposition of the joints. A length of gutter takes a slight fall toward the square. A coping stone a little farther on comes down snug against its fellow. Under the gravel, one of the buried streets gives a dry little click, perfectly audible in the water. By then there is somebody in the square.

It is possible to know one from another. The one that attends to the glass, for instance, brings the edges of two stones together first at the corners, then along their whole length. There is always that instant of hesitation, as if something might have been left between them. In the deep houses west of the square there is another who does it all at once. The distinction is as plain, once learned, as the distinction between the steps of two people on a stair overhead. It holds even in quite new masonry. Indeed, the young white stone shows to advantage a habit almost worn out of sight in the gray.

They can be in the same place. On the flight of public steps between the two tilted plates, as many as four have been distinguished at once, two going up and two coming down. Each had the full width of the stair. There was no confusion until one of the ascending ones stopped. Then, several steps below, somebody coming down made room. This happened before there could have been any need for it. The little courtesy put a pink flush into a drain at the foot of the flight.

Their hours of work are very unequal. The households near the face have the knock of the gate done with in a few moments. They put the shelves out again, shake the dust down, and carry on. Their relatives three streets back may still be in the thick of the same knock at supper time. At the copper door there are days when it is hardly worthwhile to start anything. A visitor going there from the front takes some small unfinished job along, a bright chip of mortar to be rubbed smooth, or two poor edges which can be made into one good one. It would be rude to arrive quite at leisure at a door where somebody is still taking the weight.

There are pleasures which do not seem to serve the gate at all. In the evening two or three of them will occupy the strands of green on the chipped tile. The tile settles a trifle lower; the strands draw separately through the light, each with its own narrow shadow. Nothing is collected. No part of the plant is cut. When the patch on the opposite roof first took hold, a very old inhabitant, recognizable by a double settling of every third joint, spent nearly a day getting across to it. All it did on arriving was turn the roof a fraction toward the sun. The two patches have leaned together ever since.

As for their young, there is reason to suppose that a street is laid before there is anyone to live in it, but not very long before. The first beads in the white shelf went pale in order from the square inward. On the second evening they went pale in the same order except for the fourth, which remained dark, then brightened twice. A near wall immediately folded its collecting tray away, though the gutters were clear and the gate quite still. After a little while it put the tray out again. Several times the fourth light tried this, and each time the wall obliged.

Today there is a slight hollow under that bead. It extends as far as the fifth hole, no farther. When a blow comes through, all the old streets take it up in the accustomed fashion; roofs lift, paving turns, the long work goes on toward the copper door. On the new shelf, after everything is quiet, two minute pieces of glass tip toward each other with a sound too small to hear from the path. At the edge of the square a shutter, already safely open, closes and opens once more.
