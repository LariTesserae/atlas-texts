The woman in the third house keeps the mug for callers. The handle is turned out so that a carter can take a drink without putting his dirty knuckles against her curtain. She fills it at breakfast and again when she eats her dinner. On a warm day it has sometimes been emptied four or five times in the interval. People know where the tap is.

She used to be the weighmistress. She has the habit, when somebody hands her a baby or a leg of mutton, of making a small adjustment at the knees before she takes the weight. She is very accurate about mutton. Babies are harder, on account of their putting out their arms towards the person who has just given them up. She lives with a grandson who is learning to cut letters in stone. The pale brick is no use to him. He practises on the underside of the parcel tray, lying on the step with one cheek on a folded cap. There is a good capital R down there which nobody is ever likely to see.

There are seventeen people in the six houses, counting a lodger who goes away on Wednesdays and a girl who sleeps in whichever house her mother is cleaning the following morning. The girl has a little wooden case with a toothbrush and a nightdress in it. Given her choice she sleeps at the end nearest the steps, where there is a window through which a gull will look in at about five. Seen from the bed it is an enormous bird, very clean about the throat, with a dab of something like mustard at the hinge of the bill. It has no interest in the girl. The window looks straight through the house to another window, and beyond that is the fish merchant’s yard.

People talk quite ordinarily indoors. Outside, if two stop to talk, they generally rest the sides of their heads against the same wall. There are places in the felt of the machine hall where the nap is still fresh behind framed photographs of people eating: a wedding supper, a Sunday-school tea, three men in their shirtsleeves round a single boiled lobster. It is the custom to hire the hall for occasions at which someone will want to make a speech. The man in the blue jersey takes the afternoon off, having first put a wooden cover over the gear. He has heard more speeches than anybody in the borough, since the cover has to come off again and he is usually back rather before it is wanted. 

Below the third house there is an animal not readily seen from the wells. It consists, so far as the municipal drawings indicate, of a buff-coloured cord with a mouth at either end. In reality there is only one mouth. The other end belongs to another animal, the two having crossed at a point under the tiles where the surveyor could not follow them. Each goes on for a considerable distance. When a little of the fine matter escapes the grey fringes it settles in the lee of a cord. The cord rolls over on it very slowly. Along its whole length there are minute pairs of lips, all engaged in passing the meal along towards the mouth, which accordingly has nothing to do but stay in one place and eat. One such mouth has occupied the hollow back of a porcelain finger plate since before the present weighmistress was born. You can still make out the two screw holes, through which its food arrives.

The cords are eaten in turn, though seldom right through. There is a flat fish with such a short upper jaw that it can take hold of nothing unless it can get the lower jaw underneath. It works along with its chin to the tile until it comes to a cord, shaves off a little of the buff substance, and rests there for a day or two digesting it. Transparent animals collect in its wake. What looks under the lamp like the fish’s tail continuing to move after the fish has stopped is a hundred or more of these creatures changing places to reach the fresh edge.

The inhabitants of the houses see less of this than a visitor might suppose. Once a year the grating below the weighhouse is drawn up into daylight and laid on trestles. The red crabs stay with their bars. In air they are a disappointing colour, more nearly that of the rubber ring from a preserving jar, and for the most part they sit quite still with their claws folded underneath them. Children are brought to see them. It is generally a child who first observes that one crab is knocking, is going through the full business of knocking, in fact, with a shell in its claw, but so softly that out here beside the sea wall the blow cannot be heard. The man in blue kneels and puts his ear to the free end of the bar. Then everyone wants a turn. There is a photograph in the hall of a row of people, some in good clothes, apparently kneeling to drink from a long iron trough.

At about four the girl with the wooden case comes down the street. She has been sent for the mug. Her mother is doing the third house tomorrow and would like to give it a proper scalding tonight. Under its base the sill is perfectly dry, except for one small glistening crescent where the tin has jumped the width of a hair and come down again. In the newly vacant ring a fly settles. There are very few flies in the borough. This one rubs its front feet, walks out to the edge of the sill, and there, in the wind coming round the corner, has some difficulty getting its wings open. The girl waits until it has managed before drawing the curtain across.
