There is a dipper at the pump house. You generally see the white of its breast before you see the bird. It shows between the sleepers, low down where there ought to be nothing white except perhaps a sheep’s tuft caught on a nail. Then it gives two or three little bobs and goes out as neatly as a light. If you wait for it to come up through the same hole in the reflection you can wait a long time. It has got in under the green lip of the bank.

On a bright day one can lie on the step and watch it. In water that scarcely covers its toes it walks like any other short-legged bird, rather deliberately, picking here and there. An inch farther on, the same walk takes its whole head under. There is something very odd about seeing a bird with its head in another element, looking about perfectly at home while the rest of it stands in the wind. Presently the tail tips up and follows. The dipper works its way down the sloping side of a sleeper, gripping the wood and giving little strokes with its wings. What looked above water like a plump brown bird now looks narrow and silvered over. It gets a shrimp from the roots. A second shrimp lets go and rows away on its back, and the bird passes within an inch of it without noticing.

The eleven miles out to the sheep are of very little use to the dipper. What it has instead is an extraordinary length of edge. There is the stony lip of the creek, good in almost any weather; the lee of the rails; the undercut places where a beak can be put in up to the eyes among the red root tips. These last have to be learned afresh. A whole morning’s good feeding sometimes goes off to sea with the fence posts. But later there will be a dirty yellow line across a bay, seeds and beetles and bits of cut rush collected against some new obstruction, and the dipper will be there, standing on the obstruction itself, reaching over.

There are two of them at present. They nest on a brick ledge well back in the old discharge arch, in a ball of moss so much like the moss growing on either side that the best way to find it is to look for the clean place on the brick where their feet land. One bird comes in along the ditch with a beakful of pale grubs all hanging one way. It stops on a stone, bobs once, and flies into the wall. For a second the wall makes a thin frying sound. Out comes the bird with nothing in its beak. The other has been on the wheel housing all this time, singing, though until the wheel stops it is quite impossible to hear a note. Even then it takes a moment to distinguish the song from the water still dribbling out of the ironwork. There are low scratchy passages in it and then two or three notes of surprising sweetness, delivered with the eyes half shut.

In the evening, when the step begins to darken, one of the birds goes round to the landward side of the shed. The returning water comes up there through an old wheel rut. At first it does no more than set the loose ends of the moss afloat. Then a little hump travels under a felt of last year’s grass, stops, travels again. The dipper takes hold of a straw and gives it a good hard backward jerk. Underneath is a cranefly larva, gray, thick as a bootlace, already withdrawing itself tail first into the mud. For a moment they pull against one another. The bird wins and goes bowling off round the corner with its prize. By the time it comes back the rut is a yard wide and quite clear, and a minnow is standing precisely where the bird stood, making just the smallest movements necessary to stay there.
