Look for a bowl that has been broken and mended. There are not many. With so much good clay at hand it is generally easier to pinch up another. But once in a while you find one with a long crack in the bottom and three neat staples of white clay let into its sides. Fill it with water. It leaks at quite a rate. Whoever went to that trouble had something else in mind than keeping water in a bowl.

What lives here begins in the little waists between the bowls. As the row cools, one waist after another parts with a tick. The bowls draw apart no farther than the breadth of the break. At the first tick there is sometimes an answering tick a long way off; the two sounds are so like that for years they were put down to an echo. But a row of nine bowls makes only eight sounds, and on a good still night you can hear the distant party go on trying for the ninth.

By daylight the bowls will be gone. There is nothing mysterious about their going. A film of water will carry a shallow bowl a surprising distance over wet pavement, and the pavement itself is not where it was. The interesting thing is what stays behind: a disposition, in that particular reach of the plain, to do a thing eight times and then wait. Eight slabs come smartly out of their frames. At the ninth the cord hangs slack for a moment, and the little tongue of clay that the cord should have cut off folds over onto the back. You can put your finger under it. It is the beginning of a handle, though it may be another season before there is anything to lift.

Their bodies, if bodies are wanted, are stretches of work. One of them may have the use of a kiln throat, half a mile of hot brick, a hundred loose squares and a bowl sailing freely on the flood. Another uses much the same stretch on the ebb. They have quite different touches. The first leaves an unusually deep print, with a clean little nick across the whorls; the other barely dents the clay and wipes the edges afterward. There is no use lying in wait at the place where the nick ought to have been acquired. The print was nicked for the first time by a splinter sticking up in a bad frame. The splinter washed away. The nick suited its owner and has been kept.

It is difficult at first to tell any of this from the ordinary business of the country. Watch, though, where two gutters meet. A little water is very often turned aside there and sent the long way round, over warm slabs where a good part of it will be lost. At the end of this expensive detour is the mouth of a bowl. The water strikes the lip on its way in. For perhaps a minute a clear, rather high note carries over the plain, and the kiln at the other gutter lets out a cap of steam. Next day the bowl will have been tilted a little, with a chip under one side. The note is better so. Neither gutter is improved by this arrangement.

They go to some lengths for one another. There was a kiln that settled crookedly and in time lost the use of its eastern courses. On that side the new squares came out soft. Each afternoon the water took them to pieces almost as fast as they were made. For a long while the same patch of floor went on being made there, with the same broad, light impression at the edge of each square. Then the floor of a sound kiln, some distance to the south, began to turn out alternate rows with this impression and its own. Between the two buildings ran a nearly continuous line of shallow dishes. Each fitted into the print in the bottom of the one ahead of it, as thumb fits into thumbprint. It took most of a flood to pass the pressure down the line. By the time a square came out at the sound kiln, the water had gone back from the crooked one and the place where the pressure started was dry. This seems not to have troubled either of them. It went on for years. When at last the crooked kiln fell in, the work at the other was interrupted for one afternoon. After that there were two prints on each square, side by side.

Such an afternoon is about as near as one comes to seeing an inhabitant entire. A cord lies across wet clay. The heat that would set the clay is available; the frame is in place; the water is going down and there will not be so good an opportunity again until tomorrow. Nothing is missing from the operation. Nevertheless it stops. Somewhere under the floor a bowl that has rung at this hour for years is full of fallen brick. The quiet extends out from it, from slab to slab, farther than its sound ever carried.

As for the bowl with the staples, set it empty on a level square and run a wet finger round the rim. There are two notes in it. One is the usual note of a bowl of that size. Under it is a low, loose burr, hardly musical, due to the edges of the crack rubbing very slightly together. Whoever made the staples tried at least twice before getting their length right; there are plugged holes beside the ones in use. Toward evening, when the great stretches of pavement begin to cool and give a little at the joints, you may hear that particular burr from a long way out on the floor. Presently a second bowl takes it up, not quite accurately. A third makes a wretched job of it. In the red light, if you get down low enough, you can see the third bowl rocking furiously on its foot, trying again.
