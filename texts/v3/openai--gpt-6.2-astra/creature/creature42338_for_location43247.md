There is a dipper in the meadow, though from the shelter what you generally see of it is a white spot going to and fro among the notches. It goes right out to the wet lip and stands looking over, bending its legs a little at intervals as if to get a better view. Then it steps off. On the way back it comes up well to one side of the water, flies low across the flowers and puts down both feet and lands running. There is so much wind in the last yard that it is rather like watching a person get off a moving cart.

Its nest is behind the middle notch, in a pocket in the rock. Almost the whole of it is green. It would be difficult to say where the living moss on the rock leaves off and the moss which the bird has pulled up and poked back into place begins. There is a round dark hole in the lower part, with the bleached rib of a willow leaf sticking out of it. This has been there since the first egg was laid. Both birds brush against it twenty times a day and neither has ever seen fit to pull it in.

It is awkward country for a dipper. There is plenty of water but very little of it has a top to it wide enough for a bird to get into. At one place, close under the wall, the threads come together in a pool about the size of a boot. Here the bird can go properly under. It walks in until the water closes over the small of its back; one last bubble of light slips off its head, and for a moment there is nothing in the pool but a moving disturbance of the grit. It comes out with its white front quite dry to look at and a little bundle of sticks crosswise in its bill. It beats the bundle on a stone. What it wants is the grub inside. A pipit watching from the rushes occasionally gets something out of these proceedings, though it has to come up very smartly, in the interval between one blow and the next.

By the time the young are feathered there is scarcely a loose pebble in the pool that has not been turned over. The old birds go farther for food then. One will set off down the face of the mountain and be gone so long that the little white half-moons of the nestlings' chins sink one by one out of sight in the hole. Meanwhile the other works the wet ground on foot, sometimes coming up as far as the slabs. It is this bird that keeps the gutter clear. In the spring, as soon as there is a finger of running water there, it gets its bill under the winter's felt of dead stems and rolls it back in pieces to get at the small black larvae underneath. The seat is a convenient place to stand while doing this. A person coming up in June finds the little drain in good order and the seat considerably less so.

On a cloudy evening you can sit there with your heels drawn in and hear the dipper come home. It gives one rather penetrating note somewhere out in the milk, well below the level of your feet. There is an answer, very thin, from the rock. Nothing whatever shows at the rim. But presently, along with the rattle the falling water makes against the blades of stone, there comes a little brisk flapping sound. It is the bird under the overhang, shaking the rain off before it goes in among its children.
