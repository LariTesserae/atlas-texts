If you go out along the line after the change of shift you can find the people who have stopped to talk by the little rings round their boots. The water displaced by a person standing still finds its way up through the turf and lies in a bright welt all round the sole. When the person steps away the welt holds for a moment. Then it runs inward, laying all the little green joints in the same direction. A footprint made in walking has quite a different look. The track inspector can tell, going home, where his wife met their daughter coming the other way, and which of the two had things to say.

His wife works on the fourth street down, in a room where they make the small hard rings that go inside the meters on the ceramic pipe. She has a ridge on the pad of her left thumb, from turning each ring once against a stone before she slips it over the gauge. This is not strictly necessary. No ring has failed to go over the gauge in her working life. But sometimes the stone takes a whisker off, something you see only by putting your face nearly flat against the bench, and she would rather do it there than have a man six days away hear something ticking in a pipe he has just closed up.

They have two rooms on the fifth street. In the front one, if you lie in bed, you see nothing but the opposite wall of the crack. There is a purple patch there which the daughter, when she was small, believed to be part of their furnishings. She cried the first night they took her west to visit her cousins. They put her pillow at the other end of the bed, thinking it was the light that was wrong, but what she wanted was the purple thing that ought to be there when you opened your eyes. Her mother had occasion years later to look through the transit in the survey office. What she had taken for a clump of seven or eight leaves was a considerable stretch of country, with runnels through it and pale fans of broken stone. She was sorry afterwards that she had looked. At home she could no longer get it back to its proper size.

There is a living thing in the houses which takes a little finding. People call it the hem. A good place to see it is on the underside of a sill, where the water comes creeping in over the paint and turns the corner. It looks like the edge of an old piece of clear sticking plaster which has been allowed to remain after the plaster was pulled away. Pass a finger along it and nothing happens. Put the tip of a dry spill against it, however, and a succession of tiny folds travels along the edge, away from the spill in both directions. A length of hem as long as your arm can take the news of a dry place from one end to the other in rather less than a minute.

For a long time it was supposed to be the young state of something. There were drawings in the schoolbooks with a space left at the right for what it turned into. It seems in fact to have reached its full estate on the windowsill. In the older streets a hem may go right round a block of houses, passing behind the rain collars on the downpipes and coming indoors again along the lead. Nobody knows whether that makes it one hem. In a house that is to be taken down, children are allowed to lift a length with a wet table knife. Held up to the window it loses all interest; it is scarcely to be distinguished from the water on the knife. Laid against the side of a tumbler it slowly acquires an edge again. Children generally forget about it after that, and the tumbler goes back into use.

The inspector's daughter has one in a blue saucer. She feeds it, on no particular authority, the water in which the breakfast grain has been rinsed. At one side of the saucer there is a chip; the hem has gone over the chip and a little way down the outside, which she takes as evidence that it is doing well. She has shown her father how to lift the saucer without putting his finger there.

On his rounds he looks for it now. He finds it on the backs of the distance plates, where the black enamel sweats; inside the brass cap of the points oiler; once, a very fine piece of it in the lettering on a survey peg, following each cut of the date and going round the nought without a break. Out at the permanent shower there is none at all. He has got down on his stomach there with his coat over his head and looked. Apparently there can be too much water. It wants that turning place, where there is water on one side of a hair's breadth and air on the other. There must be a prodigious quantity of it, taking all those edges together. Yet before his daughter showed him the saucer he could have said quite truthfully that in thirty years of going about the plateau with his eyes mostly on the ground he had never seen any kind of creature.

His daughter is now at the age when a person tries out the various employments. On Tuesdays she goes down with her mother. On Thursdays she walks the track. She likes best the little house where the laundry packets come out on a belt, and has asked to be put down for a turn there. If a packet is badly folded a corner of sheet works out through the paper, takes up moisture and draws it in, so that in a few hours the whole valuable parcel is no better than a heap of washing. It is the business of the person at the belt to watch for this. It appeals to her that the error is so small and that you have a chance to put it right. Her parents had rather hoped she might like the survey office. They have said nothing. There is time.

Lately she stays behind a little when the three of them come up to talk beside the tracks. Her parents go on ahead to the quiet and turn, and there she is at the very point where the sound goes away. She has discovered that by leaning forward from the ankles she can put her head in the quiet while the backs of her legs still feel the vibration. Straightening up, she gets the whole town back at once, the three deep notes and the thin fret of the works laid over them. Her mother, watching her tip slowly to and fro with her eyes shut, makes the small impatient gesture that means, down on the stages, *bring it through*. Her father catches her hand. He wants to see how long the girl will do it. Under their feet the water rises and makes two bright rims; under hers it keeps washing from toe to heel, unable to settle.
