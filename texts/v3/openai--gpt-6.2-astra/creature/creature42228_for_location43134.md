You can pick out the stair cutter by the way she comes downhill. She puts one foot on the step and one hand under it. It is not that she expects the step to give. She wants to feel how much of the cut has come through on the other side. If there is a little raised triangle under her finger she has gone too deep, and that evening she will be back with a wisp of felt to bind the stalk. Her name is Et. She has a long upper lip which she can press against her nose when she wants both hands free and there is something she would rather not smell. The stuff in a bad binding, for instance. Good felt, however wet, smells of somebody's sleeve. Bad felt smells of a cup in which a tooth has been kept.

There are eleven people in Et's house when the lid is off, and usually nine when it is on. The other two sleep wherever they have got to. This is quite a usual arrangement. At the first touch of the lid on the tall tree everyone has to look about and see whose door is handy; there is no going crosswise on the stairs once the water starts down. Consequently a house is furnished with beds of several widths and a row of pegs for things a guest may need in the morning. Et, who is broad, has spent some poor nights in the binding shed, with a bedpost making a white spot on each hip. Her youngest brother can sleep in the spoon. He has done so, in spite of being told what the spoon is for and how disagreeable it would be for all concerned if somebody took a pinch of him in the dark.

In daylight the brother goes about with a cup of warm water and a hair. He is looking for a particular small creature which the others maintain is a bit of red leaf. There is a difference, he says, in the way the two go up a hair. A leaf goes up wet and comes down dry. The creature will sometimes come down while the hair is still wet. He has got Et to watch one go up; getting her to stay for the descent is another matter. She has forty-odd trees to tend. It is her cuts, incidentally, that make the difficulty with the count. A low branch, bent into a cut by the lid, will sometimes stick there and take nourishment. Thereafter there are two trunks when one is coming uphill and only one when coming down. Et enters these in her reckoning as landings.

Their children have the same large, rather square hands as the grown people, but very little strength to close them. The first thing a child can carry is therefore something that will stick. You see them going in solemn procession from the lip to the wood with a dab of clay on each palm, their arms held out from their sides. On the return journey they are liable to put their hands together and have to be helped apart. Later they are given charge of the cups. This is responsible work. A cup set out on ground that has not yet fired must be turned a little as it fills, or by morning it belongs to the mountain. There is a whole stretch of the ridge with white rims showing through the moss, from the winter the children had a cough.

The oldest person there is the woman who goes down in the basket. She likes the work because down there nobody tries to do things for her. Also, her hearing is mostly gone, and while other people come up from the buttonhole staggering a little from the noise, she comes up wanting her dinner. She has a thick braid of hair left, though there is hardly any hair on her head. It has been lengthened with hair from the rest of the household, not always the same colour. Before she gets into the basket Et winds it twice round her middle and tucks the end in at the neck. It is no good as a rope. What it does is keep a patch of her back warm against the place where the basket has lost a strip. They have offered to mend the basket. She likes being able to reach behind her through the gap.

For some time now she has been finding, on the ledge she made the day before, a little pad of warmth. It is much too far down to be the firing. She puts her hand out behind her as the basket goes by, and there it is. Sometimes it is no bigger than the bowl of a cup; once it extended for the length of her forearm, and at the far end of it her finger went into a dent. She said nothing about it until she could get Et to come down and feel. Et put her hand there, and then, being Et, put her other hand underneath.

There was a person asleep on the other side of the ledge.

He was a boy from one of the houses beyond the tassels. He had been going down while the lid was on, along the loose end of a root. He had a perfectly good bed at home. What he liked down here was that the rain went the other way. He showed them. Even in the daytime a little moisture kept working through the felt, gathering on the points of the roots and dropping away into the blue. From his hollow he could lie dry and watch the whole shower fall, each drop for as long as he chose to follow it. He had brought down a cup, not to catch anything in, but because he liked a drink when he woke.

Since then there has been some alteration in the work. They still send the basket down full, but the old woman does not use the whole load on the outer edge. She works a thumbful in under the hollow, widening it on the dry side; the boy would like room to have somebody to stay. Et's brother is to go first. He has a hair wound round his cup and a red speck travelling on it, quite briskly now in the warm damp. There will be time down there, he thinks, for somebody to see the whole thing.
