At first it looks as if the shelf has a ragged edge. Then the raggedness draws in, all but one little scallop at the far end, which stays out in the wind and combs itself with a foot. These are the animals that make the chisel sound. A child going up for the first time is told to put a hand flat on the seat and wait until the ticking passes under it. It is a pleasure quite distinct from hearing it. Each stroke has a small afterstroke, as if somebody down there were trying to knock with both hands and could not quite bring them down together.

Lifted off the stone, one of the knockers is no bigger than a fig. On the stone it spreads to the size of a man's ear. Its belly is the color of the shelf and fits the shelf even to the pits left by the chisel; the animal might have been poured there. What one sees from below is its back, a fine coat of dun hair divided by a parting that runs crookedly from the neck to the tail. It has two dark nostrils and no eyes that anybody has been able to find. The young ride in the parting. They come out of it with their hair all brushed one way, and for a while after they have taken to the stone for themselves it is possible to recognize which way round they lay on their mother.

They live very well on the things that miss a tree. A heel-fan catches on a grain of rock; a grub strikes belly first and sticks for the length of a breath. Out from the edge of a knocker goes a pink lip with a nick in the middle. The nick opens to admit quite a large grub. It closes so neatly about a heel-fan that sometimes the fan goes on turning in the wind for an hour after its owner has been eaten. There is a sprinkling of these little wheels under the shelf, blue in one light, colorless in another. People used to think the stone flowered there.

The ticking is loudest where there is a young one abroad. It goes along by fits. Three strokes here, then three a little farther out, and the young one, a pale thing still narrow enough to lose in the groove of a thumbnail, follows it across the stone. Sometimes two will start after the same sound and touch noses and lie perfectly still in dismay. Presently one flattens and the other creeps over it. No adult ever comes out to fetch them. Only the knocking shifts about, patient as fingers coaxing a bead through a crumpled hem, till each pale sliver has found an edge to hang its lip over.

There is one that lives on the fork that holds the tray. It is a poor place for grubs, and a worse place for shelter, and nobody gathering the plugs can remember a time when there was not a knocker there. It has grown to the shape of the fork. Its parting divides in two. When a bead drops into the pocket it draws itself up and feels the wicker all over with its mouth, paying particular attention to the mends. The people who come up leave the pocket turned inside out for it a little while. Down in the crossings of the weave is a red meal, too fine to see except where the lip has passed and left a clean straw. On gathering days the old animal eats so much of this that a little of the red shows through its back. The person waiting keeps the plugs in a closed fist. They are still soft enough at the heart to give out warmth.

Rarely, in the evening lull, a person on the shelf sees what becomes of the young. One that has got too broad for its mother's parting works its way out to the very rim. Instead of putting its lip out, it raises its whole front half. The hair opens along the crooked line. Underneath, folded small and damp-looking as the skin under a new leaf, are two skirts of clear stuff. It takes some time to get them out; one edge generally sticks to the hair and has to be freed with a foot. For those few seconds the creature has the most unlikely look of an old woman sitting up in bed attending to her clothes.

The first leaves turn in the east. There is a clap, and the skirts fill, and the knocker goes out flat across the sky. It does not go toward the trees. It sails beyond them, toward all that intermittently visible country where the amber plugs fall, its little underside, molded to a particular three inches of the mountain, exposed to the last of the light. Nothing under the shelf calls it back. The others have their mouths full by then. On a lucky evening one can see two or three go, each trailing for a moment the blue wheel of a supper it has not had time to finish.
