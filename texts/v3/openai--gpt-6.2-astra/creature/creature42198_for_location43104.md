The eyelashes open on water. For some time you can see right into the animal without seeing an eye. There is a pink shelf, then a shelf a shade paler, and far in, where the light hardly reaches, a continuous trembling as of somebody trying to hold a spoon quite still. Little things go in between the lashes. Rather bigger things come out. One of these crosses the pink country and arrives at your feet with a chip of cream-colored scale held flat over its head. Six clear legs are working under the chip. At the wall it turns the chip edgewise, puts it through a joint in the ceramic and follows it in.

The great animal has eyes, but they are underneath. When it gets up there are two blue beads in the white stamping of its belly. They are on soft stalks scarcely longer than the pile of a bath towel. They look down into the print from which the animal is rising. As long as it is up, feeling its way on its short legs to another bed, the beads travel back and forth very fast. Set down on a floor that suits it, it can look for a week at one pink rib a quarter of an inch away.

Most of its length is given over to the rearing of the things with chips. On the inmost shelf they are a trembling nap, all the legs of a generation beating together in the current drawn through the animal's side. Farther out you can distinguish the individuals. Here is one cleaning its feet, drawing each in turn through a ring it makes with the other five. Here are three riding a flake too big for any of them to handle, turning and turning just inside the fringe. When a lash bends inward, all three take hold. The scale goes sailing out without them. A fish too small to swallow it takes it by one corner and is carried some way backward before it thinks to let go.

You may suppose that things which live so close together must know one another very well. Certainly a bird can put its foot down through a fan of fish and leave each fish exactly where it was, with a toe on either side of it. Yet if the bird flies up, the fan stays open until the shadow of its body has passed away across the floor. Under a footbridge, where the shadow stays all day, there are fans that have never had a bird. At night, when the vaults go dim by imperceptible degrees, the fish gather over the dark heads of the fasteners. There is a fish for every place a shadow can be had, even the long wavering shadow of your finger held out over the water. It is rather hard to take your finger away.

There are creatures for whom the plain is by no means level. The little six-legged carriers go down behind the cove in single file, their flakes standing up like ears. A grating a few feet down passes the carriers and stops the flakes. They put them down there. Through an inspection glass you can see the heap slowly turning over, fresh cream on top and a lovely biscuit brown underneath, where a multitude of blind, flat backs are pressed against it. The backs move off, and for an instant there are bare places in the heap. Then another rank presses up. In the green light it is difficult to tell where the bodies go when they have fed. Once in a long while something like a glove full of knuckles passes across the glass and wipes it perfectly clean.

Even the entries cut off from the mains have their inhabitants. Down the dry spillway, in the intervals between the drops, a thin animal stands up on its tail. It has a mouth like the cut end of a straw. You can spend a whole warm afternoon watching the drops go down and be sure they all miss it. Come back when the lamps begin to show and there will be a bright swelling halfway along its length. Through the swelling, greatly enlarged, you can see a little square of the glaze and the fault in the glaze, a pinprick bubble that came there in the firing. By morning the animal is thin again. Two small ones stand below it on the track.

It is the six-legged things you find under the lip. Having delivered their flakes, they come out by a different joint, pale and soft from a change of skin. For a time they cannot bear the full wash of the overflow. They hang in the maker's letters, picking out the enclosed spaces first, then the crooks and the undersides of the bars. If you feel along very gently you can tell an occupied letter: something lets go at one end and takes hold of you. Bring your hand up and there on the pad of your finger is a creature so recently made that the whorls of your skin show through it. Five of its feet cling. The sixth goes feeling round and round for the missing stroke of the letter.
