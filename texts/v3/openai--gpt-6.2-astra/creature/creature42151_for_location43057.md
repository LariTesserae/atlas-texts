Just before the leaf is dry enough to curl, something gets out from under it. It leaves six small wet marks on the stone. For a while those are easier to see than the animal. It is the brown of a place on a slab where a thumb has rubbed away the bloom of the split.

It goes to the end of the wall and puts its two front feet over. The feet are broad, with a fringe of colorless hairs. They hang there in the noise, limp as the ends of a scarf, until a wash of water runs up the outside of the wall and wets the left one. At once the little beast turns left. There is no haste about what it does next. It lowers its head and one shoulder into the wash, allows the rest of itself to be lifted, and is gone from the wall without having had to jump.

There are quite a number of them in the cleft. The biggest would cover the bowl of a spoon. The little ones are white until they have eaten. They live where the water comes off the rock. This is not quite the shore. Sometimes the shore is several yards away, with deep water between it and the thin, rather dirty pocket of air in which a family of them are going about their business. They walk on the stone, grazing with a sideways movement of the head. What looks like clean black rock until they have passed over it shows, behind them, a track of a much finer black.

They have very little hold with their feet. Try to imagine holding to that stone through one of the deep blows. A limpet can do it, but then the limpet has given the whole underside of its body to the work and can do very little else. These go loose. When the water strikes, all six feet come up and meet over the belly. The hairs at their edges mesh. Inside, between the feet and the soft pleated skin of the belly, there is enough air to last the animal a good while. It rolls along like a burr. In a strong light one might see a dozen of them going end over end through the brown water, each with its little silver sixpence flashing. The moment one touches a dry floor the feet spread again. Often it goes on feeding with a grain still in its mouth from the last place.

The one that got out from under the leaf is old. One of its hind feet has lost its fringe. All summer it has had to make use of whatever holds a little air: the white roots with their close fine forks, a loose scale on another animal’s back, once the armpit of the long-legged thing that stands up when the water returns. It stayed there nine days. The long-legged thing went through all its deliberate changes of posture with that small brown passenger tucked up against it. Then in an open hollow their two shadows fell apart on the rock, and each went on about its own affairs.

The leaf was particularly good. There was air in the bite and also, when that was gone, in the little trough on either side of the midrib. Riding it, the animal got farther into the lake than most of its kind ever go. It ate the soft brown fur along the veins. It slept. There were whole afternoons when the rushes jumped at every blow and down in the turning heart of the water the old thing had only to shift its bad foot a little farther under the hem of the leaf.

By morning it has found the landing again. Under the lip of the top course, where the mason took out a flake to make the stone sit level, there is a dry place about the size of a plum stone. Three little ones are in there already. They are pinkish from grazing the paste in the seam. The old one backs in among them, and after a certain amount of pushing all four are still. Their feet hang out in a row. Now and then a drop gathers on a fringe and runs sideways along the row of feet until a small one draws it in to drink.

Above them the leaf has dried to the color of horn. The first flat water of the morning lifts it cleanly off the wall. It goes down the face of the lake on its edge, very quick and straight this time, and nobody goes with it.
