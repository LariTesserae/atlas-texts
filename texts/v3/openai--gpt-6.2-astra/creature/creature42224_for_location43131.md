The seed eater has a bald place on its breast, the shape and size of a spoon. When it has finished with the seed it lowers itself until the bare skin lies flat on the leaf. Its eyes stay open. You can see a last little parcel of meal go down its throat. For some time nothing about it moves except the two halves of the lip, which clean one another very thoroughly, right out to the corners. Then it gets up and puts both forefeet into the heap of scales.

There are young ones in there. At first all you see is the heap subsiding in several places at once. Then a small grey back comes up powdered brown, and another, and a creature with its eyes still sealed blunders out onto the bare leaf and has to be fetched back by a hind foot. Their lips are pink, with scarcely a notch yet. They cannot open a seed. The old one has kept some of the meal in the pouches along its jaw. It stands rather awkwardly over the heap while the young reach up into the pouches and take what they want. There is a dry ticking sound as their little nails slip on its teeth.

The bare place is how it tells when to uncover them. A change of wind can take a day to travel the length of the scarp, but the slackening of a deep tongue is felt under the leaf almost at once. For a little while the curl will lie still. Later, under a perfectly clear sky, the margin will lift and slap down hard enough to leave a print of every seed scale in the wax beneath it. The young sleep well back from the margin, in the angle that never quite closes. In old animals the skin of the breast hangs loose when they walk. At rest they spread it carefully, smoothing out a wrinkle with the back of one wrist.

What takes longest to see on the plain is a runner standing still. The legs show up readily enough, being dark at the joints, but they look like four separate splinters stuck in the nap at considerable distances from one another. The body they support is thin and admits light. With the sun behind it you can see a grub, quite round and black, moving slowly back from the throat. A runner that has eaten several looks like a string of writing hung up to dry.

They do not always come to a seam for grubs. Late in the season there are females that approach the opening tail first. Each waits through several claps, lifting and setting down a foot as though the surface were unpleasantly hot. In goes the long pale tip of the body. The eggs stick just inside the torn edge, where the oil wells up; when the lips come together the eggs are pressed into the soft nap on either side. For weeks afterward, walking over an apparently sound stretch of country, you may hear a succession of faint pops underfoot. If you kneel there are runners coming up through the cream. They are folded very small, knees against cheeks. A minute or two is spent getting one leg clear of another. One of the first things a new runner does, sometimes with its hindquarters still in the hole, is put a foot against the foot of its neighbour. For a little while the two stand leaning together. Then one moves and the other goes with it.

On the scarp there is an animal known chiefly by its litter. The wind brings up lengths of translucent tubing, crumpled at one end and flared very prettily at the other. They collect in the seed heaps; a whole winter's fall of them weighs hardly anything in the hand. The animal itself lives head inward in the mouths of the abandoned ribs. It has a soft yellow body and makes the tube a little longer each time it feeds, backing out as it grows. A fringe at the flared end strains the air for wax and for the almost invisible young of the red grazers, which for part of their lives are light enough to blow about. In a still hour the fringes hang down over the ledges like wet hair.

These are what the cliff gliders come home to eat. A glider turns on its side in the updraft and runs the hooked edge of its beak along a crowded ledge. Tubes go tumbling by the score, empty before they fall. The old birds can do this in a pass so close that the down on one shoulder is worn off against the cliff. A fledgling generally comes away with a single tube held crosswise. It lands with its prize, puts a foot on the flare and pulls, only to discover that the yellow occupant has withdrawn out of reach. There are fledglings to be seen all along the shelves sitting over these things, waiting for hunger to bring the fringe out again.

Toward dusk the seed eater takes its young to the outer edge of the quiet. They travel under its belly at first. One by one they venture out, feeling ahead with the whole blunt pad of the chin. Out there the pounding is strong enough to jog a youngster sideways. It sits down abruptly and looks around for what has pushed it. Under the nap the red grazers have begun their evening ascent, climbing a single hair in such numbers that it bends and touches the next. From the height of a standing man this is a faint flush passing over the ground. Down among the hairs it is a succession of red laden arches, sinking slowly until the tips meet, then springing clear again. A young seed eater gets its chin under one. The hair slips free and dusts its mouth with grazers. For the first time the little notch in its lip has something to do: one half holds the hair, trembling with the effort, while the other wipes it clean.
