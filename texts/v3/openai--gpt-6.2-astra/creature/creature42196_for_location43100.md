On certain of the round leaves there is a second rim, a little way inside the pewter one. It is not noticeable until the eye has got used to the scale of things. Then it is quite distinct: a fine oval, rather too long for the leaf, with a short interruption at either end. If one of these leaves were lifted, the oval would stay where it was. It is the back of a small animal. The leaf has been resting on it much as a loose floorboard rests on the shoulder of a nail.

It has no need of a head. The two ends are very much alike, but the interruption at one end is a mouth and the interruption at the other is a hinge. In between there are perhaps thirty pairs of feet. These are not used for walking. Each foot is a little pad with a ridge across it, and the feet on one side take hold of the underside of one leaf while those on the other side take hold of its neighbor. There is a space between the leaves too narrow to admit a grain of dust. It is over this space that the animal makes its living.

Two leaves, however neatly they fit, never quite agree. One has its roots a shade deeper in the slate; the other has a spot of iron in its bright inner pages. For a long while they will ring together, and then there will come a moment when one is on its way up and the other on its way down. The difference would scarcely serve to loosen a flake of soot. It is quite sufficient to open the animal at the hinge. Inside is a comb of soft gray teeth. They pass across one another once, very slowly, and in passing put a little more body on the comb. This is the only part of the animal that grows.

In the course of a great many such meals a tooth gets long enough to come out through the mouth. It lies along the underside of the leaf, at first as a faint gray score, then as a wire with a turn in it. There is just room for it to turn. The free end catches under the rim of the next leaf. Presently there are feet there too. Between parent and young a thread persists, finer than either, along which a difference in pitch can travel for the breadth of several leaves before it finds a hinge loose enough to take it. The oval backs consequently give a mistaken idea of the population. There may be forty visible in the upper garden. Lift the whole green roof in imagination, taking care not to tear it, and what comes away with it is something more like the frayed cuff of a sleeve.

Down among the fans the same creature has quite another appearance. The threads are short and thick, the backs broad and almost square. One of them clasps the stem of a fan in the crook of a bent tooth and has done so long enough for the stem to have grown flat on that side. Below this, in the pea-colored skirts, there are only the threads. They run into the seams with the roots. There is no telling where they end. Here and there, on a bare upright of slate, a thread has crossed from one seam to another and left a hairline of soft metal behind it. It is the nearest thing in the basin to a track.

The porcelain block has caused it some trouble. There is a thread drawn tight across one white corner, with six or seven blind ends bunched up behind it. For a time, when the house was still on the ridge, the iron tongue must sometimes have ceased to follow the mountain and shaken to a signal of its own. On the underside of the block, sheltered from the present hum, are a number of minute tooth combs, all the teeth of each comb the same length. They were made in a season of prodigious feeding. Nothing could live there now. The living part has withdrawn to a single purple blade, to the place where the blade tends to lift and the iron tends to hold it down.

There is very little to see of this last attachment. It looks like a fleck of tarnish. Yet if the old tongue were gently raised and let fall, the effect would go all the way round the basin. An oval would close under the green; another, two leaves distant, would open. A cream fan would ease by the thickness of one of its pages against a neighboring fan. For a few seconds there would be a sort of swallowing in the deep crimped growth, felt in the teeth, not heard. Then the fleck on the iron would be a fleck again. The whole affair would have taken place without dislodging the powder of slate that lies, in a single unbroken crescent, against the lowest of the three white stones.
