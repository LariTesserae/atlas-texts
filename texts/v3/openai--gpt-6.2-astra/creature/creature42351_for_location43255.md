The girl who takes the eggs down from the rim carries them under her chin in a shallow basket. There is wool between them, but even so she goes sideways on the steps. What she dreads is not breaking an egg. It is arriving with five good eggs and one damp place and having to stand there while her aunt works out whose damp place it is, the hen keeper's or the egg carrier's or the woman's who has already set out six cups to bake a pudding in. She is nine and has discovered that there are things grown people will discuss long after the things themselves have ceased to exist.

Her aunt lives three shores down. She was already a married woman when the lake went out. Where her kitchen is now, she once lost the blade off an oar. The girl has looked for it. She imagines it lying flat beneath the flags, still pale where it split off the shaft, though her aunt says the boat drifted a fair way while they were fishing up the pieces and it could as easily be under the meeting house. What her aunt remembers best about that afternoon is the difficulty of rowing home on one side.

The aunt makes carrying pads, of which there are as many shapes as there are things to be carried. A man who brings up eels in a covered tub needs a different pad from a woman bringing down a pane of glass. People come to the house to be fitted. They get on their knees so the aunt can reach their shoulders without getting out of her chair. She feels with her thumbs for the bone that troubles them. Even people who quarrel with her elsewhere in the town kneel down quite readily in her kitchen; it is a great relief to let somebody put a thumb on the exact place. The girl is allowed to pull the washed wool apart for the stuffing. All the family pads have a few blue hairs in them from a skirt she outgrew.

There is a small animal living in the aunt's wall. They know very little about it. In hard weather it comes into the kitchen by the water groove, leaving prints on the flag where the aunt stands to cut bread. For most of one winter they supposed it was a mouse, until the girl, lying with her cheek to the hearth, saw it put out a long trembling snout and take a beetle. Now they call it the shrew. It has a glossy flank the colour of a plum stone sucked clean. The aunt has given up hoping to see it. When she bends far enough forward in the chair to look into the groove, her shadow falls across the opening.

The girl takes a longer way home. A boy on the lowest street has shown her where the water goes under his house. There is room at the inlet to put your head in sideways, and after a little while you can see the underside of the floor, which is covered in hanging roots as fine and white as the hairs inside a bean. The boy knows which board his father is standing on by the fall of dust among the roots. The two children have been making a map of this other town, the one you could go through if you were small enough. So far the map is mostly edges of things. Neither child cares to admit how soon it gets dark under a house.

On that street there are people who have never lived anywhere else. They have a trick of putting a hand out behind them as they talk and catching a little child by the shoulder just before it goes over the edge of the yard. They do this without looking. One of them has married a shepherd from beyond the rim, a big, mild man who still flinches when a pair of boots goes by his bedroom window at the height of his pillow. In his first spring there he went out to help a ewe in trouble and found, when he had got her lamb breathing and had time to look about him, that he was in his neighbour's pear tree. He had stepped off the end of the roof into the fork. The neighbour had watched the whole business from below and passed him up a towel.

With the last eggs delivered, the girl can carry almost anything home in her basket. Today it is the boy's old pad, out of which he has grown sideways more than upwards, and a little damp gravel he has asked her to wash. There are things in the gravel like shut eyelids. They are the shells of the little clams that lived here when you had to take a boat across to see somebody on the other side. She knows how to work them loose in a saucer without breaking the hinge. Some are scarcely bigger than the full stop she makes with a pen. Her aunt has kept the first one she ever found, in a fold of blue wool in the box with her good needles.

When the girl gets back up to the rim, her mother is on her knees at the eaves with her arm in a duck hole. There is one left in there. All its brothers and sisters have gone down, and the mother duck is in the yard below, looking up with first one eye and then the other. The girl's mother is just drawing breath to ask what has taken so long when something begins to knock quite distinctly against her wedding ring. She draws her arm back. Out comes a wet, cross duckling with a piece of shell still stuck to its poll. For a moment the two of them hold the basket underneath, as if it were eggs that went up and ducklings that came down, and this were another of the small jobs a person might reasonably be given on the way home.
