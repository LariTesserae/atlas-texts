The easiest part of the animal to see is its foot, and even that looks like a place where a foot has been. On the white strip below the notch there will be a little oval of pink that stays pink after the rest has cooled. Look along the wall instead of straight at it and there is a back to the oval, as clear and slightly puckered as the skin over scalded milk. At either end there is a mouth. The two mouths do not ordinarily open on the same day.

It eats the down that comes on a new surface. This is finer than the dust from the bud and lasts hardly any time at all. A thread of it rises, curls in the warmth, and is drawn back into the rind, leaving a freckle of darker tissue. Where the animal has been, the rind is clear of freckles. It goes along with one mouth a little ahead of the foot, taking each thread just as it curls. Sometimes a thread draws back with the mouth still fastened to it. For a while there is a dimple in the slope with a very surprised-looking star of wrinkles round it. Then the other mouth takes hold of something sound and pulls it out.

For a long time one might suppose there were a great many of these animals. In the afternoon a row of pink ovals extends quite across the new strip. The rind keeps the warmth of the foot much as a chair keeps the warmth of a person who has got up from it. Only the oval at the end of the row has anything on it. If the animal turns back, the whole row goes pink at once and the foot is at the beginning again. A meal on the way out, a hiccup on the way home. It has never been observed to go hungry trying to retrace its steps.

Under the oldest amber lip, however, there really is another one. It is yellowish, and the skin on its back has thickened to the point where in a good light you can see three creases in it. It has worn a shallow seat in the wall. For most of the afternoon it sits there making a small, regular movement with one end of its foot. The lip takes up the movement. It makes no audible difference to the hum, but if there is a drop on the tooth its reflection trembles. At the far end of the valley the feeding one pauses with a curl of down still sticking out of its mouth. A tremor passes over its back, too. Then it sucks in the curl.

Once, when the rind peeled, the seat under the lip peeled with it. For two days the yellow one hung head down over the open valley. Perhaps it could not tell the difference. But on the third day, when the ripping stopped, it let go. It fell for so long that the pink went out of its foot. The little regular movement went on in the air, where it had nothing to press against. By the time it reached the seeds the other animal had made nearly its whole afternoon's row.

Here a curious thing happened. The fallen one could have gone back to its seat; the drop that fell beside it had already gone back much farther. Instead it opened both mouths and took hold of two seeds, one with each. When the seeds rolled, they stretched it thin. A cloudy grain that had been hidden in one of the creases of its back came loose and lay in the seam. Then it was under the amber lip again, fitting itself to the old seat, which by now was nearly upside down.

There is a foot on the seed bed now. It is not big enough to leave much warmth behind it. It goes the breadth of a seed in an afternoon, eating something off the little neck which joins that seed to the next. When the green shadow passes over, it stops. The two on the wall stop also. In the clear light afterward you can just make out three small disturbances in the reflection on the drop: one steady, one answering, and one quite out of time, going on a little after the others have finished.
