The child at the plum-colored door is called Linn. There are two Linns on that street, and in writing to this one you put Linn at the Low Hinge. Numbers are all very well for the rates. For getting a letter into somebody’s hand it is better to say something about the door itself. The figure 8 on the midwife’s door has drawn out in the course of her tenancy until the upper loop is on her lintel and the lower is on the sill of the room above.

Linn has a good catch in her saucer, nearly a teaspoonful. She takes it in to her grandfather, who has a shirt cuff laid out on the kitchen table and a very small onion in the frying pan. He dips a finger in the saucer and rubs a pencil mark out of the cuff. Roof water will do this. Pump water, for all that it comes from much the same place, sets the lead a permanent grey. The grandfather keeps his accounts on his cuffs and washes them on the last evening of the month. He has been doing the accounts for the pipistrelle.

She lives under his other cuff. When he puts his wrists on the table to cut up his supper she comes out, walking on her knuckles, and sits in the pocket of his folded spectacles. She is not much bigger than either lens. There is a nick in her left ear with a clean pink edge to it, which Linn touches when she is allowed. In all the rest of the animal there is hardly a place where one could put the pad of a finger without getting fur.

The grandfather used to be a cutter in the freight works. If a parcel remained in one place past the second bell somebody had to go round it with a thin spatula, or the gallery would begin to make arrangements for keeping it. He still has a sloping shoulder from getting under heavy things at short notice. It makes an excellent run down which to launch a bat. Three evenings a week he goes out along the gallery with Linn, who carries a mealworm in a pillbox. He stands at the dispatch window and Linn goes as far as the red chalk mark beside the scales. The bat runs down his shoulder, flies the length of the wall without touching it and alights on the box. They have got her to do this with the lid on.

For the actual span Linn will have to stand on the opposite side. What worries her is not the flight but what to do about the husk. Nobody has said whether she may keep it. She has a moth husk in a twist of paper already. It has the dusty roses on its wings and both the little horns entire, but it weighs so little that it is difficult to establish whether it is in a hand or merely on it. If you shut your eyes the distinction goes altogether. A bat, even an empty one, would be something to have.

At the works office they are arguing about six inches. The proposed gallery will pass six inches below a certain puncture in the roof. There is an old man on the green street who gets the light of that puncture on his bed for nine days in March. He has been to the office in his good collar. He does not object to the gallery, which would save him a great deal of walking. He would like them to know precisely where he has the light: on the counterpane, towards the foot, a patch just sufficient to lay out his watch in while he puts the minute hand back on. The hand comes loose every winter. He has brought the watch. One of the draughtswomen has taken it home to her husband, but in the meantime the flight is postponed.

This suits most of the creatures that live along the projected route. It suits especially the little animals in the nail holes, whose noses have been out since the onion went into the pan. They are not mice. A mouse must turn round to go home; these go backwards, feeling with the long hairs on their heels. A full-grown one can withdraw through a wedding ring, though out on the wall, with its coat aired and the heels tucked under, it looks quite the size of a dumpling. They come for grease. In the morning Linn will wipe the frying pan with a crust and rub the crust on the low hinge. By dinner there will be no grease there at all, and for a day or two afterwards the hinge will squeak. Her grandfather has explained the difficulty. She oils it again.

When they have eaten, he puts his spectacles on, easing the bat out into his palm. She has left two little crescents of steam on the glass. Linn puts away the saucer. Up above them the postwoman is coming down to empty the box, and on the new, still tender seventh step the nails of her left boot make an impression that will last for years. No one below hears her. What they hear, presently, is the letter coming in, a dry intimate sound beside the salt cellar. Linn at the Low Hinge, it says. Inside is a drawing on very thin paper of a gallery with a small square opening in its floor. The people at the works would like her to bring the pillbox on Monday.
