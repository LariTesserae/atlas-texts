There are three beds in the room behind the clicking step. Two are slung one above the other from the old holes for the eave pegs. The third is a mat on the floor, and the boy who sleeps there is the first of the household to know when anybody is coming. He knows his aunt by the two clicks close together. She puts her weight on the step and then on the hook of her yoke to lift it over the door sill. His grandmother, who set the step on its chips in the first place, comes in without a sound. She can set a foot on the end that doesn't move.

The grandmother still has a remarkably true ear for a paver and a rather poor one for speech. People are always bringing her squares to try. She lies in the lower sling with one arm hanging out, and they put a corner between her finger and thumb and tap the square with a knuckle. Sometimes she asks to have it turned over. A square that sounds dead on one face and clear on the other will do very well cut into strips; there is a split running nearly parallel to the face, and a careful blow will give you two quite good shelves. There are shelves like these all round the room, holding cups and twists of lamp wick and a row of little clay heads the boy made when he was supposed to be rolling out pegs. Several of the heads have his grandmother's ears. He has given them very large openings in the hope of being funny, and she keeps them dusted.

Once in a while somebody asks her what became of the animal. How should she know? She put a step over it. That was the point. But the boy, lying with a cheek on his mat, can sometimes hear a noise after the last foot has gone by, a little vigorous brushing as of a person scrubbing a pot with sand. It comes up through his jaw rather than through his ear. If he lifts his head to listen it stops.

His aunt works on the gutters. In the morning she takes him along as far as the first kiln, where someone is generally willing to have a boy turn yesterday's squares up on edge. She goes on with a rolled apron tucked under one arm. This is a substantial article, made of little flat pads sewn into cloth, tied on at the waist and again below each knee; she can go a good deal faster on all fours in it than a man walking with a light and trying to see where she has got to. Her forefinger has a pearly thickening at the tip. At meals she uses the middle finger to try the heat of the porridge.

On mornings when the kiln wants no help, he goes with her to the new work. There are things to learn there besides wetting a joint. A person who has spent an hour drawing water east is inclined to keep drawing it east even when it has begun to go of itself. You have to take your hand away and see. Then there are the mouths under the ledges, very easy to stop with your knee, where a little water ought to get in. It is in the damp just inside these mouths that the white stuff grows. Once he put his finger in some. The surface gave like the skin on cooling milk, but underneath it was all little upright threads, and the stuff stuck to him so obstinately that his aunt had to scrape it off with the blunt back of her copper. She wiped the blade under the ledge, not on the floor. A patch as large as a saucer takes a long time to fill in.

It was on one of these mornings that he actually saw a basin animal. They had come a long way out, to a place where the gutter ended for the time being in a square depression no bigger than a washing bowl. His aunt had gone back a few courses to find out what was keeping the rain. He lay down with his chin on the rim. In the lamplight he could see a piece of the white stuff slowly coming loose from the wall of the bowl. It curled up from the bottom in a very regular fashion. Then the curled edge disappeared and he understood that he was seeing a mouth at work, and had been looking straight at the animal for some time.

It had almost no thickness seen from the front. Its head was the width of three finger ends, a soft wedge with a hard, pale cutting edge to the lower lip. Behind the head there was a succession of puckers in the skin, one for each pair of legs; when it took a step the pucker travelled across its back from one side to the other, so that the feet on either side never needed room in the same place at the same time. It was the color of the bowl. Indeed, when it moved away from its patch he saw that the part of it which had been lying on the white was white, too. This color stayed on its belly for a while and came off at the next turn in a fair print of the little pleats.

He wanted badly for his aunt to see it. He could hear her coming on her pads. He was drawing breath to call when the animal raised its head and opened its eyes. They were quite ordinary eyes, dark and wet, set much farther forward than he had imagined; it could look at him with both of them. A drop hung from the edge of his hair. He had come through a shower without noticing. The drop fell, the animal folded sideways into a joint so narrow he would not have thought to try a scraper in it, and by the time his aunt reached him there was nothing to show her but a clean strip on the wall of the bowl and a damp mark shaped rather like a frond.

She was interested in the mark. She had seen hundreds, usually rather longer than this one, and had supposed them to be prints of the cloth pads worn by the crew that laid the gutter. She took off her apron and made a print beside it with one knee. There was a likeness, though of course once you knew what you were looking at there was no mistaking the two. They stayed there comparing them until the water she had started came up behind them and ran into the bowl, spoiling both.

At home that night he got down a lump of clay and tried to make the head. The cutting lip was easy enough. The eyes gave him trouble. Each time he pinched up a place for one he found he had made the head too thick to go anywhere a basin animal would want to go. In the end he wet his thumb, smoothed the two places flat, and drew a pair of fine shut lids with his grandmother's thumbnail. She let him use it, though it took a little explaining to get her to hold still. They put the head on the shelf among the others. From the floor, in the unsteady light of the wick, you would hardly know there was anything there.
