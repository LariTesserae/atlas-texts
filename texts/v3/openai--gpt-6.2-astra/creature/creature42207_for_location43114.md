A root wife asleep looks very much like a place where a limb has been cut away and the cut has weathered. There is the same bevel all round, and a grain to the grey hair that follows the grain of the wood. They sleep on the lower face of a limb, with all four feet drawn in out of sight. One way to find one is to look for a little clean fan in the dust below. Even sound asleep the animal has to breathe.

It is a surprising thing to see that much grey come loose at once. A full-grown root wife would cover a bowl. She drops her forefeet first and hangs for a while by the hind pair, working her mouth. There is generally something left in it from the last meal. Then she comes down headfirst, quite briskly, to drink. The bowls under the overhang suit her very well. She can be lifted out by putting a finger under her chin; the forefeet come round the finger one after the other and there you are, holding something about as heavy and warm as a woman's breast. Set her down three bowls along and she will get into that one instead.

What she lives on is the white grub that works between the bark and the wood. In quiet weather she goes about tapping with the hard end of her chin. Where a grub has eaten there is a little give. She has two teeth below, close together, and can get them into a seam so fine that the point of a digging wedge would only bruise the lips of it. The people say that if you hear a root wife at work beside your hand you ought to move your hand. She is not going to bite it. But what looks like a stout piece of bark to be holding on by may be no thicker than the skin on cooled broth.

There are males too. Nobody who has turned one over has much difficulty telling which is which, but right side up they are all root wives. They go in pairs for part of the year. One will have its chin down feeling for a hollow and the other will be sitting some way off, combing out the hair on its belly with a hind claw. It is ordinary enough until a wind comes through. At the first far knock the one that has been combing rolls over and the small ones come running off it. They are no bigger than the last joint of a finger. There is a hole ready for them in the limb, a grub run cleaned out and enlarged at the blind end. Both old ones get across the mouth of it. If there is room for only one, the other sits on top. After the wind has gone by they stay there a good while longer. The heavy sheets sometimes strike back once, very hard, into the quiet.

The children on the lower table know a game that was learned from this. It takes two to cover the hole and at least one to be the wind, and the one who is the wind has the right to come back. There is a great deal of waiting, with everybody trying not to laugh. In a household with only one child it can be played with a bowl and three plum needles. There are usually some needles to be found under an overturned bowl, even in houses where the children have long since grown and moved their ladders elsewhere.

Now and then a limb comes down with a whole family still in it. On the joined tables it has a fair chance of catching before it goes very far. There is one such limb wedged slantwise among the daylight roots, the thick end stove in and the thin end resting just clear of a roof. In a wind it used to rub a bright patch on the plaster. The man who lives there cut a hollow in the roof to let it swing. He could have sawn off the end in half the time, but he had seen the dust stir. For two summers nothing came out of it that anybody saw. Then one evening, while he was on his back renewing the plaster inside, there was a little succession of weights on the roof over his face. He lay still with the wet bark in his hand. Across went another, and another after that, each so light that he could hear the separate feet letting go.
