The woman who mends the jars has quite a young face and very old hands. This is common in the lower streets, where a child is given a piece of stone to polish long before anyone will trust it with a jar. Even the littlest children have the two smooth places on the pads of their first fingers. The woman's are so hard that she can put her hand into a jar and let someone on the outside go over it with a sharp point. Where the point catches, she feels a little tug in the skin. She can distinguish a crack worth mending from one that only goes through the glaze. In the light at the bowl she is often to be seen sitting up quite straight, with what appears to be a clay jar for a head. Her eyes are shut inside it. She works by the feel of a point in somebody else's hand.

There is a deal of such work, and people who work well together do not necessarily like each other much at any other time. The boy who holds the point for her is her sister's son. In the street he walks three or four houses behind her, pretending not to be going the same way. He has reached the age at which the water must be allowed to run quite off his hair without his aunt wringing it out for him in public. All the same, if she reaches backward without looking, the handle of the point is laid across her palm.

They have hair much like wet silk, in a number of colors which are difficult to tell apart except at the bowl. Once dry, it lifts away from the head in a sort of smoke. This is seldom seen outside the houses. A person coming into the street with his hair still up is understood to be in a hurry, and room is made for him on the narrow way round. They have rather long upper lips, and shut their mouths when they smile, a habit acquired from working with both hands occupied under the eaves. Their feet are broad at the toes. In walking they set the ball down first and then, with some deliberation, the heel. It makes a stranger to a street very noticeable. He does not know which stones go through to somebody's ceiling and steps as if they all did.

Very young babies sleep through anything but a change of roofs. A baby carried from the clay street to the bone one will open its eyes at the corner and look reproachfully at whoever is carrying it. There are women well on in years who, when they are ill, go back to the street they were born in. A bed is found for them there. It need not be in the house they remember. The plock through the party wall is much the same in all six houses, and in the morning they can generally take a little food.

They eat the pale growth on the limestone. It comes up on the edge of a scraper in wrinkles, like a sleeve pushed back along an arm. The part next to the stone tastes faintly of iron. A good scraper leaves this part down, and the place fills in again; a greedy or an awkward one leaves a blue patch for weeks. Children like to watch the limpet at it. Along the front of its shell a little gather keeps appearing in the skin, traveling a short way and disappearing, and if one has the patience one can see the animal rest between one mouthful and the next. It rests a surprising length of time. There are two opinions about whether it sleeps. A girl once spent three nights with her finger on its shell and reported that it dreamed, but she was at an age to want attention, and in any case would not say what a dream felt like.

What a foot feels through a wall is by no means as plain as is supposed. A man may sit for a whole evening with his sole against the limestone and at the end know only that his neighbor has been at home. Indeed this is generally all he wants to know. For actual conversation they draw very close and speak into the little shelter made by the ear. Their voices at that distance are agreeable, low and quite ordinary; it is hearing one at arm's length in the quiet behind the quarry that is disconcerting. One hears the tongue come unstuck from the teeth before the word. People there tend to turn away from each other to speak. The boy who holds the sharp point goes there sometimes with a girl from a house on the upper fall. They sit with their backs together and talk about the people at the bowl. He has never seen her hair dry.

Of all the people in the town it is perhaps the quarryman who sees the least of stone. He works in the dark at the back of a cut, with one cheek against the face and a wedge no longer than a jar handle. He wants the flakes that come off with a feather edge, to slip under a slab without raising it more than the thickness of the flake. He brings them home nested one inside the other. His wife used to sort them with him. Now that she is dead he still sets the two stools opposite one another in the wet room; the stack rests on one, and he sits on the other and works down through it. From the way the feet of the stool sit in the worn places on the floor he can tell if he has got it the right way round. It matters a little to him that he should.

A step overhead tips the water in every jar toward the quarry and back. Someone with a jar full to the lip will take a quick sip to save it spilling. They have no very fixed idea of what goes over them. The children imagine a succession of enormous bare feet, each put down with extravagant carelessness and at once snatched up again, as if the owner had felt something move. The jar mender, who has put her head inside so many vessels, thinks there must be a considerable space up there. She judges from the quality of the sound returning through the stone. On a dry day, when the crescent overhead has nearly gone, she sometimes leaves her head in a jar after the boy has lifted the point. He has learned to wait. He lays two fingers very lightly on the clay, and when he feels her begin to laugh he takes hold of the handles and helps her out.
