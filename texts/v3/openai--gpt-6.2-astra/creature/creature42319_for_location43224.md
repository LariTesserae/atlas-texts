The white moth is white only from below. Lift a wing and there is a streak of green down the length of the body, the colour of a seed when its socket has rocked it over and exposed the side that has never seen daylight. The wing on its inner face is green too. It folds across the streak so exactly that a resting moth, seen through the water by a fish, looks like a little nick in the lip with the same substance at the bottom of the nick as on either side.

It feeds on what the roots leave behind. There is a taste of salt everywhere along the strip, but here and there, at a fork where two threads fine enough to be altogether invisible make one thread just visible, there will be a crumb of fat. The moth can sit all day at one of these forks. It has a short soft mouth which it applies to the place as a child applies an ear to a door. The fat comes very slowly. For a long time after the moth has finished there is a round clear place in the rime, and then the salt grows across it again in little square steps.

There are others, though one might walk the length of a headland and see only the one. A male keeps to a piece of root which sounds good to him. At intervals he unhooks the two hind feet and drums with them, so lightly that he makes no impression on the salt. The sound carries inland in the thread. A mile away a grazer may have a stutter in the great smooth noise coming up through its chin; nearer at hand a female feels the two taps arrive separately, one in each foot, and turns towards the foot in which the tapping came first. She walks. Almost everything the moth does on its own account it does on foot. The wings are for the young.

When the time comes she moves back from the dry strip to the last place that gets wet. A drop swells there at the end of every long stroke of the pumps and dwindles on the return. She puts her eggs into the drop. They go up it in single file like the bright little flaws that run up a piece of pulled glass. By the next working of the sacs they are well inland, by the third or fourth knock scattered among the silver hairs of a channel where the current grows slack behind the seeds. A wing comes off into the empty drop. Then the other wing comes off. They were never very firmly attached. Each swells in the water to a clear loose purse with a streak of green meal along the seam. Of all that goes in through the roots this is the one thing the hairs do not comb out. The purses and the eggs smell alike, and in a few days the young have found them.

There is a season when almost every chin in a herd is dusted green. The grazers have been feeding among these purses, taking up some of the meal and letting the larvae go in the wash from their lips. A larva has a broad forehead, a pair of black jaws and very little else that can be distinguished without good light. It lives in an empty seed socket and keeps its house turning with the movement of its back, so that it wears the socket a little deeper each year. The longest grooves of the high sea began in just this way. In old channels, under a hundred feet of water, the nap still parts sometimes to show the small paired scratches of the jaws, much enlarged, as a name cut in a sapling grows broad enough at last for a snail to sleep in one of its letters.

The mother meanwhile has gone out past the salt. Without the wings her six feet seem unnecessarily far apart. She is a green thing walking on green, and at the very turn of the lip, where her feet on one side are in the sea and her feet on the other are in the air, she stops. The fish come up to her there. They are blunt little fish with lovely mobile lips, too busy at the roots to give much attention to anything that does not tug back. One of them draws her in with a mouthful of threads and blows her out again in open water. For the first time there is enough sea about her to unfold the soft fans that have lain all her life under the stumps of the wings.

There are many such green swimmers in the upper fathom. Towards evening, when the light below the lip is still strong but the surface is beginning to reflect the sky, they can be seen going inland, rowing with an awkward alternate stroke. A few are taken by the fry. Most reach the meadow. There, on the rainward side of a stalk, each one sets her hooks for the last time, three in the black half of a leaf and three in the green. The fans go on opening and shutting for a while. Long after they have stopped, a bead of air travelling up the leaf will divide at that place, go round on either side and join again above it.
