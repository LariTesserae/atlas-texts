If there is someone on the shelf when you come in, he will have heard your knees on the beach stones long before you see him. He does not call out. He waits until your head is past the slab and you have got your back straight, as straight as the place allows. Then he says, “There’s a wide bit by your left foot.” There is. You can stand on it quite comfortably while your eyes come round.

At first he looks like an old man bathing. His shins, seen through the water, are considerably broader than his thighs, and this gives him the look of an old man who has rolled his trousers too high and is going to have some difficulty getting them down again. In fact he has no trousers. What looks like the rolled stuff is a fold of his skin. It is the yellow of the bosses, with a powdery white edge where it has dried. His face is not particularly old. The lines beside his mouth go away altogether when he speaks.

He makes room for you on the shelf. This takes some doing, and here you see the first thing about him that is hard to account for. There is no room on the shelf to make. Yet he goes on moving over, a little at a time, until you can sit with the bowl between you. If you look behind him to see where he has got to, there is the ordinary wall, warm and rather rough, with a dark damp patch the size of a shoulder blade on it.

He likes to be told what the beach is like. Not the weather; he can hear the weather perfectly well, even at the back, through the stone. He means which stones are showing. There used to be a big one like a loaf stood on end. Is that one there still? And a flat one with two white belts, a good way up toward the grass? He has trouble taking in the fact that you have come across a whole beach without noticing either of these. He is polite about it. He asks instead what was the last thing you ate. It interests him very much that an apple can be carried about for half a day in a pocket without coming to any harm.

He has tried keeping things. A woman once brought him a little wooden horse, small enough to stand in the bowl. For a long time he took it out whenever anyone came in. He would hold it by the belly and trot it along the top of a dividing rim, lifting it carefully over each spill of water, for the woman had told him a horse might refuse to put its foot in moving water. He remembers the feel of the tail against the heel of his hand. But it is no good looking for the horse now. There was a soft place underneath it, and then one day when he picked it up his thumb went nearly through to the saddle. It was after this that he thought of tipping the bowl.

It is easy, sitting there with him, to suppose that his business is to look after the spring. There is something attentive in the set of his head, and his hands are broad and scooped as if made for clearing a watercourse. In fact he knows no more than you do about where the water comes from. Once, at the request of a child, he put both arms in under the ledge. He got his cheek down flat on the surface and reached until even the tops of his shoulders were out of sight. Nothing: only more room, and that mild insistence against his palms. He was pleased to have tried. For several visits afterward he and the child discussed whether there might be a second hollow the other way up, with someone in it reaching down.

When he gets off the shelf he does not disturb the pale line. He puts a foot on the scooped step and takes his weight on it, and for a moment you can see all the small bones move in the top of the foot. Then the foot that bears him is stone and the wet step below has begun to bend. It is a perfectly ordinary bending of the knee. What is difficult is to keep track of which is the knee. By the time he is down you have the impression that you have been watching a man go very slowly down two steps, though the person you have been talking to is now looking up at you from a basin some distance off. His face is clearer there. The water runs out of the corners of his eyes when he smiles.

He does not see you out. It is uncomfortable for him near the opening; the long bar of brightness obliges him to screw his eyes nearly shut. But as you feel your way back between the bosses he asks a favour. On the beach, if you happen to see the loaf-shaped stone, would you stoop a little on the seaward side of it? From down there it used to look, not like a loaf at all, but exactly like a hen with its neck stretched out to drink. He would like to know if it still does. He will not mind how long it is before you come back to tell him.
