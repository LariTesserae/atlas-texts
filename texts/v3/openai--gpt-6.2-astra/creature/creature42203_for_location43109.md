In the woman's court there are eleven people who will answer if called from the roof by her childhood name. The oldest is her sister. The youngest is a boy who has not quite got the sound of it yet, but knows it means to put down what he is doing and come to the edge. A roof call is generally for a pair of hands. The woman herself, who is deaf on the left, is the least likely of the eleven to come.

She is setting out little grey fans of an oven plant. Full grown, each fan will be about the size of a palm. You can lean a loaf on one to cool without getting grit in the crust, and as fast as you pick them the fans grow again, provided you leave the lowermost pair. The woman has spent much of her life on her knees on one thing or another. Above each kneecap she has a pad of skin much the colour of the fans. When she sits in the sun with her skirt pulled up she likes the boy to run his finger over the pads; he gives the same little start of dismay every time, not quite believing that what feels to him like a piece of cold sole leather can feel *him*.

He follows her to the oven. He is at the age at which children in Olt begin to be interested in the absence of things. Why is there no one in the next court? There is; it is too far away to see. Why did the water not take the trays? It would have done if she had set them lower. He wants to see where lower is. She shows him on the side of a tray the wet mark, already gone pale at the edges. But it is the place he wants, not the mark, and eventually, when she has put her things away, she takes him back down.

People who have crossed at Olt are sometimes asked, in districts where the crossings are few, what sort of people they found. Usually they can say very little that is satisfactory. They found a woman much occupied with getting a twist out of a wet sheet; they helped her, were thanked in quite intelligible words, and went about their business. On a second visit the woman was an old woman, or the court was roofless and full of the tall pink variety of flour flower. There is a man in the east of Olt who has met his father several times as a young paving hand. He has never told him. They have very little in common at that age, and when they eat together on a stack of flags their conversation is almost entirely of the food. His father likes the salt skin off the top of a bake. He leaves it for him, which is as much as he can comfortably do.

In such a country it is quite possible to be homesick within sight of one's own door. There are people for whom this is a passing pleasure, almost a game. They go out on a flood with a good coat and a roll of bedding and work their way from oven to oven for thirty years. They bring home ways of doing things. In one court every shirt has a loop at the small of the back because a traveller once came there from a place where a shirt put out to dry would fill with cold wind and crack like a whip unless it was pegged down behind. The air in the court is nearly always still. The loops are useful for hanging the shirts up indoors.

Others do not willingly step across so much as a runnel with water in it. The woman's sister is one of these. She has a fine long stride, has laid pavement more than forty days' walk from her oven, and will go half a mile up a channel to walk round the blind end of it rather than put her foot on a plank. Once, on a still day, some of the younger hands moved the plank while she was on it, just enough that she felt the jar through her heel. It was the only time any of them had seen a grown person go down on all fours. They put the plank back. For a long time afterward she would speak to them about the work but not eat with them, which made the middle of the day very bleak.

Under the bridge, where the woman and the boy are going, there is a kind of life that most of the court would have trouble remembering if asked what lived in Olt. Along the underside of the sill runs a white welt, soft when pressed, with a row of dimples as neat as the sewing along a hem. Each dimple held a drop when the water stood there. Now the welt is slowly taking the drops inside itself. By evening its surface will be smooth. In dry years it shrinks until it looks like a mistake in the dressing of the stone. If the sill falls, the welt goes on living on the fallen piece, but the new sill will be bare for the lifetime of the hand that beds it. Pavers lifting a bridge stone try to lay it down the same way up, even when it is only going to serve as a seat.

The boy has seen the welt all his life from above, as a white lip beyond the edge on which he is allowed to sit. From below it is an astonishing thing. He asks leave to put a finger on it. His aunt says to warm his finger first. There they crouch in the damp grit, he with his hand under his shirt and she waiting beside him, her bad ear toward the court. Directly overhead there is the light running tread of somebody taking a short way home. A little dust comes down. When he takes his hand out he has forgotten which finger he was warming and tries them all, very gently, along the dimples. A drop slips away down his wrist.

It is only on the way back that he remembers lower. His aunt turns him round. The light has changed while they were under the bridge, and what had been one flat bed of grit is ridged all over with minute shadows. Here are the square seats of the trays. Just below them a ridge has broken, the grit drawn out in a long fine tail in the direction the water went. He puts his foot there and looks up for confirmation. Yes, she tells him. There.
