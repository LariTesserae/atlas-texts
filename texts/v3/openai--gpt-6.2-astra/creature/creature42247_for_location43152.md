When the cloud comes it is the goats on the summit that disappear first. The woman minding them can see the nearer ones chewing, and beyond them a pair of horns with nothing under them, and then the grass at her own feet goes white. She gets the animals to the ladder by knocking their milking cup against her knee. Down in the scoop they have heard that knock. By the time she comes backwards down the ladder with a kid under her arm, there are people at the parapet paying out the wool.

There are children old enough to help who have never seen this done. A boy leaves a twist in his length of screen and tries to shake it out, as he would a bedsheet. Four people down the parapet get a faceful of drops. One of them, his aunt, laughs with her mouth wide open to catch some; another takes him by the wrists and shows him how to roll the stick to let the wool down flat. First the wool darkens. For a surprisingly long time nothing else happens. Then little clear bellies hang from the bottom threads; they let go into the gutter, seldom enough at first that the boy can follow his own particular drop a good way on its journey to the jar.

At the hinder end of the town a man who used to do this work lies on his warm shelf listening. He can no longer get up either ladder. There is a sparrow with one white wing feather that comes to his bed for crumbs, and most days he has a good deal to say about her to anybody going past. Today the sparrow could take the bread out of his lip. He is listening for the change in the note of the jar. When the gutter runs well the drops go in with a deep gulping sound which grows higher and closer until somebody slips the next jar under. There are two bad jars, sound enough for grain, with cracks so fine the cracks disappear when you wet them. He wants to be quite sure nobody has taken one of those. At last he calls out about it, and a voice from behind the ovens calls back, rather crossly, that both of them are here and have lentils in.

Under the trees the hens go on scratching in the dust. Very little of the cloud gets into the scoop. A hen can stand within the length of her own neck of that white abundance and have to drink, as she always does, from the chipped saucer under the whey bag. The lizards fare better. They come out along the parapet and lick beads off the hairs on the backs of the people's hands. Some are old acquaintances, missing a toe or bearing the pale new tip of a tail. The woman with the goats wears a brass ring loose on her thumb, and a little dun lizard regularly puts its head through it. She has to wait for it to back out before she can work the knot at her end of the wool.

It is the boy who finds, when they bring up the last of the empty jars, something living at the bottom of the hole. He has had his fingers hooked under the jar's shoulder. At first he thinks one of his fingernails has come off and is lying there in the dirt. Then it opens a pair of wide pale feet and pushes itself under a curl of root. There are three more behind it. They are no longer than the joint of his thumb, the color of the fat in a boiled egg, with dark pinpricks for eyes. He has lived all his life above them and has not so much as heard a name for them.

His aunt knows them. In the wet year when she was small they came up between the floor stones in such numbers that her mother went about with a lamp and a spoon, scooping them out of the door sockets before the doors could be shut. As for what they do down there ordinarily, or how far the earth goes down, she has no idea. She puts the boy's hand into the hole. Long after the little animals have gone from sight he can feel, against the backs of his fingers, the separate delicate thrusts of their digging.

By supper the summit is bare again. The screens hang limp in the passage; a goat has to be cuffed away from the salt taste of one. Everybody who worked the parapet smells of wet sheep, even the baby who spent the afternoon tucked into the back of her father's coat. The boy carries a cup to the old man on the shelf and feels obliged to tell him about the creatures. The old man listens with the same attention he gave the jars. He asks which way the feet turned, whether the skin was bare, whether the hole smelled sour or sweet. On the shelf beside his head, in a plaited case meant for a sharpening stone, he has a little fired-clay animal that belonged to his mother. He lets the boy feel it in the dark. It has the wide feet, sure enough, the feet that turn out like little hands. But the potter has given it a long blunt nose, and the boy, who had the living face for an instant between his fingers, is quite sure about the nose. He gets a dab of dough from the ovens to show how it ought to go. The two of them sit there pinching and smoothing, passing the small head back and forth, while above their heads the sparrows settle into the juniper and the last jar cools in the ground.
