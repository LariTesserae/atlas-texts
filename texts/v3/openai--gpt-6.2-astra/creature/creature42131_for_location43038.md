The first one to come out on the limb goes on its belly. You would take it for a rat at first. It has the round thin ears of a rat, pink where the starlight gets through them, and a rat's look of having urgent business a little farther on. But it puts its hind feet down the wrong way round. It is coming out of a hole in the underside of the fig limb, and while the forefeet are already set for going up, the hind feet are still set for hanging down. For a moment it is like two small animals that have met going opposite ways around the wood. Then it swings its rump up. It sits there among the ants, turning its face from side to side.

There is very little to it under the hair. It can get into a broken cane to hunt a frog and come out backward with the frog in its mouth. A grown one weighs about as much as the water in a good-sized cane cup. It has a gray face, a yellowish belly, and along either flank a deep fold of fur that makes it look rather badly put together when it walks. In the air these folds pull out as fine and tight as the skin on boiled milk. The animal is a glider. Its people live all through the lanes of old forest. Where the strips are narrow they cross them, losing height, from a crown on one side to a trunk well down in the dark on the other. A strip half a mile wide might as well be the sea.

The one in the fig is a female. She goes as far as the break and sniffs at the wood there. Most of it has gone the color and consistency of cake, but down one side a long splinter of sound wood shines wet. Up above her is the stump from which it tore. She could almost touch the stump when the limb first fell. She can't reach it now. While she is sniffing, one strand of the creeper lets go with a small musical ping. She flattens. The limb goes down another foot.

Something inside the limb says *tsit*.

She turns and goes back in. For a while the only things to be seen at the hole are ants, some taken up short at the edge and some going straight in, according to what business they are on. Then a small blunt pink nose comes out with a fold of gray belly fur clamped in its teeth. The mother turns sideways to get through, dragging across the lip of the hole what looks like a ball of damp burrs. It is three young ones. They have wound their tails about one another in the nest and have failed to unwind them for the journey. She sits and picks at the knot with her teeth. One turns right side up, gets all four feet into the hair over her ribs, and shuts its eyes again. The others work their way under her, making little shoving motions with their heads. Her belly sags nearly to the wood.

She cannot spread the skin with them there. She can, however, walk a vine that would scarcely bear the weight of a wet shirt. She goes toward the living end of the creeper, out over the gap, using her tail underneath it for a fifth hold. There is a place where a cane has been rubbing against the vine for years and has polished the bark quite away. Here she slips, rolls under, and goes the next few yards upside down. The young sleep through it. At the trunk she takes a mouthful of water from the hollow left by a shed branch. One of the little ones lets go with its front feet and makes paddling motions in the air until she stoops far enough for it to drink too.

There are six or seven holes in this tree. She knows them. In the dry months she comes here for the gluey white grubs that lie just inside the wood, near enough to the surface to be heard. Tonight the first hole has water in it. At the second an enormous plum-colored claw appears and fills the opening as neatly as a stopper. She takes the third. It goes a short way in, bends upward, and ends in dry powdery wood. She leaves the young there. They are old enough now that when left in a strange place they keep their mouths shut.

In a minute she is out again, running back along the creeper. The dead limb has slipped still lower. She has to go down a piece of it now to get to her hole. She comes out carrying a wad of pale fiber as big as her head, carries it across, comes back for more. It is the soft inside bark of the fig, stripped off when there was still sap in the tree, chewed and slept in by generation after generation until there is hardly a piece longer than an eyelash. There is good fresh bedding to be had for the taking in the new tree. She fetches the old all the same.

A little before daylight the limb reaches the leaves. It makes no great noise after all. A pig on the nearest strip lifts her head with a cricket leg stuck to her wet nose and stands listening; before the last few leaves have slid off she has gone back to work. For several mornings afterward, in the hour when the bats are going home, the gray female will come down the creeper as far as the polished place and look for the rest of her way. By the fourth morning there will be a little track in the dew there, out and back, ending in two handprints side by side. Then the young will be getting up into the forks by themselves, putting their noses into every rolled leaf and having to be fetched out of places, and she will have other things to do.
