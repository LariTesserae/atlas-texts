There is a woman living behind the ginger. From the top step there is nothing to suggest a room there except, in cold weather, a dry oblong on the glass. Her door was meant to give access to a valve. To get a bed in she had to bring the pieces up separately and do the work of putting them together lying down, inside the frame.

Her name is Nel. She used to splice cable in the winding house. The last two fingers of her left hand are permanently bent, which is an inconvenience with buttons but very suitable for carrying two cups of tea in one hand on a stair. She still carries them that way. One cup is for drinking as soon as she sits down; the other, with a saucer over it, is for when she has finished whatever she can do without getting up. There is nearly always a bean to shell or a loose end to put through the eye of a needle.

She came up here for the warmth. It was only after she had lived through a summer over the pipe that she understood what a considerable part of her remaining life she would have to spend trying to get cool. On hot nights she takes the ticking off the bed and sleeps on it on the lowest terrace, her cheek against the unglazed back of a spare tile. It is pleasant to wake there before the cloud lifts. All she can see at first is a scallion, quite close, with a clear drop halfway down it. Then the opposite ridge appears, with a lorry going round the bend, and she has to revise her idea of the size of the world before she can sit up.

The blue blanket was hers. It had been an excellent blanket except for a place worn quite through by a heel. She laid it with the hole over a drain and was rather sorry, afterwards, that no one had been there to see how neatly this came out. There are things she has made here that people do come up to look at. A boy from the works school came with a folding rule to measure the growth of the sill. He had been told it was moving out into the air at the rate his nails grew. He lay on his stomach and put a finger alongside the fresh lime. Nel held him by the belt. What interested him in the end was that the moss, felt against the lip where the skin is fine, was warm. He made her lie down and try it too.

At present she is making a pear tree. That is how she puts it to the men below, who save the wooden wedges out of packing cases for her. The tree itself is three years old, grown from a pip, and is no bigger round than the stem of a clay pipe. It has four leaves of a dark, accomplished green and several more that are still soft and coppery and hang from the tip like a moth drying its wings. The difficulty is not the tree but the ground for it. Under the meadow there is hardly a hand's depth of soil. Nel is building a box that fits over three steps, its bottom descending in stages and its upper edge level, so that on the downhill side the root will have somewhere to go. She has put rather good handles on the ends, although once it is full nothing short of the winding engine could shift it.

No one has promised her pears. A man who knows about fruit told her it might take twelve years and the result might be a little hard thing that would draw her mouth up like a purse. She asked him what colour the blossom would be. White, he thought, unless it was pink on the outside at first. Since then, when she sits on the top step, she sometimes moves one foot a little to allow for the box. It is still in pieces behind her bed, with the screws folded up in a twist of paper, but already it has altered the way she sits in the garden.
