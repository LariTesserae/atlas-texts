In the low cup under the mouse there is one swimmer big enough to be seen distinctly. It has the shape of a comma. The thick end is nearly all eye, a brown eye with a little square of the hinge light in it; along the inside of the thin end there are seven pairs of legs. The first two pairs are clear of the water. Each foot has taken a skin of water up with it, so that the creature appears to be drawing on four minute gloves. It is the rest of the legs, those still in the cup, that make the noise like a spoon loose in a drawer.

There is another of its kind in the pinch between two skirts. Or perhaps this one is younger. It has hardly any tail to its comma, and the legs, seen against the chalk, are no more than a set of nicks in the water. Down on the clay, inside each of the clear eggs, you can make out a similar curl. The two black eyes are at opposite ends of the grain only because the creature is packed in sideways. Both look out on the same white root. It must be an immense thing to have for the whole of one's prospect, that smooth bend with a hair on it as fine as a flaw in glass.

The woodlice have broods too. One of the middling ones under the third lip has a joint in its armour sprung a little apart, and in the interval there is a crowd of white foreheads. You might take them for grit caught in the hinge of the joint, except that every forehead has its own pair of feelers laid back on it, very tidy, like the handles folded down on a basket. One youngster is far enough out to have a blue back and a white belly, the blue being entirely the colour of the recess above it. Its feet have found a purchase on the point of a needle. The needle is not bent.

The mouse is by no means asleep all over. Its ear is fast asleep, folded at the tip and easy as an old kid glove, but of the three whiskers one is pressed hard up against the roof. There is a little bow in it. Whatever the mouse knows of the weight of the sill it knows by that whisker. A red mite stands in the bow, its hind feet on the hair and its forefeet on the stone. Of all the crowded company it has the oddest lodging: a space kept open by the spring of a single hair, with the deep note of the cup below coming up through its feet.

There is room even in the toothful of moss. Where the snail has lifted the green skin away, the torn edge shows a string of wet, bright cells. In the notch left by one of them sits a thing like a fleck of oatmeal, with two fans spread in front of its face. The fans are exquisitely striped. Put your ear close to the outer rim of the hole and there is a brushing noise there, quite distinct from the coarse tear of the snail's mouth. The fans need not beat to make it. It lies along their stripes.

One is apt to think of the mouse as the great beast of the hollow and these as the lesser creatures about its feet. From the hinge that is certainly how it looks. From the other end, through the unskirted bead, it is the centipede that is prodigious. Each copper leg comes down the slope of its red hill as thick as a gatepost, and between the near legs, a long way off in the milk light, there is a speck of dun which is the mouse. Only the end of the near forefoot is in focus. It is a divided foot, pale at the division, with a grain of mortar held between its two tips. On the grain is the curved impression of a pore in the mason’s skin. His two broad ridges have a tenant; so has the small dent on the loose grain between them. A white mite fits it to the shoulders. Its hindquarters stick out into the light.
