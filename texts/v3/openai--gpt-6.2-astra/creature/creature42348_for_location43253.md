The grazer takes its knee back. For a little while the print stays in the white fans, a deep dimple with a raised rim. Then the rim begins to crawl. It is made up almost entirely of small animals that have come out of the fans under pressure, much as the loose stuffing comes out of a cushion when you sit on it. They crawl downhill into the dimple, which is exactly the wrong way to go if the grazer means to try again.

A child lying on his stomach at the edge puts out a husk spoon. Six of the animals climb in. His grandmother turns the spoon over. All six stay on it, and she lets him carry them home that way, on the understanding that he is to put them back tomorrow at the very spot he took them from.

These are the animals the people call hems. A single hem, held up on a twig, looks like a snipping from the edge of something much larger. It has a row of minute eyelets along one side and a corresponding row of hooks along the other. It travels by letting go at the front, reaching forward until it catches on something, and bringing the rest of itself up in a ruck. The six on the spoon have made one another fast side by side. As the child walks they lengthen and shorten in a single wave. At the threshold of the house the last lets go with its hind hooks and the whole party transfers itself to his sleeve. He has to spend the evening with that arm out of his shirt.

There are hems wherever two living surfaces meet without quite belonging to each other. If you lift the lip of the blue cotyledon you find a blue sort, so broad and sluggish that the track of a footstep may take half a day to pass from one end of the animal to the other. You can tread on the path going to market in the morning and on your way home feel the little lift of your own arrival. The white sort from the join are quicker. They live on the flour that falls when the growing tips strike. Their underside is a single soft grinding surface, like a tongue with a miller's thumbprint on it. Where a great many have fed for a long time the floor is worn very smooth. A polished patch in the middle of a thicket generally means there used to be an edge there, and a long drop, and nothing at all upon which a thicket could have grown.

The child's grandmother makes doorposts. She keeps three or four drying at the back of the house, laid on separate beds of husk so the voices do not run together when she tries them. She has a low voice which even through good wood feels rather like someone clearing a little space on a shelf. To test a post she sets an amber tooth upright on the far end and speaks the names of her daughters into the near one. On a poor post the tooth falls over at the first name. On a fair one it stands through all four. On the sort she likes, it walks a short distance down the grain and stops.

She used to go out to the fallen groves for her wood. Now her left knee will bend or straighten but does not care to do both on the same day. People bring her lengths to choose from. She pays for a good one in husk flour and for an exceptional one in the little bead runners she carves with their tails curled up, so that a runner can be hung from a baby's finger without its getting lost among the bedding. There are people well into middle age who still have theirs. A trader at the old shell midden has worn the legs off his down one side by rubbing it against his thumb while waiting for someone to make up their mind.

That night the six hems get onto one of the drying posts. The child wakes to the tick of the testing tooth on the floor. His grandmother has heard it too. In the dark they put their hands at opposite ends of the wood. The post is busy under their fingers. There is a long scrape, a pause, a succession of the lightest possible taps; then something that feels so much like the intake of breath before a familiar person speaks that the child answers, his chin down against the grain, before he quite wakes up.

His grandmother strokes his wrist to quiet him. The hems are eating the flour out of the fine splits in the wood. A hem puts its hooks into a split on either side, draws the lips together and works its grinding belly down between them. At the end of the split it uncouples and starts again. By morning six pale streaks run the length of the post. The grandmother tries the tooth. It goes almost to the end and pitches off into her lap.

She gives the child a carved runner for the use of his arm overnight. His sleeve is quite clean for the first time since the ridges opened.

He takes the hems back in the spoon. There is some difficulty about the very spot. The grazer has crossed and the herd has followed it. All the knees and the little backward-looking faces and the combing have left a broad track. The dimple is gone. Besides, a twist of the two fans in catching has turned what was the upper surface of one partly underneath the other. The boy goes down on his stomach again. Below him in the yellow light the white hems are hanging in a fringe four hands deep, coupled by their sides, passing the spilled flour from belly to belly. Every so often a section grows heavy, folds under, and is taken up on the other side, clean and almost transparent. Through such a section he can see a long way down the valley, under the ground he walked on yesterday.

He holds the spoon out at a guess. For a time nothing happens. Then a single row of eyelets opens along the fringe and the six go over together. A quantity of flour comes off on his cuff.

On the way home he passes the trader, carrying a short, blunt adze and two stones to weight a roof. He is going to put up a shelter on a patch of rock the joining has brought within reach for the first time. The boy turns back to show him where it is safe to tread. Halfway across they stop. Something underneath is pulling the nap of the trader's sandal through a gap, one fiber at a time. He is a patient man, accustomed to waiting with his thumb at work in his pocket. They stand there until a small black snout emerges with a mouthful of sandal hair, considers them both at close quarters, and withdraws to furnish its house.
