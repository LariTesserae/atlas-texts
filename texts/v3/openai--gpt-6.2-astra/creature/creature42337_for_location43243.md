At the head of the south stair lives a woman who takes the loads off the pack goats. A goat will go down a stair with a wide load until one of the baskets strikes the wall, and then it will generally try to get past the difficulty by going faster. She has a run of low shelves cut in the rock against which a beast can be backed. Two little boys, her sister’s sons, take the strain on the ropes while she slips the knots. They can get a train of six unloaded without setting down an egg hard enough to break it. They are paid for this work. What they do afterward, lying on their stomachs on the capstones and making the noise of a rope slipping until their aunt comes out, they do for nothing.

She has spent most of her life at the stair head. A good many of the valley people know her face without ever having seen her on her feet: they come over the last rise and there she sits, mending a girth, with the town apparently nowhere about her. In fact there are four households within reach of her heel. She can send down for a cup of something hot by tapping on the pantry roof. Once she did this on the wrong slab, having moved along to sit in the sun, and a mason came up with his bag to see what wanted fixing. They have been friends ever since. He brings his lunch up when he has a job on that tier. He likes the wind for the first ten minutes.

The people are not all equally happy aboveground. There are excellent travelers among them who can cross a reach of the plain in bad light by keeping the grooves at a certain slant underfoot. There are others who go up once or twice in the year and are glad to get down again, and who have nevertheless walked a considerable distance in their lives. A woman may go from her mother’s house to the house where she was born, and from there to the oven where she works, by three quite different streets at three heights, meeting someone who knows her at almost every turning. Her little daughter, walking beside her, has the impression that her mother knows everyone alive. Then one morning a tired boy comes in with a goat whose ears have been cut in an unfamiliar pattern, and has to take a cake out of his pocket and point to the hole in the middle before anybody understands that what he wants is the baker.

The boy has a sick man with him, his father, waiting up by the trough. The cake was baked here last summer; they have been keeping it for the sake of the bit of straight wood put through it to stop it breaking on the journey. His father wants two more of the sticks. He makes the little shuttles that pass back and forth in a narrow loom, and the wood is exactly right. As for being sick, he has eaten something that disagreed with him and would much prefer to sit where he is. It takes most of the morning to get this clear. By then a stool, a blanket, a pot of warm water and a woman reputed to have a light hand with the bowels have all gone up the stair. The woman stays to talk about looms. In the afternoon the boy is to be seen playing at the drain with the other children, quite able to tell them in their own speech what ought to go down it next.

A few creatures live their entire lives between one tier and the next. There is a little gray moth in the wool rooms whose young carry their own cases about with them. A case made in the sweepings under a loom will have a band of every color worked that month. The wool workers gather them up before they can do much harm. Children will trade quite heavily for one with a band of valley blue. Farther down, in the damp grit behind the cistern, are pale jointed things no bigger than barley grains. They swim out when the cover is lifted for cleaning, hang for a moment in the shaft of light, and go sidelong into cracks which, to anything of their size, must give access to a second plain as extensive as the first. No one who drinks from the cistern troubles much about them. There is a particular old hen, however, who knows the sound of the cover being pried up and will come down two flights of steps.

The sheep on the rim have their own acquaintance with the town. An old ewe will bed down above the common oven in weather when the younger animals stand nosing at perfectly good stone, unable to make out what is wanted of them. In a hard spring the warmth there is enough to keep a lamb dry through its first night. The bakers below can hear the sharp little blows of its feet. They know the difference between that and the noise of a loose flag, although it would be difficult to say what the difference is. A loose flag will go on knocking in one place; a lamb, even a very new one, presently has business a little farther along.

Late in the day, when the loads have all gone down, the woman at the stair head turns the empty baskets upside down on the shelves. Otherwise the little birds that follow the sheep will get into them after the grains of barley lodged in the weave. She leaves one upright, a split basket no longer fit to carry, and shakes the others out into it. For a while nothing comes. Then one bird alights on the handle, so light that the loose end of the split hardly stirs. By the time she has folded her stool there are a dozen, and the basket the boys could scarcely lift that morning is walking a little on its shelf. She sets a stone against it before she goes below.
