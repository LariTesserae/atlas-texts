There is a child living under the western lip who has never seen the red stone from which her house is cut. From the house everything is brown or black. She has a lump of it in her pocket, brought in dry from the pass by one of the freight people. Wet, it is the color of liver. She has been told that the whole edge of the world, including the part she sleeps in, looks like that from a distance, and would like to get far enough away to see.

Her people are much like the people who bring the freight. They can have children together, and sometimes do. The chief visible difference is in the feet. A child of the shelf puts down the whole sole at once, even on a dry floor. There is a particular ridiculous sight, much enjoyed up at the pass, of a party from the city crossing an inn yard in a hurry, slapping the dust as if to kill something under it. On the roofs a heel set down first is enough to send a person over backwards. The iron nap is especially treacherous. A roof walker keeps a little space between each of the toes, and the water comes up through the spaces in five brief jets, four from the foot and one from the gap under the instep. From a vent cap on a still day you can follow a walker a surprising distance by these disturbances after the walker herself, dressed in a dark coat against a dark sheet of stone, has disappeared.

There are fewer of them than the city would suggest. The names on the doors would furnish a population forty times the present one, and in the baths district there is an entire school in which the only domestic article is a blue bowl turned upside down over a piece of cheese. Somebody still comes for the cheese. They live mostly in the cross passages, which were cut a little higher than the galleries they join. A room that is dry on the day you find it is not necessarily a room to live in. Before moving a bed in, they put three little piles of salt on the floor and leave them there through two changes of the gates. A good bedroom will keep all three. A room that keeps one is good enough for a kitchen if you can contrive to hang the flour.

The mother of the child under the lip does lettering. There is plenty of work putting back the numbers on pump housings, not much on the public signs, which were made to outlast weather considerably worse than they have had. She has never touched the sign for the western terminus. Her grandfather did once, to alter the fare, and it is his four that the water lights up on the wall. It has a very long cross stroke. Each time they pass under it the mother lifts one finger and makes the stroke in the air. This is the only thing the child knows about her great-grandfather that she can actually see him doing.

They eat meal from the low country, curds brought across the pass in skins, and a small amount of very expensive green stuff. A fresh onion travels from hand to hand at supper. You cut what you want over your bowl and pass it on, and it is thought ugly to dig the point of the knife into it to get at the sweet middle. The little animals in the tanks are not eaten. There was evidently a time when they were, since the oldest word for them means the bit that sticks to the pan. Now, when a strainer is lifted, the creatures that remain folded go with the grit into a bucket, and the ones that begin to walk are picked out on a wet fingertip and set on the tank wall. No one waits to see which of the things in the bucket will open. There is work to do, and the distinction between a washer and an animal is in practice still a distinction as to how long one is prepared to stand there.

They hear very well, though not in any extraordinary range. A stranger hears the notes of the gate change as distinctly as anybody born there. What the stranger cannot hear is a note failing to come. In the middle of a sentence the letterer will put her brush between her teeth, go to the door, and stand leaning out with one ear toward the baths. Both notes have sounded. It is the small ordinary clank *after* the second note, the flap falling back on the inspection hole, that has not occurred. Quite often a rag has got in it. Once a young man had propped it open to dry his hair in the breath from the vault. He was astonished to find three of his aunts coming down separate stairs toward him, one carrying a wrench.

Much of what passes for knowing everything about one's neighbors is hearing of this sort. It has its limits. The child under the lip has made herself a place in the old western ticket office, reached by stepping from the counter to the clock bracket and pushing up a panel. She owns a spoon, the red stone, seven white buttons, and the brass pointing hand from a sign. The hand is nearly as long as her forearm. In the space behind the panel she has mounted it on a nail so that it can direct a visitor to any one of a number of places. One is the pass. One is her bed. One, an inch or two above the pass and a little to the left of it, is a country she has not yet named. Everybody in the house knows the sound of that panel. Nobody has asked her where she goes.

The polished place under the overhang is not quite a seat. Part of it is; the rest is a table, and at the end where the stone begins to slope away there are two very smooth hollows for the knees of whoever has to wash up. Five households eat there when their hours permit. Plates have to be slid in from the ends, there being no room to get behind a seated person, and if somebody in the middle wants to leave, everybody on one side has to get up. In cold weather this causes some bad feeling. Usually they stay for the gate change. The curtain comes apart from the bottom upward, leaving for a moment a fringe of water hanging from the stone, and beyond that the cloud is so much brighter than the room that the faces round the table disappear.

It is then, with the whole valley open in front of them, that they look at one another least and talk most freely. The child has tried to catch the exact instant when the water joins again. She has not managed it. Someone asks for the onion, or there is a thumb in the way, and when she looks back the valley is gone. Across from her is her mother's face, showing very clearly in the returned light, with a fleck of yellow number paint in the crease beside the nose.
