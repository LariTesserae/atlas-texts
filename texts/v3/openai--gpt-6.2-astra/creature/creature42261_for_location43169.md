From the last shelter, what can be seen is a grain going over the end. There is a place forty feet below from which it can sometimes be seen to stop.

It stays there a little while. Other grains come down, white ones mostly, falling straight on into the cloud. Then the pink grain moves inward, toward the face of the rock, and disappears into a seam. A man once spent much of a summer rigging a seat below that seam. What he saw, when he was at last in a position to see anything, was a lip coming out to meet the grain. It was pale brown and chapped in the middle. Behind it was another lip. He said the two together had very much the look of a child trying not to laugh with its mouth full of milk.

What lives in the valley lives chiefly on the underside. There are galleries there which were once the hollows under leaning slabs, when the floor of the valley came out farther than it does now. Some are quite dry. The inhabitants sleep in these head to foot, in a row, with the smallest in the middle where the temperature varies least. They have long flat backs and the skin over a grown one's shoulder blades is polished from turning over in bed. Their eyes, which a visitor might look for above the mouth, are set a little below it. This makes very good sense if one sees a creature in its usual posture, hanging by the broad pads of its heels, intent on what is falling from the sky.

They eat the clear stuff that forms over the pink. It doesn't amount to much on any one grain. A mouthful is held for a long time, the tongue working the little stones back and forth until they no longer slip easily against one another. Then the mouth opens and a little fall of clean grit goes down into the lower country. The very young can't do this sorting. An older one will give a young one its tongue to suck, and hang meanwhile with a patient, rather put-upon expression, watching excellent grains go by.

In the morning very few come up. Their heels work badly on dry stone. To cross even the width of a ridge they must get their bellies down and do an awkward thing with their elbows, one out and one in, making hardly any progress for a great expenditure of skin. But when the sheets of water begin to stand up there is a change in their fortunes. A heel set in an eighth of an inch of running water takes hold as firmly as a wet thumb on a lifted plate. They come walking up the slant sheets on the side away from the sun. Seen from a summit they are the small dimples that travel against the current and seem, from time to time, to step across a break.

It is in the afternoon, in the places where two old floors cross, that they meet others from galleries a long way off. There is quite a traffic there. A creature will have brought up a bit of bedding stone, warmed between its back and the roof of its gallery; another has a quantity of the good thick skin folded wet on itself like the skin off boiled cream. They have things with no evident use at all. A favorite is a pebble with a loose speck shut up inside it. The owner takes it into its mouth, closes its lips against a neighbor's cheek and gives its head a rapid shake. In this way the little private rattle goes round an intersection and can occupy a dozen grown creatures until the sun goes in.

The shelters at the margin are theirs, though the size is deceptive. A hollow in which a person can just squat will accommodate a whole wet company. They use the niche for the babies. At the first slackening of a stream the nearest adult pops a baby in there, facing out, and puts a handful of the soft white paste across its heels to keep it from following. What looks like one scratched line around the wall is a great many short marks. Each was made by a heel drawing up to take its bearing from the door. They have to be high enough to see the dark path and low enough to keep the belly wet. When the wash comes and the flag swings shut, there may be twenty of them abreast along that line, each with a perfectly clear idea of where to go next, all waiting in the dark.

Occasionally one gets left in a shelter overnight. The others come back for it in the morning, although this entails the wretched business with the elbows. On the dry sill they stop and let the one inside hear, through the flag, the scratching of their approach. There is generally a long pause then. If it is a young one it may have passed the night for the first time away from the warm press of the gallery. The paste will have set around its heels. It takes some persuading to get it to push the door.

This accounts for something one finds now and again in the powdered stone of a niche: two little fans of fine lines, far too regular for the marks of running water, with a broad smooth place between. They are the prints of heels that have grown a little in the night. The creature that made them may now be hanging far out under the lip, carrying something warm to an old one, or giving its cheek to the rattle. But the prints stay small. If no rain gets in through the door they will stay just as they are for years, slightly deeper at the inner edges, where it stood up at last on hearing the others outside.
