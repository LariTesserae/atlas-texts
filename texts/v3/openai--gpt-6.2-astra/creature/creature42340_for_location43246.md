The woman who walks the rail has a daughter who can sleep through the draining of a bed but wakes if anybody sets a glass on her table. The table stands on the same floorboard as the head of her bed. On school mornings her mother sets down the glass and leaves it there, full to within a finger of the rim. By the time the girl comes into the kitchen the water is quite still except for a small patch the size of a thumbprint, trembling on the side toward the mountain.

The girl keeps limpets. There are hundreds of them to be had in the channel behind the freight office, no bigger than shirt buttons, with shells the pinkish white of the cliff. Seen from above they appear to have grown straight out of the channel bottom. She keeps hers on a piece of broken plate, blue side up, in the little run of water that passes her building on its way to the next street. The blue is important. Where a limpet has fed there is a clean curving track through the green, and on the plate you can see that the track consists of a great many short strokes, side by side, as though someone had been trying the nib of a pen. It pleases her that a mouth can do such work. She has tried to see it done by putting one on glass. The foot takes up practically the whole view.

Her grandfather used to take her to the freight channel on Sundays with a spoon and a saucer. He was a glazier, a good trade here, where a pane that touches the iron frame at any point will in time acquire a semicircular crack no matter how fine the glass. Even now, in houses where he has had no business for years, people remember the sight of him on the outside of an upper window, the putty knife stuck to the back of his hand with a dab of putty while he ate his midday bread. The girl remembers principally his patience with the spoon. One had to wait until the limpet was on the move; taken by surprise it would come away quite easily. Once it had hold you could break the saucer and never get it.

He lives with them in the months when the upper part of town is cold. He and his daughter converse without much looking at one another. She passes behind his chair on her way to the cupboard and holds the back of her hand for a moment against his cheek; he takes down a third cup. A visitor might suppose that she has asked for one. In fact she has told him the air outside is getting cold, and he has concluded for himself that the man who brings the oil will be glad of something hot. A good deal of what passes for a language of signs in the city is of this nature. Away from home the people are quite as liable as anyone else to be misunderstood.

In summer a boy from the farms below the clouds comes up to stay with the oil man, who is his uncle. For his first few days on the shelf he shouts himself hoarse. Then he finds the laundry yard. Behind the stacked bales of damp sheets there is a space just wide enough for two children to squat in, where the noise of the works is so much reduced that the boy can hear himself making the ordinary low sounds that accompany an occupation: the intake of breath when a knot draws tight, the little satisfied click at the back of the teeth. He takes the girl there. They spend a considerable part of an afternoon tearing a fig leaf into strips and listening to it tear. Later she brings him a limpet on a bit of glass, wrapped in wet rag for the journey across the bed. Down at the farms, he tells her, they have a thing very like it that lives on apple trees and comes out of its shell nearly the length of her little finger. She believes the apple trees and has her doubts about the rest.

The oil man himself keeps nothing, not even a chair in the cool part of his shop where he could very well sit. He likes to lean out over the counter on his elbows and watch the feet going by at the level of his eyes. His shop was once a meter house; the street has risen. From here he has a better acquaintance with some inhabitants than anybody who knows them by sight. There is a small brown dog, for instance, generally supposed to belong to a laundress. Twice a day it goes uphill past his counter dry to the belly and comes down wet to the chin. One day he follows it as far as the old lamp hole by the post office. The dog puts its head in and waits. Presently a hand comes up and rubs the loose skin about its ears.

Below the post office a man spends his working day clearing grit from a row of screens. There is light enough to work by from five such holes in the street. He knows when the mail is being sorted by the intermittent darkening of the second hole; he knows market day by the smell of bruised fruit coming in at the fourth. The dog is the laundress’s after all. It has simply discovered a person who, given the unusual height of his ceiling and the length of his arm, is in a position to scratch both sides of its head at once. When the man is away his relief has to stand on the grit shovel to reach it.

Toward the end of the summer the girl goes out one morning and finds the blue plate clean. The weather has been hot; the run beside the house has shrunk to a string, with a dark wet margin on either side so narrow that a button laid flat would bridge it. For a while she looks for shells in the groove. Then her mother shows her where to put her fingers, up under the projecting nose of the doorstep. All along its underside are the little hard backs, still wet. There is water there that can scarcely be felt at all. She lies on her side with her cheek against the paving and her mother standing upstream of her to turn the warm trickle aside. From this position, looking along the wet stone instead of down at it, she can see under the foremost shell a delicate lip moving steadily over the green. It is broader than she expected, almost square. She stays to see it clear a patch the breadth of a grain of rice. Her mother shifts the lunch tin to her other hand. There is time before the tram.
