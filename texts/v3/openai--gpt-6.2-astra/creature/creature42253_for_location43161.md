The little one puts its foot down in the same place. This time it spreads the two outer toes before it gives the foot any weight. The beads come up between the toes and hold. Its middle leg, which has been drawn up tight against its belly all this while, lets go and dangles for a moment. Then it takes three good steps. Its mother is a little way ahead with her face in the flour at the lip of the bed. She has not looked round.

It would be hard for her to pick out her calf by sight. She has a better acquaintance with the small run of shocks that comes through the lime when it walks: four close together, two far apart, a drag where the skin of the belly still hangs low enough to brush the grit. Of all the feet that are going down about her own, those are the six she attends to. When they stop, a fold of skin opens behind her last pair of legs. Inside the fold the hair is long and clean and fits the stone like the bristles of a brush. She can stand feeding in this attitude for quite a while, with the whole great business going on inside her chest and one end of her listening for a foot to come down.

There is very little to eat for a beast whose mouth is new. The gum belts are there from birth, but the grooves are shallow; a grain that would scarcely show on a wet fingertip will fetch up crosswise and stop the works. For the first few days the calf eats what falls out of its mother's mouth. She lets the belts run slack at the end of a mouthful. A narrow spill comes down, coarse on one side and fine on the other, no wider altogether than the black edge of her lip. The calf sets its mouth across the spill. There is a faint sound like a sleeve being pulled over a hand. A single knob travels an inch along its side and sinks away under the hair.

In a close herd there may be many thousands of animals doing this. There is no difficulty about whose spill to take. What falls from another mother's mouth may be lying in plain view, may be an inch deep all round a hungry calf's feet, and yet the calf can put its blunt nose to the lime without getting so much as a pink mark on it. Its own meal comes down in what looks like an empty place. The calf eats with its eyes shut. Long afterward, when it has the strength to go to the coarse end of the bed and loosen a bead from its socket by leaning its whole weight on it, it will still shut its eyes for the first pull.

The stones are much used by the very old. In the open, an old beast can walk all afternoon among the bodies of its kind without coming upon another that will bear against it. At a stone it can put its shoulder to something. There are hollows high up the pedestals polished a soft yellow with grease, with short stiff hairs standing out of the lime. An animal will back into one of these hollows, fold the rear legs and then the middle pair, and spend the hot part of the day sitting up on its hams. From the front it has the grave, rather foolish look of a hare in a fur coat too big for it. Now and then the belts in its mouth go round of themselves. A little sand from a bed that has long since gone under the scarp falls out onto its knees.

When the last light leaves a stone, the animals against it become distinct all at once. Some are asleep. One has a flyblown place over its eye; one is a nearly white old female with a calf, not her own, asleep across her forefeet. There are so many that for an instant the stone seems to have a deep soft skirt. Then the shadow of the next rise joins the shadow of the stone and there is nothing there that the eye can sort out.

On the bed the little one has reached its mother. It goes under her from behind, between the listening brushes, and knocks the top of its head against the low hard roof of her chest. She eases one foreleg sideways. That is room enough. For a while the small sound goes on under the large one. Then the belts catch properly for the first time, and the mother, feeling through the soles of her feet the long unbroken run of her calf's first mouthful, lifts her brushes clear of the ground.
