After the kitchen vents go up, something begins to happen to the doormats. A mat at the end of a row will hump a little in the middle and a flat brown head will come out from under it, usually at the end farthest from the tram stop. The head remains there a while. It has a dry nose divided by a seam, and eyes so far round the sides that a small fly can wash itself between them in perfect safety. Eventually the rest comes out: a pair of shoulders, a length of rather loose skin, two pink hind feet. The tail stays under the mat until the animal has made up its mind which way to go.

These are called doorbacks. The common brown one is about the weight of a man's shoe, and leaves much less of an impression on the grass. Its ribs are jointed where they meet the breastbone. Held up in the hands it is a fairly ordinary round animal with a pale, discontented belly; set down on the floor it lets out its breath and becomes broad. It can pass under a closed street door nearly anywhere except at the hinge. For this reason nobody puts a saucer of milk out for a doorback. Anyone wishing to give one milk has only to leave it in the kitchen.

In the hour after milling there is good feeding on the plain. Little transparent hoppers that have gone all year head down among the fibers find themselves head up in a strong light. The doorbacks go along the edge of the combed swath picking them off. One can see where a young animal has been by the dark dabs it makes turning the nap over with its nose. An old one takes the tip of a fiber between its front teeth and draws it through its mouth like a person eating the meat off a small skewer. The oldest doorback in the south district has a single front tooth and works sideways. It is known to the inspectors, who count the dark dabs as faults and have more than once had to walk half a mile back to satisfy themselves about one.

They eat quickly, and then most of them go to water. This may be several streets away. A doorback looking for a hollow sits quite still with its chin down and lifts first one hind foot and then the other, a movement familiar to children who have been kept too long at a shop counter. What it feels is the slight taking up of slack in the layer below it as the hollow approaches. It can do this on a kitchen floor seven layers down. In several of the older laundries it is still the custom for the woman on the early shift to bring her doorback with her in her apron. She gets nearly a kettle's boiling time on the bells.

At the pool the creatures cease to look much like the animals that came out from under the doors. They run in without a splash, open the loose skin along their sides and lie in a row in the water with only their noses and the very tops of their eyes showing. From the rim one might take them for a line of stitching in a bright piece of cloth. They go where the water goes. A brown doorback that sleeps under a mat in the station quarter may spend its evening going past the reservoir, round the back of the theater and through the long brass section in the floor of the instrument shop, where the hollow goes quite dry. There they all have to get up and walk. The instrument maker, who works late, says it is like having a thought interrupted by a succession of purses.

There is some question as to where the young are born. No one has yet found a very small doorback under a mat. The first to appear are already able to flatten themselves, although they have to shut their eyes to do it. They come in pale at the edges and stained green down the back, with the long soft hair of the deep seams caught in their claws. Sometimes a barber, putting his ear to a ceiling before he cuts, hears a noise like somebody rubbing a crumb off a sheet of paper. He makes a blue chalk ring at the spot and leaves the growth inside it alone. In time there is a little tuft hanging from an otherwise excellent ceiling, an inconvenience in a public office, where it will invariably turn out to be over someone's chair. Nobody will consent to have it cut.

A young doorback does not necessarily settle at the first door it finds. For a week or two it tries them. There must be warmth, but not the dry heat that comes up from a main, and the mat must be heavy enough to give a pleasant even pressure all the way to the base of the tail. There is also the matter of the step of the person coming home. Stamp on a mat just once to make the funny brown head come out and the next day there will be nothing under it. People going home to an empty dwelling are often particular about their tread for months on end. It is a considerable pleasure, lifting one's door on a cold morning, to feel for the first time the small extra weight go sliding off.

They have very little voice. On the rare evenings when a gray bird is up, however, the animals in the moving pool make a sound together, not loud, but quite distinct in the absence of the mill drums. It is made with the seam in the nose held just at water level. The bird will be walking abreast of them with its head lowered, looking occasionally at one or another of the brown backs. When it puts its bill in, the nearest doorback rolls over and lets it pick the white water lice from the tender folds under its arm. The sound goes on. It can be heard for some distance below, especially in a room that has been allowed to grow down close about a bed. A child hearing it against the pillow for the first time is generally taken upstairs, however late it is, and there on the almost level country, going slowly away in the last of the light, is the bird, stooping now and then to the water, and the long wake of little clean animals turning right side up behind it.
