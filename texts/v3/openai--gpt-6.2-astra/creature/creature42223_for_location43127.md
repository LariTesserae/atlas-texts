What lives in the pans comes away on the side of your hand when you lift it. There is a second or two in which you can feel it holding on, and then a weight rather less than that of a wet leaf settles across the knuckles. If you keep still it will put itself back. It does this one fold at a time. The last fold, which has had farther to go than the others, goes off the end of the little finger with a distinct little tug.

It is one of the things you took for blankets. Only the uppermost fold of the heap is lifted on a hand; the rest have a good hold on the wall. Each is an animal about the size of a square cut out of a shirt to patch an elbow. There is a thick part, no longer than a barleycorn, which can be felt passing to and fro under the rest. This is the part that eats. The thin stuff gets there first and stays behind a while afterwards, so that the animal can be going in one direction at its front and quite another at its back, like a sheet being drawn in through a window.

There is something to eat even on a surface that comes smooth. A finger rubbed along the inside of a pan slips more easily one way than the other. The slip is a growth too fine to have anything answering to a leaf; it leans with the water. The animal lays itself over a patch and the little hard fellow under the sheet goes up and down it, taking a strip at each turn. Behind it the stone has the drag of a clean tooth. A pan as big as a shirt basin has room for four or five of them to feed without coming up against the place where another has lately been.

They fold up on the wall because the wall there is not quite upright. Water from a seam spreads down it to a width of two hands, draws together again at the bottom, and enters one particular pan. An animal going up against this small runnel presently gets above the place where there is water enough to cover its thick part. It stops. Its lower edge, still quite comfortable in the water, goes on coming up. It is in this way that it gives itself its first fold. The next animal coming up finds a wet back laid across its way and goes a little farther. Under the apparent heap there is a continual slow exchange of places. The one farthest in, when it wants to get down, can draw itself out of the bottom with surprisingly little inconvenience to the others. For a while there is the impression of its folds in the folds of those it has left, as there is the shape of a key in a pocket after the key is taken out.

The smallest of them are born on the back of a folded one. A narrow hem loosens, with a grain of hardness in the middle of it, and for a long time it lies in the crease with one edge free and the other held. What frees it altogether is the old one starting down the wall. Then for the first time it is flat. The smallest pans, the ones that would hardly take a tooth, are full of these scraps. If a rim were sharp it would be a considerable undertaking for one of them to leave. As it is, a corner goes over, wets itself on the far side, and presently has the weight to draw the grain over after it.

The animal on the root is something else. It has a much more decided notion of where it ends. If its track meets the edge of one of the flat ones it turns and goes along the edge until it has found its way round. There are mornings, though there is nothing to make them mornings below, when its single thread has described three sides of a patch on the wall as neatly as if somebody had begun to cut it out.

When the bark sounds, the animals nearest it pull in. They do not leave off eating. It is only the loose edge that takes a tuck, and by the time the sound has got as far as the hanging root the tuck has begun to let out again. The bark must have been a great disturbance when it was new. Now there is usually one laid over the pebble at its far end, with a little of itself on the bark. You could learn something there that you could not learn by listening. Long after the *tok* has gone clean out of the air, that edge is still lifting and settling against the wood.
