The rail sleeps with its toes spread along the vine. Its body is no bigger than a heron chick's head, but its feet, laid one over the other, would cover most of its breast. In the morning there are two dry places on the vine where they have been. By then the bird is well inside the fern. It goes in sideways, lifting its near wing a little to let a stem pass under it, and the fern stands back in place without spilling a drop.

There are rails wherever the old wood is soft enough to be opened with a bill. A pair can make a living along one side of a gully. One bird works the bank, prising up the little brown scales of bark behind which the woodlice crowd; the other walks in the bottom and takes what falls. Sometimes what falls is only grit, and the lower bird looks up with one bright eye as if it would like to see a little more care taken overhead. They keep in touch by a low double knock. In the uproar of returning herons you can scarcely hear it from the other side of the gully. The young hear it quite well. Four little black heads come poking out of four different holes in what looked like a solid bank.

There is more room in the bank than one would suppose. A great stick takes years to go; the fine stuff settles close about it and keeps its shape after the last of the wood has been carried out in the bellies of grubs. In one of these long narrow rooms a gecko spends the day, so exactly the colour of the walls that the first thing you make out is a row of tiny white mites travelling across its back. In another there are the pearly ends of a clutch of rail eggs. The bird has brought in strips of fig leaf, pulling each one backward through the entrance, and laid them in the damp at the deep end. Even in full sun the eggs are cool. When a heron puts its weight on the roof, a little sift of brown comes down and the sitting bird blinks.

The heron is hunting a stick. There is a particular piece it wants, grey, clean of leaves, curved at the thick end to go over the rim of its nest. For some time it stands with this in its bill, leaning back, while six feet away a flowering creeper shivers and the gecko slips like a tongue into the dark. At last the stick comes free. The heron goes off with its long burden held crosswise, parting the fronds well in advance of its face. A patch of daylight the size of a fig leaf lies on the floor behind it.

By the second rain there is something green in that patch, a pair of wrinkled leaves still wearing the two halves of a seed. By the third, one of the rails has taken to standing there in the mornings. It turns its flank to the light and raises the rusty feathers over its hip, right down to the pale grey fluff. For ten minutes or so the light is strong enough to warm the skin. A chick comes and pushes in on the warm side. It has lost the black down about its eyes and has the grave, somewhat pinched face of its parents, though it still has to look at a thing twice before it knows enough to eat it.

What it is looking at now is a bead of water moving uphill on the bank. The bead has legs. It is a small beetle come out of a nest cup lower down, carrying its allowance of air away under its wing cases; a skin of water clings to it, bright in the little shaft of sun. The chick pecks after the brightness and gets a wet bill. The beetle drops between two sticks. For a while the chick stands staring into the crack. Then from the other side of the gully comes the double knock, and it goes down the steep loose face on those enormous feet, scarcely moving a grain.
