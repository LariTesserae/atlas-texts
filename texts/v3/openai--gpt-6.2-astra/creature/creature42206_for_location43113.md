On the second morning after rain there is something the size of a grain of salt going round the inside of a hollow. It keeps so close to the wall that in the shade it is quite invisible. On the sunny side a copper spot shows through its back. There is only the one spot, set a little to the left of the middle. It takes the better part of an hour to come round again. By then there are two or three spots following it, at considerable intervals, all taking the same course along the lowest red line in the wall.

The creature is almost all foot. Spread out on the lime it has the outline of a narrow petal, with a thick end that goes first and a thin end that can be lifted and allowed to wave in the water. Under the thick end is a little hard comb. Where the red line has softened in the rain there is food enough for the comb to take: single green threads, not yet bound up together in the tough skin of the mat. A grazer passing along the line leaves it rather brighter than before. When a dozen have passed it begins to stand out as if freshly scratched. That is what the followers have found. They like a cut edge.

The copper spot is a plate. In an old individual it overlaps a second plate, milky at the margin, scarcely visible except when the animal turns up the wall. The two together are no great protection for anything so soft. They do not cover the foot even when it is drawn in; there is no danger in these little pools for which a suit of armor would be much use. What they fit very well is the space between two skins of the reef.

Well before the water goes, while there is still room in the hollow for a bubble to rise and burst, the grazers begin to put themselves away. Each tries a number of seams. It will lie with its head in one for a minute or two, then draw out and travel half an inch and try again. Given a seam of the proper width it goes in on its side, by small successive swellings of the foot, until the plates lie flush with the broken face. In a wall that looks finely ruled in brown and cream there may be one line that is coppery for a little way. A drop of water drawn across it with a fingertip would bring out the color very nicely. It would not bring out the animals. It takes a long soaking to do that.

What goes out across the plain is the young. They have as yet only a fleck where the first plate will be, and are light enough to hang from the underside of the water by the thin end of the foot. In a quiet pool they hang there for days. A pool filled level with its lip can have a little fringe of them all round the sky reflected in it. Let the water rise another hair and the fringe comes loose. They go over the sill in the first clear sheet, ahead of any mud, sometimes by the thousand. A slight roughness in the pavement divides the company. Some go to the right of it, some to the left. At the end of a mile the two lots may be as far apart as the two sides of a bank, one going steadily inland with a good depth of water under it, the other already left on damp lime that will be white by noon.

It is possible in this way for grazers from one small hollow to reach the sea. There are seams enough there, and green food in great abundance, but the young that come down in the rain do poorly in brine. Far more of them end their travels a few yards from where they began, in the next cup down the ribbon. A good cup has a nick in the lip on the high side to let a little water in and no nick on the low side to let it out. There, on a still afternoon, a young one can lower its thick end, take hold of the rising green nap, and begin to feed without ever quite letting go of the sky.

For most of the year the cups look empty. The black curls crackle if touched. The old grazing line has gone dull again, and dust has caught along the seam where the plates are set. But after a night of steady rain the coppery stretch of wall comes apart into its separate spots. For a while they stay just where they are, with a little clear flesh showing below each one. Then one of them lets down its foot as far as the red line, finds it soft, and turns along it.
