There are springtails under the nap of the cracked tile. The two people who change the basket have seen them without knowing it. When the basket comes up, a little ash-gray dust skips sideways out of the square. Most of it goes no farther than the breadth of the brass binding on the basket. A few grains land on dry tile and presently go skipping back.

If you got down with the lamp you could see that between jumps they walk. They go quite deliberately among the fibers, stopping at a knot or following a single blue strand down to where it is bedded in the green. There is good eating on the underside of the lint. A tuft that looks perfectly clean on top will lift to show a fine fur of mold, cropped close in places. The smallest springtails are almost clear. You can see a spot of the last thing one ate traveling about inside it.

They do not occupy the room as a person does. The cold main is no use to them for most of its length, though it is wet; there is no way up. The white saucer is a country with very little shore. Occasionally one gets out on the water there and stands in a dimple, apparently as well supported as it was on the lime. When the drop comes it goes round the saucer several times. It may get off on the far rim. From there to the colony under the chute is eleven paces of bare floor.

There are little fisheries of a sort at the edges of the damp. A spider the color of a peeled grape lives under the turned lip of the basket. She has run three or four threads down to the tile. Anything jumping strikes them from beneath, a direction in which the springtails seem not to expect an impediment. When the workers lift the basket the threads part at their lower ends and the spider goes up with it. For the minute or so that the basket stands on the ridge draining, she has a view, if she cares for one, of the brass grate and the bottoms of both men's trousers. Then her piece of iron comes down again in very nearly the same place. By the next morning the threads are fast to the floor.

The toad stays. It was not there by accident the day they found it, although getting there in the first place may well have been an accident. In behind the delivery chute one of the yellow bricks has lost its face. The recess is no bigger than the part of a spoon that goes in your mouth. A toad backed into it can keep its belly on cool brick and its chin on warm. From the room you might just make out an eye. The pupil lies level regardless of the considerable disorder of the rest of the masonry.

What draws it out is not the springtails so much as a little fly, furry in the wing, that breeds in the greasy lining of the lowest earthenware throat. These flies walk quite a distance before it occurs to them to fly. They come up over the lip of the throat and down the pier, and at the foot of the pier some go one way and some the other. The toad takes up a position on the side more of them seem to favor. There is no need for it to go hunting all over the laundry. In three hours it may advance the length of its own foot, making with each swallow a slow effort to shut its eyes.

The workers have not seen it a second time. Nevertheless they continue to put the basket down to the left. It is a heavy thing to lower accurately at arm's length and there would be no point trying to miss a toad by half an inch. They allow it a good palm's breadth. A second green patch has consequently come up beside the first, in the narrow lane kept wet by the basket's new downhill edge. It doesn't get the daylight down the chute. It gets what comes off the wet side of the white saucer, a trembling secondhand light that lasts a little longer each afternoon as the rim grows.

Once a year, while one worker kneels on the ridge and the other holds the lamp for him, the toad comes as far out as the end of the chute. Several minutes of light in the wrong place have brought the little flies down on the blue pavers. Up on the ridge a thin steel shim goes in under the straightedge and the next size does not. The man with the lamp has to set it down to write. In that interval, with the lamp on the floor throwing enormous shadows from things no taller than a grout joint, there would be no difficulty at all in seeing what lives here. Both men are looking at the book.
