The man with the rake has sometimes seen a mouthful of green going along the side of a sleeper. It goes quite fast, stops where the timber has split, and disappears, the green tips overtaking one another as they are drawn into the split. On a day when the weather kept him in the hut after his work was done, he saw this repeated half a dozen times. At last one of the loads caught crosswise. A small brown animal backed out, sat up, and cut the offending blade in two. It had a blunt head and ears that scarcely cleared the fur. There was a fine black line where the wet had got up its belly.

What the sleeper affords the voles is a sound roof on a rise of gravel. There is hardly any timber left on the underside. You could pare it away with your thumbnail, but the top, dried grey in the wind, will still carry a man. Under it the grass has been cut into galleries of just such a height that a vole's back brushes the old blades overhead. One goes up the rise and ends in a ball of shredded sedge. There are young ones in there, dark and sleek as if they had been dropped in the water. When the mother comes in they get hold of her so promptly that the last one is sometimes towed in at the door behind her.

Another gallery goes down to the water. Here there is no cover for the length of a hand. The vole comes to the end of the grass and stays there, with a piece of her face in daylight. The hairs about her nose are touched one after another by a little wind off the run. Then she is across. There is a considerable bed of sweet young sedge on that side. She takes the blades low down, turning them at the base, and carries back six or seven with an occasional white bit at the end. On the return trip the length of a hand gives her some trouble. The ends trail in the water. Once a whole load went away down the pass, opening out as it went, and she had to sit on the bank and wash the juice off her chin.

The maintenance man puts his feet well apart here, one on either end of the sleeper, though there is no reason to suppose it would give way in the middle. He has tried standing still to see what she makes of him. For a time nothing comes out. Presently the little face appears almost under his instep and remains there. He can feel in his heels the heavy running of the water below and he wonders how long she can feel him after he has gone by. On the far side of the sleeper he finds, caught in the rough of his sock, the blossom of a willow, a little yellow thing picked up at the height of his bootlace, with two minute black flies still working their way over it.

By the time he comes again the run is in the grass. It has found a way through the moss higher up and is feeding into the galleries from the side. In one place the thin roof lifts and settles like the skin on a pot. The mother has taken the young ones out. He happens on the last of the removal, not at the sleeper but well up the rise, where he has knelt to lace his boot. She comes labouring through the grey willow with something almost her own length held very inconveniently under her. It has little pink hind feet. For an instant she sets it down on his lace, which is laid out on the gravel, gets a better hold and is gone. There is a faint pull on the eyelets, quite distinct in all that uproar.

Later in the summer he sees several of them at once along the sunny edges of the track. They have grown their brown coats and their feet have gone dark. One sits up to eat, letting a sedge blade down between its forepaws in short measured lengths. From where he stands he can see the valve house over its head. He goes on down to rake the screen. At the uphill corner, among the usual willow leaves and white twists of root, there is a tight little armful of dry grass. The outside is black from its journey; the inside, when he eases it apart with his thumb, is quite clean, pale straw rubbed as fine and soft as the lining of his pocket.
