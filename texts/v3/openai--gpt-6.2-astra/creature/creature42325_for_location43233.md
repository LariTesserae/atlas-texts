The shrimp does not keep all its feet in the seam. The two in front are generally out of it, turned back under the head and occupied with a little parcel of grit. It passes the parcel from one to the other, getting something out of it with its lips. The feet farther back have to do the work of holding on. Each ends in a hook with a pale tip. In clear water the tips are about all one sees of the shrimp, a set of white commas travelling along the gray line. When the dust comes over, the body shows up dark inside it. It is a good deal longer than one would have thought.

At the quiet end of the saddle it stops. There is a piece missing from the upper lip of a cup here, and under the break a flake of shell lifts a little as the shrimp goes in. For a while its feelers still project, held apart in the current. One bends at a touch and the other goes on waving. A small fish that has been following them waits a reasonable time for the rest of the shrimp to come out. Then it settles with its chin against the flake. It is quite prepared to eat something else.

The fish has a fringe of short hairs under its jaw. By moving its head slowly from side to side it can feel a breathing hole in a bed of grit as smooth as skin. There is one close by. The fish gets up on its breast fins and stands over the place, working its jaw forward until its mouth is almost a tube. A dimple appears in the grit. Then there is a considerable hole, and the fish, with a jerk surprisingly like a sneeze, blows white meal out through its gill covers. It has got a pink worm. For some time after the worm itself has gone down, the fish sits opening and closing its mouth, taking in a little clean water between each two movements. There are bristles on that kind of worm.

Under the flake the shrimp has joined six others. They do not fit into the cup in any ordinary arrangement. Two are head down; one is bent so far round the inner wall that its feelers lie back along its own tail. The largest is a female with a mass of eggs slung under her belly. Every so often she makes a rapid stirring movement with her little swimming feet. In the stillness between strokes one can distinguish, inside each egg, two black specks. When she stirs, the specks stream out in rows and draw together again, and it looks as though something much larger than a shrimp were lying in the cup, sleepily opening one complicated eye.

For these animals the quiet side is only quiet by comparison. Water comes in through the broken lip, goes round the cup and issues through a hole in its floor scarcely wider than a hair. The hole was made by a sponge and is still in use. In the thickness of the floor there are yellow passages so fine and so numerous that a piece of the stuff, snapped off, will bend a little before it breaks. The sponge takes from the water things too small for the shrimp to bother sorting from the grit. Beyond it the water comes out into another cup, one made when the top of the bank stood the height of a shrimp lower in the sea than it does now. That cup has no daylight in it at all. A white brittle star lives there with four arms coiled up and the fifth put through the outlet of the sponge. There is a clean place on the arm where the small steady jet plays.

When the current slackens, the first shrimp comes out backward. The fish is gone. Everywhere along the saddle flakes lift, tilt a few degrees, and settle on the feet of animals testing the wash. This is the time when one sees how many ways there are of getting across a few inches of open shell. The crabs go in spurts, stock-still after each spurt with one claw raised. The little snails cast loose and are carried. A worm the color of the bank sends its front end over first, thin as a pencil stroke, leaving nearly all of itself behind until it has found a hold worth bringing the rest out for. The shrimp goes up onto the crest and stands quite freely on its hind feet. The water that pinned it down an hour ago just ruffles the two fans of its tail.

It is not alone there for long. From under the broken lip the female emerges, walking heavily, the eggs nearly brushing the shell. She passes the first shrimp without touching it and climbs to a small prominence above the seam. For once she has got the change of water exactly right. A loose flake beside her rises edgewise but does not go over. The dust hangs a little above the ground. She straightens, beats her swimming feet once, twice, then violently for several seconds. What comes away from her is at first a cloudy rag, still the shape of the space beneath her belly. The rag opens. It is a crowd of little glass splinters, each with two eyes and a quick independent hitch to its motion. A few strike the shell and spring clear. In another moment they have lifted beyond the prominence and are travelling over the bank where neither the shrimp nor the bottom fish ever goes, a handbreadth above their heads.
