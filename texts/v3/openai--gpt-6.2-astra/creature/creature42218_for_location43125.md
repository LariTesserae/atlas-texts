The feet are worth looking at. Each ends in a little cream pad with a dimple in the middle. Where a foot has just come down, the dimple is flattened out and the gold-green skin shows through it. In a foot that is lifting, the dimple is deep enough to hold a grain of salt. You can find the whole tread of the animal, though not in any one animal. One has its hind feet tucked up, one is stretched to its length, one has turned so far onto its side that all the feet on its left are clear of the leaf. None is above twice the length of the erect one. The great difference in their apparent sizes comes from their way of standing a fold of skin up about themselves, as a sleeper will draw up a quilt.

There are young ones, too. Look in the narrow pleat just below the bite. What seem to be stitches holding the two sides together are pairs of feet belonging to grubs so young that their bodies have scarcely any color. The leaf is drawn in a little at every stitch. A grown grub could let go at one end and reach across an open mouth; these little ones would fall in. They go along with one side of the pleat under the belly and one against the back, quite snug, eating the fine frill the two edges make where they meet. In the light through the bluff a bellyful of frill shows as a green comma. There is one so small that the comma is all you can see of it.

For all the bites out of it, the plant looks the better for its animals. In the second thicket, on the side away from the light, there is a lobe with no feet against it. It lies flat, satin face to satin face, with a yellow line along the bottom where the grease has settled. On the other side the grease stands high in the skin in a fine forked figure. At every fork a foot has pressed it onward. And the mouths there have a brave full curl to them, like the lips of someone about to pronounce a word with great care. It is these well-trodden parts that give out the low note. The thin new rims add something to it almost too fine to hear, though if you draw your cuff a little way down over your hand you miss it at once.

There is evidence of excursions. On the bare pink reach between the nearest thicket and the stair are two rows of dull spots. They stop at the foot of the first step. On the step itself, which is turned with its rough face uppermost, there is nothing to be seen, but on the smooth underside of the second hangs a grub, holding on by its dimples. It has a bit of green skin doubled under its chin. Whether it was taking this up or bringing it home is not to be known. Its head is turned out toward the opening. The teeth, seen from below, are not quite a ring; there is a gap at one side, and through the gap sticks the end of the green parcel, neatly gripped without being cut.

Of the stair maker there is less to tell. The notches are all the same width, even the mistaken one halfway along the fourth flake. Something with a square tip has bitten there and come away. There is a matching nick in the loose flake you are holding up, in just the place where, to spare your wrist, you have let its weight rest on the knuckle of your finger. It is a comfortable place to work from. The maker could have seen that little upturned head very well.

It is tempting to put something in and give the seventh step the least touch it needs. But the bright sound is there already. Shift your ear from the opening to the solid bluff and you can hear it apart from the vowel, a single clean knock with a little body of salt sounding beneath it. The grub on the underside has six feet flattened fast and the others lifted, green parcel and all, to go up over the edge. There is nothing pitiable in its posture. It has found the next hold. Only from this side of the flake does it look a long way off.
