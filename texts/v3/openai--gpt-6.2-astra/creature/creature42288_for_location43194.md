There is a moth on the underside of the lowest step. It is the colour of the stone when the stone is dry. If you put a hand down to steady yourself it will sometimes walk out from between your fingers, keeping its wings shut. It goes over the edge of the slab and hangs there head down, with the rather affronted look of a bit of the step which ought not to have been disturbed.

There are others well back under the ledge. They face different ways. Their wings look dusted with the powder that comes off a slate when you rub two pieces together; across the dust run two fine black lines, neither of them quite straight. They spend the day there. Towards evening the one on the step begins to work its feelers, drawing first one and then the other between its forefeet. It may do this for some time before it stirs another part of itself. The others are walking by then. To see them all on the move in the last of the light is to discover how much of what looked like a rough fracture in the wall consisted of little roofs of wings.

They go out on foot. There is a particular root, pale and nearly bare of hairs, which comes through the roof above the steps and lies for a while along the stone before turning up into the turf. They take that way, though it involves going a little farther into the dark at first. Where the root turns there is a fork just wide enough to put a wing through edgeways. A moth at the fork looks for a moment as if it were being threaded into the hill. Out on the grass it spreads its wings. The under pair are a handsome warm yellow, quite a surprise after the long business of being grey.

What flies out over the thrift at dusk is only the summer part of the creature. Most of its life is spent an inch or two below the sheep's feet. If a piece of the turf comes away in a hard frost, there are passages to be seen on its underside, flattened tubes of silk with crumbs of earth adhering to them. At one end the silk is clean and close woven. At the other it dwindles to a few strands drawn round the base of a grass stem. After dark a little brown caterpillar comes up these strands to crop the grass. It cuts part way through a blade and bears down on it until it folds; then, with a surprising number of feet engaged in going backwards, it takes the bent length home. By morning there is a green stump with a ragged white hinge on one side. The sheep leave much the same mark on a larger scale.

Some of the old tubes hang out from the cut edge of the turf. The wren knows about these. It catches one by the loose silk, braces a foot against a thrift root and pulls back so hard that when the tube parts the bird goes swinging under the grass with the empty end in its bill. The occupied ones seldom give so easily. A caterpillar has hooks on its hind feet and a good deal of silk behind it. You can see the whole pale root bend before the wren lets go.

In wet weather the moths come farther in. One has found the recess with the pebble. It sits in the pecked groove, its head just below the level of the rim and its feet on the little pits left by the tool. In that position the black lines on its wings go a short way towards completing the circle where the cord used to be. The air passing over it raises a narrow fringe of hairs along its back. Nothing else about it moves.

On a still summer morning you might find one that has not yet gone in. It has settled on a sheep-cropped blade just above the opening, too late to do much about the daylight. The wren comes up through the roots below. Before the little round head is properly out, the moth lets go. There is the least flash of yellow against the grass. Then it has dropped through the seam, folded its wings and taken hold of the ceiling, and the wren, which was looking for something to pull, goes on along the underside of the shore.
