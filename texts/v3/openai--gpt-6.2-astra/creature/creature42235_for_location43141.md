A pear that has lost its stone is nearly invisible. There is a little stir of the narrow end among the other pears, and where one expects to see the black grain going round there is a dimple, opening and shutting on the water. The animal can go a fair distance in this condition. It lets go with one side of its belly and takes hold a little farther on, then brings the other side abreast. A journey of half an inch may take it past a hundred excellent stones, all in use.

There are drifts of spare ones. They collect behind the raised edges of the granite, the big black grains underneath and the finer stuff over them, red at first, then almost white. A pear arriving at such a drift lays its socket wide against it. There is a small disturbance like the boiling of sand at the bottom of a spring. Presently the pear draws away with a stone in it. Sometimes the stone is white. In that case one can see what the socket does. A wave goes round its wall faster than the stone turns; the stone is always slipping back a little against the wave. The heavily polished black grains have given a false impression of smooth running. There has to be that little catch and slip or nothing gets ground.

In the quiet water behind each drift there are animals smaller than the eggs in the hollows. They stand on three long hairs, with a fourth hair trailing loose. When a grinder comes looking for a stone these little things go down all at once, not into the sand but flat along it, the three hairs coming apart at their tips into a number of finer hairs that hold. A good deal may be swept away above them. When they stand up again some are on the stone inside the pear. Through the pear's side one can just make out the loose hairs describing circles. At each turn they pass through the stream of fresh water coming in at the neck. They were feeding on the drift; they feed rather better here. By the time the stone is worn out they have given it the faint nap of a peach. It is this, as often as its smallness, that makes an old stone slip its socket.

Most of the stones last longer than the pears. Where the current has shifted there are little congregations of black grains still set in the old working order, a neck's length apart. The bodies that held them have gone into the gray backs and the clear soles. Sometimes there is a very small grain beside a large one. A young pear has no socket to speak of, only a tough spot in a bag of jelly. It settles against a working animal and is knocked gently and continually by the motion of the big socket. One side of the bag thickens. Grit sticks there, and for a while the young animal has a dark, untidy cheek. The first grain it keeps is seldom larger than a speck of pepper. Its note is too high to be heard through the rock with the others. In the flesh of the old pear there is a little answering tremor.

The white jointed animals in the uphill cracks have a different look according to which side of the quartz they are on. Against the dark granite they seem made of solid milk. Where one crosses the white seam its outer wall disappears and there is a row of quite distinct brown parcels going slowly along it. These are the eggs it has taken. Not all of them are digested. An egg well along in its development has a crease already, and can shut itself so closely that it goes the length of the white animal and comes out in the new crack fit to open. This accounts for the sprinkling of gray backs on stone quite out of reach of the milk. They are shut nearly all the time. A crack need only widen by the thickness of one of their lips for a thread of used water to find them. Then a pink line travels a surprisingly long way into the mountain.

At the very end of one recess a jointed animal has been caught by the return of the slab. Its middle is held fast. The segments on either side still perform their patient shortening; a blunt end comes up against the quartz, rests, and goes back. There is room beside it for a second one to go through edgewise. For several days the second animal stops each time it comes abreast of the first. There is no sign that they know one another. The stop is for the eggs that have passed through the captive and lodged in the lee of its body. They make a particularly good bed there. When the slab lifts again, the white length draws itself free, leaving on the stone a perfect jointed outline of little gray backs.

Under the roof, in the yellow of a sole, there is more going on than can be seen from below. Its upper surface is ridged in fine parallel folds. Air out of the ice collects in the folds, bead joining bead until a bright wire runs almost the length of the animal. A sole about to come down first lets these wires go. They slip out at the edge and flatten against the ice, silver marks where there has been no silver a moment before. Then the sole gathers itself a little thicker at one end. It takes so long to peel that the patch of sun may pass it in the act and show it again next day, hanging by no more than a clear button of flesh. On the floor it spreads in a matter of seconds. The grinding stops underneath it. For a considerable distance round about, the stones can be heard separately.

In the window overhead the scattered bubbles stay for a while. Each has in its curved underside a picture of the floor: a black dot, a pink slit, a short piece of white bent at a joint. A tremor goes through all these pictures together when a slab settles. Usually no more comes of it than a sift of red meal off a ledge. But along the bright uphill break the shut mouths open, first one and then half a dozen farther in. Water has come their way. The outermost mouths close again almost at once. Deep in the seam, past the place the daylight can reach even at noon, the last ones go on feeding.
