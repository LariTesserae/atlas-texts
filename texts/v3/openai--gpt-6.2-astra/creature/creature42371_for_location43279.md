The girl with the hide bag has one cheek puffed out. She is keeping a little water in her mouth for her bait. The blunt-faced young get better at their work toward the end of a meal. At first they drag at whatever sticks out from under a shell and generally pull it in two; later on they put a paw on the rim as their mother does, and there is nothing left for the girl. She has been watching one youngster in particular. He has a stiff paw and goes through a great deal of breakfast to very little purpose.

When the family has gone she gathers up the rags of flesh and wets them and puts them in her bag. By the time she reaches the river she will have to be quick opening it. The little bag dries hard as a nut.

There are fish under the door, flat, clear fish of which the most visible parts are the two dark eyes and a twist of green matter some way behind them. The girl lies on the door with a baited hair over each edge. It is a good way to fish except that people want to cross. When she hears feet coming she sits up, gathers the hairs into one hand and turns sideways. A man passes with a grinding stone against his belly; two women pass with nothing at all; then she lies down again. She knows nearly everybody who goes over the river by the undersides of their chins.

It takes a good many of the fish to make a meal. What she wants today are the long clear bones that run down their backs. Her father sets these in the rims of drinking bowls before the clay dries. After firing, each leaves a fine channel through which water can be drawn by a person who cannot lift his head. There is an old man on their pavement who has drunk in this fashion for so long that a bowl without the channel seems to the girl a bowl not quite finished. He used to set teeth in files. He can still tell by the pull on a shaving of horn whether a tooth wants turning a little to the left or the right, and for this reason people bring him quite as many files as he cares to see. Sometimes he sends one back saying there is nothing wrong with the file, the horn is too dry.

The women come back over the door before the girl has her sixth fish. Each now carries a rolled hide dark to the elbows. They have been wiping the shaded sides of the horn-colored rocks. This little harvest is not worth making every morning, nor is the water, once wrung out, particularly nice to drink. It has a taste as of a thumb held too long in the mouth. But it leaves no chalk on drying. Near the upper stair there is a woman who paints eyes on the white clay birds given to children, and she will take any amount of this water the two can bring her. In payment they have a bird apiece when they want one, and the use of her oven on the days she fires.

The real birds come down to the bank to pull hairs out of the discarded hides. At close quarters their whiteness is seen to be all on the outside. When one stretches a wing, a lovely dull apricot opens and shuts under it. Their nests hang in the rents far overhead. Now and then a whole season’s work comes down, a long stocking of hair with little rooms opening off a common passage, much like the city. The smaller children always hope to find a bird in one. What they generally find are beetles and some very ancient droppings and, almost at the closed end, a tuft of somebody’s good wool. People lie on their backs to pick out the rents where that wool might have been all winter. From down here it seems astonishing that anything which once lay across a person’s knees can have been so high.

Late in the day the girl brings home her fish. She cooks them on a hot flake of shell, and eats the flesh directly off the bones so as not to spoil their curve. The old man beside her watches the shell animals going by. One has a streak of blue clay across its back. In the morning a potter’s boy dipped his finger in slip and drew it through the moving gray, marking perhaps forty of them, to see where they would be by nightfall. Most are still within a few feet of his stool. This one has come from the upper stair, gone round two soaking pits, and crossed a drain on a lip of shell not much wider than itself. The old man asks the girl to go fetch the boy before it gets away.

She puts a drinking bowl over it instead. In the morning, when she lifts the bowl to show him, the animal is gone. There is just room for a small one, with its shell held slantwise, to get out through the notch where a sick man puts his lip. The two children try a few empty shells this way, tipping them and passing them through, until they have it quite clear how the thing was done. By then most of the blue has worn off the others. They take the bowl up to the stair to try again.
