The people who work at the two works live mostly in timber houses along the northern meadow. A house and its yard are not necessarily on the same piece of ground. At one house the kitchen garden has gone down far enough that the woman can sit on her back sill and pull carrots without bending over. She remembers having to kneel in the dirt to do this. Her daughter remembers neither arrangement; she has been away eleven years and comes to the back door expecting a step which is now stored underneath the washstand.

There are small brown animals in these houses, though not by invitation. In the accounts of the western works they are called mice. Wire damaged by mice, felt eaten by mice. A man called in to deal with them will write mice on his bill and then spend the evening explaining that they are nothing of the kind. One almost never sees a whole one. There will be a little hand, the color of the skin on boiled milk, feeling along the underside of the sill, and a minute later, some way off, a blunt brown nose coming up between two floorboards. This gives an exaggerated idea of their length. A grown one laid out in a shoebox occupies about half the box. Its tail takes up very little room, being flat and usually folded forward along its belly.

They live on the wiry grass, not on the seed as one might suppose but on a little thickening of the stalk just below the head. To eat it the animal lies on its back in the interlocked grass with all four feet overhead. It draws a stalk down through its teeth until the head comes against its lips, bites, and lets the head spring back. In June the crumbs fall into the open flowers. If you wait on the maintenance stair long enough you can see a dozen yellow flowers give a jump in succession and presently a round ear come through the ceiling, stay a little while and go back in. The ear is almost transparent. There is usually a nick out of it somewhere; the grass has cutting edges.

It used to be said that these creatures came into the works for the warmth. In fact there are as many in the disused eastern end of the cooling room as anywhere. What they come for is the packing felt. A narrow strip of good felt, worked through the grass and pulled tight with the teeth, makes a sleeping pocket that will keep its shape through a whole season of damp. In the wild they use the long skin that peels off the white trees, a much inferior stuff when wet. There is a stretch below the western roof where nearly every nest is blue. For three years the works took delivery of its polishing powder in drums lined with blue felt, and though there has been no blue felt in the stores for a considerable time the animals go on unpicking the old nests to furnish the new. The color has persisted farther down the seam than daylight enough to see it by.

A girl from one of the northern houses once brought a nest up entire for the school table. The class picked out, besides the felt and bark, a shoelace, the paper band from a bunch of leeks, and a length of fine copper wire with both ends polished bright. In the middle lay two young, which nobody had noticed on account of their being quite hairless and the same gray-pink as the lining bark. The teacher took the class down to put it back. This proved a worse job than getting it out. You could reach the branch from the stair or you could see it from the stair, but not both. In the end the smallest boy stood with his face to the wall and told the teacher where to put her hand. For some days afterward the children visited the place at noon. The mother had drawn the shoelace across the opening and there was nothing whatever to see.

The glass workers know the animals best in winter, when there is not much to eat overhead and they come along the bottom edges of the windows after dead moths. On the inside of the glass a worker can put her finger against one of the pale little palms and feel nothing, though the fit of skin to skin looks exact. The animals pay very little attention to a person behind glass. What alarms them is a movement of the frame. They will leave a window all at once if someone at the far end of the room should try a sticking latch. For this reason they were once credited with knowing in advance when a slab would drop. Several expensive attempts were made to put that belief to use. The surviving instrument is in the school now: a brass wheel, a smoked drum and a little felt-lined runway which the makers evidently expected the animal to follow. Across the felt, at right angles to the direction intended, goes a neatly eaten channel an inch and a half wide.

They are not much troubled by the cloud. When the grass gets too wet to hold them they go down the trunks to the dry side, where they sit close together, their tails unfolded under their feet. A person coming home late sometimes hears them there. The sound is a very light rasp, interrupted and taken up again. It carries well through the wet air and has been mistaken by newcomers for somebody in the meadow striking match after match. It is only the animals cleaning the grass from between one another’s teeth. On a clear morning, standing above them before the branches shed their drops, you can see one tip its head back and another take the narrow jaw in both hands.
