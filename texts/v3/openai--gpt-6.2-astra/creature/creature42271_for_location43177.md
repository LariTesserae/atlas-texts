On a tree going over into the lagoon there are sometimes little shelves of crushed shell, one in the first fork, another a good deal higher up, all quite level. They are feeding places. In the morning there may be a crab on one of them, holding in both big claws a lid of shell hardly bigger than its eye. It turns the lid about and scrapes the soft hinge with its mouthparts. The animal that opened it has gone down into the tree. Its wet tail has left a broad clean sweep across the grit.

It is a climber about the length of a forearm, with close brown hair through which the pink skin shows in the armpits. The forefeet are wonderfully loose at the wrist. It can come along the underside of a sloping trunk with its hind feet still on the bark and both hands going over the shellwork below. It feels for the thin growing lips of the shells. A good one it rocks very gently for a time, then fetches away, usually with two or three little ones adhering to it. It does not do this quite by touch. It puts its face down from time to time and looks between its hands with the absorbed, cross-eyed look of a person threading a needle.

On dry ground it sits up to eat, the flat of the tail folded forward under its heels. In the little white shelves in the trees one can see the mould of this arrangement, two hollows and a shallow gutter between them. There is room beside the animal for a young one. The young one puts its hand wherever the old one has its hand, even in the middle of a blow, and occasionally has to be gathered up and set down on the opposite side. A shell knocked out of the fork goes bouncing down through the fans. Both lean out to follow its progress, but neither goes after it. There are any number more within reach.

It is surprising how seldom one sees the climbers swimming. They enter the water backwards, keeping a hand on a root until the face too has gone under. Their ordinary journey is through the bottom of the bank, where there are long rooms under the shell roof and roots coming down into them that have never been exposed to daylight. A shrimp disturbed in one room goes flickering through the ceiling into the next. The climber goes more deliberately, drawing itself along by the roots, coming up to breathe in places where a little air stands between the roof and the water. On a very still afternoon one can lie on the hard ringing ground and hear, somewhere under one's ribs, a long comfortable expiration, followed by the sound of someone washing his hands.

The mothers use the trees that have tipped, but not the newly tipped ones. There must be a dry way down the trunk and a second way out through the crown, and the crown must have taken a good hold of the bottom. In the elbow of such a tree there is often a hollow with an entrance underneath and another, scarcely wider than a thumb, above. Through the upper hole comes the smell of warm milk. Small flies collect there, and a lizard generally has the place on the bark just above it. The young are born with the hair on their tails laid in two neat swirls. Before their eyes are open they can hang by all four feet from the mother's belly fur while she goes down the slant of the trunk. She stops short of the water for the first few days. One after another the little tails unroll and dip in it.

Farther inland the same animal lives without ever having to swim. There it breaks snails on the upturned roots and drinks from the pleats of the low palms, drawing a finger down a pleat and licking the finger. These are the ones most often surprised in the daytime. A climber surprised does not run off at once. It goes round to the back of whatever is supporting it, even if this is only a fern stalk too narrow to conceal its nose. Presently the fingers appear, four on one side at a considerable height, four on the other lower down. The whole soft face comes out sideways to see if anything has been done about it.

Towards evening the families come out on the long boughs. The herons move up a little to let them by. There is no great exchange of calls, only a small pursing sound a mother makes if a young one delays too long investigating a knot. At the very end of a bough they go down singly into the round-leaved herb. For a while their course and that of the fish can be told apart: the fish sends up one travelling ridge, the climber makes two little openings in the green and shuts them behind it. Then the leaves grow too thick for this to be seen. Here and there well in towards the forest a single leaf stands up on edge, held for a moment by a brown hand while its owner breathes.
