There are snails under the pine. A grown one could sit in the hollow left by a fallen needle without touching either bank. The shell is brown and low, with a few white grains lodged in the turn nearest the mouth. Seen from above, it has much the appearance of a speck of stone in a drop of resin. There are seven such specks on the underside of one branch. Six are empty shells. The seventh has a skin drawn across its mouth and is fastened to the wood.

The branch is dead at its tip and alive a little farther in. Water coming down the live part darkens it almost to the break. For some time the snail stays where it is, though a wet streak as broad as its shell has passed within reach. Then a clear edge comes out between shell and wood. It spreads a little, takes hold, and the shell tips off the place where it was stuck. Two horns go feeling along the wet. The left one touches a grain of grit and shortens to a button; the right goes on at its full length. For a while this is all that happens in the way of travel.

There is a fine green skin on the wood here, particularly thick in the little pits out of which the needles grew. The snail puts its head into a pit. When it takes it out there is a paler place in the green, rounded at the end. It feeds around the rim with its shell hanging out over the dark. A gust that lifts the whole branch and sets it down again does not disturb it much. The pit, the shell and the little piece of wet ground occupied by the foot have all gone up and come down together.

Very few of them live near the spoonful of water. Its stone rim is bare for the width of a finger, a considerable crossing, and in a cold night the water freezes right to the bottom. The good places are where two things lie close without quite touching. Under a scale of bark there are eggs, so like the beads of water beside them that it takes the drying of the bark to tell which they are. In the fork of a willow root there is a patch of shells so small that the whole patch might be taken for grit. One has the dark thread of a meal showing through its first turn. It is on the move already, carrying that one green thread towards the pine.

The new wedge of rock has crushed a little rotten wood into the earth. On one side its weight is on the wood; on the other a narrow space remains, roofed with clean stone. By morning a snail has gone some way into it. There is a silver track on the roof, visible only from below and only as far as the light goes. When the plate begins to knock, a last pinch of white flour sifts down off the roof. The snail draws in its horns. A little later, with the knocking still at its height, it puts them out again.
