There are more of the little ovals than a walker would suppose. At the lip of a shelf you see the ones that have come to the end of their grazing. Farther back, where the shelf is hardly the breadth of a finger off the bed below, there will be half a dozen working abreast. Their combs leave pale tracks in the grey. A track is about the width of a grain of rice, and so straight while the food lasts that where two cross you would take them for scratches made in cutting the stone. At the end of each is a little fan of marks. The animal has stood still there and felt about for more.

The white on its back is a burden. On the older ones it grows in overlapping lips, quite handsome, with a pinkish line of dirt under each lip. It is possible to tell an old animal by touch in the dark. After a time one of these goes down to the pool and settles at the shallow end, at just the place where a hand feels the two temperatures. It moves about a good deal before it is satisfied. Its feet are in the warm water and its back is under a tongue of the cool. There it stays, sometimes for days. The lips lose their edges; presently the stripe down the middle of the back shows through, and you can see that it is green. When the animal comes out again it can turn over in a space where before it could only have backed out. The underside of almost every shelf has such a space, with good untouched grey stuff beyond it.

The very small ones have no white on them at all. What looks like a little ruffle along the back edge of a grazing oval is often a row of young, each holding on with its front feet, its hind end loose in the wash. They come off readily enough. The difficulty is getting one to let go of your finger afterward. One foot at a time comes free, reaches, and takes hold again a little farther up the print. There is nothing in its whole experience to suggest that a warm wet thing might shortly be lifted into air.

Most let go of the parent at a forward stroke of the water. They go a considerable way. There is a moment after the clack of the pebble when water streams along the seam under a bolster and every hanging drop leans the same way. A young one travels in the belly of a drop, runs with it into the seam, and is lost to sight. This is how there come to be grazers at pools between which not even a wet footprint could last the walk. Their country, if you could lay it out flat, would be a dark sheet with long slits of daylight in it. The great white distances on which you judge the basin scarcely enter into it.

Something eats them down there. At certain pools, if you lie with your cheek on the notch, you can see what seems to be a fine red root growing out of a hole. For several beats it will trail slackly one way and then the other. Then a length of it lifts clear of the stone of its own accord. It has pairs of minute dark cups along it. A cup touched by a drifting young one folds up like a purse; the root draws slowly back, and for a while only its blunt tip shows in the hole. No one looking down from above would notice it. Against the dark of the hole it is red, but against the light from the bottom of the pool it is the colour of the water.

Once in a while the water brings up an empty white back. It comes up rocking, catches in the polished notch, and stays there long enough to dry. It is surprisingly large and substantial then, a little corrugated awning under which the animal itself would hardly seem to have had room. By the time the shadow of the shelf reaches it, it is usually gone. A lift of the pool has floated it off the notch and turned it hollow side up; it fills and goes down. For a few strokes you can follow it by the flash as it crosses the bead of gas. Then there is only the bead, arriving a little late, and after it the next one in the usual place.
