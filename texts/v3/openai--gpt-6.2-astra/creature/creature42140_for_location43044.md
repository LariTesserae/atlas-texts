The first thing to flower in the tower is the nap on the cable. Its flowers are little white cuffs, each turned back on itself like the rim of a sock. For two or three days the black rubber shows between them. Then the cuffs grow broad enough to touch and the cable looks as if someone had done a very neat job of winding it in tape. This happens as far as the junction box and no farther. Beyond the box the cable was replaced at some point with a grey one, and on that there is nothing at all.

The broad-leaved crop flowers too, though it was a matter of some dispute downstairs whether it did. In the old days a plant was cut when a man could put his two hands side by side on a leaf without showing a fingertip past the edge. It takes another year's growth to make the flower. It comes low on the stalk, almost in the cup, a green ear with a single slit in it. Out of the slit come three hard brown grains, one after another, at intervals of about a week. A grain usually drops back into the cup and has a poor time of it among the established stalks. One has found its way into a countersunk screw hole halfway up the fire door. The plant there has two leaves the size of teaspoons. They are quite as handsomely veined as the big ones in the beds. In the morning they lie folded together; when the light comes round from the side they open out and between them obscure the screw.

There is a different crop on the upper walk. The pressed squares hanging there were cut from several leaves apiece, the edges overlapped and rolled so flat it was once a point of pride that you couldn't find the joins. Now every join has a line of white buttons down it. They are the beginnings of roots. A sheet held in four clips stays fairly square, but where a clip has given way the weight at the joins has drawn the sheet into deep flutes. In the hollow of each flute the green is still the old workman's green, the colour they used to approve by holding the finished article up against the frosted pane of the pay office. On the ridges it is almost yellow. The sheets are growing appreciably bigger. On the nearest, the little punched hole that took the corner clip is now three inches in from the corner, and is no longer round.

There are things in the room that have never been given a name, even an incorrect one. Under the lip of the second tread is a row of minute grey pears on short stems. Each has a bright point at its lowest end, which on close inspection proves to be a bubble held in a few crossed hairs. The pears get no direct light. Their light comes off the white tread below, and if a fallen leaf lies there long enough the pears immediately above it dwindle to dry-looking knots. A little farther along, where a chip out of the ceramic admits a pencil of afternoon sun, a whole bunch has grown on one stem. From the landing it is just possible to see their bubbles light up together.

The press itself wears its growth in two perfectly matched halves. On the underside of the upper platen is a russet felt; on the lower is a russet felt with a pale place corresponding to every bolt head above it. While the jaws stand apart the pale places slowly fur over. They never quite disappear before the jaws come together again. At the back edge, beyond the reach of the pressure, the felt is an inch thick and ends in soft points like the teeth of a very worn comb. A new shade leaf sometimes grows in over it, and goes into the press with six or seven little teeth indented along one margin. There are old sheets on the upper walk that carry the same mark. The felt, or something very like it, was evidently here when the men were.

Of all the plants it is the white grass on the motor that alters most in the ten cold days. It doesn't die down. The blades grow slack at the base and fall outward, leaving the crown bare, a tiny pink pucker directly over a raised letter in the maker's name. When the pumps start again the first blade to stand up is always the one against the letter. The rest follow in a widening patch. For a day the name can be read in standing grass among the fallen grass. Then the whole slope is white again.

Down at the station the remaining sheet is dry and rather brittle. For a long time no one knew it had anything to do with the tower. It is on the inside of the skylight, over the bench at the end where the parcels used to be weighed. In wet weather a little water gets past the putty and runs along the lower edge of the sheet. If you stand on the bench you can see, just there, three of the white buttons. Two are no bigger than pinheads. The third has opened, and a single pale root has gone down into the paintwork of the frame.
