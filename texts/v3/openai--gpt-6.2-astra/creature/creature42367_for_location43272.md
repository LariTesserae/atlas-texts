The people whose names are on the stair collars are rather smaller than the drawings of their feet would lead you to suppose. In the drawings a foot is generally the size of a hand. The foot that made the drawing is scarcely longer than the last joint of your thumb. It is as broad as it is long, with a tuck down the middle. One half can be let go without disturbing the other. This gives a walking person a curiously deliberate air, even when going very fast, as of somebody taking up a strip of sticking plaster and laying it down a little further on.

They have no particular attachment to the underside of a leaf. They live on the crease where it joins the mountain. Here there is a long wedge of sheltered country, deep at one end and tapering at the other to nothing, with rooms made by picking the soft inclusions out of the rock. On a still day you can see right into a house. There will be a row of little sleeping hollows, a number of yellow tufts hanging up to be combed, and, on the wall that is going to come down over all this, a second set of hollows corresponding exactly to the first. When the house is shut a person can turn over in bed. There is very little more room than that.

A child is much inconvenienced by growing. There is a morning every few weeks when somebody has to lie on the floor with the leaf up and be drawn round in charcoal. The dust is then picked away inside the line. They do this for one another and are not always kind about it. In an abandoned crease above the outer stair there is the outline of a perfectly ordinary child with ears extending for a good three inches on either side. The ears have been laboriously excavated to the proper depth. It seems some time passed before anybody noticed.

Most of their work is done with a comb. A ripe brush has a little swelling halfway up each wire, grey on the outside and white within. The white part is good to eat. The trick is to strip the swellings without pulling either toe of the wire out of its bed. A person can work along a brush for half a morning and leave it looking much as before, except for the white meal all over the front of their coat. The long animal with the eyelash legs is after the same thing, but takes the brush home entire. It will occasionally take a comb as well. In the depth of its run, where nobody of a convenient size can go after it, you can see the teeth glinting.

For company the crease people prefer a place where two leaves do not quite meet. They sit on opposite edges with their feet stuck down and talk with the tips of their fingers on the strip of bare mountain between. You feel a voice of this sort better with your elbow than with your hand. There is a little pointed knock to begin with, quite distinct from the great loose bang of the leaves, and after it a succession of pressures so light that the grain of the stone seems to be shifting under your skin. Two old acquaintances can sit there for hours. When one leaf rises the conversation simply goes up at that end. The speaker keeps a fingertip on the rock until it is nearly overhead.

The names in the dust belong to people who go out looking for shut houses. A leaf can come down a fraction out of true. Then the hollows do not match, and though the people inside are usually quite safe, there may be no way from the sleeping room to the door. At the collar the searchers draw the sole of a left foot for a house they have felt knocking, a right for one they have got into. This is why the feet are drawn so large. Inside each toe there has to be room to put the little marks for how many. A right foot with six marks is good news and will often be left in the dust for days, with all the subsequent business of the stair conducted on a narrow bit of brass round its heel.

There is one inhabitant you are unlikely to see except by waiting at such a house. A few moments before the seam opens, a grey, limp-looking thing comes out, thin enough to pass for the shadow of a hair. It draws more and more of itself out of the joint, lays its forward end flat, and unfolds a pair of fine square sails. By then even a stranger knows to stand back. The leaf goes up. Meal, combs, scraps of bedding and sometimes a very cross person still rolled in a quilt come skidding into the light. The grey thing is already halfway across the green, sailing on the first puff of air squeezed out of a house as the roof begins to shift.

For a long time the people took it for a reliable judge of the movements of the mountain. They used to follow wherever it settled, in hopes of finding another family to bring out. In fact what it wants is the meal. Put a pinch in the tuck of your foot and sit quite still on the sheltered stair. Presently there will be a slight tickling. Then two little square sails will come up between your toes, deeply creased from being carried all that way folded. They smooth themselves. The animal waits with great confidence for you to open.
