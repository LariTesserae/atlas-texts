The young of the lid animals are good swimmers. There is so little occasion for swimming on the heights that this is easy to miss. In a pool that has stood for a week you may find one hanging head down from the skin of the water. It is the size of a grass seed, amber all through except for two white flecks where the back is beginning to harden. Its fringe is divided into little fans. At a touch it lets go with all of them at once and goes a surprising distance on the force of that first stroke. Then it hangs again. In still water it seems to have no use for a second stroke.

On a morning when the pools join, the young come out into the old river. For an hour there are fine bright scratches going against the light in all the reaches where the water is moving downhill. The young swim uphill, keeping so close to the two banks that a finger laid across the course will collect a dozen against its warm side. Those that get well up into the grazing beds find a place under the fringe of a grown animal. By noon the white flecks have met. For the rest of its life the swimmer will have something a little too broad to swim with on its back.

The ones that set out late get caught on the bare sills between pools. They draw their fans under them. As the water goes they come to look very like the pale grains in the rock, except that the pale grains stay bright at whatever angle you look at them, and these go black if you put your head between them and the sun. The birds know the difference. A bird will walk the length of a dry sill with its bill shut, picking nothing up. Behind it, the little bodies in the very middle of its track are gone.

It takes a close view of a bird’s foot to see where they go. The toes are long and divided underneath by a crease, rather like the crease in the kernel of a peach stone. The crease stays wet long after the rest of the foot has dried. The stranded swimmers fasten there. A bird asleep on a round stone may have quite a freight of them, tucked out of the heat under the one foot drawn against its breast. When the bird steps into water again they are off before the toes take its weight. There are small grazing beds in the upland pools that no river, even in a very wet year, could ever have carried a swimmer to.

In time the birds themselves acquire a little of the color of these animals. A newly fledged bird is brown right down to its ankles. The pale plumage comes in first on the breast, where the wet foot goes, and spreads in two long panels under the wings. It is not the feathers that turn white. If you find one caught on a grass tip and run it between your lips, a little chalk comes off and the feather is brown again. The chalk is from the houses. A bird going in or out wipes both sides on the door.

Far back in the largest houses there are rooms a hand cannot reach. Their floors are lined with feathers, brown ones below and powdery ones on top. There is generally just one egg. It is round and faintly pink, small enough to roll some way down the passage if it were not for the worn hollow of the floor. Both birds sit, though seldom for long together. At the changeover the one coming in must back a little way out with the one coming out pressed breast to breast against it. For a moment the doorway has a single bird in it with an eye at either end.

The chick stays inside until its legs are nearly as long as they will ever be. It learns the distances of the place in the dark. Even quite a young one, put down at an entrance, goes along the crooked passage without brushing a wall. Out on the bare stone it is much less certain. The first few days it will run at a grass tuft as if intending to go through a door, then stand with its head among the stems and its whole long body outside. At evening the old bird brings it home by easy stages, stopping at the few stones high enough to have a shadow of their own.

Towards the end of the dry season there may be a dozen of these brown birds at a house, though only one was hatched there. They gather on the uphill side, out of sight of the grazers coming up to shed. At sundown, when the first tile ticks, all the young birds stoop to listen. From inside comes a second sound, very faint: the rasp of a soft new back drawing free of an old one. The birds go on standing with their heads down long after there is anything to hear. By morning the grazer is halfway back to the river, white as a chip of fresh stone, and in the roof of the house there is one more dark, cool place just wide enough to admit a folded wing.
