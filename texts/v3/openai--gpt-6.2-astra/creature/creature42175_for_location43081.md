The woman who lived in that last room is in the basket court for the year. She has brought remarkably few things with her: a sleeping net, a comb with one wide end for the hair and one fine end for clearing grit out of the gill pleats, and a little pot with a lid that screws into the pot by a whole turn. There is white sand in it. She puts a pinch of this on a stem that slips under the mallet. Her old sill had a hollow in it where she could keep the sand. In the basket court she is forever having to take the lid off and put it back on, and sometimes says rather feelingly, into the bone below her sister's ear, that she will be glad to get home.

Otherwise she likes the change. There is somebody awake at every hour in the basket court. If you come out of your net in the middle of the night with a cramp in your foot you can generally find somebody sitting on the sill who will take your heel in one hand and fold the toes back with the other. The making of the flat hinges is her business. A hinge has to bear hardly any weight; you could lift a loaded basket on one finger down here. What it has to stand is a hundred little twists as the person carrying it turns a shoulder to go through the roots. She tests them by putting one round her wrist and going about her work. Those that last a week go on the seed baskets, which must not come open all at once.

Her sister's boy has a hinge round each of his wrists, though he has made neither of them and nobody is waiting for his opinion. He is at the age when a child begins to want an occupation. For a while he went about taking the snails off everything and putting them in straight lines on the common-room sill. By the time he had finished the line it had two ends going in opposite directions and a considerable gap in the middle. Now he is learning the rails. He goes along with his eyes squeezed shut and his free arm over his head, a precaution not much use against anything except being fallen on from above. He knows most of the grips. What he does not yet know is how far it ought to be from one grip to the next. He will go solemnly round and round the rail of the same bed looking for the way out to the street.

Few of the grown people are tall. They have long arms, with a very slight outward bow in the last joints of the fingers, just sufficient to let a finger lie snugly round a cord without closing the fist. A person's hands keep quite still when there is nothing to say. People from the lower lakes, where the water lies over mud and you cannot see a hand distinctly at much more than an arm's length, sometimes find this stillness forbidding. In fact a whole court may be following with the greatest interest the efforts of a guest to get a tuber out of its jacket, waiting for the first faint movement that will give them leave to help. The polite sign for help is made small, close to the body. The sign for *look out behind you* is beautiful, a great unfolding movement which looks most unlikely to have anything to do with danger. Even babies will turn for it.

They do go up into the air. Somebody has to go for the antler. Early in summer, when the meltwater is running brown along the deer paths, two or three of them will be out all morning working up a gully. They wear collars of wet moss and keep well to the trickle, lying down in it from time to time. Nothing is quite the weight that they expect. An antler which one can tow home by a thread once it is in the water has to be handed down a bank tine by tine, with a person below to settle each point before the person above lets go. They come home with scratches on the insides of their arms and a queer recollection of having heard separate sounds, a stone falling after a foot had moved on, a fly passing one ear and not the other. For a day or so afterwards the noise of the town seems all one piece to them. Then the snails come clear again.

There are people who have never gone farther into the air than the length of the hand, held out on a warm day to feel the extraordinary swiftness with which the skin goes cold. The hinge maker is one. Her sister is another. For them the farthest place is not the rim but a chamber under the public beds where the water wells up through fine gravel. There is scarcely room to turn in it. Once a year the two of them used to push their way down there belly first and lie with their chins on their crossed hands, letting the little stones leap up against their faces. They have not been for some years. It has just not occurred to either of them when the other was free.

This afternoon the boy comes to the end of a rail and opens his eyes and finds himself at the western court. He has come by the street, a much greater achievement than getting there across the roofs. There is nobody to have seen him do it. His aunt is quite near, hanging feet up with a half-finished hinge in her hand, but she is looking into her old doorway. The trout has moved a little way in. What the woman can see of her room past the fish is the place in the sill where she used to keep the sand. A caddis has worked itself backwards into the hollow and is busy turning round in it, testing it for size. She feels a touch below the ear. The boy wishes to know if she can find the way home in the dark. She puts the hinge round her wrist and shuts her eyes and gives him her other hand. There is a long interval in which nothing much happens except that their hair lifts together in the pull of the outlet. Then he finds the first swelling with his thumb and the two of them set off rather slowly, down at street level, the long way home.
