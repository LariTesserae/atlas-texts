The four green growths live there. The easiest one to look at is the smallest. It stands a little apart from its varnish dot, on a neck so short that in poor light the two would seem to be one smear of green. The light through the window shows a band of white glaze between the bottom leaf and the dot. It is a leaf with air on both sides of it. One can go on looking at that for quite a while.

The dot is still part of the plant. Its rim is round; at one edge there is even the slight thickening where the wet stamp lifted. Inside that rim, grains of tin which were well apart when the varnish was printed have been drawn together into fine crooked lines. Several lines end blindly. The others meet under the neck. This is nearly all there is of a root, and it lies in the varnish, not in some unsuspected soil below the glaze. If the green part could be lifted, it would take its little allotment of ground with it.

The smallest has a fork with very unequal arms. One is a mere tooth and looks as if it came to grief early on. In fact it is an excellent tip. It stands where quite a short point suffices to give up charge to the gas. The long arm had to go much farther out to do as well. Both ends have the same fine finish. There is no scar on the short one, no tender new green on the long one. Neither has anything left to do by way of growing.

The largest is harder to see entire. From the window it looks rather like a sprig laid out to be pressed, every division displayed beside the one before. Seen from the end it consists of several sprigs at different heights. There are reaches of quiet one above another there, with very little clearance between. A leaf has followed each reach; its divisions are spread in the plane in which it could divide. The result is handsome, though it makes a poor arrangement for catching light. Some of the lower blades hardly see the window. They have as good a color as the blades above them.

The two others could be taken for a pair, especially from the bank of glass, where their dots fall in line. Close to, one is all edge and the other all little flat faces. The quiet runs up steeply through the first. Its leaves stand like the leaves of a book opened only far enough to find one's place. At the second it is possible for a blade to turn a little as it rises. Here, exceptionally, you can see the underside of a leaf without having to imagine yourself underneath it. There is a small satisfaction in finding it green.

It would be a mistake to take the stillness of these things for the stillness of the duplicated stairs. The press could be broken up for scrap and both flights would remain, including the rather narrow top tread on each. The leaves are still attached to what made them. At the ends of all those rigid divisions something is being given away which, an instant before, was coming up through tin. The hum that makes so much of the country uninhabitable comes from the same supply. In the racket, at each of four old printer's marks, a little green body stands quite steady and conducts its living.
