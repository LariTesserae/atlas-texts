At the green edge, after the draw, there are little rolls of rubbish about the length of a willow leaf. Most of them come apart when the fans stand up. A few stay rolled. If one of these is turned over there is a clean oval on the stone, wet as a place where a thumb has been. Presently the roll gives a small hitch and the wet place disappears under it again.

The animal inside has a foot nearly as broad as its back. It carries a piece of dead fan, cut from between two pleats, with the fur left on the outside. On a young one the piece is narrow and folds right under, so that the little thing goes about in a muff with only its nose showing. An old one, spread out to feed, is broader than its covering. The exposed margin is a beautiful clear amber. Grains of dark grit can be seen going slowly along a channel in it, one after the other, until they are discharged at the tail. It takes most of the interval between two ticks of the same joint for a grain to make the passage.

What it eats is on the stone. Along the course of a trickle from the fans there is a film, hardly green enough to see except where something has written in it. The writing consists of loops, very close at the bottom and wider and less certain at the top, where the water gives out. An animal goes up the trickle while it lasts and comes down again on the narrow thread of wet it has left behind it. Sometimes two descending by the same thread meet one going up. They pass by lifting their coverings and putting one foot partly over the other. For a little while the three can be seen quite distinctly, without their clothes. They are not much alike. One is freckled all through with brown; another has a milky place in its back, in which half a dozen small feet are already making the movements of going along a stone.

The young ride for some time after they are able to get down. There is usually something to eat on the underside of the parent's piece of leaf. When they grow too heavy the leaf begins to drag, and the parent works its way in under a low willow branch and comes out bare on the other side. The leaf stays caught there with the young on it. A bird walking under the willows will sometimes tread on one of these nurseries. The young press themselves flat; when the foot lifts, a little amber animal goes with it. There are beds almost a day's walk apart where all the animals have the same dusting of brown freckles.

For the grown one the loss of the leaf is a considerable business. It must get another before the seams draw down. Good pieces are to be found under the old fans that no longer shut. The bird has done some of the cutting there. The animal finds a triangular hole, puts its head through, and goes forward until the thin stuff tears straight along the pleat on either side of its neck. Then it draws back with the flap over its shoulders. For several hours it wears the new piece attached to the living plant. It feeds as far as it can reach in a rather exact semicircle. By the time it has cleaned the stone, the torn edges have dried and curled inward, and the last few threads let go almost at a touch.

There are places where the film grows a long way from the fans. Water travels under a skin of stone and shows itself far out in the basin as a stain the size of a saucer, with nothing apparently coming to it or going away. A bird leaves an animal there now and then. It feeds well enough. But there are no leaves to cut, and in time, when the old piece wears through, it has to do with what comes sliding by. There are some dressed in willow down and some in the silver skins of grazers, laid on one over the other like scales. At the lip of the shelf there is one with a roof made almost entirely of seeds. A seed has taken hold in the grit beside it. When the animal moves off in search of the thread of wet that brought it here, the seed coat slips off the white hook and goes along on its back.
