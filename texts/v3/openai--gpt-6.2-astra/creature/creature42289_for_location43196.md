When the big dish empties there are things left hanging on its inside. At first they look like the shreds of an old stocking. They are nearly the colour of the stone and the water drains out of them in long pleats. A pleat will shorten, though, and take up the weight of the wet stuff below it. Presently the whole lot has drawn itself up into a pocket no bigger than the pad of a thumb. There may be eighty of these pockets along one terrace and none at all on the next.

In still water one would hardly think them the same creatures. Each puts out two arms, and on the inside of each arm is a fringe fine enough to spoil the reflection of a cloud without otherwise being visible. They feed on what comes down with the seep. A speck that has taken ten minutes to drift the length of a dish makes a quick sideways journey along a fringe and disappears at the fork. Sometimes it comes out again. A grain of white sand will go the rounds of quite a company in this way before somebody lets it fall.

There is a little water held in the pocket when the dish is dry. In it, particularly in the spring, one can see the young. They are a deep bottle green, the only strong colour here apart from the ferns; seen through the skin of the parent they look like the heads of pins stuck into a piece of chamois. They have no fringes yet. What they have is one good foot apiece. A young one set on a saucer will go round the rim all night, feeling ahead of it with the folded bump which is to be its arms. In the morning there will be a clean ring in the film on the china. This was how the woman who used to read the gauge discovered what to feed them. She had been bringing up a flask of perfectly clear bath water every day, taking considerable trouble over the temperature, when all they wanted was the dirty saucer.

She called them hems. The name has stuck better than hers. There are the long hems in the boat dish and the very short thick ones under the stair, where the water runs hard in both directions. For years it was supposed that the hems stayed put and the stone grew up around them. But the brass peg has given a fixed point from which to watch. A hem moves. It takes about a summer to get the width of the peg, and it travels with its arms spread whenever there is water, feeding as it goes. Behind it is a terrace with a minute scallop in the edge, made by the feet of all those creatures holding on abreast. The lines go all the way down into the white bottom of the dish. Some of what looks so remarkably like workmanship is the track of a meal that has been going on longer than there have been stairs.

There is one time when the young leave the pockets in numbers. It has nothing to do with the hour of the day, though it is a fine thing to see in the apricot light. The big bubble comes up and for once stays up. Pebbles cease running in the gutter. In that silence the little green things come out, hundreds to a pocket, and stand on the tips of their feet. The fall of the bubble takes them. Most are carried no farther than the next dish. A good many go down the slots. A few come in on the long low wash that reaches the stair.

This is what the crab is waiting for on the landing. It need not stir from its pink shell. It holds the small claw open on the upstream side, and after the wash has gone by it has something green to eat. Higher up, on the riser below the shoes, the young that have escaped it collect in the hollow backs of the carved letters of a name. There is just enough water caught in an R to keep twenty or thirty alive till the next wave. If you sit on the warm step and lean down when the water draws away, you can see them going round the loop, one behind another, and one sometimes striking out along the leg of the letter and coming, with its whole soft length extended, to the end of the world it knows.
