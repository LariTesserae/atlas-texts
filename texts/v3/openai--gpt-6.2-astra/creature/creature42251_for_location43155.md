For a while you suppose the bird has come in to sleep. It makes itself narrow coming past your face, one wing drawn so close that you can see the little hollow under its shoulder. Then there is a sound like a person parting their lips to speak. There are young ones further in. What the bird has brought them is a single pale fly, almost all wing; it takes longer to get it into the open mouth than it can have taken to catch it.

The young have disproportionately large feet. Even the smallest can stand, though when it has stood a while it begins to incline gently forward until its breast comes to rest on the stone. There is no nest to speak of. They have a saucer of fine dark stuff against the back of the shelter, with three or four of the round plants' outer leaves in it, dry and curled like parings from a hoof. You would have said nothing took those leaves. They overlap so neatly that there seems to be nowhere to begin.

It is the other grown bird that brings them. You see it the next morning well up the wall, working its bill under the bottom course of leaves on a plant. It has a white toe on one foot; otherwise you would never know it from the first. It pulls backwards with its whole weight. When the leaf gives, the bird sits down quite hard in the mud. It stays there a little, holding the leaf. Out in the river at just its height, there is a thin succession of notes which might perfectly well be the cry of a bird being laughed at by its mate.

They are not particularly good climbers. They go up by a long slant where the rock has split into little steps, making considerable journeys sideways to gain the height of your hand. Coming down they open their wings. A leaf carried in the bill makes a bird slew to that side all the way down; the white-toed one lands well short of the roofs and walks the rest. There is a grub in the curl of the leaf. The grown bird gets the grub out for itself before taking the leaf indoors.

By the end of the day the smallest of the young is missing. The others have moved out to the very edge of the slab, where the light shows the pale rims of their mouths, worn quite clean of down. From the upper air comes a small insistent piping. Both old birds continue to feed the two at the edge. It seems an awful place in which to have to find a third.

In fact they know where it is. The white-toed bird goes up the bed of the ford, past the place where you hear the gravel start, to a second roof not twenty paces off. There the lost one sits in a wedge of sun, blinking. It has got there on its feet. In the quiet under the first roof you had heard it setting out and taken the sound for the bird coming home.

Once you have seen this you begin to notice the paths. There is a faint polish on the low places between the stones, nowhere wider than two fingers. It goes from roof to roof and forks at the last one, one branch running to the pond, the other up the long slant to the beds of mud. In one place a lip of limestone too high for a young bird interrupts the path. Beside it lies a fallen piece of roof with a groove worn diagonally across its broken face. Generations of birds have gone the long way round by that piece of stone. If the roofs were ever houses for anything else, it was a long time ago.

Toward evening the smallest one comes back. It halts in the open, and for the first time you see the short black quills of its tail spread out like the wet hairs of a brush. It opens its mouth. High up, the current closes over a little fretful sound. The young bird goes on standing there, apparently listening to itself at that great distance. Then the big pebble turns; in the space after the knock you hear two voices very clearly, close together, pitched a little apart.

The little one puts its head down and runs. Its brothers have left just room for it under the lip of the slab. For a moment there are six feet in a row in the grey dust. Then they all shuffle backwards into the warm place they have made.
