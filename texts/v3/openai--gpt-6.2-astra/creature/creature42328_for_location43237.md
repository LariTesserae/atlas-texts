The first one you are likely to see is in the lower gate socket. It has got itself crosswise in the square hole, about half an inch in from daylight. What shows is a dun-coloured edge, like the edge of a scrap of soaked leather, with a glass bead at either end. The beads turn independently. When the wrinkle of water goes in, the leather lifts in the middle. When it comes out, the leather lies flat. It does this four or five times before you make out the feet underneath.

There are a good many of them in the hollow. In the cooler slots they grow to the size of the bowl of a teaspoon. The back is in one piece, soft at the rim, with a shallow groove running fore and aft; the underside is complicated, and pink where you would expect it to be white. There are three pairs of walking feet, each ending in something like a split thumbnail. Behind these are a number of fine paddles which can be worked with the animal standing quite still. It stands on the upstream side of a saddle and paddles a little of the current up under its back. Small edible matters catch there in a comb of hairs. Every so often a foot gives the comb a deliberate wipe and folds the proceeds into a mouth too far underneath to see.

A newly exposed saddle may have one on either face, their backs almost touching over the crest. Both are right about which way is upstream. The one on the pump-house side has its feet in the salt run; the other, a little higher, is working the thin return of fresh. When the run gives out they do not immediately leave. They go on paddling for a while in the old direction, and this makes two patches of faintly ribbed water on an otherwise quite still ledge. Then one will put a foot over the saddle and feel the other. There follows a short, civil exchange of places. Neither turns round. They can walk backwards as readily as forwards and seem to prefer it when the way is narrow.

The chain is a particularly good place to watch the small ones. They fit in the slack between iron and stone, where the grown ones cannot follow, and come out when the first beads of water stand in the link eyes. At that age the backs are clear. There is a greenish speck inside each, shifting a little as the feet take hold, and a pair of eyes disproportionately far apart. Six or seven will work the circumference of one eye. Sometimes the current plucks one off and takes it twice round the little whirlpool before it gets a foot down again. It resumes at the new place without troubling to recover the old one.

The larger ones have lodgings of their own. A black stone which turns with that audible click may have a chamber underneath it, scarcely deeper than the thickness of the tenant. Seen from above, the tenant is a poor match for the stone: buff with fine rusty freckles, quite conspicuous on black. It is the *underside* of the stone it matches, down to the pale crescent at the rim where the stone rocks in its bed. If the stone has shifted too far, the animal spends some time towards evening levering out grains of shale and carrying them, one to a journey, to the lip of the next slot. The split tips of the feet are good for this. By low water the door will usually sit quite snug again.

At full tide, for those ten minutes when there appears to be nothing but the doubled pump house, they swim. They let go almost together. The paddles open out beyond the edges of the back, surprisingly long, and the whole company rises a few inches off the shale. They hang there level, freckled side up, with their walking feet tucked away. From the track you can see them best in the reflection of the bricks: little pale ovals holding steady in the red. Now and then two touch rims and coast a little distance together. One or two roll right over, showing a brief pink flash. It is the only time there is room for that.

Long before a blade has actually broken surface, the ovals begin to sink. Most come down near their own stones, though not all. There is some walking to be done along the saddles, some waiting in blind slots for a lift of water sufficient to get over the end. The one at the gate has farther to go than most. If you stay until the dial has come well down, you can see it arrive along the wet seam below the lip. It climbs the last inch sideways, tries the upper socket with one foot, and goes on to the lower. A moment later there are the two beads of glass again, looking out along the empty track.
