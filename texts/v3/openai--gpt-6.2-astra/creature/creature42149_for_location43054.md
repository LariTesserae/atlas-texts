The animal the cable men called a hem is easiest to see where one has died. The skin on the back comes loose in a single sheet. For a day or two it stands up off the body, blowing first one way and then the other, like a pocket turned inside out. Underneath is a strip of very ordinary pink flesh. It is a surprise to see so much meat where, on the previous crossing, one would have sworn there was nothing but the joint between two stones.

A grown hem is about the length of a man's forearm and rather wider at one end than the other. It lies across the set of the water, generally a little way to one side of a feeding seam. One long edge is tucked into a crack. The rest lies flush with the bottom. Small grains of the local stone stick to its back and presently sink into the skin, so that a hem which has grown across a band of flint has a black stripe in exactly the right place. If it moves, it takes the stripe with it. A narrow black mark in the limestone with neither end accounted for is worth watching.

There are no eyes. Under the free edge is a row of little apricot-coloured fans. On one stroke the animal shuts down tight. On the other the edge lifts and the fans open, each one touching its neighbours, and for two or three seconds there is a fine low fence across the stone. What the fence catches is mostly the flour dislodged by the blunt-headed fish. A fish can be seen at one end of a seam and, considerably later, its meal being eaten for the second time at the other. Each fan, as it fills, folds inwards and is drawn through a pair of lips. They are cleaned in no particular order. A well-fed hem goes on putting them out with several missing, like a person who has come to the window in the middle of dressing.

For a long time the broad end was taken to be the head. It is the nursery. If the edge there is lifted very gently one can see the young lying crosswise in shallow pockets, the smallest quite white, the larger ones already peppered with grit. They put their own fans out from under the mother's edge. A large hem with a full complement of young has a curious pink fringe beyond the apricot one. Now and then a little fan comes out during the shut stroke, gets folded flat the wrong way, and is taken in again in haste.

The young leave before their backs have properly hardened. This accounts for most of the travelling a hem does. It has two pads underneath, one at either end, which it can let go separately. It lets the water swing it about the pad that is still fast, puts the loose end down, and waits. By changing ends at the right moment it can get some distance across a pass without once being altogether off the bottom. The resulting course is a succession of rather handsome semicircles. There is great inequality of progress. One little animal, identified by a fleck of green paint it had picked up at a shed, crossed six metres of open gravel between breakfast and the afternoon dive. Its fellow, which had started level with it, spent the same time going round a bolt.

What keeps the adults in place is not so much the strength of the pads as their good judgement in the matter of letting go. They do not, for instance, attend to the double clap. A clap may come when the water in their particular pass is still going hard in either direction. What they attend to is the movement of a few grains on their own backs. The grains are loose in little pits, roofed over with clear skin. One can turn a hem over in perfectly quiet water, rock it in the hand, and see a wash of apricot run along the belly as the grains roll. On the pass this gives it notice of the easing of the water immediately against its skin, which is all the notice it requires. A diver hears the whole range; the hem has news of a space about as thick as a visiting card.

There are hems on the concrete footings of the tide works now. Their backs contain sand, brick dust and small bluish pieces of aggregate which the cable men brought out from shore in sacks. One lives on the iron sill of a flooded shed and is a good match for it except at the nursery end, which projects past the iron and shows up square and rusty against the sea. The working sheds are less well liked. The suction at a bilge outlet is slight, but it persists through the turn of the stroke. The fans there never get a clean interval in which to come in, and after a few days the animal swings itself away.

At the edge of the passes the hems stop almost as sharply as the white gravel. There is nothing to prevent one going down. The same limestone continues under it, and at intervals there are seams with plenty of flour. But on the steep face a young hem that misses with its forward pad swings down instead of sideways. At the next failure it swings farther down. Those seen below the lip have all been small ones, a little too far apart for one to be looking at the same lot twice. Their backs are still glaringly white when they pass out of reach of the light.

Up on the saddle by the loose slab there is a hem that has moved very little in eleven years. It was already large when the first photographs were taken. A chip of flint shaped like a boot lies just beyond its free edge and makes it easy to find the place again. In a still photograph there is the boot, a crease in the stone, and nothing much else. It is necessary to stay for the turn of the water. Then the crease opens; along a length of apparently solid mountain a hundred little hands are put out for what is going by.
