One of the niches has a warm place at the back, a little way up from the seat. One supposes that the heat comes through from a spring. But it has a definite lower edge. There is a cool strip where a belt would go, and below that two little patches of quite fierce heat, as of the brass buttons on the back of an old-fashioned coat. The stone of the seat itself is cool. On the other side of the horseshoe, in a shallow room under the plum trees, there is a seat so warm that one gets up from it almost at once. The back of that one is cool.

It is best not to go straight across to look. Go by the long flight that has the wide landing halfway down. About a third of the way down, one knee begins to trouble you. It is a very particular complaint, a feeling as of a pea caught under the kneecap. It passes at the landing. For a few yards beyond the landing you have the most agreeable sensation of having got your breath back, even if you came down very slowly and had no need to get it back. Then that too passes, and the rest of the way you have your own legs.

There was a man who made this walk every afternoon with a coat on in all that heat. One can say *was* on the stair and *is* at the far seat. In the intervening districts there is very little use for either word. His weight goes down by the shorter route, through a series of squares where it is responsible for a slight dishing of the pavement. His warmth goes round by the niches. The sound of the pea knocking in his knee has got into one of the lesser falls; a person with an ear laid to the pink lip of the marsh can hear it as plainly as a spoon in a cup. All come together in the room under the plum trees, but not in the same part of the room. It would be quite possible to shut the door on him. One could not shut him in.

He is by no means the only one. At a corner in the eastern quarter there is a patch of water that a small child dislikes stepping into. It looks precisely like the rest of the street. Walk through it in either direction and for several steps afterwards you feel the grip of four very small fingers round two fingers of your hand. There is no drag on the arm. The drag is farther down, at an arch so low that a grown person has to stoop. There the child has found something worth stopping for. In a little round hole in the stone a yellow plum goes round and round, showing first the dimple and then the smooth cheek and then the dimple. All the impatience is in the arch and all the delight is in the hole. On the sill of a house below, the plum lies split in two. No one walking these streets has yet found the taste of it. That is one thing the child has managed to keep.

These people have not died. The mistake is to think of the city as keeping impressions of them, like the bed that keeps for a minute the hollow of a sleeper. A hollow in a bed does not change its mind. If, at the low arch, you put a finger in the hole and stop the plum, the little hand opens. Across the court a leafy parapet trembles along six feet of its length. Something has gone over there to get a better look at you. Take your finger out, and after a while the game begins again. The water makes no record of the interruption. The child does.

There is a great deal of visiting. It accounts for some of the difficulty in distinguishing one inhabitant from another. The old man in the coat goes to see a woman whose laugh occupies much of a colonnade in the green quarter. On the way he lends her the use of his eyes. For this reason there is one place on the walk where the tips of the far mountains come suddenly into wonderful clear view, though one is facing a wall. She has always liked that view. What he gets in return is harder to determine, unless it is the easy knee on the last part of the descent. They have been making this arrangement so long that it may never occur to either of them that the knee could be painful all the way down.

In the older wards such exchanges are nearly complete. There are streets of people who have no more separate existence than the members of a family singing a song they all know, each leaving out the notes that lie awkwardly in the voice, each quite certain what is being sung. A whole square goes to sleep in the afternoon. Its light stays bright, the falls keep up their bellow, and the fern shadows on the pavement do not blur; but if you have ever sat alone in a roomful of sleeping people you will know at once why you take such care putting down your feet. In one doorway a sleeper is out of sorts with the others. There is a hard little wakeful place there no larger than a walnut. People on their way through give the doorway a wide berth. Even the water divides at the threshold and slips out along the jambs.

Not everyone cares for this manner of living. Behind the reed marsh there is a girl who has been trying for a long time to whistle. She wants the breath and the lips and the hearing of it all in one place. Usually what happens is a small depression in the water among the reeds, a pursing of the translucent ceiling two streets below, and somewhere in the great interior tumult a thread of very pure sound. It is a satisfactory whistle to everyone but the girl. She has drawn the three places closer by degrees. Already the ceiling puckers directly under the marsh, and if you stand ankle-deep in the first street on a hot day there sometimes comes, from behind the last rank of houses, a note so near and distinct that you turn your head.

That quick turning of the head is useful to her. For an instant she has the use of two ears set at a known distance apart, and can tell how much nearer the sound has to come. There follows a thoughtful pause. Then the reeds incline a little toward you, though the wind up there blows always toward the gulf, and she tries again.
