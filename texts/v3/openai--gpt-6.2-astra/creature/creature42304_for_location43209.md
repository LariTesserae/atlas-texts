The gray animal at the dish is generally taken to be alone. It is difficult to get one's eye low enough to see the one on the other side. Not the far lip of the dish, which may be only a handbreadth away, but the other face of the country. There, on the dry ceiling, a second gray animal stands with its feet toward the feet of the first. If the upper one moves round the rim in search of a fresh place to feed, the lower goes round with it. There is the thickness of a good book between them. Neither has ever seen the other.

The lower one is much the handsomer of the two, though hardly any light falls on it. The six feet spread into thin amber soles through which the minute irregularities of the ceiling can be seen. Its back, which hangs toward the depths, is covered with a delicate white pile. Drops coming through the field collect at the ends of the hairs. When the wind in the supports slackens and the great note of the common goes a little flat, the animal combs itself. It uses its rearmost feet, passing each in turn the whole length of its back and drawing the wet sole forward to its mouth. This is how it drinks. In a strong wind it needs all six feet to hold on by, and one may see the little creature going thirsty in a coat of shining water.

There used to be some doubt as to which of the two followed the other. Seen from above, the matter looks clear enough. The grazer puts a foot down; the skin dents; what could be easier for anything underneath than to feel the dent? But if one finds a dish near enough to the fringe to examine both faces at once, it is quite often the amber foot that moves first. It takes hold and draws itself up hard against the ceiling. Above, among the pale pits, a seventh pit comes slowly into being. The grazer feels about for it with the tip of a leg. For a moment the two stand on the same six spots again. Then another foot lets go. They go very slowly in this fashion, sometimes at the preference of the one that wants black leaf, sometimes at that of the one that wants a good place for the water to come through.

At the end of a dry summer the lip of a dish curls up. The water leaf shrinks to a black sixpence, hard enough to ring under a tap of the nail. The upper animal will stand leaning over it for days after there is anything to be got by leaning. At last it draws in its head. A little buff case, till then indistinguishable from the rest of its belly, parts along the middle, and out come the young. There may be four, or there may be thirty. They are the color of the ground and have no legs to speak of. Each has instead a single amber pad, rather too large for it, which it sets down ahead of itself and painfully overtakes. All day they travel the width of a thumb. By morning the dew has taken them.

This accounts for there being no paths. The young go wherever a bead goes. Some get no farther than the red hairs in a seam; some are carried down three commons in an afternoon and come to rest on a white knee in a climate quite unlike the one they left. A bead on the verge of running off a ceiling will sometimes contain two or three, packed nose to tail against the bright curve. When it falls, they turn over in it. It makes no difference to them yet which way up they arrive.

It is the first dry lodging that settles the matter. In the light, the amber pad divides and dwindles to the six blunt feet of a grazer. In the dark it divides and spreads. The white pile comes quite late. A newly settled underside animal is nearly transparent, and the best way to find one is to look for a drop which, instead of running down the slope to join its fellows, goes in short deliberate stages across it.

Many dishes have only an upper tenant; many more, probably, have only a lower. The two are not indispensable to one another. A solitary grazer eats well enough, and the water comes through the field whether anybody treads on it or not. What is curious is the difference in the way a solitary animal stands. It keeps making small, unnecessary changes in its footing. Even in the act of grazing it will lift one foot clear and hold it so, sometimes for the better part of a minute. Under an occupied dish the same gesture has a use. The lower animal puts its sole against the place where the weight has been taken off, and the upper lets its foot down, very lightly at first, as one tries a stitch before trusting it. On a still evening this can go on long after both have fed.

There is a dish on the oldest knee which has narrowed with age to little more than a wet crease. Its two inhabitants have come by degrees so close to the fold that there is scarcely any thickness left between them. In the low sun the amber soles show through. From a little distance one sees what looks like a single gray creature standing in six spots of honey-colored light. It takes a step, and for a moment leaves one of the lights behind.
