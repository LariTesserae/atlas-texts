The cricket on the ceiling looks rather broad until it lifts a leg. Most of its breadth is shadow. Its body is thin enough for the white of the line to show through it, and when it turns across the line it seems for a moment to have been neatly cut in two. It calls in short bouts, backing a little between them. Near the opening the sound it gets is hardly worth climbing up for. Farther in, a single stroke of its wings makes the water in the palm-sized basin gather to one side and come back. The swimmer, encountering a little more rim than it is accustomed to, goes up it, and is left there when the water withdraws, a fat comma on the wet stone. It feels about with the fine hairs on its belly until it finds the water again.

There is something living on the white line besides the cricket. It is easiest to see just after the cloud has gone, when the ceiling is black on either side and the line is a little road in daylight. There are specks traveling along it against the tilt of the rock. At a rough place several pile up, quite visibly, before the first one gets across. They are mites, each carrying on its back a grain nearly as big as itself. The grains are not all white. One is the purple of a bird's foot; under a good lens it would prove to be a scale from between two toes. Another is a transparent cup with a few short hairs still standing round the edge. This was the socket of a feather. They have come quite a way with these things. There is very little that can fall upward in the hollow.

Halfway to the back there is a place where the line divides round a chip no bigger than a grain of rye. This is where the mites put down what they have brought. One can see a mite go in heavily at one end of the chip and come out briskly at the other. Behind it there is just room for a bed of soft rubbish and the clear eggs laid in it, each with two red points already quite distinct inside. The ceiling cricket braces one of its hind feet against the chip to play. At the first note all the mites out on the line stop. At the second they go on again. Those two things, the stoppage and the going on, are so exact that the line appears to have slipped and caught.

The birds pay very little attention to any of this. One does, a young bird that has only lately got rid of the last white threads of down about its ears. It sleeps on the highest shelf, on its side for lack of headroom, with one eye looking along the ceiling. All afternoon, at intervals, this eye opens on the specks going by. Once the bird takes one, or tries to. Its bill makes a small hard sound and it draws back holding a fleck of white stone. For some time it keeps opening and shutting its bill on the fleck, as a person will work a seed out of a back tooth. Finally the fleck drops among the birds below. Several wake up. Nobody gets up.

It is from this shelf that a bird must leave when there is no room for another pair of feet at the sill. The young one goes out backward, shoving with both feet, its tail for a moment spread flat against the white air. Until it lets go it has all the appearance of a bird trying very hard to get *into* the mountain. Then it is gone, and the silk sound runs uphill across the floor. A bird scarcely older than itself is already fitting a shoulder into the warm place.

Far below, the fallen wedge has a pocket of its own on the side that faces the cliff. What was once a hollow in the ceiling is down there a little upright trough, open at the top. A bird coming home from the falls sometimes lights on its lip to put its feathers in order before riding the air up. It can spend a long time there with its head screwed round under its wing. Under that wing, disturbed by the bill, a pale cricket will occasionally work its way out as far as the foot. For a minute it stands astride plum-colored skin and stone. Usually it goes back into the feathers. There is at least one, though, that has gone the other way. On still evenings, after the last bird has left the wedge and the blue over the water has thinned to nothing, there comes from somewhere quite low down in the snow the sound of a single small vessel being filled.
