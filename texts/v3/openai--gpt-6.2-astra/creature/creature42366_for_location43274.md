The white angle has a tenant already. On the side that casts the shade, just at ground level, there is a purple dash. At present the shade is wider than the thing that needs it, though if a grain were to fall from the top of the angle it would make a considerable difference to both. Under the dash a single fan has opened. It has two lobes, one lying under each arm of the angle. A seed that opens on level ground puts out a fan just as readily, but by the time the fan has made a roof for itself it is usually too late. Out on the plain one finds a great many little round wafers, white on both faces, with the brown remains of a seed pressed between.

The finest plants are in the low house with the courts. There is one in particular that shows in five places. It is possible to tell that the five are one: when a skin of salt lifts from the wall, one sees, for the few minutes before the skin comes altogether free, the colour of a damson running in a single sweep from the joint at the foot of the wall to a lintel on the second storey. On the other side of the lintel it is somebody else. The two ends lift at evening, approach within the breadth of the lintel, and hang there. Below them there is room for the grey animal to pass with its fringe quite spread.

It does not appear to see much. Coming to the edge of an arch it will put out rather more of its body than seems prudent, and wait until the forward part, bending under its own weight, finds the upright. Then the rest comes round. There are no separate marks of feet in the powder at the entrance, only two margins of a track, one neat as a mark ruled in flour, the other interrupted at fairly regular intervals. The interruption is due to a notch in the fringe. One can recognise its track a long way out on the plain by that small fault, even where the powder is so shallow that the white belly has left no mark.

In eating it is very particular. It passes the fringe along a lifted thread and takes whatever comes loose without pulling. The seeds lie for a moment in the notch, where a little fold of the white underside turns up to receive them. A seed that catches farther back may travel with it for a day or more. If the animal sleeps under an arch the seed stays there too, caught between two dark hairs. It is when the animal comes down and takes a corner on its side that the load is likely to be rubbed off. Several corners in the town have a line of young plants at exactly the same height. In the mornings these too short to have found anything to hold to stand out stiffly from the wall, each with its split husk still on the end, like a row of very small unlit lamps.

There is one other place where the animal goes. Beyond the last discernible track, the plain has a barely perceptible dip in it. The grey powder lies deeper there. If one waits until the animal has gone in and the powder has settled, nothing distinguishes the place; but while it is entering, the surface lifts over its back in a long low ridge, and presently, a little to one side, a second ridge lifts to meet it. This one is shorter. So far there is only the one set of tracks at the lip of the town.

One evening the shorter ridge follows the long one almost to the surface. The powder parts and a fringe appears, folded back, quite pale still, with no notch in it. For a while it lies across the old track. Then it draws in again. The larger animal has gone on down through the diminishing lintels to the court where the seeds are ripe; by the time it comes back, with a husk adhering to its side, the little line its companion made has filled.
