At the torn place there is a curl that does not belong to the leaf. It has the same wet white colour as the others and the same slight translucence at its thin end. The difference is on the inside of the turn. A shaving of leaf has threads in it, all broken off short by the stone. In this one the threads go round the turn and come back on themselves. There is a minute blue eye in each bend. These are not eyes for seeing. In the dark under the rind they are blue just the same.

It is the foot of an animal. The rest of it would be difficult to take up with the hands. A breadth of film goes down from the foot on the brine side of the leaf. It adheres rather better to the flesh than its own layers adhere to one another; someone stripping it off would get a little clear skin, then another little clear skin, and conclude that this was the lining of the leaf. Yet a long way from the tear the film has a free edge. It is scalloped there, and the scallops can fold shut. A grain of black grit which comes down on the edge is passed from fold to fold until it falls off at the lowest one. There is a clean fan on the stone beneath, in the midst of the soft grey deposit.

The blue eyes are knots of a stiffer substance. One can feel them with a nail. When fresh water comes down the face of the tear and wets the foot, the substance swells, each knot by a very little, and the sum of these swellings is enough to unroll the curl. Its thin end lies out then on the stone, clear of the white shavings. In brine it winds up again. The upright reach of the stone, which looks dry, is continually washed in this way. A tongue put to it would find it salt at one hour and almost sweet at the next.

What the foot is feeling for is a place to put the young. They are made far back in the film. For much of their lives there is nothing to distinguish them from flaws in it, small cloudy places at which the layers might be expected to part. As a cloudy place grows it works its way between the layers towards the foot. There is room for only one at a time in the curl. Here it takes on weight. For the first time it has two distinct faces, one sleek enough for water to run off it, the other covered in a close white pile.

A washing of fresh water lays it down, pile first. There may be many thousands of these washings before one leaves a young animal behind. On the smooth stone the foot picks it up again as neatly as it set it down. But if the pile engages in a pit, even the small pit left by the loss of a single grain, it holds. At the return of the brine the foot rolls slowly away from it. What is left looks like a wet scale of skin on the rock.

There is food on the stone. A little of the juice that smells of pear comes down with the fresh water, and where the leaf rubs there is always some of the soft inside of the rind. Very little of either goes to waste. The young animal takes these things up all over its sleek face. The pile beneath it grows sideways into the grain of the rock. By the time the scale is as broad as a thumbprint it would be easier to move the stone than to dislodge it whole.

The great extent of the adult is mostly empty and costs it little. Between two pools of its living substance there may be a metre of film so thin that the red of the leaf flesh shows through unaltered. A gulp in the leaf flattens one pool and shifts a little of what was in it along to the next. In this way a meal taken at the torn edge travels out into the dark. It can be years on the way. A furthest scallop may still be putting on new clear skin from the last abundance of pear juice at a wound which has long since drawn shut.

Nothing about this arrangement requires the animal to come up into the open. It is the stone that sometimes brings it there. A shelf rising under an old split will lift out a whole company of the scales, some no bigger than the pits in which they caught, others run together edge to edge like a badly laid pavement. The brine drains from them. Dew does not suit them. Their sleek faces thicken to a horn, grey at first and eventually of so dark a blue that they pass for patches of bare stone. Under the horn the pile stays white and wet. It has the deep minute wetness of a tight knot in a string whose two ends have long been dry.

On the lip that has caught the leaf there are six such patches above the white curl. The highest is also the smallest. A drop sliding down the rock divides on its upper edge, runs round it on either side and joins below, leaving the face dry. Farther down, where the two little runs of water meet the curl, the blue knots are beginning to swell. The end with its cloudy passenger has lifted clear of the shavings. There is a pit just within reach of it. At the rate at which the end is unbending it ought to touch the pit some time after the next drop.
