A child from the lowest quarter can generally reach the red cup with a pear hook by the age of seven. Knowing this makes the instruction to leave it alone considerably harder to follow. It is usual to ask the well keeper what became of the child who dropped it. She married into one of the damp-wall families, the keeper says, and had the making of the wool screens in the upper garden for many years. If it is her own grandmother who is asking, as sometimes happens towards the end of a life, the keeper tells it just the same, only she brings out the stool.

The people are not themselves preserved. Their hair comes out in the comb and the roots of their teeth give them trouble. A woman who could jump the cut where it narrows above the last bridge will come in time to need two tries to get out of bed. A good deal of work goes on in the houses to accommodate these changes. There are pegs at three or four heights beside a door, and often a short piece of banister on a perfectly level wall, where somebody once had to get from the kitchen to the privy. The next occupants may have no notion whose hand it was for. They hang the onion bag on it. In a house that has been in use a long time there is scarcely a stretch of wall a yard wide that has not been made useful to somebody at some particular height.

They have fine ears. A person working with both hands in a fleece will know by the sound of a cart which of the two brothers from the dye house is coming in. One wets the bindings of his wheels before coming over the grit; the other does not. It is not that they are especially quiet people. At supper in the gardens the noise is considerable, since everybody has to talk past the person next to them in order to be heard by the person they want. But they dislike a noise that goes on after its cause ought to have been attended to. A shutter knocking will bring a neighbour out in a shirt with a loop of cord between his teeth. If he finds someone there ahead of him he gives them the cord and goes back in.

In the grey spaces between quarters a curious courtesy prevails. Though you may have been watching a person approach for an hour, it is bad manners to look up much before you would look up on meeting them in a passage. There is no other way to be alone on the shelf. Two sisters who have fallen out may consequently have a tolerable walk home from market, provided they start at opposite ends of the road. On the other hand, if you should sit down rather abruptly out there, you will see people turn towards you from three roads at once. You have a long time to think of what to say to them when they come.

There are households that make their living taking things away. A young couple who have been given a room may find it occupied by a loom bed let into the floor, the curb of an old pickle tank and a handsome stone press for a fruit nobody grows any longer. They send for a taker-out. She comes with her wedges rolled in a skin and spends the first morning finding out which of these things the couple really mind. Often it is only the curb. The place for a loom makes a good bed for a baby, below the draught from the door, and it costs nothing to leave the press till spring. What she does take she carries to the back of the town. From the rim the heaps there look like part of the white circuit. Up close they consist of a great many quite intimate things: a basin with a hollow for a bad elbow, a doorstep cut obliquely for a house that used to stand at an angle to the sun. Wrens nest deep in the heaps. On a cold day you can put your face to the mouth of an abandoned oven and hear the young ones going like the little rattle of seeds in a pod.

Perhaps the happiest people there, for an afternoon at any rate, are the ones who have just finished something. A mason will take off her shoes and go down her new stairs in her stockings, very slowly. She is feeling for the place where the chisel strokes cease to run together underfoot. If there is such a place she marks it with spit and goes back for her tools. If there is not, she sits on the bottom step and has her dinner. Before long somebody comes down carrying a bowl in either hand, thinking about something else altogether, and passes her without having to look where he puts his feet. That is the part she likes to stay for.
