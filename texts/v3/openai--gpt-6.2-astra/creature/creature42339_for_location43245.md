There is something in the middle patch of moss which, when the moss is nearly dry, looks like a shaving off the felt. It is the colour of the felt and has the same habit of curling at one end. If a bit of the green is taken away and put in water under a glass, the shaving can be seen to have a succession of rather handsome transverse folds. For some time nothing happens to it except that the folds get farther apart. Then one end opens into a mouth, the other puts out two toes, and the piece of felt goes over the edge of a leaf.

It is a rotifer. There are a number of them in the felt, though seldom more than two or three out on the moss where there is room to see what they do. One puts its toes down, extends itself until its head is well past the next leaf, and puts its head down there. The toes come up close behind the head. In this manner it can get a long way through what appears, from the grating, to be a single green blade with a drop on it. There are leaves behind leaves in the blade, and between two of them a little brown store of things that have washed in and failed to wash out. It is generally here that the animal stops.

With its toes attached and its body drawn up rather short, it unfolds from its head two discs edged with hairs. There is an appearance of very rapid turning, first one way and then, if the light changes, the other. A speck lying well off to one side gives a jump, goes round the rim of a disc and disappears between the two. Farther down inside the animal a pair of hard little jaws keep working. The felt has a perpetual crop of minute life on its fibres; what the water loosens from them comes past those hairs. A rotifer can go on feeding for a good part of the interval between the darkening of the joint and the filling of the spanner dent without once lifting its foot.

It is not much disturbed by the fall on the other side of the wall. The glass is needed to see this, too. When the shiver comes, the rotifer, the leaf it is on and the crumb on the end of the leaf all go sideways together. The crumb continues on its curved way into the mouth. But let the tip of a loose fibre touch one of the discs and the whole forepart of the animal is gone in an instant. There remains a little pursed bag, fastened by its narrow end. After a while a blunt transparent knob comes out to try the water. The beautiful wheels are among the last things to reappear.

There are eggs farther back in the felt, oval and clear, caught so snugly between the fibres that the water which brings grit in past them scarcely stirs them. This is how there come to be several sizes of rotifer on one scrap, including a very small size with the whole equipment of the larger ones already in working order. How the first one came up is harder to say. There was a time last winter when the shelf was lying on trestles below, and the felt was in a fitter's pocket along with a carpenter's pencil and some string. There is also the cloud, which leaves quite a respectable line of dirt wherever it dries against a bolt.

Occasionally one gets as far as the dent. In the first rush it has no more say in the matter than a grain of zinc, but when the pool is cut off it gets its toes against the bottom and proceeds by its usual measured reaches. There is very little to eat on that new metal. It goes up a slope of the dent, trying the surface from time to time with its mouth, until it reaches the place where the rim of the water rests on the steel. Here there is a fine deposit, not yet visible as a mark to anyone looking down through the grating. The animal settles to it. Above its back there is at first the whole reflected flight of the iron stair; presently there are three steps, then a bright patch too narrow to hold the likeness of anything. Before that goes, it has drawn in its head and toes and shortened until the folds lie close, one against the next. When the dent is quite dry there is a little crescent of grey dust up one side of it. Most of the crescent is dust.
