The woman who keeps the drain this winter has a black crescent under the nail of her left thumb. It belongs to the thumb. It was there when she took the hand out of drawer nine, and after her first bath, when she had all the rest of herself quite clean, she went to the hospital to ask about it. The attendant looked up nine. A former occupant had caught the thumb in a roof bearing. The dark stuff was the cement of a very old repair.

She likes it now. When she has her hand spread on the plate at the back of the exchange she can see, even in poor light, which side of the little inspection hole her thumb is on. There is a right and a wrong place to put it. She used to have to lean down to look.

There are seven of them this season. In their outdoor things it is fairly easy to tell them apart. One has cut his coat short to clear the seat of the tram; one, who is seldom out for more than a minute at a time, goes about in a bath wrap with a freight tally tucked into its belt. Out of their clothes the resemblance is quite close. They have broad, rather flat feet, excellent shoulders, small ears laid back against the head. The surface of the skin is a dull cream colour except over the joints, where the folds have a silvery lining. The hair on their heads is fine and scanty and dries almost as soon as they come out of the water. They have eyebrows. A good deal can be done with these. The man in the short coat can express an opinion about a reading without anyone at the other end of the calibration hall being the wiser.

They know one another, though, by things that come up the wire. The woman in nine has worked three winters with a person who, whatever hands he is wearing, holds a cup by its base and turns it a little way round before he drinks. She knew him this time before he had finished his tea. He hadn't recognised her. She had to remind him of an afternoon they had spent trying to get a wet mattress round the turn of the hospital stairs. Then he moved his chair over beside hers. They are on the best of terms, which in their case means that either can come into the other's house and sit for a considerable time without being offered anything to do.

The houses are allotted after the bodies. There is a plan at the terminus with a little brass slide for each address. Most newcomers put in for a view; by the second winter they have discovered how pleasant it is to live at the back, where a cup left on the floor stays warm and the porcelain does not flash white with every break in the cloud. Nine lives halfway between, on the street with the garnet. In the morning a red quadrilateral comes through her upper window and lies across the bed. She has moved the bed twice to keep it there as the season advances. It is one of the few things in the town which has to be moved by pushing.

They do a surprising amount of visiting for people who could call to each other from their doors. A visit requires a dry place on which to set the things one has brought. There are six privately held views about how best to sweep the grit off blue tile, and the seventh inhabitant has made a brush. He brings it round to the exchange wrapped in paper, waits until the woman has a moment free, then gets down on his knees to demonstrate. It is a very good brush indeed. The outer bristles are long enough to reach into the angles beside the pillars; the short stiff ones in the middle loosen the deposit between the tiles. She asks if she may keep it. He says that was the idea. They fold the paper carefully, since paper up here is worth having, and he stays to help her do the steps.

There is no one on the strength whose business is to look after the midge. When one comes in they get the broad shallow freight pan and leave a little water in it beyond the terminus, out of the way of the tram. This has never interested a midge in the least. It wants the drain, where it can stand with its forefeet on the top step and lower its head between the square pillars. Seen from inside the exchange, its face is astonishingly hairy. The woman in nine once spent most of her dinner hour on the landing looking down at it. For several days afterward, whenever she passed a dark window, she was conscious of how bare her own face was, the two tidy strips above the eyes and nothing for a long way on either side.

What she finds hardest about a changeover is the last walk back to the drawer. There is generally still some useful thing the body could do on the way. A catch wants lifting on the freight gate; the bathhouse door has swollen and will not stay on its prop. One likes to arrive at the terminus with the hands clean, not because anyone requires it but because it is disagreeable to think of a hand lying all summer with something caught between the fingers. Last time she stopped to free a wheel and had to go back to the exchange to wash. The station keeper, seeing her coming up the same street for the second time, put the kettle back on.

For now the drawers can wait. After a shower the whole shift generally ends up at the bath, even the freight clerk who has spent half the day dressed for it already. The woman in nine swims a length on her back with her reliable old thumb leading, turns short of the steps and lets the wash take her out again. From the quiet end she can hear the others discussing whose turn it is to close the door. Nobody does. A little cold air comes in over the sill and moves along the water, perceptible first at the ears and then at the wet tips of the shoulders. She lies still until it has gone by.
