The lizard stays where it is when someone comes in. There is barely room for the two of them. The newcomer has to ease one shoulder between the canes and bring its bundle in after it, holding the bundle high so as not to sweep the lizard off the silver stuff. Then it sits down. Its knees stand up on either side of its face. The lizard puts a forefoot on the nearest ankle. Evidently it knows how much more moving about there is likely to be.

Standing, this person would come about to your breastbone. Sitting, it is astonishing how little of it there is. Most of the height is leg, and most of the leg can be got out of the way at three very serviceable joints. The belly, on the other hand, is round and tender-looking and rises steeply with each breath. There is a place for it between the thighs. The skin is brown, with a fine bloom on it like the bloom on a plum, except on the palms and round the mouth, where the color is a clear warm yellow. Its ears are quite ordinary ears. It has a nick in one. Somehow that is the part you keep looking at. Something happened to it once.

It undoes the bundle. These are leaves, folded endwise while growing so that each has come away with a little stiff trough down the middle. There is a mouthful of water in the bottom leaf, which it drinks, tipping it with great care. Then it separates the others and lays them out according to length. While it is doing this a drop falls, quite close, with a sound like a palm brought down flat on a bare leg. Its ears do not prick. They couldn't. But one yellow thumb stays where it is on a leaf until the second sound comes.

The second sound doesn't come.

After a while it gathers up three of the leaves and goes out again. The lizard's forefoot is left hanging for a moment before it puts it down.

Out in the canes you could lose sight of a great many of these people. Their backs, when they stoop, are much the color and shape of the bare places on the rootstock, and they seldom need to stand up to see where they are going. Each foot has a long yielding sole that comes down at the front first and feels its way into the nap. What looked like an idle, almost dainty way of walking proves to be a way of walking very fast indeed. By the time the next drop falls the person is under it. There is a leaf there with a torn edge. The drop is running down a stem instead of going on its way from leaf to leaf. At the foot of the stem a shoot as fine as a hair has fallen over in the little wash of water and stuck to the ground.

It takes all three troughs to put this right. One goes in the fork of the stem; one reaches almost to the torn edge; the third, the shortest, is to carry the water out beyond it. There are no fastenings. Each leaf has just enough spring left in it to hold the others under a little pressure. It is pretty work, though not much to look at when done. The person squats back on its heels and waits. Presently there is the slap and, in good time, the tick. Farther off there is another tick, so faint you might have missed it altogether if someone, far off in that direction, had not given a little cough of satisfaction.

They have rather carrying voices. They don't use them much for finding one another; the sounds of the valley serve well enough for that. A person hearing a change in the fall of water can generally tell who has made it. There is one who always leaves rather too long a fall and one whose work makes almost no sound at all. The cough, it turns out, was a joke between two people who have known each other a long time. For the rest of the afternoon, whenever that particular drop falls, the worker with the nicked ear lets a little air out through its nose.

Toward evening more of them come down to the enclosures. One has a child riding snug in the hollow behind its knee, a position from which the child can see a good deal of the country upside down. It objects to being taken out. Set on its feet, it immediately lowers its head to the ground to see if things can be got back the way they were. There is food under the silver cover: little swellings that part from their threads at a touch and have the smell of the dry ground, only sweeter. They eat lying on their sides. Everybody seems to like the ones from the very edge best, though they are harder to get at, and for some time the only evidence of so many living people in the enclosure is the sight of the outermost canes bowing a little and springing back.

Nobody planted the whole valley. Nobody could keep all those acres poised at the point of opening. They attend to the stretch they can hear from the place where they lie down. Beyond that, water goes where it will, canes fall across other canes, a green knob opens or stays shut for reasons of its own. But this half mile has been lived along for a very long time. The shoulder gaps are not all of one width. In the oldest, the canes grew together long ago and somebody taught a new pair to bend a little farther round the circle. You can find the old entrance by laying your cheek against the join. It is smooth on both sides up to the height of a shoulder, and smooth a little lower down on one side only, where a hand coming home used to reach ahead in the dark.

The child has found the fallen shoot. It works a finger under the wet hair of it and lifts until it stands. Then it takes the finger away and the shoot lies down again. It does this a number of times. The others are beginning to fold themselves away for the night; the light has gone out of all but one seam in the salt. At last the child lies down too, on its face, with one yellow finger still under the shoot. In the morning there will be a bend in it a finger's breadth above the ground. It will be quite impossible to say, looking at it, that it did not grow that way.
