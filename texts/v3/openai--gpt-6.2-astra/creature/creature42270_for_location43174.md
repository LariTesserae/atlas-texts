The first one you notice is hanging from the underside of the bench. There is a little open seam in the wood, and along it, in a row, six brown toes. Then a pointed nose comes out so close to the toes that for a moment you have the animal assembled quite wrongly. It has doubled back on itself in the seam. It comes down the iron leg head first, taking quite a long time to arrive at its hindquarters, and sits on the wet concrete with its tail laid out behind it like a strap someone has unbuckled.

The operators call them hem mice, from an old habit of hanging their coats on the rail and finding, when they put them on again, a considerable weight in one corner. They aren't much like mice except about the face, which has that look of having been drawn out fine between finger and thumb. The ears are small enough to disappear altogether when laid down. The fur parts along either flank where the long guard hairs turn under the belly; a hem mouse just out of the water has a bright beaded line on each side, like the stitching on a good boot. On a dry floor it proceeds in a series of rather expensive-looking bounds. Under a spillway lip it travels almost as easily upside down as right way up. Those toes close of their own accord on anything narrower than a pencil.

It was generally thought that they came for the things the falling water leaves behind. Certainly a mouse will make a meal of the pale grubs exposed in a draining bed. It will roll one briskly on the concrete to get the grit off. But watch the one at the bench. It takes no notice of the water coming over the wall below. It goes up, along the square outer edge of the landing, until its forefeet are on one casting of iron and its hind feet on another. There it stays, flattened out in the rain. At the far end of the walkway, where it comes clear of the fog for a yard or two, there is another one lying in precisely that attitude.

When the load changes, they get up. The near one goes between the bars and is lost. The far one runs in plain sight all the way to the yellow iris trench. It gets there a good half minute before the first rush of water, in time to climb out on a bent stalk and wait. What comes up the trench ahead of the water is a scurry of little jointed things, some swimming, some running along the meniscus, one quite large black beetle rowing desperately round and round with a bubble fastened to its tail. The mouse lets the beetle go. It puts a foot down among the others and licks it clean, puts it down and licks again. By the time the stalk bows into the current the mouse is gone and the stalk springs up empty, making several small bows afterwards on its own account.

There may be hundreds of them. It is difficult to get even two people on the same shift to agree about this. A man working the length of a race will see a mouse at every third basket and suppose it has been keeping him company. Seen from the settling-house roof, the same walk sometimes disturbs a dozen: a little brown succession going to ground in front of his boots and coming out again behind him. They have regular crossings. At one expansion joint the concrete has been rubbed clean in a channel the width of two fingers, though nothing the men use ever passes through it. The joint used to lead straight into a bed of rushes. When the bed was taken out of service, the mice went on coming through the joint for a time and sitting at the end of it, high above a floor of cracked mud, washing their faces.

The young are brought up in the older walls. In winter, when a section is shut down for pointing, the masons quite often find a pocket behind a sound-looking brick, lined with the down from willow catkins. It comes out in one piece in the hand, a little slipper with a roof to it. There will be a dry chamber at the back and, near the mouth, a ledge of packed orange mud where the wet animal can sit before going in. Not every pocket has young in it. Some contain nothing but the hard wing cases of beetles, all bitten through on the left. In one there were three red washers from the float gauges and in another a strip of bus ticket so thoroughly combed apart that the blue figures had come away on separate threads.

The strangest sight is the moving of a litter. It happens when the upper beds take their first heavy load after a dry spell. A large female comes out carrying nothing. She goes a little way, turns, comes back. Presently a smaller nose appears against the fur at the root of her tail. Then a second mouse comes, holding to the first, and after a minute or more a third, in a dark plush coat still too big for it, with the pale soles of its hind feet showing at every step. Attached like this they can go through gravel that would hide any one of them and come out entire at the other side. At a gap the old one turns and gathers them against her for a moment. Then she goes over and they follow separately, each making the same small error of landing, for the far edge is lower than it looks.

By full dark the works bus has gone. The water stands level with the rim of the bed and there is no longer a reflection under the bench. If you stay very still on the landing you may feel a slight pull low down on your trouser leg. A mouse has come to use you as it would an alder stool or a man left standing there from yesterday. There is scarcely any weight to it. It gets as far as your knee, where the cloth is drawn tight and offers less purchase, and pauses to consider. Through the cloth you can feel its feet open and close. Somewhere below, too far down for you to feel it in the rail yet, a pump starts turning. The feet go still.
