A young wader is quite flat. Three of them will fit under a leaf no bigger than the foot of their mother, and there is room for her foot as well. When she bears down they feel the water go out past them in six little streams, one on either side of each body. They turn their heads into the streams and eat what comes off the hairs. The mother can be distinguished from a wader without young by her way of lifting a foot. She peels it up from the heel, very slowly, to leave a little water under the leaf. An unencumbered wader pulls its foot up all at once with a noise like a cork coming out.

For most of their first summer the young go about stuck to the undersides of her feet. They have a pair of suckers which will presently become the bare places on either side of the adult throat. Sometimes at the end of a step there is a momentary view of one: a cream-colored sole with a dark eye looking out of it. The little ones can let go when they please. In a drainage full of newly hatched fish they do, and the mother stands in the same place all day, taking such fish as happen to come within reach. Toward dusk she shifts her weight from foot to foot. There are nearly always three little pulls in answer. If one is missing she puts her chin down on the skin and makes a noise too low to hear, though the beads of water for several yards around draw themselves up very tall and fall over.

Their legs grow bent double, the shins lying in grooves along the thighs. A young one may be as long in the leg as a man is tall and still swim quite comfortably between two leaves. What brings it out at last is the thickening of the joints. It begins to catch on things. There are old leaves far down in the felt with a series of paired rents in them, at first small and well apart, then close together and very large, ending at a hole through which daylight comes pale green even in fine weather. At the top of such a hole stands a wader on its first morning, its legs still crossed from the strangeness of having space to put them in. The heads of the grown ones barely stir in a gale. The new ones sway in weather too slight to move the nap.

They have a trouble peculiar to this age. They can feel a difference between the pressures under their feet long before they know what size of difference to expect. At the evening joining, a yearling will sometimes step straight across the common water onto the farther bank. It takes no longer than stepping over a runnel. Then the water parts and there it is in the east, in snow up to its ankles, with the wader whose feeding ground this is walking slowly toward it. The older animal goes down on its knees at a little distance. After a time the yearling does the same. For as long as the snow lasts one can see the dark heads together there, very low, and now and then the long pink inside of a bill as the older one yawns. In the morning there is generally only one. It is a leisurely business, the getting of a feeding ground; it may take a dozen such mistakes before a young bird finds a stretch of chambers where nobody comes to meet it.

An old wader keeps to its own hollow even when the flaps have worn out and there is little to be had there. Its feet by then are a considerable part of the place. The rims have split into long soft fringes in which the six-legged animals take shelter when the water runs hard. A foot brought up after a week in one chamber will be green with leaves no larger than scales; the grazers hang on until the last of the water drips off them, then fall in a little shower. In summer there are often small birds standing under an old wader, waiting for it to take a step. They wait looking away from the foot. What they watch is its shadow on the nap, where the first lifting of the heel lets in a thin bright crescent of sky.

There is one hollow in the west whose wader no longer rises at all. The legs are folded away and most of the back is overgrown. Only the long head comes up, generally in the afternoon, to strip moths from the gum along a seam. In sleet it turns the bill sideways and the moths walk onto it for the sake of the shelter of the upper mandible. Some it eats. Some are still on it when the head goes down. Long after dark, if one lies with an ear against the skin in that hollow, one can hear, between the great strokes of the drainage, the faint irregular tapping of wings in a dry place.
