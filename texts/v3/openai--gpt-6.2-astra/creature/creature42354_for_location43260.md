For a little while after the buried stations have taken the load, the new station two streets inland receives parcels at its west door that ought to have gone to its east. The mistake is always in the same direction and amounts, at first, to the width of the old roof. A parcel travels the extra distance under the floor, cushioned in a loop of tow. As the roof goes cold against the cliff the loop draws in. By morning a parcel put in at the winding sheds will come up under the right door without having crossed its own track. The loop, now no bigger than a buttonhole, stays warm for a very long time.

It is in such allowances that the inhabitants are easiest to distinguish. Take a fitting out of a belt and another comes along to fill the interval. Take the second one out too, and the belt will put by a third a little short of the place where the first was taken. Presently there will be a blister in the selvage with two or three fittings in it, pads between them, their growing tips tucked into wells of oil. One could work there for years, drawing a particular gauge of cork plug at quite irregular intervals. There would nearly always be a plug. In the meantime the belt itself might have gone over the edge and its successor might run at right angles to it. It is no use looking in the material of the belt for what has come to know about the plugs.

Nor does the whole surface know. There are districts which, on being asked for the same plug twice, will send back the hole it is meant to stop, very neatly excised, on the assumption that it is the hole that is giving trouble. The cut edges are held together until an unpierced piece of pavement can be grown between them. Such a district will spend considerably more heat in closing a hole than another will spend in keeping it supplied with corks. Where the two abut there is sometimes a strip of pavement with a row of blind dimples in it. On one side of each dimple is a little cork tree. On the other the pavement is flawless. Neither practice has prevailed there in all the thickness of the plateau.

A better way to find one of the inhabitants is to wait for the interval after a great fold has passed. The shutters have come down; the thousands of temporary hollows in the floss are filling out again. Very softly, in the inspection pit, something taps. Two ordinary seed gauges a long way apart have begun to test themselves out of turn. Between one tap and the next the oval of the interchange yields along its short axis by the breadth of a fibre. In that slack a belt slips past another belt, a nuisance of long standing is cleared up, and for the next hundred circuits the parcels come through with their corners square. The taps cease. On a second occasion the same gauges may tap and nothing whatever be done about the belts. Something is attending elsewhere. There will be an exceptionally fine selvage that year, or a nursery bed which keeps an old variety of hinge in crop long after the last canopy of its gauge has gone down into the dark.

There are several of these attentions within reach of the pit. One is exceedingly local. It has for its province a turn in a muscle-root where a heavy reel, coming round, tends to lean outward. For this it grows a pad no larger than a lip. The pad meets the reel a moment before it leans. When the turn is straightened the lip appears at the next turn down the line; when the reels cease for a season it can be found, with some difficulty, on the underside of the bushes that bear them, sustaining the fruit. Another ranges over both interchanges and a great depth of the cliff face. It is slow even in a jam. Its response to the flattened parcel may be a slight eccentricity in a spindle that has not yet come up through the ground. One cannot settle their number by counting the things done. The lip under the reel has occasionally yielded at just the instant necessary to let a fold go through six wards without uncovering a single winding shed. For that instant, at least, it had the use of six wards.

They have their idle occupations. Under a shutter which has not been raised for several thousand circuits there is a patch of floss bearing the impression of a spindle with three unequal flanges. No such spindle is now in service. From time to time the floss fills out, lies smooth for a moment, then sinks in again, first at the broad flange, then at the two narrow ones. The spindle used to be lifted out at an angle. Tow grown since its disappearance still makes room for the little sideways turn at the end. Nothing is conveyed by this operation. The heat expended on it would ripen a tray of fittings.

Farther down, these distinctions grow hard to follow. Oil can take the better part of a season to pass between the laminations of two old pavements. A pressure started in one roof travels a few inches along a rib, waits through the fall of another roof, and continues through a socket which did not exist when it began. Much of what comes up from there has the character of an answer offered after everyone has forgotten the difficulty. The little ripple under the freight incline is one such answer. Every circuit the rounded teeth come down on it. Every circuit the ground supplies, for the space of one tooth, the hill.

The freight incline will go over in its turn. There is already a hot furry piece of ground well inland on which nothing has ripened, though oil is being drawn to it from both sides. A straight ridge can be felt there in the dark, and underneath it, close set and still quite soft, the beginnings of teeth. There is no sign of a grade. Only, if the hand is left flat on the ridge while the oldest circuits pass, there comes a moment when it takes a little of the weight of the hand, as a tread does in climbing stairs.
