There is a lodger in number eight. For a long time the evidence for it consisted of a cup on the third hook. The cup was of the thin white kind with a blue thread round the lip. It had lost its handle. It ought not to have been possible to hang it up.

When the lights reached the street there was generally a little movement at the rim of the cup, such as one sees at the rim of a full glass carried carefully across a room. After that it was still. The thing holding it had plenty of time to get a purchase before anybody could see it properly. A few joints of one foreleg went through the hook. Its back fitted the cup, and the rest was tucked away inside. The blue thread was a worm living in a groove of its own making. No part of the arrangement had ever been in a pottery.

Loose on the shelf, the lodger is a surprisingly long animal. It lets itself out of the cup from the small end, like a man getting out of a jersey and leaving the jersey where it is. First come two narrow feet which feel a surface and take the load. Then comes a length of soft grey pleating. There is a quite unreasonable amount of this. Last comes the head, a blunt stamp with a fringe round it, rather too broad to go through the hole it has just come through. On the yellow tiles it leaves a row of clean round places the size of sixpences. The fringe gets into the grout. It takes what little grows there and a good deal of the grout as well.

The cup is worth going back to. Its inner skin, which the animal has laid down in winter after winter of turning round in the same confined space, keeps out brine. Water in the cup is ordinary sea water even when the shelf is awash with the heavier stuff. In the back of it are the young. For the first part of their lives they are attached by a short stalk, each to a separate dimple in the glaze. A current runs in past them and out again through two holes under the rim. This is what gives the cup its look of being carried. If the current fails the young beat on the inside with their feet, all at different times. A cup taken down and held in the hand feels as though it contained a number of very small people trying keys in a lock.

There are other lodgers, though not many have cups. One has grown its lining inside a porcelain fuse carrier in the meter house. One occupies the ivory mouth of a speaking tube. In this last case the young have a difficult start. On coming loose they follow the inner wall, and as often as not set off down the tube, which goes without a break to the office of the baths superintendent. It is quite common to find a dozen of them there, all the same size, cleaning the same patch of blotting paper. They have little sense yet of a place having been gone over.

What keeps the grown ones apart is the reach of the pleating. The two feet seldom let go together. An animal may work a long way round a corner, or up the back of a cupboard and out through a knot hole, but in measuring what it can reach one must always put the point of the compass at its house. It is possible to make out the lodgings on a street by their margins. There will be a grey cupboard door with a quarter circle of sound green paint at the bottom, a curtain of lagging wool ending all along one side at precisely the same slant. In number eight the circle takes in three hooks and the shank of the fourth. The curl at the end of that hook is furred like a twig in frost.

Once in a great while a lodger has to move. A sill gives way, or its house tips mouth down and the little current, not a strong current at best, begins to take in silt. It goes in the interval of the seventh stud. The legs come right out this time. There are two more pairs than one would suppose, shorter and poorly finished, as if meant for no such public business as walking. They carry the house with the young inside it. In the dark it is possible to follow one by touch without laying a hand on the animal at all. At each stair it sets the hard rim down and draws its weight up after it. The tremor goes a good way along the rail.

By the next turn of the light the cup at number eight may be on the first hook instead of the third. There is nothing to show how much work this has cost. The fringe is out already, testing a yellow tile that has been beyond reach for years. In the angle between the shelf and the bend a little powder falls the other way, off the backs of the feet as they are put away.
