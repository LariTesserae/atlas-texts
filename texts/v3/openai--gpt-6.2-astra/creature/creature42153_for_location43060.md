There is a little life in the roof, though you would have to take a bit of the roof out into the day to see it. It is near the mouth, above the woody arm of the saltbush, where a thin scale has come off the sandstone. The scar is the color of the rest of the ledge. Only at its upper edge, where the stone is broken across the grain and a thickness no greater than the rind of a seed is exposed, is there a trace of green.

The green belongs to small colonies of algae. A colony fills the crotch between two grains and sends a few cells round the clearer grain on the side toward the plain. Those grains let in a little light. In a wet season, when rain has stood on the slab and found its way down along the bedding, there is water there too. It hangs in the crotch long after the face of the stone has dried. For a few mornings the green deepens. A cell divides; its two halves stay side by side in their clear coat. By the time there are four, the water may be gone again. A whole summer passes over the four.

They occupy very little of the ledge. A thumb held against the scar would cover most of them. A little farther back there are pores of just the same size, and water sometimes lodges in them, but scarcely any light comes through. Farther out there is light enough and the stone dries to its heart. The place that suits them lies between, in an irregular strip that follows the buried face of a grain, turns a corner with it and ends. It bears no particular relation to the shape of the hollow as seen by something tall enough to walk under the lip.

Now the cells are dry. The clear coat has drawn in close about them. Below, the few leaves at the end of the branch stir when air goes by outside; there is a momentary change in the light entering the stone, too slight to be seen on the floor. Nothing stirs in the red sand at the back. The grains there have come down from a part of the roof too far in for anything green, and lie loose beneath the band that shed them.
