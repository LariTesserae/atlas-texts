There are little beasts in the hem of red leaves. The easiest part of one to find is a foot. Look along the toothed edge of a blade when the garden is shut and there will be, among all the teeth pointing one way, three or four pointing the other. These are the tips of its toes. They have a dull ivory polish from taking hold always in the same fashion. The rest of the foot, and the long joint that carries it, are folded away between that blade and the next.

One seldom sees a whole beast until the patch of light comes down. As the leaves begin to turn, it keeps its hold with the feet on one side and lets the other side of the garden fall away from it. For a moment it hangs in the open. It is hardly longer than a grain of barley. It has a flat belly the color of the yellow earth, a back in overlapping slips of smoky horn, and a head so much narrower than the back that it can put its face into a fold no toe could enter. A pair of soft white brushes come out of the face. With these it sweeps the pale base of the leaf, first across and then up toward its mouth. By the time the next blade comes round it has taken its meal of oil and put the brushes away. It catches the passing edge without looking. An old one can feed along half a dozen blades in the course of the opening, without once setting foot on the wall.

The young ones miss. They can be found afterward well down in the spent foliage with their bright feet full of dust. They go about tasting everything. A slip of stone freed by a root is much the shape of a furled shoot, and one will spend a long time gravely brushing the dry thing before it goes on. Their backs are quite clear. You can see the bead of oil working its way down inside, dividing at one joint and coming together again at the next. It takes many meals before there is enough of the dark horn to hide it.

The two ranks beside the bare oval are full of them. Here the wind has done them a service: a leaf at the very edge of the cut is liable to be stripped to its midrib, and where it breaks off there remains a little ivory stump with a hollow in the end. The beasts lay in these hollows. They back into one and stay there through the hot part of a day. When they come out there is a row of amber eggs inside, each with its long side against the next, and a stopper of mineral meal tamped into the mouth. The first thing a new beast brushes is the inside of that stopper. When the last of the meal comes loose it has a view, from the height of its broken stalk, of an immense clean floor curving up at the far side into another wood of red blades.

Ordinarily it will spend its life on the side where it came out. There is no purchase for its toes on the floor of the oval, and the moving grit there would take the brushes off its face. But for a little while at the end of the day the leaves on both sides lie flat in the same direction. The longest of them reach across the hollow. While the note holds at the entrance, there is traffic on these bridges. A beast goes out along the sheltered underside of a blade, feels the teeth of a foreign blade lying against its own, and changes its hold. Sometimes two meet belly to belly and hang for a moment by one another's feet, each waiting for the other to make a move. At the cutting off of the note they take hold of whatever is nearest. The leaves stand up and carry them apart.

After the stone has fallen and the last grains have landed, there is another sound, much too small to hear from the curb. It goes on irregularly at one end of the border, stops, and starts up at the other. It is the ticking of those hard little toes against the leaf teeth as a beast, brought home to a part of the garden it does not know, tries fold after fold for one that fits its back.
