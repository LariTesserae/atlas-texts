One of the black midges has spent the morning under a willow. There is room there to walk upright. A twig bent down by a drop of water makes it change direction; it goes round the end of the twig and comes out on a sill, into sunlight. From that height it can fly. Down among the trunks its wings were rather a nuisance to it.

There are others in the water. If you shade one of the little cups with your hand you can see clear husks, split along the back, clinging to the rim. What came out of each husk is black and quite conspicuous. For a while the pool is full of pairs, a black stroke standing beside a clear one. Then the black strokes begin to go about on the water, and a little later the dun bird comes down the steps. It is light enough to stand on a leaf. The leaf sinks until water comes in over one edge; by then the bird has taken what it wants and stepped onto the next one. It leaves a succession of brimming cups behind it.

Seen close, the bird has a warm brown eye and one white feather under its chin. There is a place in the sun at the foot of a wall where it sometimes sits with that feather showing, its head tipped back and its bill a little open. One foot disappears altogether into its belly. It can look quite at home like this and still be gone for three days. There is no shelf of mud under an overhang, no nest in the willow wood. What keeps it coming back is the brief abundance of things of just the size its bill closes on.

The willows are here in a fuller sense. The one nearest the sill has lost a branch, and on the exposed wood you can make out rings so fine that after the first few your eye slips and has to begin again. Its roots go under several of the other trees. Indeed, where a little soil has fallen in, you can see that two of the twisted trunks are the ends of one elbow of wood. The wood has gone on growing at the bend, in the dark. Each end has its own way of leaning into the light. In the fork of the nearer one is a red grain dropped from the wall, held fast now with a lip of bark coming up around it.

After the bird has gone, a midge that has been standing quite still on the underside of a leaf crawls round to the top. By now there is scarcely a cup left, only a shining hollow with a little dirt at the bottom. It spends some time there feeling its way along the ribs. When the shadow of the wall reaches it, it sets off across the sill on foot. It takes most of the remaining sunlight to get back under the trees.
