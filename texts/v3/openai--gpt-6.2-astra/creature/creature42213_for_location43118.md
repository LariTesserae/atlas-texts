At the places where the wet seam runs out into grit there is a little animal the colour of the skin on a hazelnut. You are most likely to see its nose. The nose comes out from under a flake of stone and travels an inch or two along the margin of the water; behind it comes one small hand, which takes hold of the fringe of moss. For a considerable time that is all. One of the drops on the moss grows long enough to fall on the hand. The fingers tighten and the whole piece comes up like the corner of a rug. Underneath are the little white jointed things that have been grazing there in the dark. The nose goes in among them.

It is a kind of shrew. Out on a stone, drying itself, it would scarcely fill the bowl of a tablespoon, though the tail would hang a long way over. The fur is dark at the roots. When the animal is wet it divides into pointed locks, and what looked a fairly plump creature under the overhang turns out to be mostly shoulders, with a narrow ribby middle and two surprisingly substantial hind feet. There is a comb of stiff pale hairs along the outer edge of each foot. On dry grit these leave delicate brush marks to either side of the prints.

It will go right into the water. In the deep pool where the bottom mud stirs, there is sometimes a bright moving spot under the surface, like a flaw travelling through a piece of glass. This is the air on the shrew’s belly. It goes down headfirst, holding to the boulder with all four feet until it reaches the overhang below the waterline; there it lets go and swims upside down along the rock, feeling in holes with its snout. It comes up in the same place nearly every time, under a little stone beak where there is room to breathe without coming into the open. After several such journeys it climbs out backwards, its mouth so full of a long pale crustacean that it cannot use its chin to help itself over the edge. It eats sitting up. The brittle pieces of the meal fall between its feet.

There are dry places it visits too. A petrel feather that has lain some days on the floor may move quite smartly against the air and settle again a little farther on. The shrew is underneath, going after beetles. It does not carry the feather on purpose. A barb has caught in the coarse hair over its rump; sooner or later it turns to bite at the nuisance, and the feather is left on another hundred yards of apparently untrodden grit.

Its nest is up out of reach of the seep, in one of the old water flutes on the underside of a tilted roof. Seen from below it looks like a grey stopper in the open end of the flute. The outside is made of petrel down felted so thickly with grass that it keeps its shape when empty. Inside, when there are young, there is a nearly continuous working about, very feeble at first. A pink foot presses out between two grass stems and is withdrawn. On her way back from the pool the mother climbs the flute in a series of quick runs. Just short of the nest she stops to squeeze the water out of her belly fur, drawing one handful after another through her teeth. She goes in almost dry.

For a few days when the young first come out there is a curious procession along the seam. One takes hold of the mother’s rump fur with its teeth, the next takes hold of that one, and so on. At a low step in the stone the whole string contracts and lengthens, each animal having to find its own foothold before the one behind can come on. The last is occasionally left sitting by itself. It makes a sound so fine and high that even quite close by, in a lull between the hollow notes of the birds, one might take it for a grit of stone squeaking underfoot. But the mother hears it. The little file doubles back along the black edge, collects its end, and goes on.
