The woman with the pudding bowl lives in the first block below the ferry slip. She works in the last district but one. It takes her eleven minutes to get to work, provided she goes outdoors as soon as she is dressed. If she stops at her sister’s to collect the little girl, she has to leave before breakfast. The sisters live on the same landing. It is a very long landing, and the little girl is at the age when she must be allowed to carry her own stool.

The stool is for looking over the sea wall. Her name is Lena; her aunt is called Bette. There used to be a B on the underside of the pudding bowl, painted on before the glaze went on. Bette has had it so long the B is gone and the glaze with it. She can carry it full of water on one hand without its slipping. It was not bought for the anemones. In winter she still makes a pudding in it, putting a clean sixpence in the batter, although there is nobody left in either family who can spend a sixpence.

Quite a few of the older people below the slip were grown before there was anything there to live on. Bette used to sell tickets on the ferry. She has forgotten the fares but remembers the change. Give her a sum in the old money and her thumb moves twice against the side of her forefinger, pushing out one coin from under another. She remembers, too, which people wanted to be left alone on the crossing. Most of them still do. On her way to work she passes a man sitting in his shirtsleeves on a folding chair, reading the racing results in the poor light outside his house. They have known one another for forty years without having to say good morning.

It was the man in the chair who discovered what had become of the harbor crabs. For years after the water went away people went on finding their empty shells, each with its pair of little glass lamps still in place, and concluded that that was all there was to find. Then one evening he laid his paper across his knees and a crab measuring rather less than the width of the racing column fell out of his cuff. It was the color of the small print. He put it down beside his chair. It went under the glass edge of the house, not into the crack where the mint grew but into the house itself, and the next morning his wife found it upstairs in the soap dish.

They live wherever there is an outside bathroom wall. At night, when the walls go cool, they come out in sufficient numbers to leave a sort of darn in the dust, a close little piece of needlework between the house and the nearest bed of shells. Nobody minds them much. A crab will sit quite patiently on the plug while you wash your face. When you have finished you have to put a fingernail under one side of it and turn it over like a button. Its underside, which hardly anybody sees, is a surprising blue.

Lena keeps a tally of the blue ones, under the impression that these are a different kind from the gray. Bette has not put her right. There is a pleasure in getting down on your stomach for a better look, which will survive the discovery, but also a pleasure in announcing to a whole landing that there are now seventeen of the rare kind, which will not.

On Thursdays the sisters play for the dancing. The one plays a concertina and the other has two spoons and the square end of a cake tin. They sit on the red paint line, where they can see the dancers’ feet. There is a figure in one of the dances that takes the couples diagonally across the ripples, the leading partner going backward. People who learned it in the upper town have a way of tightening their hold at this point. Those born on the harbor floor let an arm hang loose. You can see the difference particularly well when a mother is dancing with her grown son. She tries to steer him over a ridge he has known through the soles of his shoes since he was two, and there is a moment of family difficulty, got over in time to the music.

Sometimes when the dancing is finished Bette lets Lena stay the night. They walk home by the indoor way, though it is longer, for the sake of the things people leave out on the landings: a dish of early figs split at the stem, a basket of odd knitting wool, a chair with a card saying its fault is in the back left leg. At one turn there is a melon almost ready to come away. A child has made it a sling out of a liberty bodice, buttoned very neatly to the rail. They stop and smell it. It will not be tonight.

In bed the little girl can hear her mother putting away the spoons. It is a peculiarity of the two flats that although the walk between their doors is a long one, the backs of the kitchen drawers come very nearly up against Bette’s spare-room wall. Two knocks and then a little run as the spoons slide forward: her mother is home. On the mornings when her aunt goes to work without her, Lena wakes to a different noise, a small rough circle drawn on the other side of the partition. For a long time she supposed this was the sound an anemone made on waking. It is Bette, turning the pudding bowl right way up on the draining board to fill it before she goes.
