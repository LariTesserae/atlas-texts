On the underside of the floating beds there is a little six-footed animal about the color of the leaf it stands on. Its feet are by far the largest things about it. Each ends in a pair of soft gray lobes which spread when the foot is set down, so that the creature appears at rest to have twelve feet and no legs. There is just room among the fine hairs of the leaf for a film of air. The animal puts its blunt nose into this and stays there for a considerable time, occasionally moving one of its feet to let a smaller one pass.

It is easier to find their work than to find them. The yellow underside of a leaf darkens with age, except along certain crooked lanes which keep the original clear color. At the blind end of each lane there is likely to be a broad head, turned a little to one side to bring the horny edge of the mouth against the leaf. It is the brown stuff that comes away. Under a newly opened leaf, quite clean from stalk to margin, one finds none of them. On an old one there may be so many lanes that the brown remains only in little countries shut in on every side by yellow.

The young have narrow backs, nearly transparent. One can see a twist of brown matter passing through them, and the pale soles of the feet applied to the leaf on the other side. They go out along the red roots as readily as the grown ones go along a leaf. A root drawn up out of the water will sometimes have half a dozen hanging from its last fork, all with their noses pointing up. Let it down again and they continue their descent. Where the tassels brush the drowned hillside they pass into the wood of green stems. Down there the things they clean are the dead leaves. It is largely owing to them that a brown leaf, wasted past the point at which one would think it could hold together, has such exceedingly distinct veins.

They come up for air, though seldom by the shortest way. A little one at the foot of a stem will start up it, turn out along a leaf, go the length of it and look over the point. If there is another leaf within reach it will step across. Otherwise it comes back to the stem and tries the next pair up. In the clear evening light one can follow the whole ascent, even after the animal itself has become too small to distinguish: a fleck of silver runs out and back, then out and back at right angles to its former course. The silver is air caught in a crease of the belly. On returning from the surface it has so much of this that its hind feet occasionally come loose and wave above its head.

The broad-backed ones do not all stay under the rafts. Late in the dry months, when the outer leaves lie aground, they can be seen on the black stone, still setting their soft feet down with the deliberation necessary to keep hold of a ceiling. In air the back loses its yellow cast. There is a line along the middle of it, and on a warm afternoon the line opens. Two exceedingly fine wings come out from under the hard halves of the back. The first time one sees this it is hard to account for the length of wing that goes on coming. The creature draws each one smooth with a hind foot, waits a moment, and is off. It flies low and rather heavily until it comes to the lip. There it shuts its wings and drops out of sight.

Far down the face, on the ferns, there are the same crooked lanes, green on a ground of blackish green. There is scarcely a frond without one. The animals there are dark and hard and small-footed in appearance, the gray pads having drawn together to little points that fit between the spore cases. In the wettest bands they lay their eggs. A clutch looks like six or seven drops of water on the underside of a rib, too evenly spaced, perhaps, but otherwise quite ordinary. A real drop gathering beside the row grows heavy, travels down it, takes in the whole clutch as it goes and hangs trembling at the tip. When it falls the eggs are still there.

For much of the year the young of these outer colonies live in a depth of water hardly greater than the thickness of their bodies. They walk up the wet rock by night. Wherever a seam opens wide enough to admit them they turn in, and some of the seams go a long way into the mountain. A stone lifted on the lake side of the rim may disclose a crowd of them, white from their recent casting, easing themselves out of a crack against the almost imperceptible outward passage of the water. They wait under the stone until the new backs have stiffened. Then they go up among the jointed plants to feed.

Sometimes, at the very edge, one comes upon a single grown animal walking slowly about on the surface film, feeling here and there with its forefeet for something firm. It leaves six dimples in the sky. Let a blade of grass touch the water and it catches hold at once, climbs out as far as the blade will bear it, and sits there working the wet out of the joints of its wings, with the clouds going by underneath.
