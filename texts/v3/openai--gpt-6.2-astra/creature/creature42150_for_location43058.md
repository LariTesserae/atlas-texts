There is a little fish on the shelf that the road crew call a hem. The name is usually plural. “Mind the hems,” one of them will say, turning back the wet cuff of his trousers before he starts down. There will sometimes be one in the fold, lying very straight and still, its two gold eyes a surprisingly long way apart. You let the cuff trail in the water and presently it is empty. Nobody ever sees the fish go.

In the morning they lie under the lips of the islands. The turf is undercut all round, and the fine white ends of the palms’ roots grow out into the water and turn upward just short of the light. A hem rests crosswise in two or three of these turns. It is about as long as the paler joint of your thumb. There is very little to it behind the eyes. Seen through the water, the tail looks like a fault in the surface of the rock below, something you could put right by passing your hand over it.

The pale flowers on the vine have a good deal to do with the fish being there. A flower opens up full of little white lice. They have been eating the inside of the bud in the dark, and when the petals fall apart they keep their places for a time, as if the bud were still around them. One fish goes in among them and several more wait a little way off. You see a white peppering in the water and then it is clean. The first fish backs out of the flower. It has picked up pollen on the rims of its eyes and for perhaps half an hour it is quite easy to follow.

At drawdown a hem will stay in its sling of roots until its back is in the air. Then it drops. There may be a hundred of them under one bank, and all you hear is three or four drops, the little bodies falling in close succession on the same patch of wet weed. They make their way down the slope under the strands. It is these the rail feels for with its toe. Now and then the toe comes down on a fish crosswise, and the rail has to stand there on one foot and consider how to reach the head without letting up on the tail. Quite often it loses it. There is a narrow clear track behind the departing fish, and the bird looks along it with one eye, well after it has ceased to be of any use.

Some of the hems get down into the pink seams. You would think there wasn't room for a fish, but there are the eyes, side by side in the stone, and then there is only one eye and then neither. A few minutes later, if somebody has let you into the booth, you can watch them come out under the white flower in the ditch. Against the red mud down there they are a perfectly ordinary olive brown. They swim strongly upstream. It is odd, after all the trouble you have taken to get a look at one on the shelf, to see thirty or forty fish in plain view swimming out from under your knees.

No one has established whether they come back. By the time the shelf begins to fill, the ditch water is brown with rain again and hard to see into. There is an old woman in the village who went up with her father in the days when the booth was level and a man could step out of it onto the road. She remembers the fish. She remembers particularly the trouble they took to get upstream, and her father lifting her across so she wouldn't spoil their run. Asked where they were going, she says it was a roadside ditch; they were going to the blocked end, where there was a little more depth and a patch of cress. People seem to want another answer. She remembers the cress quite distinctly. They used to bring a bunch down wet in a handkerchief to have with bread.

Late in the day you can sit on the step of the booth and put your feet out in the cooling water. If you sit there long enough a hem may come to rest between two toes. It weighs almost nothing. What you feel is a small steady working against the skin, the gill cover opening and shutting on one side of its head. When you lift your foot the fish goes with the water. For a while afterward the place between your toes feels colder than the rest of you, as if you were still standing, to that extent, in the ditch.
