One of the gardeners is still out when the knife is laid down. She is sitting on the back edge of her paddle, doing up a clog. The ties go twice round the ankle and once between the two long toes. It is possible to go barefoot, and after a day of pressing roots it would be pleasant, but the grease takes a little skin with it every time a foot comes up. She has a bald place under one heel from the summer she was too grown up to carry spare ties.

Her name is Tesset. Seen from the divided stair she looks dressed in brown, with a pale collar turned up about her neck. Nearer, the brown resolves into strips of old and new grass plaited together, the old almost chestnut, the new still faintly green. The collar is part of Tesset. It grows in two halves from behind her jaw and lies ordinarily against her shoulders. On the inner face of each half there are curved lines as fine as the lines inside a lentil when the skin slips off. She lifts one side now to catch a repetition of the knock. There isn't one. From the direction of the sound, and from the fact that the handle came down first, she thinks it was her sister, who cuts with her forefinger extended along the back of the knife and has to open her whole hand to put it down.

In the daytime the collars are shut. A little child going to market in somebody's arms will sometimes keep hers spread out, interested in the great commotion, until the person carrying her passes a hand down either side of her head and folds them for her. This is why a stroke from crown to shoulder is soothing to a grown person here, even one who was not, on the whole, a happy child.

Tesset has a good deal of yellow about the wrists. The cook will object if she comes in like that. She takes from her pocket a coil of used hinge and draws it to and fro across the creases of her hands. The wax rolls off in neat little worms. She puts these back in the pocket. At home there is a joint in the kitchen wall which whistles in cart weather, and since the youngest boy discovered that picking the wax out will alter the note, it takes a surprising quantity to keep it stopped.

There are eleven people living behind Tesset's door at present. Two sleep elsewhere but eat there; one eats elsewhere but for reasons of his own still comes home promptly to complain of the supper. They do not think of themselves as a particularly large household. The rooms left high up by the sinking street are still perfectly good rooms. To get to bed the older children climb a strip of plait hung down the face of the house and swing in at the old front door. They rather like going out of doors to go to bed. In very warm weather they bring their bedding to the sill. From there a person lying on her side can see the last of the light go out backwards through the spar, a clear long way into the stone.

Tonight the youngest is waiting on the new threshold with a drawing scratched in a shaving of wax. He has drawn the ant again. It has six upturned feet and, beside each foot, a clog. He wants his aunt to settle something. His brother says that if the ant had come down the steps in clogs it could never have got up again, because of the trouble of getting six soles to the ground at once.

“You don't put them all down at once,” says Tesset.

He wants to see. She sets her paddle against the house and goes down on her hands and feet on the paving. For the missing pair of legs he offers to put his arms round her middle and reach down, an arrangement which, after some adjustment, gives them six points of support and no means whatever of advancing. The sister appears overhead, at what used to be the kitchen door. It was indeed her knife. She looks down for a little while without saying that the supper is ready.

This is a better likeness of the inhabitants than the one that could be had by coaxing a single specimen up the seven steps and setting her under a glass in the square. There one would see a small brownish person, remarkably steady on her feet, holding the flaps of her collar so tight to her neck that they would probably be taken for gills. One would miss the pale flash of them opening when, in the brief quiet under the tread, a woman hears her sister begin to laugh.
