The first time I saw one of the roof people she was sitting on the stack of slabs with her feet in the salt. I had been looking at the other end of the shadow, trying to count the steps. She had taken off one shoe and was easing a clot of pink salt out of the seam with the long nail of her little finger. There was a red place on her heel. It was agreeable, after a day in that country, to see so plainly what someone was doing and why.

She was a broad, short woman, dressed in a coat of overlapping leaves with the nap worn quite away at the elbows. The hair above one ear had been shorn close and the rest hung in a black plait down the middle of her back. She let me feel the shoe. Its sole was nearly as thick as the door of the hut. There was a thin place under the ball of the foot where several plies had been cut out on purpose. Putting my thumb there I felt a note as fine as a needle go all the way up to my shoulder. She laughed at the face I made. The roof people like to have a little sense of where they are going.

We walked back together. At the door she turned me by the shoulders until I faced out across the salt. There, in the entrance of the far hut, a woman was taking down her hair. It was too far away to see this by ordinary light; nevertheless I saw distinctly the twist of the wrist with which she undid the end of the plait, and even the pale band where the fibre had been wound tight around the hair. My companion, whose hair was still bound up, considered her a moment. Then she went in and turned the door over and showed me, on the fourth of the little maps, where the roof slabs were to go.

There were children in the low hut by then. They had come up the path behind us with a pot of butter wrapped in wet cloth and a bag of the small sour pears that grow below the clouds. The smallest child kept a pear for himself and rolled it from the woven door onto the stone and back again, attending with great satisfaction to the disappearance and return of the little knocking sound. He had to be put bodily to bed before anyone could get on with supper. His mother sliced the other pears into the hot butter. All the eating and talking was done standing up on the door; there was room if we kept our elbows in. When a person wished to be alone he had only to step down.

I came to understand that they spend rather little of their lives up there. There are houses well down the mountain, among the pear trees, where a leaf brought down from the roof will keep a cupboard sweet and still for three generations. A good many of the people have never been higher than the notch. But in most families there is somebody who feels, on putting a bare foot on the kitchen flags, the beginning of a thin persistent ache, generally mistaken at first for trouble with a tooth. It makes very little difference which side of the mountain the house is on. After a few weeks of this the person goes up to see what wants doing. It is possible to get quite old in the course of these visits. In the valley they say of such a person that she has two ages, the one you can count on your fingers and the one you had better ask her about.

The woman with the sore heel had seen the far hut since she was a girl. For many years there had been no one in it. Then one morning, on her way to lay the first stone of the doorstep, she had seen the chipped lintel and, below it, a hand held out to feel whether it was raining. The sight had distressed her. She had known at once whose hand it was, and had not liked to think of herself so old and bewildered as to have forgotten there could be no rain on the tables. Now that she was older she said it seemed quite a sensible thing to do. One ought at least to put a hand out now and then.

I asked if it did not save a great deal of work, being able to see how things would turn out. She said it was of some use with a house, provided you could see all four sides. People were another matter. A man of her acquaintance had been coming up for eleven years to look at a little figure sitting with its back to him on the third step from the top. It might be either of his daughters. The girls themselves had grown impatient with the business and now ran a laundry together a long way down in the blue country. They sent him up a clean shirt every time he went.

In the morning we carried a slab from the stack to the doorstep. I had supposed, seeing the size of the thing, that there would be some roof-country trick to it, some way of slipping the weight of it a little ahead of us into the clear interval. There was not. It took the two older children as well as ourselves and we had to set it down twice. On the second occasion the woman crouched and laid her cropped ear against it. Whatever she heard pleased her. We got it up on edge beside the sill and chocked it there, and she put the mallet away on top of the diminished stack.

Before I left, the little boy came running after me with the pear. He wanted me to leave it at the notch for his father, who would be coming up that afternoon. The fruit was warm and rather far gone from its travels across the door. He showed me the safe place to put it, in the space between the third stone and the fourth, and stood watching until I had got it right. There was just room.
