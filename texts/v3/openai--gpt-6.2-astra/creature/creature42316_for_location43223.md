There is a fishing cat in the bend, though up to the time the moon clears the trees she has been well above the pools. She has been asleep in the fork at the bottom of a fig. Two roots have run down the bank almost touching, and clay and leaf mould have filled the cleft between them to the depth of a cat. The fur on the side she has lain on is pressed into little ridges. When she gets up, a white seed from one of the figs goes with her, stuck just below the eye.

She is a heavy animal for her height. Much of what would be leg on a house cat seems in her to have gone into the length of the body and the breadth of the forequarters. She can pass under a low stem without bending her legs very much. Her tail is short; when she turns to come down the bank headfirst it stands out stiff behind her, with a crook at the black tip. In the moonlight the rows of spots along her flanks are quite plain for a moment. Then she passes into the shadow of a ginger leaf and all that can be seen of her is the white mark on the back of each ear.

She does not go to the deep pool. There is another, scarcely longer than she is, on the inside of the bend. In daylight it looks empty. The water is the colour of tea and the bottom can be seen right across it, down to the fine comb marks left by the feet of a prawn. But on the side under the bank the pool goes back some distance among the roots. A fish can lie in there all day with its back out of sight and its belly in cool water.

The cat comes to the outer edge of this pool and sits down. For a while her head moves with a swimming beetle that has taken a bubble of air below. It goes shining down, catches on a twig, frees itself and goes on. She lets it go. A little later a different disturbance comes out from under the bank. There is hardly a ripple, only a fig stamen afloat on the surface which moves a finger’s breadth into the open water and stops. Her ears are still turned towards the deer behind her. It is only at the shoulders that the shape of the cat begins to change.

There is a slap loud enough to lift the moth off the heron. The cat has both forefeet in the pool and her face in the water between them. She comes out with a fish held crosswise, so long that its tail beats against her ear. The deer have gone a few lengths up the slope and stopped. By the time they lower their heads again she has the fish on the clay with one foot on it. A runnel of water goes down from her chin and takes the white seed with it.

She eats the head there. The rest she carries up over the crest, not by the easy slope she came down but through a place in the cane where the dead stems have fallen all one way. There is a hollow beyond, in the high part of the bank, left by the turning out of a tree. Before she reaches it a small blunt face is thrust between two of the fallen stems. A paw comes through much too far to one side of the fish and closes on a cane leaf. There is a second cub farther back. It is this one that gets hold of the tail while the first is still trying to get its paw free. For a little while there is quite a business of hauling in opposite directions, conducted almost without a sound.

When the channel fills, that small pool will disappear sooner than any of the others. Its outer rim is low; the first tongue of river water will cross it and the fish under the roots will have the length of the bend to move in. But the hollow in the cane is above the old water stain on the figs. The mother leaves the two cubs there with their fish and goes back as far as the crest. The moon is high enough now to show the tips of her wet forelegs, the fur gathered into dark points. She sits licking the clay from between her toes. Below her the heron is standing on one leg again. The moth, having lost its place, is making slow circuits of the pool.
