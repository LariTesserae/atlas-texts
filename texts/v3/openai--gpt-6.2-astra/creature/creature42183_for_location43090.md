The first thing to come out in the morning is a little grey foot. It comes out sideways, from a seam a little above the six stones, and feels along the underside of the slab. It has four toes, very widely spread, with a pale button at the end of each. For some time this is all of the animal there is to be seen. A drop gathers on the lowest button. The foot goes in again.

There are seven or eight of them in the pass. It is difficult to count them because they go about in the stone edgewise. The part that shows for longest is a ridge down the back, grey with a streak of cream in it, which seems to continue the bedding of the limestone quite faithfully even when the creature is on the move. Out on the knee place, with room to let itself down, one is about the size of a child's mitten. It has a soft, rather broad belly. Its head is narrow enough to turn about in a spoon. There are two black eyes, much closer together than seems necessary in so small a face; between them, clear down to the lip, runs a fine groove. The drop from the ceiling goes into that groove and disappears. Then the animal sits for a while with its eyes shut, holding the stone with all four feet.

The wooden noise is theirs. It is not a call, though sometimes it brings one out when another has begun. Where the water backs up behind the wall there is a very fine meal continually settling. Most of it is indeed stone. Mixed with it there is whatever has come up in the weather: pollen from the yellow pines a mile below, bits of the skins of seeds, the oily dust of moths. A whole pine catkin once came over the sill, froze there, and fed the pass for two seasons. They take a mouthful of the wet meal and knock the underside of the jaw on a stone. A little fan of white issues on either side. What they keep amounts to very little. The young ones make scarcely any sound at this, but the biggest has a knock that can be told from all the others, two strokes close together and a pause in which its throat goes in and out.

They did not cut the groove. In the first year there were only two, living a long way down the eastern fall, where the thread of water used to go. The person with the chisel carried one of them up without knowing it, in the turned-back top of a damp wool stocking. For the better part of a day it had the marks of the knitting on its belly. The other came up afterwards, in stages, following a wet stain which in those days still went nearly to the top. Both are dead now. A thing they did which their descendants still do is to go to the abandoned head of that thread in the afternoon, although there is nothing there to eat. Sometimes three will be lying up there with their little chins over the east lip and the whole bright extent of cloud below them. They lie quite still until the shadow touches their tails.

What they use of the pass is much larger than the open part of it. Down between the scales are chambers made by the accidental lodging of one stone crossways on two others. There is no light at all in them. A creature descending goes tail first, folding its toes into a single narrow bunch and reopening them at each change of level. At a certain depth the stone keeps the warmth of August far into the winter. Here they leave their young, which are quite round at first and have no mark on them to distinguish back from belly. Even before their eyes are open they take hold. If a parent wishes to shift one it has to offer its wrist and wait for the four small feet to let go of the floor one at a time.

When fresh stone falls in the afternoon there is a slight rearrangement all the way down. In one of the lower rooms a ceiling settles the thickness of a fingernail. A little farther in, two edges that have touched for a hundred years draw apart. The sleepers feel this, not enough to wake, only enough to change their grips. Very occasionally a way opens into a room that none of them has been in before. It has perfectly dry dust on the floor and, high on one wall, the white inside of a shell. They like such a room. By the end of winter the shell has a polish on it from their backs, and the dust has all been carried out on the buttons of their feet.
