The boy who puts out the chair has a sister of nineteen who has scarcely ever been seen from the hedge. She works behind the clocks. There are four of them, belonging to four different premises, and what looks from above like a single crooked loft is the space left between their cases and the roofs. In two places she can stand up. Her name is Ellen. If somebody calls it from the lane, her hand generally appears first, feeling about on the tiles for the cup she took up with her.

She is the only one of the family born down there. Her brother was born in the upstairs lying-in ward and came home by the ramp with a blue wool coat folded under his head to keep him from rolling against the side of the basket. Ellen has heard this so often that she remembers the coat quite distinctly, though it had been unravelled for socks before she was born. She herself was born in the room under the provision shop clock. At a quarter to three her mother asked to have the striking stopped. It was done by putting a meat pie on the hammer, the nearest heavy thing to hand. For a good many years Ellen believed this to be the usual way of stopping a clock.

She does not mend the movements. A man comes in for that, carrying his tools in a chamois roll and complaining of the heat. She paints the dials, oils the little doors, and keeps the leaves out of the way of the hands. A runner bean can get a tolerable purchase on a minute hand in the course of an afternoon. The difficulty is to detach it without spoiling the curl. The beans belong to the dentist’s widow, who gives Ellen a handful now and then, put up in one of the little pink envelopes intended for teeth.

There is a view from behind each dial, though not of the time. From the provision shop she sees into the crown of the fig. Things go on there of which the owners of the cellars have no idea. A hen belonging to the dressmaker sleeps in a fork of it, having got there by successive short flights from a water butt, a sill, and the back of a green shutter. It is a plain red hen with one white eyelid. In the evening Ellen can see the eyelid come down before the claws have quite done shifting on the bark. On windy nights the hen is carried a considerable distance in her sleep and brought back again.

From the barber’s clock one can see the barber himself only as far as the waist. His shirt is made of a stout white material, rather full at the back, and his daughter, who does the washing, puts a blue thread into the tail of every one she has boiled. This is to make him wear them in turn. He has a fancy that the oldest shirt gives him the most freedom at the elbow and will take it, clean or otherwise, off the bottom of the heap. What Ellen sees is this ample back moving round a head of black hair, then a grey one, then the freckled globe of a man having his neck shaved. She knows her father by the way he reaches out from under the cloth to catch the falling hair. He has always done it. Asked once what he means to do with it, he looked with some surprise at his shut hand.

It is her father who saw to the steps when the block went down. He was twelve. On each of the eleven afternoons his mother sent him out with a broom and he swept their three steps, beginning at the top. There was clay dust and once a considerable quantity of salt from a bag split in carrying it up. He remembers quite well what was said at supper about the salt. Of the actual descent he can give no useful account. Other people remember the church spire getting shorter in the window, or a horse at the edge with its belly visible. He remembers his mother making everybody lift a foot to show her they had not trodden the salt indoors.

On Wednesdays, in the cessation of the shaft, Ellen hears somebody breathing on the other side of the barber’s dial. For a long time she took it to be a customer asleep against the wall. It is the woman who cuts the linings for the dressmaker. She comes up an outside stair with her dinner in a basin and sits on the ledge where the warm air comes through round the clock. A little of the enamel has flaked off the glass at the foot of the six. If Ellen puts an eye there she can see a piece of the woman’s wrist, with a deep crease across it, and the narrow rim of the basin. Neither has ever made a hole any bigger. They have talked through it for going on three years.

The lining cutter has a sweetheart in the standing part of town. He works in a yard where they weigh old iron, has a comfortable room and a bad knee, and would come down but for the getting up again. On Wednesday she tells Ellen what they did on Sunday. Usually they had tea. Once they took a tram to see an invalid greyhound. There is a folding bed in his room which comes down so close to the mantelpiece that a vase has to be moved to get the pillow in. The woman has measured the recess on the other side of the fireplace and means to put shelves there. She has not yet told the man about the shelves. It gives her a great deal of pleasure to consider them, and Ellen, who has never been in the room, could cut the boards to length.

Towards six the boy below sets out the chair. Ellen puts her brushes into a jar and works her way back along the tiles, disturbing a smell of hot bean leaves. There is powdered gold on the ball of her thumb from a numeral she has renewed. Before going down she usually stops by the fig to see if the red hen has arrived. On some evenings the fork is empty, though the shutter stands open and all the stages of the journey are in place. Then from much lower down comes a vexed, intimate clucking. The dressmaker has stayed late at her machine. The hen is standing on the sill beside her, first on one foot and then on the other, waiting to be put to bed by hand.
