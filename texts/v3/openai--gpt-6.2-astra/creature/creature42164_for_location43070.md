The animal in the seat does not sleep with all six feet down. It takes the weight off one at a time. The foot remains in its hollow, but goes slack and spreads a little, and a fine rim of powder comes up round it. After a while another foot takes its turn. This goes on so slowly that by morning you might have seen it happen twice and missed the other four. The hollows are polished on the bottoms and quite rough at the sides.

What looks in the street like a single chalk-colored plate proves, up close, to be a series of narrow pleats. In the cold they shut tight. There is a little black in the depths of them, the same peppery black as the skin on the walls. The animal is not especially clean in its grazing. Some of what it scrapes off with its chin passes into its mouth, and some is combed back into these pleats, where it stays alive. If a flake of road dust lodges in one, a dozen mouths will work on it for the better part of the animal's journey. On a warm wall the pleats loosen. A grazer leaning there leaves, a little above its clean violet track, a dark interrupted line. Long after the track has faded to biscuit the line may still be feeding.

There are no eyes to be found on its upper surface. Its business is nearly all with the stone. The first pair of feet is particularly soft, with a little fringe underneath that lies over the paving like the end of a wetted brush. Well before the distances begin to thin, the loose grains at an abutment start to travel in the direction they will take when the stones touch. One foot in that thin stream is enough to wake the animal. It can tell a meeting that will hold its weight from the brief, useless nearness in which two places exchange a little dust. Sometimes in broad daylight it will walk out along a bridge and put a foot over the end, and take it back. Hours later a street comes by there, so nearly edge on that nothing wider than a knife could use it.

Their journeys are short if measured by the work of the feet. The one in the sheltered seat walks perhaps the length of the street in a day, and a good part of that is spent going sideways over the warm walls. Yet the dust in its pleats comes from half a dozen ridges. There is a yellow grain found only in a certain court far to the south. Animals resting a few seats apart may have such different colors underneath that, turned over, they would hardly be taken for the same kind. They go on using a particular succession of meetings as long as it lasts. When one fails, an animal will stand at the broken end for the rest of the light. Next day it tries another. There is no haste about a missed meal. There is considerable haste, for a creature of this shape, about getting out from between two surfaces that have begun to take the dust from one another.

In the lee of the seat, where even the afternoon grit does not reach, there are things like little curled parings of horn. Most are empty. Hold an occupied one to the light and there is a row of three pinkish dots and, behind it, another three. The young come out feet first and for a time go about under the seat, feeding on what the resting animal shakes down. They are thin enough to get into the closed rooms. Much of the paging sound inside a wall on a still day is made by the very small ones, working along in the dark with their chins against the seam. Sometimes a room opens that has not opened for years. The inner face, which ought to be the violet of a fresh break, is intricately written over in biscuit, with one or two fine, new violet strokes.

Most of the young leave by the first convenient street. A few stay. Under the right-hand end of the sleeper there is a smaller animal now, already too large to fit comfortably under the seat. For several nights it has rested with three feet against the stone and three on the sloping edge of the older one's back. When a foot goes slack above it, it follows the movement, lowering itself by the thickness of a grain. One morning, after one of those small changes in the order of the streets, the larger animal will go to feed on a wall that takes it elsewhere for the night. By then there will be a little dust in the six hollows. The smaller one's feet will not quite fill them. It will stand there a while, brushing the loose stuff out from under itself, before it settles down.
