There is a little animal at the upper station that the woman who works the cable calls a hem mouse. It is not particular to the station. There are hem mice at all three pump houses and a great many more out on the shelves, where there is nothing sewn for miles. But a hem is a good place to look for one. Take down the blue shirt, bunch the body of it in one hand, and run the other down the sleeve. Just before the cuff there will sometimes be a little resistance, as if someone had left a thimble in there. You must give it a moment to come out of its own accord. Its toes are very fine and hook into the weave.

It is long in the nose for a mouse, with scarcely any ear to be seen. The fur divides cleanly down the middle of its back, lying away on either side like hair that has been combed with water. When the animal is quite young the parting shows pink. Otherwise there is not much to see by daylight. You may have the toes, the nose and a considerable quantity of whisker sticking out of a crack and take the whole arrangement for the remains of a cricket that a bird has eaten there.

Its proper quarters are in the cracks under the herders' flat stones. The stones raised on three pebbles suit it better than those raised on four, not on account of the number but because a stone on four generally rocks a little. A goat need only step on it once. Under a good steady stone the mouse makes a bed of leaf skins, the thin pale things left when a grub has eaten out the middle of a leaf. A bed that would fill a teaspoon represents a great deal of picking up and bringing home. The mouse does none of this by the shortest way. It has certain crossings where its feet get purchase, and to go from one end of a man's shadow to the other it may go all the way round the man.

In the white hour it comes out to hunt. It does not seem to see the crickets very well when they stand still. It puts its nose down among them and walks, and at the first tap of a hind leg against the stone it turns and has one. After two or three it sits up and washes. The washing takes longer than the meal. A drop brought up on the forefeet goes over the eyes, along the part in the fur and out to the sides, until the little creature sits in a damp oval of its own making. Then it is off after another cricket, with one dry tuft between the shoulder blades where its feet will not reach.

The station woman knows a thing about them that the herders seldom get to see. The young travel attached to the mother, not on her back but under her, holding on by their mouths. Out on the stone there is room for this. At the station the mother has to get up over the brass lip of the door track. She puts her forefeet over, pulls, and is brought up short by the row of bellies underneath. After a little trying she backs away, lies on her side and pushes the young loose with a hind foot. She carries them across separately. They weigh so little that when she sets one down on the far side she generally has to free a whisker from its fist before she can go back for the next.

One summer there was a family in the dock itself, under the board on which the woman put her foot to steady the swinging yoke. She found the entrance by watching a rind of cheese. She had dropped it at breakfast. At noon it was still there, but standing on edge. A while later it was flat again, two inches nearer the board. At the mouth of the crack it stayed for most of the afternoon. The mouse could bring it that far but could not both tip it up and pull it through. The woman split it lengthwise with the end of her pole. Both pieces were gone by the time she came back with the cart.

In the evening the mice at the station drink from the pulley spot with the finches. There is a little order to this. The birds take the drops while they are round and standing; the mice come when the birds have gone and lick the wet undersides of the grains of grit. By then the rock has begun to give back the heat of the sun as well as the heat from below. The dock boards are among the few things in the basin that are cool on both sides. A mouse will come up through a knot hole and stretch itself full length on the wood, spreading its toes, with its chin laid flat and its eyes open. If the woman is still there she can feel, through the seat of her skirt, its running approach. The weight when it stops is too slight to feel.
