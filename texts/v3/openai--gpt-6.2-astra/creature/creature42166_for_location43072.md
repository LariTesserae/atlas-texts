When the works closed for a week to have the flywheel bearing out, the men expected the field to go quiet all at once. In fact it went quiet in strips. One could follow the last of the load down a slab by the sound of rain retreating along it. At the far end, where there was scarcely stone enough to take a charge, a single brown animal went on for nearly a minute. It had a rather agreeable little trot, heard by itself. Then it too settled on its belly.

In the quiet they discovered an animal with a considerable length to it. It had been there all along. What had been taken for a fault in the nearest slab was a row of clear square joints, fitted so closely together that the orange light ran the length of the creature without a break. One end lay under the blue thread. The other was out of sight. With the field at rest it began to draw itself out from under the thread, joint by joint. The freed curl of cotton stood up a little.

It took the surveyor most of the afternoon to make out what it was doing. The creature kept one end pressed to the edge, while the free end passed over the motionless brown backs. Wherever a heel had broken off, a lip on the last joint opened, took up the heel, and shut again. Quite often there would be nothing to take and it would go over a whole patch like a woman passing a flat hand over a sheet to find a lost pin. The old heels were amber and hard to see against the spar. Once swallowed, they showed up very well. By teatime the clear line had a dotted line inside it.

There is a good deal of breakage among the feet. An animal which has lost a heel continues to beat time with the soft stump, though it gets nothing from that stroke. During the stoppage the electrician was able to turn one over. The new heels were there already, like a second set of teeth showing through the gum. He put it back the right way up by a pencil mark and looked again next day. Three old heels lay beside it. The new ones had the dull bloom of an unused file; on the old ones the working face was polished quite black.

By the third day the long creature had gone back under the cotton. In its place along the edge were a number of short ones, none longer than a clipping of wire. Each had a single black heel in the middle joint. Whether these were its young, or whether it simply came to pieces at intervals and joined up again for the working week, nobody could say. One was watched for two hours. It travelled the breadth of the electrician’s pencil mark in that time and turned aside for a grain of dust.

The mites in the copper leaves did not come out. Without the warmth from the pile the leaves drew together until there was only a little pale mouth visible at each seam. Four of the mouths were still. The fifth kept combing, and in the absence of the heels one could hear the nap spring back after each stroke. It was this sound, a small dry *zip* repeated at long intervals, that the night watchman heard through a chair leg and reported as a mouse in the canteen.

On Monday the fitters turned the wheel by hand before putting the drive on. That was enough. The brown backs lifted clear of the stone, the two-coloured ones tightened their fold, and the noise came up through the floor before the wheel had made one revolution. The short clear creatures disappeared end on into the lighted edges. A little later the leaves at the pile opened and the five mites had their separate rooms again.

There is still one indication of the week off. The blue cotton, which used to lie quite flat, now makes a loop just high enough for a brown animal to stand under it. One does. In the light from the cage its feet throw a fine blue flicker against the rock behind it. Upstairs no one can distinguish its contribution from that of the others, but a man who was there when the wheel stopped says he would know its step.
