When the grain rolls away there is a stir in the heap by the shell door. A lump of it gets up and walks as far as the nearer root. Seen from above it is still a lump, dark at one end and rather sandy at the other. Seen from the level of the clay it has six legs and a head like a split oat. The two halves of the oat are its jaws. There is a white springtail skin caught on its back by one sleeve, waving as it walks.

It is a young lacewing. Much of what the sowbugs have been bringing out has gone onto its back. It takes a crumb in the points of its jaws, bends nearly double and puts the crumb down among its own bristles. The difficult place to furnish is just behind the head. There it has to make do with what tumbles forward when it goes downhill. For all its care it is seldom the same color at both ends.

Where the lacewing lay, a flake of yesterday’s wall leans out a little at the foot. There is a space behind it which the animal fits without its coat, and that is where the two jaws came from at noon. Farther up, the flake lies so close to the wall that a springtail can get up behind it but the jaws cannot open there. There are generally a few springtails overhead even while the lacewing is eating. Their feet show through the flake as rows of small grey stops. When one cleans a feeler the two front stops disappear.

Tonight there is a smell in the room that was not there when the lacewing went under its coat. It comes from the faces of the two seed leaves that have never until now been apart. The lacewing goes up the shoot, shedding a little earth at every step. At the joint it finds a green louse, so nearly the green of the shoot that in daylight one sees only a place where the stem appears to have been badly drawn. One of the jaws goes on either side of it. After a while the lacewing backs down with an empty green purse to put on its back.

There is another louse on the leaf that is pushing at the roof. It is smaller than the first and was born here this afternoon, hind feet first, in the little space that was widening between the leaves. Its feet have never touched anything but that one surface. Now a few hairs of the roof come within reach. It feels them, draws back its leg and stays where it is.

By morning the leaf has gone up through the turf and unfolded level with the sheep track. The little louse is on the underside, still in the cool. In the room below, a fleck of green passes slowly across the mustard plain and comes to rest among the crumbs at the door.
