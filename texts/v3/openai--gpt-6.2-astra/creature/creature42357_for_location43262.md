The tips brush and part. On one of them a drop stays behind, though the runner has sprung back hard enough to shake all the other drops off. It goes from the underside to the upper, against the slope. Where it has been there is a dull track about as wide as the mark of a wet thumb.

Seen with the pink light coming through it, the drop has a coil of something brown inside. It lets itself down on the far side of the tip and spreads until it is hardly thicker than the skin on boiled milk. The next time the runners brush, a little of it is left on each. For a while it hangs between them, the brown coil travelling slowly to the middle of the span. Both tips bend under the pull. They touch rather sooner than they would have done in the wind, and stay touching rather longer. When they part for good the animal goes with the northern one. A rag of clear stuff stays on the southern. It will be washed off in the night.

There are a great many of these on the underside of the country. In the older beds they grow to the size of a platter. The space they live in would scarcely admit a knife blade. Their backs take the print of the roots above them, their bellies the fine grain of the leaf below. If you peel two leaves apart quickly you may find one still bearing both impressions, though already drawing in at the edges and thickening in the middle. In a minute it looks like a lump of brown glass that has been taken out of the fire too soon. It cannot get itself flat again on a dry surface.

There is food in the narrow space. A root that has pulled free leaves a white nub which softens in the heat; along its edges grow little yellow beads, just visible separately in a good light. The flat animals take these off so cleanly that the track of a single night's feeding shows as a pattern of pale forks on the underside of a leaf. Their mouths are along the rim. The brown part, which looks so much like a single coiled thread in the young ones, is a multitude of little bags, filling in turn as the rim passes over the food. Sometimes a whole animal will go slowly round a particularly good nub for days, like a cloth caught on a nail in a current. In the stillest weather there is a faint sweet smell wherever a piece of the bed has opened. It comes from the unvisited forks.

They do well under lakes. The weight overhead keeps the two leaves very nearly the same distance apart; a large one can spend a season there without once having to draw itself in. But an hour before a lake goes, the animals begin leaving its bed. They come out at the shore in such numbers that the water seems to have acquired a second, more sluggish way of lapping. One rim feels for the next and passes over it. In this fashion they climb a little way uphill, going no farther than the first firm seam. It is the stretching of the floor that bothers them, the gradual widening of all the spaces in which their bodies fit. They would come out just the same if the water were lowered gently. They have no particular foresight. Quite often they go uphill on precisely the side where the water is going to break through.

After a flood you can tell which ones got back under cover and which were carried away. The former leave nothing but a patch of cleaned nap at the entrance to a seam. The latter leave the whole upper skin of the body, intact down to the delicate puckers over the bags. These are the husks at the old water level. Lift one by an edge and shake it open: it is surprisingly large, and has no more substance in the hand than a flake of dried lather. As the water drove against it the animal held on underneath for as long as it could. In the end it came away inside its own skin. Most of those rolled brown parcels in the crest of the flood will unfold quite safely wherever they are put down. The unlucky ones go into the cups. There is plenty of water there but very little purchase, and a creature that feeds with its whole circumference can starve in the midst of food if it has nothing to press against.

Under the lip of a cup there are other creatures, more easily found, though at first they too seem to be marks made by the water. The exposed yellow fibres are tied here and there in knots. A knot consists of four or five turns of a striped cord with a soft blunt end protruding. Wait for the rope of water to strike and you will see the blunt ends all point into it. Each opens a slit, wonderfully red inside. The washed-down scraps that go into those slits are no bigger than dust. For some time after a fall the cords stay stretched out, wavering like the ends of a worn brush; then, one by one, they take up their turns again. A patch of them under a cup in a gusty wind makes and unmakes the pattern of a coarse yellow plait.

It is a mistake to try to untie one. The turns are its growing part and may have been put on in different years, around fibres that were then close neighbours and have since been pulled a hand's breadth apart. In places the animal is all that holds them together. Down in the bottom of the washed patch are cords as thick as wrists, buried in the plain on either side, with only an inch or two of striped back showing. Their mouths are yards away. You can run a finger over one of those backs and see, some time later, a red slit close high up in the splash. Where a cup has fallen and there has been no water for years, the turns are still there, bleached to the colour of the fibres, and the little mouths are sealed as neatly as healed cuts. The next lake may form over them. Some open again.

As for the white stalk, it is soft. On certain evenings when the plain is very wet it comes up close enough for the sun to show through its western side. There are flecks rising inside it, stopping, rising again. At its greatest height the top bends over and lays itself lightly on the nap, several hundred feet from where it emerged. The flecks go down into the leaf there. Then the bend travels back, taking up the stalk without drawing its end so much as an inch across the ground. It leaves a small, perfectly round wet place which is slow to dry even in the morning heat.

Once, in the newly torn edge, a length of white tube could be seen passing through eleven layers. At each crossing it narrowed to a waist, then widened in the dark wet interval beneath. Small flat animals were gathered against its sides. The lowest visible length ran east and ended flush with the cut; whatever had continued it had gone with the missing country. For three days clear water welled from the cut end. On the fourth it had closed, and two little white knobs stood out on either side of the scar. By then the runners were already well past it. From above, there was nothing to mark the place except a slight quickening in the rise and fall of two tips, far out over the clouds, that had at last begun to move together.
