The holes have loose bottoms. What looks, in the failing light, like a little circle of mud at the foot of each is the back end of a worm. A pair of gills lies folded there, one lobe over the other, with a nick through which a bubble will sometimes come. In water the gills stand out like two very small wet ears. In air they make a fairly good lid. The snipe takes six worms in the space of a minute and then has some trouble with the seventh. It leans back until its chipped front claw comes clear of the mud. A strip of alder leaf comes up on the end of its bill. The worm has gone down.

There are thirsty hours in the life of almost every worm here, though few of them ever get more than a worm's length from water. A hole which has been under a foot of brown water since morning can be high on a beach by noon. The little animal goes on eating its way headfirst into the silt, the lobes at its other end growing dry and rather stiff. Deep enough down there is always damp. It takes a badger's patience to find one in a long spell of clear weather. There are, in fact, badger scratches under the roots of the leaning alders, though the nearest sett is well up the bluff where the sand comes in above the clay.

When water returns, the gills soften. A worm can back up almost imperceptibly, keeping its grip on its hole with rings of short fair bristles, until the two lobes just clear the rim. Much of the dark fringe along the new beaches is accounted for in this way. Weed does not all draw back together when the shadow of a bird goes over it.

It is the young that go by water. They hatch in the holes, out of soft blisters on the walls, and for some days live on what grows on a grain of silt. Each has a few bristles already but no rings to speak of. At the first good sounding of the bed they come loose in clouds. Some of them settle within inches of where they started. Others arrive in water so different from the water they have left that, though one can see right through them, for a while one can see them very well indeed: a little streak of peat brown goes with each into the clear. A man once lowered a white enamel cup into a well on the far side of the bluff and brought up a dozen, still trailing their brown. It was three days before the well cleared. He laid a saucer over the cup. By morning they had all worked their way under a flake of enamel no bigger than a scale from a trout.

For something that travels so far the worm has a small choice of good ground. It needs silt that will hold the shape of a hole, leaf mould enough to eat, and something fairly solid within reach when the silt begins to dance. In the iris beds the old roots are riddled with its workings. Cut through a dead iris root and there are little blind passages lined in blue, and here and there a living worm doubled neatly about a bend. They do well there. The bill of the snipe slides off the root, and the holes can stay open for years, too deep and crooked for frost. On a bare bottom there is nothing for it but to cling to a leaf or a twig and go when that goes. One sometimes sees a stick travel upright across a pool, slowly turning, with a pink brush of worms streaming from the rotten end.

The snipe leaves such places alone. It is an old hen, recognisable even among the autumn birds by a white feather behind one eye, quite round, like a fleck of clay she ought to be able to shake off. In still weather she feeds along the ordinary wet margins. When the two notes come close she takes to the gravel. There are nine of these humps within an easy flight, but she generally walks, going quite openly through the cranberries, picking up her long toes one at a time out of the wiry tangle. There she can stand without the shivering of the peat coming up through her legs. She turns her head until one ear is down. Long before there is mud to feed on she will have picked out a place and be walking towards it. The young snipe fly up and wheel at the ripping of the rushes and come down where there is already a beach. By then the best of the holes have shut.

Tonight she feeds until it is too dark to see the white feather. There is a small sucking noise where she stands, then a footstep, then the sucking noise again. It stops well before the beach goes under. Water has begun to enter the holes from below; the worms have room to turn and get their bristles against the walls. One by one the folded ends give a little and part. For a while, before there is so much as a shine on the mud between them, each hole holds its own drop of water with the gills moving freely in it.
