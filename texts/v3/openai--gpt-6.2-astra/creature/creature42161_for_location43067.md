A new swimmer will bark at almost anything. It will bark at a fly getting up, at the cold shadow of a bird going over, at the touch of its own hind foot on its chin. Its first bark is a bubble with a little grit in it. The old swimmers do not answer. Let a boot come down on one of the hollow places, though, and the smallest swimmer in the pool can have the whole company up in arms. What a person hears as a cough is felt under water as a smart push, quite sufficient to shift a hatchling out of the dent in which it has been sitting. By the time the boot comes down again there will be nothing in that dent but a pale, square impression in the silt.

They are easier to see from the bench than from the bank. At that distance the eyes cease trying to make them out individually and discover instead a stretch of pool bottom that is breathing. Then one sees the little pads at the corners of the mouths, seldom quite still. The swimmers go over the mineral collars with these, tasting their way around each rim. There is something on a collar, too fine to give it a colour, that does them a great deal of good. After feeding they prop their chins on the stalked pebbles and let their hindquarters float. Sometimes two have chosen the same pebble. They will hang there nose to nose for a good part of the morning, each apparently under the impression that the other is holding it up.

The flies come out of the water blunt and brown, dragging shreds of themselves behind them. It takes direct light to make them silver. In cloud one can see the actual wings, which are veined with red and have an extra fold near the body, like a little gusset let into a sleeve. A fly caught by the advancing hem of water can open a wing under the surface, where an ordinary wing would crumple. It goes shooting out sideways.

There is a pool bird with one yellow leg and one grey one that has learned the distance of this manoeuvre to an inch. It doesn't watch the flies struggling at the front of the little flood. It stands off to one side, picking them up as they arrive. Its young, almost as tall as itself and still downy about the ears, watch its bill instead of the water. At the note from the instrument they all take up their stations, very gravely, one bill's length to the side of their mother's bill. She has to walk away to swallow.

The most settled inhabitants live in the rushes. A heavy spider need not have a large web; she needs a web that will yield the depth of her body without letting her strike the stone. Each has a sort of silk underslung room between three or four stems, with a scrap of last year's rush worked into the floor. In wind the rooms knock softly together. The spiders sit at the doors with their front legs on the fishing lines. They are furred, the colour of the bench when wet, and have on the upper side of the abdomen a bare place about the size and lustre of a shirt button. This is what one generally sees of them: half a dozen buttons going indoors at the approach of rain.

On a fine evening the small males go visiting. There is scarcely room for one on the threshold beside the female. He hangs underneath and passes her a fly through the weave, turning it until it will go through narrow end first. If she takes it up and begins to eat, he climbs in. If she leaves it where it is, he retrieves it with some difficulty and tries elsewhere. A fly that has gone the length of the rush bed in this way is a poor enough present by the end of the evening.

There is also the woman who mended the mug. She lives two hours down the mountain, but there is no other place where she spends so much of her waking life. She comes up with a sandwich sealed in a tin and a little roll of augers tied in a stocking. Once a month or thereabouts she makes a new hole in the bench. She could move the bench, which is not fastened down, but she likes the record the holes make. When she first began coming up she could sit dry-shod with both heels in the earliest pair. Now she has to stretch a leg to reach them with her toe.

She has been credited in the village with looking after the instrument. In fact she has never been nearer to it than a place where the shell gave slightly under her left knee and a bead of warm water came up through her trouser cloth. What she looks after is the steps. On the lower one the mountain grows its ordinary black skin of lichen; on the upper one the shelf grows collars. Between them things lodge that have no business on either: twist ties, feathers, the paper off a cough sweet, once a little blue comb. She takes out what will snag a foot and leaves what might rot. It was she who first noticed that there was soil behind the steps. For a whole summer she kept herself from putting a finger in it to find out how deep it went.

Lately she has noticed something in the abandoned egg cups. The youngest cups hold water, the oldest a felt of fly husks and fine bits of rush. Between the two there are cups in which a very small grey creature lives alone. It is jointed like a shrimp but walks about the lip quite competently on dry land. At the deep note it backs into the cup and pulls over itself a round lid cut from a swimmer's cast skin. A little bristle sticks up through the middle. The returning water lays the bristle flat, first one way, then the other; when it stands upright again the lid lifts. On the underside are the red threads of fly wing, grains, all the tiny edible sweepings of the flood. The creature runs its many feet over these and then over its mouth. Presently it puts its clean lid away, edgewise, in the cup.

The woman found the first one by accident, reaching down for the mug which had slipped off its nail. Now, once she has got her eye in, she can find a dozen without leaving the bench. They were there all the time she was watching the birds and thinking how well the birds had come to know the place. One afternoon she sees a grey one no larger than a comma set off over the fluting, some way out from the last occupied cup. It goes up and down three empty ones before it finds one of the right depth. Then it has to go all the way back for its lid.
