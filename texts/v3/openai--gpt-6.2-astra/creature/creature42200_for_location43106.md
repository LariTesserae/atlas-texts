The first thing a child learns to milk is a stone. The old people sit the children down on the warm back of the divide with a pot between their knees. They find a runnel with two fingers, work their way up it to where the water is scarcely more than a wetness on the rock, and lay a twist of felt there, with the tail in the pot. By the time they have eaten there will be a drink in it. A child who can do this can be left with the goats. It is no good taking them down to the pasture to water, though there is water enough there to float a house. A goat will drink the iron water when it is thirsty and spend the rest of the day with its forehead pressed into somebody's stomach, trying to bring it up.

They are small goats. A grown one can stand under the belt in an engine house without quite touching it, and for this reason the machinists keep the lower half of the door shut even in the driest weather. The hair hangs off them in two quite distinct coats. The outside hairs are coarse enough to stitch with; the inside ones come away in the hand like the stuff in the little hollow behind a bird's knee. The felt for the bells is made of this. A familiar goat need make no sound to be known. Coming up behind you in the pasture it puts the bony bridge of its nose against the back of your knee, and there is the little notch or bump you know, as unmistakable as a name.

Down on the hanging woods there are animals so nearly like these that for a long time people supposed they were goats which had gone over and continued, against all expectation, to live. They come into the lift baskets for the salt caught in the wicker. A lift hand once brought a kid all the way up inside the fold of her coat. Its lips were cold and very busy; it licked the salt off the buttons one after the other and went back to the first button in case there was more. When she set it on the floor its feet folded up beneath it. There was nothing amiss with them. Put a broomstick under its belly and all four closed round the stick, the forefeet one way and the hind feet the other. It slept in the coat cupboard for a season, hanging among the sleeves. They sent it down again when it began to prise the porcelain pegs out of the wall.

No one knows how numerous these animals are. A basket passing through the woods will sometimes take on a weight for half a mile of the descent and then lose it, without so much as a tug. The lift hands call this stretch the crossing. On the way up they stop there to eat. There is usually a soft investigating mouth at the back of the basket before they have finished, and once a hand, feeling for the mouth, came down instead on a pair of minute feet locked together in the hair of a much greater belly.

Of the people who live out on the pasture, the ones most welcome at an engine house are the menders. There are seams in a mat which will grow together if the two torn edges can be kept at the same depth for a few days. The trick is not in sewing them. Anybody can get a rope of pith through a hole. It is in finding, some distance back from the tear, the place where the turf is still feeding. A mender lies flat and passes a hand underneath up to the shoulder. In good turf the little jointed things run up the arm in such numbers that they lift the sleeve. In dead turf there are only a few, large and slow and inclined to hang on. It is possible to spend a week stitching a piece of dead pasture to a living one and have the whole breadth of it come away inside the stitches, as neatly as the sole out of a rotten boot.

Menders grow the nail of the first finger long and keep the others pared to the quick. With that nail one of them can slit a rope lengthwise, put an ear to the slit, and tell you whether the far end is still fast. Once in a while what comes along the pith is the small regular gnawing of an animal shut up inside it. Then the mender opens the rope a little farther and lets it out. It is a miserable article when first delivered, wet and doubled on itself, with wings like the skin that comes off a boiled tongue. Held in a loose fist for a while it stiffens, gets its six feet under it, and begins to tick. At this the mender opens the fist. A bird will take it almost before it is out of the hand. Menders do not particularly mind. What they object to is stitching a noise into a seam and having to listen to it all afternoon.

The engine folk are apt to speak of a mender as somebody who lives nowhere. This is not how it feels to the mender. There may be a turn at the end of the sleeping shelf which fits the elbow, a pool no bigger than a washbasin with a single fish in it, three nesting birds which have to be lifted off the bread crock individually every time the lid is wanted. All these things will still be there after a passage of twenty miles. It is the engine house that has gone away. A mender coming indoors after such a passage has a curious moment of distress on putting a bowl down. The table does not give at all. For a meal or two the hand goes on accompanying the bowl all the way to the wood, lowering it with the tenderness other people reserve for a sleeping child's head.

Children sometimes go away on the islands and come back grown, with a different length to their vowels and a great many relations nobody at the house has ever put hands on. There is an old machinist at the second western wheel whose daughter came back in this way. He knew her at the lower door. The drumming there is too great for speech, and he had only a wrist to go by, a lean adult wrist with a cord of mending pith wound round it. But as he drew her over the sill she turned his hand over and put her thumb in his palm to step up, the way she had done when she was little and needed helping onto stone. He kept the wheel running a good hour past the service stop that day. He wanted to take her round the house while it was sounding as she remembered it.
