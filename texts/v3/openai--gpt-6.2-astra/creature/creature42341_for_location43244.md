The first part of an inhabitant you are likely to see is a hand. It comes over the sill from below, although the stone there has been in plain sight all morning. The hand is brown, with pale pads at the ends of the fingers. It feels along the inner edge until it finds one particular nick. Then it lies still. Some little way off, in what you took to be another sheet of water sliding over the first, a shoulder turns over. The elbow between the hand and the shoulder comes clear quite suddenly. It was a long way off when you began looking at it and is now just where an elbow ought to be.

There is room in one of the bowls for the whole person. Out on the stone they lie so flat that it is a surprise to see how comfortably they fold up, the long shins on either side of the ribs and the chin resting on the crossed feet. Down each flank is a loose frill, considerably the worse for wear at its outer edge. In water it spreads into an almost transparent oval around the body. They can admit a little of the current under one part of it and let it out at another, and in this manner travel across the plain without ever getting high enough off the bottom to be tumbled. Under the roof the frill hangs in pleats. Small things fall out of it. The person picks through these in the lap, eating some and putting others back outside. A white snail gets particularly careful treatment. They tear a strip from a broken rush leaf and hold the snail to it until it takes hold of its own accord.

They make very little use of their voices on the plain. The pads of the fingers are their chief means of learning what is coming. A hand laid flat picks up the beginning of the noise; by lifting the fingers one at a time they can tell which seam is about to open. They like the narrow, shrill ones. What comes out of these besides water is a soft yellow curd, the growth of the dark miles below, squeezed through in threads so fine that the water has to be full of them before it looks the least bit cloudy. The worn appearance of the frill is accounted for by several thousand little mouths along its margin. Each has a stiff lip and a row of teeth like eyelashes. While the person rests, the lips go on working for a time, combing the threads into little hanks and drawing them in. The mouth in the face is for larger food and, under a roof, for talk. It has a full low voice, a voice you would be pleased to hear from the next room.

It is the children who make it necessary to have houses. A grown person can manage well enough in a country where the reach of an arm varies. They know better than to jump for anything and will wait half a day with the fingers open for something that is coming into the hand. A baby has no such patience. It shuts its hand on what it sees. Out in the wet this may be a bit of rush ten basins away, and it may go on holding to it when the basins draw apart again. The little finger bones of the very young are soft, and almost every adult has a finger with a long thin place in it, the mark of some early enthusiasm. There are worse things to take hold of than a rush.

A baby lives at first against the breast, well up inside the frill. In the shelter it is allowed out on the floor between the bowls. This is why the old floors shine so, even in the corners where a seated person could hardly have worn them smooth. The baby goes round and round slapping at the stone, delighted at the short journey from the intention to the blow. Its two elders sit opposite and send things back and forth over its head. There is a cup, very often. They make them of the greenish lining of the fissures, peeled out wet and allowed to curl as it dries. One person holds the cup and the other pours water from a fold in the wrist. There is a peculiar luxury in pouring. Outside, a stream leaving the lip of a vessel may thin to nothing on the way to a mouth, or fall into it with a force quite out of proportion to the small tilt one has given the cup. Inside, they pour from as high as the roof permits. The baby looks up through the little clear pillar with its mouth open.

Not all the shelters are in use at once. A pair about to be delivered of a child will try several. They put the tips of their longest fingers together across the middle of the floor and keep them there through the change of rain. If the fingers slip past the first joint, or if daylight comes between the pads, they move on. Occasionally one finds a house in which a thin wedge has been driven under one of the three upright stones. How anybody knew to do that is a question to which the present occupants have no answer. They keep the wedge clean of grit and do not disturb it.

By the time a child is ready to leave, it has learned the nicks on the sill. Each is the width of a finger and each is worn on the underside, where it cannot be seen. When the three go out together, one elder stays on the threshold while the other goes ahead with the child. At first they are all close enough to touch. Then a few inches of pink stone open between them, with water washing over it in long brown gusts, and the one at the door seems to have spent the better part of a life sitting there alone. The child is turned to look. Above the moving water there is a small stationary darkness, less conspicuous than a bed of rushes. Someone in it has a hand on a nick. The child is allowed to reach back and find the hand.

That is the last thing they teach on dry stone. The rest has to be learned belly down in the din, with the yellow threads catching in one's new teeth and the wonderful shapes of the hills rising and coming to pieces overhead. For years afterward a traveler caught out at the meeting of the rains will turn up at an empty shelter and put a hand unerringly on the place where a particular finger fitted. Sometimes the finger is there.
