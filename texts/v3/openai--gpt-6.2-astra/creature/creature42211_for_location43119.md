The pale ones are almost all leg when they come out of their tubes. Inside, one sees a blunt little face and what look like two rather dignified hands. Outside, there is the whole business of getting the hind hooks off the sill, three on one side and then three on the other, while the forelegs are already feeling for a purchase on the blue ice. Between the two lots of legs the body hangs in a slack curve. A tenant crossing a so-called street is more like a person going hand over hand under a table. Its brown streak goes with it, bent in the middle: yesterday’s fall of blades, still passing through.

A house in the young quarter will sometimes walk. It moves less than its own width in a day. The tenant adds grit at the end toward the seam and takes it off the other end, fetching a particularly satisfactory grain all the way round the outside for use again. There is a black grain with a white vein through it which has traveled in this fashion from the edge of the hollow to very nearly the middle. Once two tubes touch, this kind of progress is over. What the neighbor does at the shared wall can be felt quite distinctly, down to the small repeated knock of a grain being seated. The eggs are kept on that side, out of the draft from either door.

The red swimmers have no use for a door. They hang off the quarter in clear water, far enough apart that each has room for a stroke. Most of their redness is in a single spot. As a swimmer turns, the spot disappears behind its clear body and comes back surprisingly bright, so a dozen of them at their rowing give the impression of a much larger gathering. They take the bits the tenants lose in eating. An entire blade is an embarrassment to a swimmer. It gets hold of one edge and rows, and the blade comes round across its face; it changes its hold and the blade comes round again. Sometimes it has to wait for a tenant to take the other edge before it can tear its mouthful free.

As for the milk worm, there is considerably more of it than comes out at any one door. While the head is inspecting a porch, a length of the middle may be seen through a breach four houses away, and at a third place an unsuspecting tenant is laying a grain against what it takes to be a whitish patch of wall. The worm eats the fine stuff that collects behind the lining of a tube. It puts the tenants to what seems an unnecessary amount of trouble, since it would fit past any of them if it went on edge. But to get at the lining it has to lie flat. After it passes, the insides of the houses are clean enough for light to come through the amber grains.

At the back of the hollow the youngster has still only the one grain. It hatched with feet quite as serviceable as its mother’s and with nothing whatever to stick a stone to except stone. This is the difficulty with the first grain. Held flat to the ceiling, the chip looks splendid and falls as soon as the legs come away. Turned up on its narrow side, it catches between two ribs of ice. The youngster lets go with one foot, then another. For a while it hangs beside its stone, the two of them separately fastened to the bay. A scrap of brown blade comes within reach. The youngster catches it and, having as yet no inside to retire to, eats its first meal out on the step.
