The first thing you might notice is a place in the fringe where your fingers will go in quite easily and will not come out. There is no great force holding them. It is like having put your hand in the pocket of a coat which somebody has since put on. If you wait, the whole pocket moves a little way up your wrist, loosens, and goes off among the roots. It leaves a very definite sensation of having had your fingers counted.

The animal is mostly hem. There is a small thick part, about the size of a plum stone, which contains the mouth and most of the other things it has only one of. From this a thin skirt extends on every side. A full-grown one, spread out, would just cover a supper plate. All round the skirt at very short intervals there are feet, each with a little cleft in the sole. The cleft will take a drinking hair, or the edge of a mat, or the lip of one of the smaller cups. The feet work very well separately. It is possible to be walking in several directions at once, and the animal often is, until some one direction proves worth taking and the rest of it gradually comes along.

It spends the dry part of the year folded. It will have twelve feet on a root, four in the water, a few busy turning over bits of the iron mat, and all the remainder fastened up and down its own flanks. In this condition it has plenty of outside but no particular shape. The pocket you put your hand into was very likely being used to hold a shell. It takes a long time to open a shell with nothing but a number of small soft feet. The animal is not obliged to give up its other occupations while it does so. When at last the shell breaks, it stops everything. For the space of the returning sound there is not a movement anywhere in it. Then the little thick part travels along the underside of the skirt to where the meat is.

There are usually nine or ten of these animals in the hollow. This is much less animal than it sounds. You can follow the edge of one for a considerable distance among the cups and arrive at a mouth you have already met farther down the wall. Two will sometimes take hold of each other and go on feeding. Their feet fit one another as well as anything else there. They come apart without difficulty, though seldom at the same moment at both ends, so that for a time one goes about with a long, rather inconvenient acquaintance trailing from it.

What they do on the floor is different. The first water to stand there is hardly enough to put a shine on a shoe. At that depth a shrimp thrown out of a cup can kick a long way but cannot turn itself over. The skirts come down before the shrimp do. They travel edgewise through the wet, with that little gathering motion by which a person draws a blanket across a bed without waking the sleeper. Out on the stone they let go of themselves. It must be a considerable change, after a year spent with one part of the body always touching another, to have nothing against the whole upper surface but air.

They make for the three humps. One foot is sufficient to hold a place on the crown; the rest of the skirt lies downstream. As the water deepens it lifts the skirt, which bellies out and stands off the stone on the one foot. Shrimp strike it from underneath. Each blow raises a little tent in the skin. Most of the tents run straight out to the hem and disappear. Some go round and round, growing smaller, until the mouth gets there. With a hand laid very lightly on the upper surface you could follow the whole business, including the passage of a shrimp that got away because your hand was there. It would feel like a swallow going the wrong way in your throat.

They eat remarkably little of what passes under them. Their other business at the humps takes up most of the flood. On the second or third night the clefts in certain feet open wider than usual, and let out small hard things like grains of uncooked rice. These sink. When the water begins to go, the skirts settle over them. The thin crescents behind the humps are the last places in the hollow where a skirt can be taken up by the water, turned right over, and laid down again without a crease. By morning all the hard grains are on the animals’ backs. By the following evening they have feet.

A young one has only six. They are nearly the whole of it. It can walk from the back of its parent onto a snail and spend a month travelling a distance it will be able, when grown, to reach across without stirring. The good thing about a snail is that it always ends up somewhere damp. The bad thing is the upper staple. There the force of the water coming into the world is sometimes enough to wash a young animal off its snail and send it down through the whole colony, catching hold and losing hold a hundred times before it lands. Some of the very big skirts near the warm seam have never been anywhere else. They have grown up through the snails like cloth through a hedge, and when they go down to the flood a length of wall that has been quiet all year begins to drip aloud.

Afterwards the adults take several days getting back into the fringe. There is less and less to lift them. First they have to carry the mouth, then the weight of the skirt itself; finally even a foot, once set down flat, has to be peeled up from one side. The last few paces leave a faint smooth track. This is some of the greasiness on the floor. At the very edge, within reach of the roots, there is often a long pause. One small cleft takes a hair and shuts. Another finds the rim of a cup. The mouth, which has been riding for days in the middle like a stone in a sling, goes slowly off to one side. By the time the last foot comes away from the floor there is quite a lot happening in the roots, and nowhere in particular that you could put your hand and say the animal had gone.
