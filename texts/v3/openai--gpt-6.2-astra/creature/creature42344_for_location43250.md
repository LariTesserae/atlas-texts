The last thing out of the mug is generally a little foot. It takes hold of the white enamel just inside the rim, lets go, takes hold again. The woman has learnt to hold the mug still. Presently a creature the length of an apple pip gets over the edge and hangs from it by that one foot, its other seven gone quite colorless in the light. A drop gathers about it. The weight of the drop does what all the rinsing failed to do.

There are a great many of these little octopuses on the shelf. An empty limpet shell with one edge a little higher than the other is quite likely to have one under it. So is the curled skin of a fruit. They have a liking for things that are dark on one side and can be lifted from below. Very early in the morning, before the birds come out, one can see a whole company of apparently windblown leaves making slow headway in different directions through perfectly still water.

They ride out the first hours of daylight in these lodgings. One arm goes abroad. There is a fine sense of taste in it; it travels quite knowingly along a groove in the rock, passing over the living limpets and stopping at the dead one that has begun to come loose in its shell. The arm shortens. The shell comes a little way downhill, then quite suddenly all the way under the leaf, which gives a heave. A shrimp in excellent health may stand a hair's breadth from the tip of that arm with nothing to fear. It is the loose, the cracked, the just dead that these small creatures can afford to take. There is scarcely a dead thing left by the time the light gets strong enough to see them at work.

One old female lives in the middle staple. She would fill the woman's mug very comfortably now. There is a fault in the firing, a long bubble in the ceramic which has worn open on the inner curve, and she can get into this with surprisingly little of herself left over. Usually all that shows is an eye the color of the unglazed clay inside the break. Along the back of the bubble she has fastened her eggs. They hang on short stems in separate bunches, white in the shade, quite pink when the sun reaches in. She runs the small ends of her arms through them. A grain of silt comes away; the whole bunch nods for a minute after she has passed on to the next.

At the turn she has water enough to keep them clean without working. The first sign of the opening, earlier than any change one can see in the gap, is the way all the little bunches lean out together. Then for a while her narrow room has a prospect of the undershelf. There is no sun down there. What light there is comes in sideways through the other two staples, so that the shadows of the worms lie immensely long across the silt. A ray going over puts out a whole window of it. The female puts an arm down among the worms and leaves it there in the cool. Sometimes a young ray settles on it. She waits till the ray moves of its own accord.

Her young come loose a few at a time. For perhaps a minute the new one holds to the empty egg skin, a fleck with two disproportionately good eyes. In one eye there is the bright floor with the bird legs coming and going on it. In the other there is the long dim bed of silt. A pulse of water goes through the bunch and the little one is off, down among the roots or up into the light according to which way the bunch was leaning. The ones that go below have been seen again. When fresh water comes welling out at the first staple it sometimes brings up a grown octopus, milky brown all over, with bits of mica stuck to the suckers. It feels for the ceiling that ought to be there. In the absence of a ceiling it pulls a leaf down over itself and is shortly indistinguishable from the ones that went the other way.

Toward the end of the opening the female has a busy time. Bits of weed driven through by the feet of the birds catch on the rough lip of her room. She rolls them out in little wads. She eases away the disc of a ray that has come to rest too close and is cutting off her water. Once, when the woman had set the mug down hard and chipped it, a green scale of enamel washed in among the eggs. That took most of an afternoon to work out. She carried it some distance from the staple, feeling the ground ahead of her with the arm that held the chip. At a deep turn of the gray band she let it fall. It is there still, green side down, with three young limpets established on the white.

After the tide the old one draws herself in till nothing shows. The little ones under their leaves have stopped moving. From the path there are the six or seven distinct trees again, the clean spaces between them, the woman carrying something green away through the reeds. Only by getting down very low, with one's cheek almost in the water, can one see the leaves along the bottom lifting at one edge and falling, each at the rate of the small animal breathing underneath.
