One of the clean tracks ends under a stone that has never had a snail on it. It is a flake of limestone as broad as a thumbnail, held a little way off the floor. Look along the gap and there are six more flakes behind it, each overlapping the next like the plates of a small stove. The last is so far down the side of the cup that it looks blue. Now and then the first flake tips up. A fringe of short white legs takes hold of the clean track and pulls the whole length forward about the breadth of a hair.

It is a woodlouse of sorts, though much larger than the ones that fall out of wet timber. The plates are its own. On an old one they have a bloom of lime, with the same black pinpricks that occur in the limestone, but just after it has shed them it is the color of the inside of a mussel shell. It does not eat what the snail eats. It follows the snail for the slime, passing a pair of little brushes over the track and then drawing them through its mouth. Where a young snail has gone into the salt water and turned back, the two passes make quite a good meal. The woodlouse stays there long after the snail has blundered on, working at the turn.

There are more of them than the tracks would lead you to suppose. Most live upside down. Every cup that is the roof of another has, on its lower face, a population going about its business within the compass of the cup above. A grit of food that sinks through a hole meets a hand coming round the lip for it. This accounts for a curious hesitation of falling things here. A bit of worm goes down an inch, stops quite dead, then goes on down with a small crescent missing from one side.

At the tick of the rod the woodlice keep still. They have nothing to gain by swimming up with the fish; a woodlouse off the stone swims on one side in a wide, useless circle. What they do instead is let go with all but the hindmost pair of legs. When the underground water comes, it lifts them like trapdoors. The first harsh stuff goes racing under them. Then they come down on the softer drift and there they stay, with the edges of their plates lifted a little by the quantity of dinner underneath. A shrimp will sometimes pull a tail of worm out from under a plate. The plate comes down on its whiskers. For a moment the two creatures are joined, the shrimp perfectly clear and the other indistinguishable from the ground.

Under the dipping roof there is one with young. You can see them best on a falling tide. She waits on the underside of a ridge until the bright line of the water has gone past her; then she turns on her side in the wet and opens a seam along her belly. What comes out looks at first like a row of teeth on the stone. Each tooth unfolds a surprising length of legs. For the first few minutes they walk on their mother, whose back, after all, offers very much what they will have to get used to: the long slope, the transverse cracks, a little edible dirt in the hollows. One gets as far as her head and puts a brush into her eye. She shuts it.

By the next low water they have spread over the ridge. Their plates are still clear. Through each one you can see a single gray stroke, the first meal going the length of the gut. They are particularly numerous by a rivet dimple that holds one drop. The water there is fresh enough to keep them out, and the rim stays damp enough to suit them; they go round it in a loose procession, polishing a pale ring. When rain comes through the rivet, the hanging drop strikes the standing one squarely. The ring empties at once. Then, from what looks like solid stone a little farther down, a dozen pairs of brushes begin feeling their way back.
