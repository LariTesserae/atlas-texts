In the lull before the tide it is possible to hear a sound very like a person drawing a wet finger round the lip of a glass. It comes from inside the gray humps. There is usually a little fan of grit below the place where it is loudest, and in the fan, two grooves about a hand’s width apart. By the time the tide has turned the grooves have filled up with hunters on their way inland. A hunter with a new plate is tender all along its underside and will go a considerable way round to avoid getting up on an edge. The grooves suit it very well.

What comes out of the rock is at first only the two little plows that made the grooves. They are ivory colored, blunt, quite unlike claws. They emerge side by side, separate, and take a purchase in the grit. For a while nothing happens. Then a piece of what looked like the back wall of the hollow comes down between them, folding as it comes. There is a head in the fold. It has the pinched, aggrieved look of a face on which someone has tied a kerchief too tightly. The head can turn freely inside the fold, and does so before the rest of the animal ventures out, looking first at one plow and then the other as if to make sure both belong to it.

It is a bigger animal than the opening seems to warrant. In a narrow place the ribs slip past one another; out on the shingle they settle with little separate jerks, until the body is broad enough to shade a dinner plate. Salt held in the wrinkles of the back gives it the exact grain and color of the rock. Underneath it is a lovely dark blue. This shows particularly well where a mother has young clinging to her. Each young one holds up a little tent of skin with its back, and there is a blue crescent of clean skin ahead of it and another behind. They ride with their plows tucked in. One occasionally loses its hold and hangs by the mouth for several yards before it finds its feet again.

On their infrequent journeys these animals do not go to the deep basin with the hunters. They stop at the first of the pools to fill, sometimes at a place where there is no pool apparent. One lies down, puts its head flat on the dust, and slips the plows in to their full length. Its whole back gives a heave. What comes up is a cake of salt and silt, quite neatly balanced, dripping from the edges into the hole it has left. The underside is a mass of pink threads. Exposed to the air the threads shorten into little fat commas, and it is these the animal eats, turning the cake slowly past its mouth. Some of the commas let go in time. They fall into the water and uncoil there, extending a crown of fine hairs and rowing very fast straight down.

The young slip off to feed in the hole. They are still too soft to lift a cake for themselves; they work along the cut face, one on top of another where the feeding is good, until there is a shelf under the crust on which half a dozen can stand abreast. Their mother has generally gone on to open a second hole by then. At her call they come tumbling out quite white with silt. It is her plows she calls with. There is a ridge inside each one, just where it passes the cheek, and she draws the two ridges slowly through the corners of her shut mouth. Out in the open it makes a small squeak scarcely audible over the fizz of the shingle. It takes a rock hollow to give it that full singing note.

There are not many cakes of just the right thickness. Where one of the animals has been feeding for a long time the floor of the basin is paved with them, pink side up, the early ones already beginning to grow beards again. Water gets in between them on its way back through the stones. For an hour after the neighboring basins have gone dull with salt this one still shines in a pattern of crooked lines, and the little fliers come up out of the lines in such numbers that the fleece in the lee of the nearest hump comes loose and drifts downwind to meet them.

It is generally dark again before the last family gets home. The young go into the hollow first. For all the haste they made to get back onto their mother they show very little inclination to get off, and she has to scrape them gently against the lintel. Then she turns round and backs in over them. The folds go up, the ribs give way, the face withdraws between the plows. A little gritty water comes trickling down the fan. By this time the hunters are hammering again. In a minute or two a pair of grazers will come apart on a stone close by and one of them will travel across the closed hollow, taking a narrow band of the day’s growth off the animal’s back without ever finding out where the rock leaves off.
