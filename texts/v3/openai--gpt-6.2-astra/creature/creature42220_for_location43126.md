On the pink stone the track makers are much the same pink as the stone. Where a vein of white runs through it there will be a few with a white dash across the back. It takes them about half a season to cross a vein like that, and rather longer to lose the dash. Their colour comes from what they eat. They put a lip down over the gritty fur on the stone and draw it in, fur and grit together, leaving a shallow runnel with two raised edges. Very old tracks can be told by the little sedges that have come up in these edges, in pairs, for miles.

On the underside each has a foot as soft and intricately wrinkled as the skin of scalded milk. On top there is a hard knob, worn flat, around which its flesh gathers in a loose cuff. The knob is the part that is there first. If one prises up a very young one, which is easily done with a grass blade, there is practically nothing to it but the knob and a damp mark that it is a shame to have disturbed. The smaller sort of up-rain bead forms in just such a damp mark. All through their lives beads come welling up under them, a little off centre, lifting one side of the foot at a time. The old ones go with a perceptible list.

They are commonest under the grey animals. A grey animal lies up for much of the year among the pale fingers of the trees. Its deep sides give a young grove something to lean against until the trunks thicken. In return it can sleep there without drifting out to the rim. It eats the long fronds that brush against its mouth, taking them in from the tip until it feels the small tug that means it has come to the root. Then it lets go. On its back, with several inches of good black soil to grow in, there are some plants not found on the bed of the sea at all. One has a coppery oval leaf. The grub that eats it takes a single mouthful from the edge and rolls up inside the leaf to finish growing.

The grub is one of the few creatures here that has any great distance to go in a hurry. It must get from the grove where its leaf opens to a flower on another back, and both backs may be under water by the time it arrives. In its winged state it looks like a flake of mica. You see it in the air and then, without seeing it alight, you see it a handspan down in the water, still flying, with a bright flaw along each wing where a little air has bent but not broken. Its flower grows down into the soil. Only the scent gets out, especially in wet weather: a smell like black wood freshly notched.

The people who set the posts knew that smell very well. They still come this way, though they have no use now for the landing. Their boats had to be given up when the river rose. For a while they went to the trouble of carrying them up into the water on poles. This was hard to do with a laden boat, and a child left in one for the length of time it took to fetch a second basket might by then be a considerable way upstream. In the end they took the boats apart. There are houses under the lifted river now with a narrow curved piece of timber over each door, so low that a tall visitor has to duck a little to go in.

The houses are made of the yellow moss. This sounds less substantial than it is. The moss grows a new top every year and has, underneath, a close brown felt through which one can push a finger only with difficulty. They cut it in long pieces and turn it on edge, green side out. A house just built has stripes. In an old one all the stripes have grown together, even across the door, which has to be found by passing a hand along the wall. Indoors it is cool. The light comes up from the floor, in shallow bowls of water in which the eyelash plants go on shining for a day or two after they are picked.

They are long-armed people, broad in the pad of the thumb from doing so much work overhead. At breakfast someone reaches up into the river with a forked stick and winds down a length of weed, much as one might wind wool. They like the tender last turn of it best. A person eating alone will stop winding just short of that turn, so the weed will go on growing; where several are eating, there is usually someone who says to go on. Then they have to leave that reach of river alone for a year. There are little domestic quarrels here that can be read quite plainly in the ceiling.

Children go out on the shoals at evening with pieces of old boat fibre and lower them into the tracks on the warm stone. They are hoping for a track maker to come up on the end. It never does. The game is to see whose thread, laid out carefully in all the turns of a single track, will reach the farthest when pulled straight. Once a girl kept at it until the water came back. She had run out of fibre and used the cord of her tunic, and then her sister's, and at the very head of the track she found the animal itself, as wide as the palm of her hand, with the dash of a white vein still faintly visible. She stayed to see which side of the foot would lift. By the time it did she was wet to the waist.

It was this girl, grown rather old, who noticed what happens to the copper leaves at the rim. Most go over empty, the bright insect long since out and away. A few, bitten late in the day, go down with the grub still in them. From the last post, on a clear evening, one can see them turning, showing first the red upper side and then the pale rolled lip, until they pass into the cloud. She used to sit there after her hands got too stiff for winding weed. Once, quite distinctly, she saw a grey back break through the top of the cloud below. It stayed in the light a good while. There were little trees on it, but from that height she could not make out the colour of the leaves.
