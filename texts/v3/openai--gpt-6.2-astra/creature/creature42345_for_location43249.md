The woman who takes the parcels is a mender of sacks. There is nearly always something in her hands when the carrier draws up, and he waits while she sticks her needle in the stuff and rolls the stuffed part of her apron down from under her chin. He used to drop the parcels to her. Then somebody sent for a pair of spectacles, and thereafter everything, even a twist of boot nails, passed from hand to hand. To the carrier she is the most accessible person in town. To her sister, who lives almost directly under her worktable but opens on another court, she is a good five minutes away.

She knows a surprising number of people by the tops of their heads. She can tell which of the two brothers from the lime yard is coming for a parcel well before he turns up the stair. One goes a little thin at the crown; the other has the habit, acquired under a low lintel in his own house, of ducking his head on the third step from the top. She gives him his parcel without saying anything about this. She would be rather sorry if he stopped.

The children find the town a less confined place than their elders do. A child can get from the fig court to the bottom of the carrier's stair by a way no grown person considers a way at all. It involves going on hands and knees behind the wool frame and coming out very dusty at the head. On the other hand there are great stretches of smooth inviting floor where a child would no more set foot than on the face of a clock: the sweep of clay in front of a thrower's bench, the patch where a woman has laid out the pieces of a coat with stones on the corners. A stranger with the best intentions will plant himself in the middle of one of these places. It is generally a child who takes him by the fingers and moves him a little to the left.

They are not all potters. There is a man who makes the thin boards on which the wet ware travels, and does quite well making nothing else. His daughter went up to the pasture to work with the sheep and has stayed there. When she comes down on a visit she brings the smell of the lanolin with her, very noticeable in a room where usually the strongest smell is that of a board newly planed. Her father keeps a chair for her which is greatly in everybody's way for most of the year. People have suggested hanging it from a peg, as is done with chairs in several houses. He says he would only have to take it down again. She comes at midsummer and for a week after the rams go in.

There are quarrels, particularly over light. The people of the middle quarter still speak of a winter in which a dealer stacked empty oil jars three tiers high along the top of his wall. They were entirely on his own wall, and there was not a jar among them that his neighbor could have put a finger on without reaching across. Nevertheless she had to carry her sewing into the street at two in the afternoon. In the end the dealer spent half an hour in her room trying to take a splinter out of his thumb. That evening he took the top tier down. They never became friends. When asked what sort of man he was she would say, quite fairly, that he had taken the jars down the very day he saw fit to do it.

The water warden, whose tapping gives him the appearance of exercising a solitary office, is actually subject to more advice than anyone in town. There are women of eighty who remember what the cistern sounded like before its lining was renewed and consider the present note deceptive. A small boy once followed him for a month, tapping after him with a spoon. The warden endured this until the boy began stopping at a different level and frowning. Then he gave him a proper stick and sent him to sound the settling jars. The boy is a carter now, mostly away, but if he is at home when the north seam starts to run thick they still send for him. He can tell by ear which jar has got a bellyful of silt.

At present a young couple in the brown quarter are waiting for the house at the braced door frame. They have been married long enough to have accumulated a bedstead, a chest, a cradle in which there is already a child, and six large dishes of a pattern the wife liked very much when she was sixteen. All this is in her mother's back room. The husband works at the digging. He has an exact idea of the quantity of his future kitchen that he has wheeled away, though when his wife asks how big the kitchen will be he spreads his arms to a different width each time.

She went out there one evening with the child, who had been cross indoors. There was a little wind at the eastern end, finding its way down through the barley, and he quieted as soon as she put him against her shoulder. Her husband had left his jacket on one of the braces. For the first time she could make out which side of the door was to be inside. She stood there considering it until one of the basket men asked if she wanted to get through.

“No,” she said, and then, because there was no particular reason not to, she did. The child caught hold of the doorpost as they passed. He had a good grip for so small a person; she had to go back a step and loosen his fingers one at a time.
