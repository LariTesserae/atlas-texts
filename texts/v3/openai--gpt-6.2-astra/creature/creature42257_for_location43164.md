There is a conger in the room, though on most days the length of conger to be seen amounts to about six inches. It lives under the foot of the middle pier. The brick there has worn out of its joints and gone away a piece at a time, leaving the concrete standing on a sort of heel. The space under it is deep enough for the eel to go in, turn, and put its head out alongside its tail. There are two ways out of this place. According to which pump is working, a blunt grey head shows at one opening or the other, always on the quiet side.

Close to, the head is rather handsome. The cheeks are plump, the upper jaw projects a little, and there is a pale rim to the large dark eye. Behind the corner of the mouth a small crescent opens and shuts, letting each mouthful of water out. A prawn sometimes stands on the eel between this crescent and the eye. It makes busy excursions over the skin, feeling ahead of itself with a pair of white threads, quite untroubled by what happens to prawns a little farther forward.

Not everything in the intake makes the distinction the eel makes between one day and the next. The long strip of dog whelk eggs low down on the grille has had the same aspect through a good many changes of pump. Each little upright capsule, the colour of a grain of boiled barley, has a lid at the top. Some morning the lids will be gone and there will be whelks no bigger than crumbs travelling over the iron. The youngest limpets on the first pier make a journey of a few inches in the dark and come back to the same place. One has a bit broken out of its shell edge that fits a grain of quartz sticking out of the pier. For these animals the country between the roof and floor is of a considerable extent. The conger can cross it with one stroke.

It does this sometimes at night. It is much longer than the space it comes out of would lead you to suppose. First the thick round neck comes sliding over the brick, then a quite unexpected quantity of level grey flank, with a row of pale pinpricks down it; then the whole business narrows to a point and the hole is empty. The eel goes over the floor with its head just clear of it. At the sill it turns on its side to pass under the ladder. The prawns go up in a body. For a second or two afterwards a single prawn will still be letting itself go backwards by violent jumps in a part of the room the eel has already left.

What it finds on these rounds varies. There is a little butterfish that spends the day doubled up in a broken bottleneck. It can do this now; by autumn it will have to find something else. There are shore crabs with an excellent purchase on the brick until the week they have to get out of their old skins. In that week the remains of the ladder are particularly well inhabited. What look like crabs sit out in full view on the rungs, light enough to shift when a prawn lands on them, while the actual crabs, soft as wet gloves, are squeezed into the slots from which the rungs have rusted loose. The conger tries the slots with its nose. Sometimes a scrap of green shell comes turning out of the grille on the making tide, with several legs attached to it and not a morsel taken from it. This is no indication one way or the other of what has become of the crab.

During the change of pump the conger seldom comes all the way out. While the grit is still falling it draws its head in. On the other side of the pier, where there has been nothing visible but two anemones and a rusty nut, the same head presently appears. Fish which have held their places there for hours by making small, economical movements of their fins find themselves slipping sideways. Most turn at once into the new run of water. Occasionally one comes broadside against the pier, and there is a short hollow clap, quite distinct from the tack of the bar. A scatter of scales goes through the room. In the strips of light at the grille each scale shows up separately, finer and brighter than the grit. The gull above sees the fish that get out. Of the one that stays behind there is, even in that bare room, very little to see.
