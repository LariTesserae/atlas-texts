There is one animal you are very unlikely to see from either end. Its back is the colour of the root seen wet, and its belly is the colour of the root seen dry. It generally has some part of itself in each condition. If you take the two parts for one animal it looks quite wrong, like a bit of leaf half turned over; more often you see just a brown stroke in the green, and some way off a little pale joint that might belong to anything.

A good time to find it is when a drop has struck one of the small flat snails and tipped it up. The snail's foot keeps its hold along one edge. Under the other edge there is now a wedge of quite clean space, with the soft underside of the shell for a roof. Before the snail has settled you will see two pale hands laid on the rim. They are feelers really, but they have that way hands have of taking the measure of a thing before anything is done with it. The little animal draws itself forward until its head is under the shell. It does not eat the snail. It eats the fine stuff caught in the fold where the snail cannot put its tongue. The shell comes down on it. For a while the snail goes on grazing with what looks like a short brown thread coming out from under its house.

When it comes free you can see how it manages. It is thin in the middle and wide at either end. The back end, which is the wider, has a soft rim like the edge of a limpet, and seals so well to the wet nap that the front end can be knocked a surprising distance without the animal being carried away. A drop takes it in the face; it streams out straight below its hold; the pull comes off and it gathers up again in little sideways folds. There are legs in these folds. On a dry crossing it walks in the ordinary way on six of them, rather high off the ground, as if it disliked getting dust on its stomach. In the flutes you would say it had no legs at all.

It makes the dry crossing for the sake of its eggs. These are laid in the white places where a new knob of root is just beginning to lift the felt. A knob no bigger than a grain of sago will do. On the uphill side the green still fits close; on the downhill side there is a little overhang, clean underneath, that the spray cannot quite get into. The animal goes round the knob testing with its feelers, sometimes lying flat and putting its head under, sometimes standing away from it with one feeler on the white skin as though it could feel the root growing. The eggs go in a single layer. There may be room for five and there may be room for only two. Each has a spot of green in it almost from the first. What looks, a week later, like the felt closing over the white place is the young lying with their heads out.

They come out backwards. The broad end adheres at once, so that the first thing a young one does on leaving the egg is to get stuck to the spot where it was born. For a run or two it lies there being washed. Its colour comes on quickly. A pale one under a white knob is easy enough to keep in view; a brown one touching green at one end and white at the other is almost impossible. You may think you have watched one for a whole afternoon without its stirring, until a snail tips over halfway down the next flute and there are the hands.

Only in a long spell of dry weather do you get any idea how many there are. The bladders go on working, but with wide intervals, and a run that starts briskly may give out halfway along the arch. The upper parts of the flutes lose their shine. The little animals go down to the well then, walking, and take their places behind the brown seam of fan mouths. There is a damp streak up the back of each standing animal where water has climbed through the hairs. One of the newcomers sets its mouth to this streak and stays there drinking, its six feet planted tidily on the bank. Seen close, it is a ridiculous arrangement: the one below with all its apparatus spread to the water, and the one above receiving a drink through the back of its neck. There are sometimes three in a row, the last very small, with scarcely anything reaching it. In the reflected light you can see it swallowing.

At the first full run they let go. For a moment there are little brown bodies walking uphill all over the floor, taking the shortest way home, across the bare white knobs and through the open space between the liverworts. Then the spray reaches them. What you see after that is three green flutes with the water lying along them, and here and there, for no very evident reason, a flat snail lifting a little on one side.
