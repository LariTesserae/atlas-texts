A little way under the bench, where two pieces of river gravel have fallen together like the halves of a partly opened book, there lives a pale shrimp. Laid on the brass shelf it would scarcely cover the worn head of one of the screws. It has never been as high as the shelf, or even as high as the underside of the paving. There is generally water under the bench when the square above has been dry for weeks.

It lies on its side in the cleft. This is not a posture of distress. It is built rather deep and very narrow, and most of the ways open to it are slits. It goes along with one cheek to the ceiling, trying the way ahead with two long feelers. Where there is room it turns over in a leisurely fashion and proceeds on the other cheek. Its shell is so clear that the last meal shows through it, a short brown stroke which has lately acquired, at one end, a speck of fig red.

The first news of a rise down here is food moving the wrong way. A shred of leaf that has hung for days from the hairy underside of a root lifts by its free end and stands up. Fine brown stuff comes out of the gravel. The shrimp stays where it is and combs this stuff from its feelers with its forefeet, drawing each feeler through to the tip as a woman in the loft draws a thread between her lips. When the pumps take hold, it hooks its feet into the root. The root saws back and forth a little. This is the largest movement in a life that includes neither the journey of the river nor the lifting of the white sheet, though it occupies a place exactly between the two.

There are young folded against its belly. For some time after they hatch they ride there, all facing different ways in a pouch no larger than a grass seed. Once the water eases, one of them works itself clear. For a moment it clings to a joint of its mother's leg, so nearly the color of the leg that the two seem to be one limb with an unusual number of intentions. Then it steps off onto the stone. A grain of black mica which the mother must go round has space beneath it for this one to walk upright. It goes in there. Overhead the bench has stopped ticking. The mother passes a feeler across the face of the mica, gathers something too small to see, and eats it.
