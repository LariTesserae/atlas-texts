If the cup is left on its side, it is generally upright again by morning. This is not housekeeping. There is something down there that likes the water under the cup, and the easiest way of getting at it is to put a shoulder against the rim and walk forward. The cup rolls until the chain pulls it over onto its foot. What is left on the stone is a crescent of water with a perfectly dry spot in it, the size of a sixpence, where the creature stood to drink.

The people who have seen it disagree principally about its length. The woman at the fish stall saw two pink feet come out of the chain hole, take hold of a scale that had stuck to her shoe, and withdraw with it. She would have said a mouse. A lift man, down on his stomach to look for a dropped washer, saw the same two feet go past him along the foot of the wall, followed at considerable leisure by two more. He put the distance between the pairs at a foot and a half. What stayed with him was the sight, at that distance from his own face, of little elbows. They bent and straightened under a glossy fold of skin like the elbows of a child trying to change its shirt without getting out of bed.

There are, in fact, quite a number of feet. The animal is narrow through the body and made up in short lengths, each with a pair of limbs tucked under it. Ordinarily it walks with only the front and rear pairs out. If the stone is particularly wet, a foot comes down in the middle, takes the weight until the others have found their purchase, and goes up again. Its back is the brown of a pear where a thumb has pressed it. Its belly, which hardly anybody sees, is a beautiful stippled orange. It has a small blunt head with a mouth underneath, excellent for going over the red stone after the rain. Where the dark skin in the gutter is thickest one can see its feeding marks: two clear parallel swipes, then a little untouched island, then two swipes again. The island is where it puts its chin while it chews.

It lives in the space formerly occupied by a lead pipe. The pipe was pulled out through the south kitchen during a refit, leaving a smooth bore in the masonry with a slight uphill turn at the end. The uphill turn matters. Water can get into the first few feet but not as far as the bed, which is made of things brought down from the market in the treads of shoes. Mostly these are threads. When the block was opened for some work on the bathhouse, the men found a wad of purple wool as big as a plum, picked apart and laid lengthwise, with fish scales set along one side of it. One of them took the scales for decoration until he saw that the rough edges of the wool had been caught neatly under them. He put the block back with the hollow face in, as he had found it. He had a ferret at home and knew a made bed when he saw one.

In late summer the feet seen at the hole in the wall are much smaller. The young come out backwards for the first few days. They get a grip with the hindmost pair, telescope out to an astonishing length and then, at the slightest jar, let go. There is just enough spring along the belly to carry the whole arrangement back inside. After a week one will be standing entirely out on the floor, looking rather at a loss, with a piece of purple wool still clinging to it. The old one fetches these wanderers by lying down alongside them. A small one will take hold of almost anything warm with all its feet. On a good evening the mother goes home twice her usual width, walking slowly on account of the chins dragging on either side.

She has no great fear of the trams. At the wooden *tok* she flattens herself, feet out, chin down, in whichever place she happens to be. The soft parts of her settle wonderfully into the irregularities of the stone. Once, when she had stopped over the blue comma, a man could see the shape of the tile rise through her back. He had seen much the same thing happen to a flatfish laid on a key. As the chord dies away she peels up from the head end, a pair of feet at a time. The young have to learn this. For their first few journeys they will be standing up quite cheerfully after the *tok* and then the note comes through and bowls them a little way downhill. There is no harm done. At the bottom they untangle themselves and start back, making for the warm length of their mother stretched out on the stone.

The best view of her is from the wet half of the bench just before the first drops fall. She comes along the diagonal against the direction the water will take, nosing into the pencil line, stopping sometimes to worry at a bit that has dried particularly hard. There is a minute or two in which the cricket is coming the other way. If they meet, the cricket puts both antennae over her and waits. She walks the length of herself under that high arch, as indifferent to it as a woman passing under a line of washing. By the time the rain comes they have exchanged ends of the square.

Very few market people have had that view. What most of them know is a pressure on the outer edge of a shoe, usually in the last quiet interval before the bread flat. She likes to settle there when she has eaten. Any shoe will do that has been on a foot all day; a boot set down empty is of no interest. The person wearing it gradually becomes aware of something on the other side of the leather answering the small unsteadiness of the foot, adjusting itself whenever the weight shifts. They wait until the wooden knock. She will have laid herself flat then, and it is possible to get up without treading on her. Occasionally a newcomer tries in the dark to feel what has been leaning there. A hand goes down beside the shoe and stays down rather longer than anyone expects. It takes time, with so many feet, to let go of a finger.
