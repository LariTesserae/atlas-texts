The worn oval is the length of a foot but considerably narrower. A person sitting on the bench can put a heel in it without looking for it. It is the right heel, unless the person is left-handed, in which case there is a little awkwardness about the first few taps. These people have a preference of foot as marked as their preference of hand. A child asleep on somebody’s knee, with both feet dangling, will go on working one foot in its sleep and let the other be quite still.

They are leggy people. One might have expected them to be low and broad, like most things in the parting; but almost all the going that matters to them is done in a very few spacious galleries. They have long shins and a remarkably economical stride. The upper part of the body takes scarcely any part in walking. A cup filled to the brim can be carried in the free hand, and frequently is, since water is found a good way off some of the roads on which it is wanted. From behind, by the unsteady light of a wick, a party going away looks for a time like a row of stationary figures from under whom the floor is being drawn.

The hand on the groove does not slide. The first two fingers walk in it, overtaking one another so lightly that an old road hand can go over a sleeping seam animal without disturbing it. This spares the pads. It also makes it possible to feel which way a chip was struck out of the groove, a useful distinction on waking from a nap by the roadside with no very clear idea which way one has come. Makers cut consistently towards their own settlement. Where two stretches of groove have been made from opposite ends there is an inch or so of peculiarly rough work. People who live about a join can identify a stranger by the small check in the sound of the fingers.

Their eyes are good. A carried light gives a poor impression of them, for the lids come down almost to the lashes and this, together with the rather forward set of the ears, gives the face a suspicious look quite foreign to its ordinary expression. In the old galleries where the spar is plentiful two friends can recognize one another a long way off by turning a little aside and looking past. An intimate accomplishment is to be able to walk beside a friend without the groove and without touching. It requires a fine sense of where the other person intends to put a foot. Lovers bump together a great deal. An old couple can go like that for miles.

Their settlements begin at a lip of wet stone. There is usually a cold joint in a wall, no thicker than a hair, out of which water comes with enough persistence to have made a hanging bead by the time the previous bead has been taken away. A number of joints are gathered by means of little peaked tiles into a covered cistern. One can tell how full it is by speaking into a hole in the cover. Children learn to do this while they are still so small that they have to be lifted to the hole; they tend afterwards to give the depth in the high piping voice in which they first got a reliable answer.

You can be well inside a settlement before you know it. There are no front doors. A family takes a place between two folds of stone, levels the worst of the floor with chips and lays down a thick mat. The mats are made from the black grazers, whose skins, unpromisingly small, will stretch a great way when wet. The joins come out as little hard knots. A child in bed can occupy itself for a long time pressing down one knot with a thumb and feeling, with the other thumb, for the knot that rises. Beyond the mat there will be a food slab, a warm place for putting wet things, a rubbish crack with a well-fitting lid. All these things are private. What is done on them is audible to everybody. It is a fault of upbringing to fall silent in the middle of one’s own conversation because a much more interesting conversation has started on the next mat.

They eat the grey animals, though seldom from the seam where they grow. A strip of spare stone is bedded edge down in a good seam and left until the nap has grown up both sides. Then the whole strip is lifted, put into a tight box and carried home. When the gas gives out the animals loosen their grip and can be shaken off into a dish. Fresh, they have a taste not unlike the smell of the seam, with something salt underneath it. They keep poorly. For a journey they are worked into a dull red paste and baked on the hot side of a pillar. Each cake has a hole made by the cook’s little finger so that a number of them can be strung about the waist. On the long walks, when everything one carries has acquired the temperature of everything else, there is a particular pleasure in finding a cake near the buckle that is still a little warm.

The ivory backs do no work for them, despite a persistent belief among children that enough of the animals, suitably encouraged, could be made to carry a mat with a person on it. They are of use in another way. From time to time a ledge of backs that has lain beside a settlement for longer than anybody remembers begins to part in the middle. A finger’s breadth of bare stone shows through. There need be no haste, but it is wise then to start emptying the cistern into jars. The change of warmth that has disturbed the animals generally means a movement in the wet joint. Sometimes it opens and a household becomes rich in water; more often the bead dwindles until one can put a dry lip to the stone and draw nothing from it. Then the mats are rolled up. People go to live with cousins. The groove still leads to the cistern and the tiles are left in place. Water has been known to come back.

Of the person who made the house of two echoes they know surprisingly little. The fit of the slabs belongs to no very distant period; the bench, on the other hand, is of a spotted stone no longer quarried, and may have been a piece of furniture before it came there. There is an account according to which the maker lost a travelling companion in the chamber and spent the rest of a long life trying to hear two sets of footsteps again. It is a favourite with people who have not been to the house. The note has nothing of a footstep in it. It resembles, if anything, the sound made by a full jar when another full jar is set down very gently against it.

Visitors generally make the mistake of tapping too hard. The room fills with the little, near sounds of grit under the heel, a tremor in the bench, the slap of cloth at the ankle; the note itself is lost. After several attempts the visitor stops to consider, shifts a foot to get up, and hears it perfectly. Some go back to the bench then. Some are satisfied. A surprising number, on the walk home, discover that they can make the sound quite accurately with the mouth, though this is of very little interest to anyone who has not heard the original.

There is no groove to the house. For the last part of the way one must have the company of someone who has been before, and there is a stretch where the floor is so smooth and the air so even in warmth that both people, almost without thinking, put out a hand. This is sometimes the first time in years that a grown person has held a parent by the hand. Neither generally mentions it. When the floor roughens underfoot they let go, and a little farther on the one who knows the way shortens a step, reaches out in the dark, and saves the other a knock on the lintel.
