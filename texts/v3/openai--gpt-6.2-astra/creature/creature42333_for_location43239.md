By dusk the pink animal in the eastern pool has gone round eleven times. On the twelfth it comes to a place where the water has drawn back from the stone. There is a little crescent of grease left high and dry. The animal gets its first two hooks into the crescent and lifts half its body out after them. Seen clear of the water it is not much like a purse. It has a long seam down the middle, with the hooks growing on either side like the teeth of two small combs laid back to back. In the seam something quite black is stirring.

It is a young one. At first the hooks are all that can be made out of it. They take hold of the mother's hooks and it goes along them, hand over hand, until it comes to the stone. For some time the two graze side by side. The young one makes no track that can be seen. You have to watch the track of the mother, which widens a little wherever the young one comes up to her mouth.

There are young like this in the main hollow too. Very few get as far as the stone. The clear beasts have a way of walking with one broad foot laid lightly on a purse's back. The purse goes on scraping the skin, and the beast walks beside it, accommodating itself even to the small backward jerks with which the purse pulls loose a stubborn bit of grease. When the seam opens the foot goes in. In the eastern pool the mother keeps giving those backward jerks long after she has left the dry crescent. Each time, the young one goes over on its side. By morning it has learned to let go a little sooner.

The cloud has settled on the mountain for the night. Beads are running in from both sides of the split. Across the dry interval between the pools one bead comes very slowly, growing at its forward edge and dwindling behind, with no more disturbance of the surface than if a glass lens were being moved over the stone. It brings a white shell, one of yesterday's brood. The shell is still so light that the opening and shutting of its fan turns it head over heels. When the bead touches the eastern pool there is a long shiver of the two skins. The shell tumbles through. For the rest of the night it lies with its lips apart on the red stone and nothing comes out of it.

In the morning a single hair appears between the lips. It draws back; it appears again, with another beside it. The water here is cooler and does not well up as it does in the groove. In the main hollow the white animals have only to open themselves and let breakfast come against them. Here the little fan has to beat. For a while the shell goes backward under the force of its own strokes. Then it comes up against a grain of red stone and stays there. A fleck from the mother's track lifts, hangs for a moment in the water, and travels in a gentle curve into the fan.

With the sun back on the ice field, the whole eastern pond shines like a lens again. There are two tracks in it now, one fine and clear, one so fine that it shows only where it crosses the other. At the crossing there is a minute bright square of bare water. The mother comes to it, finds nothing there to eat, and turns aside. It is her first deviation from the circuit since the pond began. Behind her, in the shelter of the red grain, the white fan keeps beating, and the end of the finer track bends slowly toward it.
