The man who puts down the bowl has a baby tucked inside his shirt. He holds her head when he stoops. It is her bathwater he is catching; the bowl will stand at the back of the hearth until the fat that keeps it from leaking begins to smell of mutton. By then it will be just warm enough. The child on the red sled is his elder daughter. She comes in with one wet knee and a quantity of news which she has to deliver in a whisper, her sister having just gone to sleep.

She has spent most of the afternoon in the company of a boy she has never seen entire. His father's roof comes down below the level of hers, and there is a slit between them through which he can put an arm up to the shoulder. In winter they play at trading things through it. She knows a good deal about him: he has a loose tooth, he can tie a knot in a hair, he is allowed the burnt skin off the porridge on days when his father cooks. Today he has given her something wrapped in a shred of red wool. When her father has got the baby out of his shirt she opens it on his knee. It is the tooth. The little snag of flesh on it has frozen quite stiff. The two of them consider what would be a fair exchange.

There are friendships here which last well into old age on much the same footing. A woman who has to turn sideways now to get through her own door can still pass a platter without tilting it through the slot behind her hearth. For forty years she and the woman on the other side have cooked on alternate days. One likes the fish heads and the other the little crisp tails. When they go to the judgments they sit a long way apart, since one hears well only on the left and the other has to be close enough to see the speaker's mouth. Visitors have taken them for parties to a quarrel. In fact they are both generally there on account of a grandson who sells the same catch to several households while the fish are still swimming about under the ice. It is a nice point at what stage a fish can be said to have been delivered. He has grown tall in the time it has taken to settle it. The salt hollow is well below his chin.

Under his grandmother's hearth, in a pocket of grit into which a little warmth comes down through the stone, there lives a small pale animal with nearly as many legs as a shrimp. It goes on its side. The first time the girl with the sled found one she put it in water and was distressed to see it thrash; on the back of her wet hand it righted itself and went running up under her cuff. There are thousands of them in the notch. They feed on the soft black underside of old hide, leaving the hair and the hard outer grain so nicely intact that the first sign of their work may be a mouse coming through the ceiling in its sleep. The mice are good at catching hold of things on the way down. One hung for half a morning by the tassel of a man's cap while he sat at his work, each of them accounting for the small occasional movements of the other by supposing there was a draft.

The girl has faults as a carrier. She will stop anywhere for a thing like that. But she remembers, without ever having been taught it, whose hands shake and who must have the rope let down another foot; which of the covered lanes has a man asleep in it after a night tending the bath fire; where to knock with the heel of her mitten and where the knock of a bare knuckle is all that can be heard. Once over the roof of the judgment hall she felt the rope go slack behind her. She lay a little while looking up into the white sky. Then she took off a mitten and tapped. The hearing below stopped in the middle of a sentence. They passed a bench up through the smoke hole and she came down it with her basket, to the considerable satisfaction of the grandson, who had been asked a difficult question.

Tonight she decides to give the boy a place on the sled. He can have the front and do the pulling; she will steer with her heels. Her father doubts there is room for two of them. They go out with the hearth lamp and measure, allowing for the boy about the length of his arm three times over, which is as near as either can get. Behind them the baby wakes in her bowl of warm water in the crook of her mother's arm. She gives the flat of the water an experimental slap. Somebody in the next house, mistaking it for the familiar signal, slips a platter through the wall, and there is supper, a good half hour early, with all the crisp tails.
