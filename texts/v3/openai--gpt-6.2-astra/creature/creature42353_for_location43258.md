At the southern works the first one generally comes in with a little water under it, before the top step has drained. It has a dirty yellow back the size and shape of a man's wet thumb. There is a line down the middle of the back where the yellow parts meet. One would expect the parts to open and disclose wings, but the line is a hinge that works the other way. The two halves fold down till their edges touch beneath the animal, shutting in a mouth, a quantity of very fine grey hair, and all six of its blue, rather beautiful feet. In this condition it can go through the buckets of the dredge. There will be a little yellow stone in the clay that comes rattling down the chute. Presently it will open and walk away.

The feet are blue only in water. On the bricks they fade to the colour of the mortar, and as most of the animal's height is in its legs, the back appears to be travelling by itself, at a level about half an inch above the floor. It travels in fits. At each halt it sets the tips of the front feet down together and sweeps the other four slowly outward until something in the floor satisfies it. Then it makes another run. Five or six may do this under the light at once. They get to the wall by quite different routes and disappear behind the same loose brick.

There was a name for them when there were men at the station. On the inside cover of a book of belt dressings someone had drawn one upside down, with an arrow at the grey fringe and the words *do not oil*. The name, in the same blue pencil, was *stair lice*. It is still a good place to find them, under the worn step at Fourteen. Get a hand into the gap there and the whole underside of the step feels like the surface of a belt going by. They will flow round a finger without nipping it. They eat the cream-coloured skin that forms on iron in the warm silt, and a softer, almost liquid skin that forms between beds of clay. There is plenty of both. A single bolt left in the bottom for a season will come up black except for winding tracks of silver, each the width of a thread.

The small ones are the only ones that come indoors. They grow by adding hinges. Behind the first yellow shield another develops, at first no bigger than a grain of damp meal, with feet of its own; then another behind that. There are seldom more than twenty shields exposed at one time, however long the creature gets. The rest of it lies in the extraordinarily thin space between one thing and the next. Pull up a length of drowned rail and there may be twenty shields coming out from under it and twenty going in, with so little between that the sky shows green through the connecting film. Let the rail down and the colour runs back. A ripple passes the whole length of the animal. Its many feet recover their hold with a sound like the soft side of a brush passed over paper.

For years the largest one known was the one that lived under the engine bed at the dredge. Each eleventh second, as the full bucket came over, a length of yellow flank would bulge out between the timbers. The men supposed it was being squeezed. They shut the engine down once to get a rotten crosspiece out, and the flank went on coming out and going in, a little more slowly each time, until an empty bucket at the top of the ladder began to rock. They waited until the rocking stopped before they put their hands in. That was the afternoon somebody made the drawing in the book.

There are much larger ones than that. In a fresh bluff the yellow bed between the blue clay and the black mud is sometimes sand and sometimes a stair louse. The difference is easiest to see toward the ends, where the bed divides into little wedges that overlap like the scales at the base of a cane shoot. From between each pair of wedges a foot goes down. The feet are not massive in proportion to the rest; they have divided and divided again, till what carries an acre of black bottom is a blue nap scarcely deep enough to bury a staple. When it shifts its hold, each hair lets go a fraction before the one beside it. A person standing on the black bottom would feel nothing. The cane would go on dripping into his collar. Only the station he was walking toward would now be on his left.

It is difficult to say what an old one is doing when it carries a piece of the basin. Feeding accounts for some of it. A newly opened trench has a milky gleam along the lips, which goes away in minutes, and under a lens the grey mouths can be seen working at it, hundreds to the inch. But there are movements which cost the animal a great deal and bring it to quite bare clay. The steps at Fourteen, for example, go on making the journey to the southern works long after the iron skin on the door sill has been eaten clean. Not the steps themselves now; those are caught fast in the roots. It is their weight and the spacing of their treads that keep arriving. In the fresh clay there is even a shallow dip where a man would put the ball of his foot. No one has stepped there.

What the small ones get behind the brick is warmth enough to make their second shield. They wedge themselves in side by side, blue feet to the wall, and stay there for the length of a rise and fall of the water. A few come out by the door next evening. Most come out somewhere in the buried length of the parent, as far from the station as the bluffs. If the light is good when a sheet of water lifts, one can see them falling out of the exposed seam, yellow specks opening six little legs in the air. They land without a sound among the clear leaves. For a while each carries a bubble, silver and perfectly round, in the hair under its belly.

At dusk there are always a few of the smallest that miss the steps. They come running across the bricks just as the last tread sinks, stop at the edge, and set their forefeet together on the sill. Below them in the brown water something very broad is going past. They wait in a little uneven row while the light of the bulb travels over hinge after hinge. Then the blue nap comes up against the yellow brick, so gently that the red ferns caught there do not stir, and the sill is empty.
