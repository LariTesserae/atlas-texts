The leaves that go farthest back under the flakes are not the youngest, as one might suppose from their size. They are old leaves that have never had room to open. A whole green edge will be packed into the breadth of a pin, and the moss in there has quite a different habit from the moss under the loose folds by the dish. It grows flat from the outset. Once, when a flake split and let a little more room into the end of the hollow, a leaf broadened by the width of the split. It was white along the part that had been shut up. For two summers afterwards there was a white stripe there, with the old green of the edge beyond it.

The moss makes very little demand on the plant in some respects and a great deal in others. It will live on the ladder of a leaf after there is nothing in it that could feed anything. But it must have the two surfaces, one to rise from and one to meet. A stem that grows up through a missing rung and finds no ceiling comes to a blunt end with a clear knob on it. In a good light the knob would be handsome. It lasts until the first dry spell in the shale. The stems that have turned along a ceiling last for years. Water travelling down one of them comes to the elbow and hangs there, inside the fold, quite out of reach of the dry air that sometimes draws through the seam. After a rain the weight of a leaf is partly the weight of all those elbows.

The rusty stuff at the studs is neither root nor moss. In the dead of winter it can be seen a little better, for the ice takes an impression of it. It is made up of exceedingly thin cups laid on their sides with their mouths against the iron. On the side away from the strain the cups are some distance apart. On the working side they are so crowded that their mouths are pushed square. It is on the backs of these square cups that the root puts out its finest hairs. Where a stud has worked loose by even a fraction, there is a crescent of empty cups still fastened to the wall. They keep their colour a long time. The live ones have almost no colour at all just at the rim.

There are winters when the middle stud does nearly all the work. The floor will give its little jump morning after morning in the same place. By spring the root at that stud is as plump as a soaked thread, while at the others the hairs have gone and the roots lie smooth in their beds. Nothing much can be told from the look of the leaves above. The leaf that opens in the spring may be at the other end of the hollow. Its first green shows far down in a fold already occupied by moss, and for a while the new leaf goes up carrying a section of the old forest on its back. Afterwards there are two low storeys where there was one high one, and in the lower storey a number of stems bent double, still perfectly clear and sound.

At the front, on the bare side of the dish, there is room for a leaf to open to a size no leaf in the hollow has yet attained. The difficulty there is getting started. The broad shale takes the noon light and dries before the plant has lost the weight of its water. More than once an edge has put down hairs there and had to lift them all again. A few grains of shale have come up with them. One of the outer leaves has a little grey hem on this account, heavy enough that it hangs lower than its neighbours even when dry. The next time the dish fills, that edge will touch first. Already, underneath it, the moss has grown down over the crimp and turned along the undersides of the grains, as if they were the roof.
