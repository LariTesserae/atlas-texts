On the low side of the chip that stands on its point there is a small sole of flesh. It shows when the silt lifts. It is the grey of the soft stuff under a mussel shell, with a milky seam across it. A grain of brick going down the slope will sometimes stick at the seam. By the following tide the grain will have gone in. There is no other sign that the seam opens.

What fastens the sole to the chip is a ribbed neck, thin enough to lie in one of the scoring marks left by the brick mould. It comes out on the high side and goes a little way up into the weed. There it divides. One lobe lies against the face of a fold and the other reaches round the fold and lies against its back. The lobes are beautifully fitted to their places. The winter one has a turned edge which the outward pull keeps tight, as wind keeps a shirt tight against a line. The one on the seed side is rather slack. There is a little pocket in it with the two halves of the husk dimly visible through the skin.

It feeds by the difference. What one tide stretches, the other is slow to let go. A faint travelling pinch goes down the neck to the sole. It takes the best part of an hour to reach the milky seam, and there is just strength enough left in it to draw in a film of the pelt along with whatever grit happens to be on it. On two sides of a fold of much the same age the creature would starve, though the water might be full of food. Sometimes a lobe comes loose. It hangs then like a little wet cuff, feeling along the weed for a year unlike the one on which its fellow is fastened.

There are others, though it is hard to count them by their soles. At the back, where the threads go down among the four stones, the necks run together. One can follow a pinch from a pair of lobes above the scree and see it taken up, much weakened, in three seams at once. The smallest of these is on the green stone. It has never taken in a grain. It lives on the few mouthfuls of work that come to it from farther out, and has grown a fine fringe all round itself in the cold.

In that fringe the young begin. At first each is only a thick place in a hair, with a crease at one end. The hair doubles back from the water of the hoofprint and the thick place gets left in the bend. For a long while it has no need to go anywhere. There is cold on its lower surface, and, on its upper, the minute warmth that comes and goes when the wall is stirred. The crease deepens. Two lips form, shut very accurately against each other. When the wall grumbles the lips gape, and if a bit of tuft brushes them they shut on that instead. The next time the leaves part, the young one is lifted bodily out of its bed. There remains a loop of clear thread down to the green stone. It pays out surprisingly far.

One has been lifted as far as the standing chip. It is up under the edge, in a notch where the pelt has worn quite smooth, and its two lips still have hold of the same brown shred. The sole above it belongs to a much older animal. Each time the chip leans a little farther, that sole works a little farther up the slope to keep the neck free. The young one stays where it was put. Already the point bears so lightly that when the white jointed thing backs out against the weed, a little weight comes on the young one's lips and goes away again.

When the chip lies flat, the old neck will still come out by the scoring mark. There is room there. Underneath, the brown shred will keep, on one face, the last inward draw of the water before the chip came down. On the other there will be scarcely any pull but the small cold one along the thread. The lips are beginning to differ. Even now, after the weight has lifted, one stays pressed to the shred a little longer than the other.
