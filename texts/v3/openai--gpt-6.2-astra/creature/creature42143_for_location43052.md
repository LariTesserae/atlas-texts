What looks like a second pale seam in the bedrock is the back of a fish. It begins to come clear at the head end. Two little clouds of flour rise a thumb’s width apart, hang for a moment and stream away. They rise again. They come from under the gill covers of a sculpin lying chin down against the stone. The pale seam runs crookedly over one eye and part of the way down its back. Beyond it there is a patch the green of the striped stones, and beyond that the fish is almost black. It is shorter than a hand. Most of it seems to be head and the two broad fins on which the head is propped.

It can stay here in quite a run of water. A little way ahead of its nose a knob of rock divides the stream; the two halves go by on either side, lifting the loose margin of one fin and letting it down. Its belly is in still water. To move the length of its body would be to enter a different sea. Presently it does move, with a single kick that takes it slantwise across the fast strip into the lee of the next stone. A shrimp that was there is gone. The sculpin has a feeler hanging out of the corner of its mouth. This takes some time to get rid of.

There is a fair living even on the swept side of the stones. One of the black pebbles with a white ring has a second ring on it, not quite meeting at the ends. This one is a tube of lime. The worm inside has laid it down a little at a time, following the curve of the pebble until its head has nearly come round to where its tail was when it began. Out of the open end comes something like a pair of rust-red eyebrows. A grain strikes one of them and both are gone. Later, in a gentler stream, they come out again, unfold considerably farther, and prove to be a crown of fine branches. What they take from the water is too small to see. A speck too large for use travels along a branch, hesitates at the base and goes off downstream, tidily bundled in slime.

Under that pebble is quite another company. A brittle star has got its disc into a space that would scarcely take a shirt button. Two of its arms come out on the valley side, two on the bank side; the fifth goes down through the gravel and comes to daylight in a place one would never connect with the first four. Only the very ends show. When the sand begins its little jumps the ends on the bank side curl in, joint by joint. On the other side one arm is still at work, passing a soft scrap along its underside towards the hidden mouth. The tide has turned for less than half the animal.

In summer there are eggs here. The sculpin keeps to a particular hollow then, under a slab of green stone tilted on a lump of granite. The roof is stippled with yellow beads. They stick to it so well that a grain of sand driven in at one end of the hollow will rattle the whole way through without bringing down a single one. The male lies with his tail out and his great head in the doorway. At slack water, when the red weeds are standing up, his fins go on beating. A thread of dirty water issues from under the far edge of the slab; clean water goes in past his cheeks. By the time the weeds have fallen over he is still again.

For a while the beads merely darken. Then a pair of black points appears in each, and one day an eye inside a bead turns to look out. The young that comes free has none of the weight or the good stony look of its father. It is nearly clear, with an absurd sufficiency of eye. An upward flick takes it out from under the green roof, another into the water above the stones. There it goes inland with the flour, upright and perfectly distinct for an instant against the dark valley side. Under the slab the broad fins go on stirring water over those that remain.
