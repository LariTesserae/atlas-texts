The woman counts the saucers by putting her thumb down the stack. She can feel the difference between six rims and seven more easily than she can see it in the light from the street. There are seven. One has a scallop missing from its edge; she puts that one on the floor, tips a little water into it from the kettle, and pushes it under the cupboard with her foot. Something under there begins to drink. The boy waits to see if it will come out. It never has for him.

The other six saucers belong to six different households. The woman has marked the backs in pencil. She serves fried fish throats in the morning, and most of her customers prefer to eat off their own china. They leave it with her until the shop has to close for high water, when everything goes to the wash together. There is a man of eighty who has eaten off a saucer with a yellow plum on it since he was a little boy. He used to think it was a picture of an egg. It was years before he saw a plum.

Although she lives at number three, the woman sleeps at number eleven, in a room two doors above the painted summer line. So do the boy and his father, and a young couple neither of them knows particularly well. The five beds have curtains. The couple work on the morning side of a gate and are sometimes asleep at noon with the bright light coming through the red curtain and the shapes of their bare feet visible in it. Everybody is very careful then about the lid of the blanket chest, which gives a bang quite out of proportion to its size.

The boy's father keeps the telltales at the district gates. A telltale is a glass tube with a yellow bead in it. There are instruments as delicate as anything in the city on the other side of that panel, but the bead shows what the water is actually doing at the height of a man's eye, and you would be surprised how often it is necessary to know this. His cuffs are permanently yellow from retrieving the beads. He has a habit of touching his son's wrist halfway through a sentence and leaving his fingers there, having forgotten whether he is at work with his hand on the glass or at home with his hand on the boy. The boy used to pull away. Now he puts in the end of the sentence himself, in the broad slow accent of a man explaining a stoppage to a platform full of people. His father laughs every time.

There are other things waiting for the boy to leave number three. Wedged behind the brass 3, in a space no thicker than a folded letter, are perhaps fifty of the young of the wall grazers. At this age they are soft and the color of tea. They come out after the boat has gone to eat the pale streak left by the boy's knuckles where he held the sill. A hand removes the old scales and exposes the new skin of the wall, which is much easier eating. Some doorways bring up a generation of grazers in half the usual time. These are the doors that stick, the doors of popular shops, and the doors, rarer now, that you have to knock on.

A diving animal has discovered this about the shop. She comes on the full water, folds her long neck back along her body and lies quite still against the lintel, looking like a length of the tarred stuff they use to seal the joint. When a fish stops to take the grazers she straightens out. It is astonishing that anything so quick should have to lie in wait for anything so small. She swallows, shakes her head with a little shudder of pleasure that goes all the way down to her hind feet, and folds herself up again.

The woman knows her by a hole in the web of one foot. She has watched her bring two young ones to the lintel, in successive years. The first learned the trick. The second never could be still; it would see its own chin in the brass and have to investigate. It grew up quite adequately on things that fell off the fish stalls. On quiet mornings now it can be seen asleep in the weighing pan of the disused public scales, a substantial animal weighing, according to the arrow, slightly less than a small onion.

The thing under the cupboard is none of these. The woman first heard it when she was a girl washing the floor for the previous tenant. She slopped some warm water under the cupboard and heard a soft, industrious lapping, followed, when she put her face to the gap, by absolute silence. The cupboard is tiled to the wall; to shift it would mean losing the blue border, which was old even then. She has never cared to. Sometimes for months the saucer remains full. Once, during the long closure of the eastern gates, there were two little sets of sounds, not quite in time.

Tonight the boy has a better look than usual. There is a wet print just this side of the gap, a pad and three fine toes, no bigger altogether than the plum on the old man's saucer. He gets down on his stomach. The woman is about to tell him not to put his hand in when he reaches sideways instead and touches her ankle. *Feel*, his fingers say.

She takes off her shoe. Through the tile under her bare sole she can feel a faint scratching retreating from the cupboard, passing under the wall and going on down the street. At number five it stops. There is a pause, then the small hard knock of china being pushed across a floor.
