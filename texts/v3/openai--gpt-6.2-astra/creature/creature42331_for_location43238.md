The woman who cut the three steps is called Bet. She takes people across the inlet, two if they have baskets, four if they have nothing with them and will sit where she puts them. She can judge very well from the way a person stands on the landing whether he means to get into her boat or merely help somebody else in and then stand talking. She charges for the crossing, not for the talk. On wet days she wears a square of sailcloth over her lap. There are two pale places on it where she used to go down on her knees to get over the lip of the bank.

One of her passengers is an old man who comes across once a week to have his hair cut by his daughter. There are people on his own side who could cut it. He likes to sit on the high porch with the cloth tucked into his collar and see, across the water, how his house looks in relation to the other houses. A person has very few occasions to see this. His daughter turns his chair a little when she changes sides so that he need not give up the view. Afterward she walks him down to the boat, going first on the clay steps, backward, with one of his hands in hers. He is quite steady on his feet. It is the business of having to look down past his own toes into the bright water that bothers him.

The little girl who sleeps under the potter’s bowls knows which ones are ready to come off the shelf. In the morning, before she sits up, she lays the back of a finger against their bottoms. As long as there is a cool spot left, the potter lets them be. The girl would like to make bowls herself. So far she has made a clay whistle with a fine round belly and a hole in its tail, out of which no sound whatever will come. The potter has offered to open it up and show her what is wrong inside. But the girl has pressed two eyes into it with the end of a rush and doesn’t want it opened now. It sits with the sound whistles on the sill, looking, if anything, rather better than they do.

There are small brown eels in the runnel under Bet’s old door. They lie so exactly along the wavering shadows of the rushes that a child looking for them generally finds a rush first and puts a hand into the water over nothing. When the light goes off the bank they come out from under the door and nose about among the steps. Bet has felt one pass over her foot. It gave her the queer sensation that it was her foot that had moved, while the eel held perfectly still in the water.

On the low end lives a man who was in a boat the night the bank came up. People bringing visitors through the porches sometimes stop at his place and ask him to tell about it. What woke him was his little cooking pot sliding off the thwart into his ribs. For a good while he thought the boat had gone aground through some fault of his own, and the first thing he did, in the dark, was try to push her off. He can still show how he set his shoulder against the gunwale. There is usually a pause here while his visitors wait for the great event to begin. He has no more than this to give them. In the morning there were shells and a long way to drag the boat.

It is this man on the stool in the evening. The trouble with the gutter is that the peg which holds it has been driven into one house and the bracket that takes its weight belongs to the next. The two houses have settled companionably toward one another for years; now for a change they seem inclined to draw apart. From up on his stool he can see neither the thin stream nor the place where it falls. The girl from under the bowls has put a finger into it and is telling him, a little importantly, how much water there is left.

“Less,” she says, and then, “Less than that.”

He has gone as far as he can with the peg. Bet, coming up with her fare for the last crossing still shut in her fist, puts her free hand under the bracket and lifts. The thread draws up to a bead. The girl waits, for a bead is quite capable of becoming a thread again. Behind her the old man’s daughter turns sideways with a basket to let somebody past. It is getting on toward supper; there is a smell of onions from the room they all have to go through. At last the girl touches the bead off the lip of the gutter. Nothing takes its place. She puts her finger in her mouth and moves out of the way.
