The woman who keeps the silence shed in the east ward wears a skirt made from an old street plan. It was printed on good oiled cloth at considerable public expense, and went out of date before the surveyors had finished hemming it. If you ask her where she lives she can generally find the house somewhere behind her left knee.

She does not wear it as a joke. Most of her clothes were made by her sister, who buys condemned municipal fabric by weight. Half the men on their stair have the depth of a bridge footing written across the seat of their drawers.

Her name is Eda. She has a son grown up and a father who still comes in to work, although his hands have got too unsteady for the fine part of the job. He can attend to the door. There are two leaves to the door, with a space between them about the size of a cupboard. A visitor waits in there while the old man takes his foot off one treadle and puts it on the other. He likes the moment of opening the inner leaf. People always have the same look on their faces, as if he had led them much farther than they had agreed to go.

Most stay a little while. There are special rates for people sitting up with a tooth. There is a bench long enough to lie down on, though surprisingly few people can sleep there. Eda's father tried it once when his room was being painted. All night he was troubled by the impression that the water had gone out of the world and everybody but him was too polite to mention it.

The fine work is on the underside of the shed. The shed stands on six hollow feet, each with a stack of loose ceramic saucers inside it. Between one saucer and the next there is just room for a certain small dun-coloured animal. Nobody has ever succeeded in getting the same result with felt. Over time felt packs down hard, and the noise comes up through it. The animals shift in their sleep.

They are not related to the callers, though both can be taken up on the edge of a knife. A caller lifted in this way goes on knocking at the blade. The dun thing holds still until it has got its bearings, then flows over the edge and hangs underneath in a little drop. In its own country, which is the underside of a stone, there would be something there to take hold of. Eda presents it with a saucer. She does this quite deftly even in the cold. On washing days she may handle several hundred without once having to get down on her knees and look for one.

There is a dish of clean brine in her kitchen for the ones that are not doing well. Her son used to bring his friends home to see it, on the understanding that nobody was to stir the dish or tell his mother what she ought to do. The sick animals collect around the unglazed foot of the dish. When they are getting better they climb up the sides. There was one, a particularly pale one, that lived for a long time on the underside of the kitchen table, well beyond any reasonable expectation of damp. At meals the family took care not to put anything very hot over its place. It went away in the end. Eda still sets the tea at the other end of the table.

Her son lives across the gap now, in the town with the iron bell. He helps run the ferry. The ferry is a much less impressive object than the bell: two benches, a canopy, and a box under the nearer bench in which the passengers put their shopping so that nobody will make a grab for a parcel if it slips while they are over the drop. When there is a new baby to be taken across, they sound the bell only after the ferry has landed. It took a petition from both ends of the line to get this small concession out of the company.

Eda goes over every third daylight to cut her son's hair. He is perfectly capable of getting it cut in his own town. What he cannot get there is his grandfather's account of who has been in the shed. This is supposed to be private, and the old man takes some pains to describe the visitors without giving their names, so that a good deal of the haircut is taken up by a complicated history of a person who is not the woman who salts the small pink fish but very like her in the shoulders.

Coming home, Eda can pick out her own roof quite early. There is a short white beast on it lying crossways to the rest. It has a bare patch about the size of a soup plate where two of its fringes never grew back after an illness. From the ferry, if there is any light at all, one can see the dark oval of the patch and the six pink feet all tucked up on the uphill side. The animal has been there since before she was born. For most of her childhood she believed that this was how everyone found their way home, and felt a keen, almost unbearable pity for the people who lived under an ordinary beast.

Recently the little groove in the east ward has begun to draw water away from her street. The change is quite plain beneath the shed. The last two times she opened the downstream foot she found the upper saucers dry. She has put the animals from those saucers into her kitchen dish and written to the works office. A man came with a float. He worked for an afternoon and left a brass stud, which is handsome of its kind but does nothing for the animals.

There is talk of moving the shed. It would not have to go far. From the old site to the new one a person could carry a cup of tea and drink it hot at the other end. Nevertheless her father has begun to come very early for his shift. He sits on the bench outside before he opens up, with his heels on the lower rung and his chin sunk deep in the collar of his coat. Eda thought at first that he was sorry to be leaving a place he knew. Then one morning she sat down beside him and he held up a finger. In what had once been an unbroken wash of knocking, there were intervals now. Far back under the street, one caller struck its knob three times, rested, struck twice more. They had lived over callers all their lives. Neither of them had ever heard one finish.
