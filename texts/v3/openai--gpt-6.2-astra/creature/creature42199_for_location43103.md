A young ledge cow can get down into the water several weeks before it can get out. There are usually two or three of them in the slack behind the wheel, treading water with their chins laid on the stone. Their mothers sleep on. Now and then one wakes, rolls heavily into the stream and offers a flank against which a calf can set its hind feet. Quite often the calf goes on hanging there. What it wanted was company in the water.

The upper lip grows faster than the rest of the face. On a calf it hangs down over the mouth and gets sucked in with the milk; on an old cow it is a capable member, forked a little way up the middle, with an edge as fine as the edge of a worn coin. A cow feeding in the gray stuff on the bottom leaves two clean sweeps on either side of a ridge scarcely disturbed at all. Up this ridge the shrimps go skipping ahead of her. She pays no attention to them until she comes to the wall, when she takes the whole accumulation in one rather delicate sideways bite. People standing in the bath above can sometimes hear the bite, a single knock coming up the waste pipe between their feet.

The cows travel farther than was once supposed. The proof of this was a china cistern handle, an oval ring big enough to admit a folded lip, which a young bull got caught on his face in the baths at Derry Steps. For the next thirty years there were quite reliable accounts of the handle from wheel chambers up and down the western cut. It was possible for a child at Derry Steps to grow up, go into the bearing trade, marry and take a room four days east by freight, and one morning on the way to work look down through a grating and see that familiar piece of the municipal fittings going by in the green dark. Nobody knew a route by water between the two places. Nobody knows one now.

There are families whose business it is to listen at the gratings. They go out after the last train with a square of felt to kneel on and a short iron rod, bright at one end from being set against the teeth. Each has a round of perhaps sixty gratings. A grate commonly gives a note of its own, over and above the noises of the water, since the space beneath it is partly full of air. A change of note means that something has altered down there. It may be a bird wedged in the throat of an outlet, or a backing up of the gray nap behind a loose tile; once it was found to be the roof of a hitherto unsuspected room coming down by inches into a school swimming pool. The listener on that round was a girl of fourteen. She had reported for three nights that the B flat outside the school had gone sour, and for the rest of her life was a little vexed at being asked to sing the note. Through the iron it had been perfectly plain to her. In the air she could do nothing with it.

The listeners get to know which houses have babies in them. People up with a baby like to see somebody else out at that hour, and will stand in a lighted doorway rocking from heel to toe until the listener comes along. In summer there is often an eel lying across the sill for the sake of the wet draught under the door. It has to be shifted gently with a bare foot. A shod foot makes it knot itself up, after which the only thing to do is fetch a basin and wait. Most of these are the common slate-colored eel with a pale blind side to its head. The yellow ones from the school drains have two good eyes. Children there will tell you that if you take a yellow eel as far as the corner in a bucket it turns gray. What actually happens is that long before the corner you meet someone who makes you take it back.

In the houses the white crabs are tolerated up to a point. They keep the channels under the paving sweet and pick out of a wet net almost everything that would otherwise have to be picked out with a needle. They also have a liking for the paste behind wallpaper. In a room that has stood empty through the warm months the paper may still be hanging in perfect order, held by its own stiffness a finger’s breadth from the wall. Open the window and the entire landscape of some distant cut, with its improbable red sunset and its three little freight trains, settles forward on the bed. Behind it there will be a dozen white crabs, thin as wafers, that have been living there since spring. These are not the same as the curb crabs, though it took a printer’s widow, who kept both sorts in a type drawer, to establish the difference. The paper crab, turned on its back, rights itself to the left every time.

On the wheel ledges the animals sleep through all this small industry. At the end of a shift a keeper counts them, mostly from habit, and puts the number in the book beside the head of water and the state of the bearings. A calf is entered as a half until it can get out unaided. On one page, among figures for a summer of very poor flow, there is a seven and a half in the afternoon column and an eight in the evening one. Somebody has gone over the eight twice in ink.
