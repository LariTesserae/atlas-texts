The milk depot keeps a boy whose business it is to get the empties back. He is thirty-four now. He has a daughter on the eastern decking who is almost as old as he was when he took the job. The two of them have always lived in the same house, in the sense that he goes home to it every evening. On the map in the depot his house is still shown separately, in a little box with its own east and west.

He knows the valley best on a Monday, which is the general wash. He can take his crate in at the window of the infirmary and come out in the pantry of the house where he was born. His mother is usually there with a dab of butter ready on the end of a knife. He eats it, inclined over the crate so as not to put it down. On Thursdays, when the infirmary sends its linen out to boil, he has to take the stairs instead. There are parts of his mother's face he has only seen on a Monday, with the light from the long ward on it.

Several things besides the milk boy make a living collecting. The animals in the drain holes will take anything long enough to have two ends. If you give one a knitting needle it will take it neatly by the middle and be unable to get back into its hole. It does not occur to it to turn its head. It will sit there whiskering, sometimes all morning, until another of its kind comes up on the inside and takes the needle by the tip. The two then disappear with an ease agreeable to see. What they generally collect is hair. A woman combing a child on a doorstep will let the combings float from her fingers, and before they have travelled the width of a tread there will be a small hand up through the grating to receive them.

People used to think the hair was for nests, but when the west stair was taken up they found the nests, which were round bare places in the iron dust, warm from the pier below. The hair had all been plaited into lengths and stretched across the drains, about a finger's breadth off the bottom. There were hundreds of these low crossings. For three days after the stair was put back one could hear the little feet overhead in kitchens where nobody had heard them before. Then the old ways were re-established, except for one colony that still comes out behind the bread oven in the school. The cook puts aside the first burnt crust. She says there is one with a white muzzle that was there before her, though it is difficult to know how she tells it from its children once they have been in the flour.

The milk boy's daughter has a line of her own. It runs from a hook by her bed to the house opposite, both ends in the part that is coming down. As far as her father can see there is nothing to be gained by it. The front doors are six paces apart. But the girl opposite is deaf and at night, when their parents think they are asleep, the two girls lie with the line lying slack across their counterpanes and talk by tightening it between finger and thumb. They began with one pull for yes and two for no and have got so far beyond this that they can be unkind to one another. Sometimes the milk boy, easing his boots off in the yard, hears the hook knocking for a long time on one side and no answer from the other. In the morning the girls go to school together with their arms crossed behind each other's backs. Each carries the other's slate; it is easier on the elbows.

Neither remembers any other arrangement of the town. When asked to draw a house they both draw the underside first, a shallow curve like the bottom of a cup. They know which cupboards in the school to avoid leaning against towards the end of the afternoon, when the children below are at work with the brooms. The bristles on the copper make hardly a sound in the street and a very considerable one in the cupboard where the maps are kept. Once the deaf girl took her friend in there during geography and shut the door. They stood with their stockinged feet on the bare metal and felt somebody write a great slow E under them, then the three uprights of an M. They stayed until the teacher opened the door upon them. By that time they were stamping out the rest of Emma for the child below, who had begun too near a strap and run out of room.

Of the creatures at the bridge pool the least regarded is the mussel. One sees the shells everywhere, washed to a lilac colour, serving as scoops in the salt cellars; one seldom sees a live one do anything. The night watchman at the bridge has seen a mussel walk. It does so on a single fleshy foot, very white against the tin, carrying its two dark sides a little way apart as a man might carry a pair of boards through a narrow passage. On nights of heavy cloud the wet runs a long way up the riverbed and the mussels set out in both directions. The watchman goes round in the morning with a wooden spoon, bringing back any that have mistaken the duration of the weather. Nobody pays him for this. On the other hand he has not wound the bridge clock for several years, and nobody has mentioned it.

He and the milk boy generally meet at the step at the end of the last street. It is a good height to sit on with a crate between one's knees. At first the watchman always sat on the felt and the milk boy on the enamel, out of deference to the older man's bones. Now the felt has worn thin at that end. They have begun to sit the other way round without having spoken of it. There is a ring on the tread where a bottle stood one frosty morning and took the glaze off; a red-legged bird comes up from under the bridge and stands in the ring while they eat. It used to come alone. This spring it brought another, which stood on the bare enamel and waited, shifting from foot to foot, until the first had finished whatever there was to do in the circle.

The straps have a little play in them now on warm days. You can see it in the washing on the eastern edge: the shirts hang straight and the straps beside them do not. The milk boy has been trying to remember which of the things in his house are actually fastened down. His wife thinks the dresser is; his daughter says it can't be, because when the broom children get as far as their house the blue saucer walks to the front of the shelf. On the first evening of each month the three of them lift down everything that might fall and eat their supper with the good china on the floor around them. In the morning they put it all back. It takes very little time now. The girl opposite passes the plates through the window and his daughter, standing on a chair, settles them into the pale grooves in the wood.

Down at the depot there is already a revised route for the morning after the landing. It gives the milk boy nine minutes from the last house on the old street to the first house on the new one, allowing for a change of level and for people who will want to talk. He has walked it on the plan with his fingers. The first house is his own. For nine minutes of a working morning he will be coming home by the front door, a thing his daughter has never seen him do. She has asked him to knock, even if it is standing open. She would like to be the one who lets him in.
