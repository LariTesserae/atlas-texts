There is a beast in the beds, though a man looking down from a punt is likelier to see where one has lately been. What shows is a light mark about as long as a shovel blade. The long leaves have not been cut. They have been put back, with a few of their pale undersides showing. At the inland end of the mark there is sometimes a little combing of loose roots, very white in the green water.

The beast itself is low enough to go between the courses of the grass. A grown one has a back the size and something of the shape of the sole of a man's shoe. There are no scales on it. The hide is made in narrow pleats across the width of the back, dark in the hollows and olive on the ridges, so that when the animal shortens itself under a weight it is much like a handful of leaves that have fetched up against a stick. Underneath it is almost colourless. There are feet there, in two long rows. An outer toe on each foot turns quite round a blade; the others, set down together, will take a remarkably good hold of stone.

It is the feet that receive the knock of the pole. Those nearest the knock draw up first. A little run of lifted toes goes down that side of the body; then all take hold again, and the animal stays where it is. The knocking may go on all afternoon. What will bring it out is the much smaller disturbance of another of its kind coming through the grass. There is a particular soft pull and release as a burden passes from one leaf to the next. Two beasts going contrary ways will turn aside for one another well before their heads could touch. On an old bank, where there is sometimes only one course fit to travel, the smaller will get underneath and let the greater pass over its back.

Its head is the least of it. Two soft lips project a little beyond the front edge of the hide and can be worked separately, one taking hold while the other feels farther in. It feeds where a new leaf is on the point of slipping free of its sheath. What it takes is the soft white length at the foot of the leaf; what it leaves is a perfectly good green blade, hardly marked, that will come away at the next tide. In summer these blades collect across the drowned thresholds. They are still green when they go over into the cuts.

A cut is as much of a boundary to the beast as it ever was to a man on foot. It can let go and swim. In a dish of water it does so handily enough, putting a travelling curl down the thin edge of its hide. But let one come out of the grass over deep water and it will fold itself backward, feeling with its hind feet for the last leaf it held. Consequently there are beds quite near together whose beasts differ a good deal. Across one narrow cut they are all speckled yellow, down to the little ones; on the other lip, within the length of a punt, there is not a speckled one to be found.

In the dark months they gather on the wicker. Ordinary willow will serve, but they seem to do best on a rod whose upper half is gone. They fit themselves to the surviving curve of bark underneath, in long files, with the soft lips of one tucked out of harm under the heel of the next. The young are there too. For their first winter they are translucent; by the light admitted through a crack one can see in each the single green thread of its last good meal. A rod lifted incautiously out of the mud will come up bare. Lift mud and all, and wait for the cloud to settle, and there will be the whole file still holding on, some no bigger than the bud scars on the willow.

They begin to move wherever the snow first wears thin. It need not be warm. Two inches of new leaf in a sheltered place are enough to draw the first of them off the rod, and in a day or so there will be pale marks all along the sunny side of a bank. At a correcting bend the marks make the bend too. Sometimes in spring a whole company comes to the place where a gate slab has slipped. There is grass on the slab and grass on the sill, and between the two a hand's breadth of running water. For days the beasts will be thick on the sill, the forward ones reaching down with their lips and drawing back. Then a long uprooted blade catches by its butt in a joint of the masonry. Its tip goes down and lodges in the lower grass. By morning there are two or three pale places on the slab. By the time the blade pulls free there may be no more on one side than on the other.
