At first light there are sometimes little yellow flowers going upstream among the palms. They travel a foot or two, hang against the current, then go on again. They are caught in the coat of a small brown animal which, when it comes out on a stone, proves to have a good deal less body than the flowers suggest. The outer ends of its hairs are broad and glossy and lie over one another like wet grass after a flood. Under these it is quite dry. It sits up, draws its forepaws down over its face, and eats anything it finds in them, though not the flowers.

There are a great many of these swimmers on the bench. One seldom sees the whole of one except on a stone. In the sedge what shows is generally a blunt nose, pale at the tip, moving along just under the surface with a bow of water over it. The animal walks on the bottom as long as its feet will reach. It has long, rather loose toes which close about a grass stem without bending it much; by taking hold a little higher with each foot it can walk up through three feet of water into exactly the same attitude it keeps in three inches. Its nose comes clear and two little shutters in it open. For a moment it breathes in full view of a rail which has been looking hard for it, but looking down.

Its ordinary food is a flat snail that lives on the undersides of the palm leaves. To get at the far side of a leaf it turns on its back and goes along it hand over hand, nibbling with its head thrown back and its hind feet trailing free. Each snail leaves a clean oval in the green felt on the leaf. In a reach where several swimmers have been feeding, a crown tipped up by the wind shows a whole spotted underside, like the wing of quite another plant. There are little ticking noises as fragments of shell fall on the dry upper faces of leaves downstream.

By midday most of the swimmers have gone in under the ridges. They do not burrow in the stone. Where a flow cooled around a fallen trunk there is sometimes a long round hole, faithfully grained on the inside with bark that has been gone for ages. The lower mouths of these holes are under water. Farther in, a swimmer can climb out on a dry shelf and go up a little way through the dark to a bed of split sedge. It carries the sedge in wet and spreads it out, turning it several times before building with it. A good bed is springy, pale as straw, and just warm to the touch. Where a thin roof has fallen in one can look down and see the young lying there, their feet already the size of their mother's, each with a black seam down its back where the two slopes of glossy hair will presently meet. A fly walking the length of this seam causes small successive twitches without waking the sleeper.

It is not quite the snug arrangement it seems. A long clear day may end with water well up the passage and the bedding afloat. The old swimmer will come out two or three times in an afternoon, leaving the little ones asleep, and visit a particular notch in the rock at the mouth. She sets one forefoot in the notch. If the water is still below her wrist she goes back by the way she came. If it washes over the foot she climbs the ridge and goes down on the other side, where there will be another hole to inspect, higher by a few feet. By evening she may be seen on the crest with a young one in her mouth. She carries it by the loose skin behind its ear; its great soft feet open and shut against her throat. It is on these journeys, rather than in the meadow itself, that the cat takes them. Sometimes a mother spends half the afternoon at the lip of a hole with a youngster tucked under her chin, waiting for a patch of tawny shade under a tree to go elsewhere.

Not all of them live near the ridges. There is one old male in the notch at the end of the stream, recognizable by a white forefoot. He goes down a dangling root of the crooked tree every evening and feeds on the dripping wall. There are no palm snails there, but a small soft crab comes out in the dusk to comb the wet moss with its claws. The swimmer takes one, backs up the root a little, and eats it sitting in a fork, holding the white foot cupped under his mouth. Bits escape him. The black birds drying overhead cock their heads at the faint taps going down the wall.

When the birds have gone he often stays. The root hangs well out from the rock; with his tail wound loosely round it he can let himself down till the spray beads on his whiskers. From the meadow side, against the last of the light, he is as plain as a fruit on a stalk. A little farther down, the root and the spray and the falling water are all lost in the same dark. After a while the white foot reaches up out of it and takes hold.
