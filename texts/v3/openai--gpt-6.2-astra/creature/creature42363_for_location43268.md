There are little brown frogs in the bank. In the daytime what you see of one is generally a forefoot. Four pale toes come through the curtain of roots and take a loose hold of it from the outside. The rest of the animal is tucked into the wet earth behind. A drop running down one of the roots will follow the curve of a toe, hang from its pad for a moment, and fall. Sometimes the foot lets go and comes through again a little farther over. There may be twenty frogs in plain sight before you know what a foot looks like, and after that it is hard to see anything else.

They are small enough to sit in the hollow left by an orange fruit, though it is only the young ones that fit very well. A grown frog sitting there has to keep its elbows out. The back is the brown of a sodden leaf, with a fine raised line down it that ends well short of the nose. Under its chin the skin is pale and loose. It can turn its eyes down to look at something between its feet, and does this with an air of close attention even when what it has caught is only a bit of grit. It opens its mouth then and rubs it against the stone, first on one side and then on the other.

They come out as the floor cools. A frog puts both forefeet through the roots and hangs for a while with its belly pressed to them and its hind legs still folded away in the bank. Then it lets itself down. On stone it walks rather more often than it jumps, lifting its feet high, for the pads stick a little. The first ones out hunt along the bottom of the bank where there are winged things so small that all one sees of a meal is a frog suddenly a little farther forward than it was. Later, when the dark lines have joined, they go right out among the islands of grass.

A fallen fruit will keep several of them in its neighbourhood for days. They have no use for a sound one. It must have a split in it and that faint white bloom at the split which means that by tomorrow there will be flies. They take up places round it, not facing the fruit exactly, but a little askew, so that each has a view of the stone beside it on which a fly may settle to clean its feet. As the fruit subsides they move in closer. Once, under the middle tree, there were five sitting round what had been a fruit, and between their solemnly inclined faces there was nothing left but a comma of seed in a wet stain.

For laying they go up into the crowns. Deep in a fork, where the old leaves have packed tight, water stands in little pockets the size of a cupped hand. You can tip back a fern and find one of these bits of brown water full of life. The tadpoles are almost clear except for the eyes and the dark coil of the gut; at a disturbance they all go down together into the soft rubbish at the bottom. Long after you think you have come upon an empty pool a pair of eyes will rise through the reflection of your face. The froglets, when they climb out, are greenish and remarkably narrow. For a few days they have inconvenient lengths of tail which they carry curled beside them on the leaves. Their first journey down the trunk takes most of a wet night. Some get no farther than a lower fork and begin the next evening scarcely a yard below the place where they were born.

It is the grown males that go to the lip. There are little notches there, hardly deep enough to take a thumb, in which one can sit quite snug with the whole drop behind him. He backs into one and sets his forefeet wide apart on the wet sill. His call in the hand, if he could be prevailed upon to make it there, would be a slight sound. At the lip it goes down into the hollow stone. You hear it over by the bank, quite low and near, as though something shut inside the earth had given a small experimental knock.

When the two great notes of the water draw together the frogs fall silent. Their throats go on moving. The loose skin swells a little and subsides, and their broad toes lie flat on the sounding rock. Then the water passes its outlet somewhere below; the floor goes still; and from far apart along the edge come two or three knocks, wonderfully distinct. A female out in the grass turns herself about. She goes a few steps and waits for it to happen again. Behind her the blades she has walked through stand pearled on either side of a small dark passage, already beginning to close.
