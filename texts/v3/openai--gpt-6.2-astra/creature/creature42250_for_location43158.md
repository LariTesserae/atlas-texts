On the underside of a stick of stonewort there is a little more grey than on the upper side. It is hardly enough to spoil the outline of the stick. Seen very close, it is a company of little cups, clear at the rim and faintly brown farther down, each on a stalk finer than anything the plant itself puts out. A cup is wide enough to take in one of the loose grains of lime, if there were anything to put it there. What it does take in is much smaller. Around the rim there is a continual stirring, and a fleck that seemed quite at rest a little way off comes nearer by degrees, then makes a quick circuit of the rim and is gone.

These are bell animalcules. Each has its own attachment to the stonewort; where they are thickest, the stalks cross and give the impression of branches. There is room underneath them for a fleck to travel a considerable way without being taken up. It goes in toward the stick, along it, out past the bells and in again. From the distance of the nearest cobble none of this water differs in the least from the water farther up, in the spaces between the whorls. The shadow on the granite is still the shadow of a plant.

Once in a while a bell draws up. The stalk, which a moment before could scarcely be made out, is now a distinct little spring, with the closed cup at its end close against the lime. The others go on feeding. By the time the stalk is out to its full length again it is difficult to tell which one it was. A shadow passing over them makes no such difference. Nor does there seem to be any relation between their movements and the note in the stone. If the work down the lake were suspended, it would be a matter of some delicacy to establish whether a single cup had noticed.

There are not bells on every stem. On one they extend as far as the first whorl and stop there, though the footing beyond it looks equally good. On another there are three well out toward the tip, standing apart, their rims turned in different directions. Between these places is a length of quite bare lime. When one of the bells divides, one of the two stays on the old stalk. The other grows a girdle of beating hairs, lets go, and travels for a time under its own power. It might get across that bare length in a little while. It need not cross the bay. There is another stem a short way above, with the same light coming past it and much the same drift of things too small to see. For the time it takes to get there, there is one inhabitant of the bay out in the clear water, with no particular up or down to it. Then it touches lime, holds, and begins to put a little distance between itself and the stonewort on a stalk of its own.
