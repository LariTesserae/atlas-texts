One of the people who put the pavement back that winter had lost a shoe under it. She could feel it bobbing against the sole of her bare foot. It was a good shoe, a single scale off the flank of a perch, worn down until she could feel a comb mark through it nearly as well as with her skin. But every time she tried to get her toes under the edge of it she felt the shrimp let go all along her leg and settle again, facing the new current. She took her foot out. For a long time afterward she would say, in giving directions, to go as far as her shoe and take the stair.

She was an ordinary-sized woman for the root. Standing upright in the gutter she could just put her chin on the pavement. She had four long toes on each foot, with a little webbing left between them, and hands so nearly the color of willow that when she laid one flat to rest it, all that could be seen at first were the dark crescents of wear at the ends of her fingers. In the half hour of silver light one could see two faint fans of red behind her ears. These had been much fuller when she was small. Like everyone in the root, she had spent the first part of her life in water.

The children are kept in a settling yard of their own. A square of rush pith floats there with holes in it of different sizes, and if there is to be a run of water, the smallest children back into the holes and spread their ear fans. It is rather like watching a handful of flowers arrange themselves. The larger ones are supposed to take hold at the four corners. Sometimes they all take hold at the same corner in order to send the square round and round. A grown person whose turn it is to sit by the yard can tell by the tug on the mooring thread what is going on, without having to get up.

When a child begins to come out, there is usually an interval of wretchedness. The old slits itch and the skin splits at the heels. Nothing tastes quite right taken in air. For this there is a particular food made by scraping the wet meal from the inside of a fallen catkin and pressing it into cups no bigger than the empty husks of the eggs in the curb. A child can sit on the step with its feet in the yard and eat the cup too. Very old people like it. There is always somebody in the market with a tray of the little cups under a damp leaf, though there may not be a child coming out in any of the six clearings.

The woman with one shoe kept the tray for several seasons. Her other work was at the reed frame. She had a better touch than most for the difference between a thread that was biting into the root tip and one that was only tight. One of her fingers would lie on the thread and another on the wood just ahead of it, and she would wait there, sometimes through the emptying of a whole gutter. A root under too much restraint swells on the free side of the binding. By the time the swelling can be seen it may be too late to ease it. There are, laid away behind the houses, two or three beautiful little staircases that go nowhere, made out of the curled ends of failures.

It was she who first felt the swimmer in the join beyond the frame. It gave a stroke, coasted, and gave two strokes close together on coming about. The impulse went some way through living wood. Once she knew it she could pick it out with her stocking foot from halfway down the new street. Nobody could agree about what the creature ate. The water coming out of the join was perfectly clear, and no one liked to slip anything in there for fear of wedging the green faces apart. They did lay a strand of teased nettle across the mouth to find out whether it ever came out. The strand stayed as it had been laid until somebody's small son admitted to straightening it every morning on his way to the yard.

Late in the cold weather the woman was at the frame with him when a duck set its foot down overhead. At the first knock she closed her hand around the boy's ankle. At the second he was quite still. So were the hands holding the tray in the market, and the feet of the long-barrow man braced against the knot, and the four children afloat on the square of pith. In the quiet the swimmer made its turn. The boy felt the two strokes through his heel. For a little while after the duck had gone on he kept his foot exactly where it was, and presently the strokes came again.

He is one of the people who renew the comb marks now. At the back of the last clearing there is a length of pavement he still does by hand. The arch has taken, and there is room under it for a person with a tray to pass another coming home empty. The join is opening rather farther along. A comb drawn hard over that thin place would make a very fair runnel and drain it in a night. So he goes down on his knees and presses in the three grooves separately, a nail's breadth at a time, feeling between pressures for the little kick. There is more than one by now. They turn at different places.
