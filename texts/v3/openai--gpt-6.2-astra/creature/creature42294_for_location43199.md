The small one laid crosswise is not asleep. Its nose hangs down between two of the broad backs, and every time one of the sleepers breathes out, its whiskers part in the middle. It is doing this for the pleasure of the feeling. Presently one of the backs begins to rise. The small one puts out a foot on either side and rides as far as the door, where the ceiling is low enough to wipe it off.

On their feet the striped animals look much less substantial than they did lying down. Most of the breadth is in a fold of hide that runs from the foreleg to the hip. In a well-fed animal it hangs in two soft pleats. The feet, on the other hand, are as big as you would expect, and quite bare underneath, the color of the stone except for a white button at the heel. They go up the sloping street with a little outward turn of the hind feet. You can tell the old ones by the way they begin to make the turn for the square a good six paces before they get there.

What a muzzle wants is the bloom on a young column. An animal draws its upper lip back and passes its lower teeth up the column as delicately as you might take a curl of butter. There is a white damp line for a moment. By the time the next animal comes along, the line has gone clear and green, and light from the street has found a way into the room behind it. In a room which has been slept in for many seasons the light comes in at the precise height of a comfortable chin.

For all their many feet, the golden animals have trouble getting across these polished places. One comes to the top of a green streak and feels down it with the first four or five feet on each side. Then it backs up, goes sideways almost its own length and tries again. Usually it finds a tooth mark it can follow down. Seen close, the gold is a dust caught in the nap of its coat. Under a loose flap at the base of a column you sometimes come on a whole family just out of their skins, white as cut almonds, with a single gold parent passing to and fro over them. Each time it passes it leaves a little of its color behind.

The barley-sized things have more to do with the gray fliers than you would suppose. Stay in the street toward evening, when the light no longer throws a shadow, and you can see a flier arrive instead of merely seeing where it has been. It comes up under a red fork and catches hold with its wings still spread. The little face in the fork tips so far forward you expect the rest of the creature to come tumbling after. There is scarcely any rest of it. There is a mouth, a pair of bright eyes and a bag of white skin fitted to the hollow like a thumb to the thumb of a glove. The flier puts its mouth to the small mouth. For an instant you can see an amber bead between them. It may visit eight or ten forks in this way, skipping one here and doubling back to another, before it folds its wings and goes creeping headfirst into a crack to spend the night.

Once in a long while you find a fork with the white skin hanging empty and two damp gray strips stuck to the column below. A forefoot comes out from between the strips. Then the whole draggled business walks purposefully up the column into the sun.

There are creatures farther in that no one coming over the notch would count among the inhabitants. Where an old root has shrunk, it leaves a passage about as thick as a knitting needle. At the mouth of the passage, in the dark deposit, you may see a fresh white stroke ending in a hook. Wait quietly and the hook straightens. It is the end of a pale animal easing its way out backwards, bringing with it a crumb of black grit clasped in the last two joints of its body. It lays the crumb down, feels for the hole again and draws itself in. A night of such journeys will leave a heap that would hardly cover a thumbnail. In the morning the warm breath scatters the heap, and the striped sleepers nearest the wall wake up with black powder along the ridges of their ears.

Out at the newest roof there is no grit yet, no polished streak, not even a fork deep enough to hold one of the little white bags. The three gold animals on the underside have the place to themselves. Two are working along the rim. The third has found the slight hollow left by a stone that pressed against the blade when it stood upright. It goes round the hollow a few times and settles into it, tucking its feet away in order, beginning at the tail. From the plain, if anything down there had eyes good enough, it would show as a spot of gold in a green sky. From here you have to lie flat on the warm rock and put your cheek very near the edge to see it at all.
