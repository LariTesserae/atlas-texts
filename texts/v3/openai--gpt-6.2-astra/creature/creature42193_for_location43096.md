On the underside of the raft there are mussels. The easiest one to see is on the iron dog at the corner that has lifted. It has the size and general shape of a cobbler’s thumb, and is dark blue except at the lip, where there is a crescent of quite tender-looking pink. A little skirt of water slips into it on one side and comes out smartly on the other. A loose hair of root, caught in the returning water, writes the same small e over and over against the iron.

There are enough of them to account for the white dust. They grow tremendously fast and live a long time and are consequently rather obliged to get rid of shell. It comes off in leaves, like the outside of an onion. Down beside the intake pipe it is possible to see a leaf standing entire in the water, a translucent white mussel with nothing in it, rocking a little on its point. The next one along has a hole in it, and the next is a piece of lace. What goes through the motor is finer than flour. If a drop from the spout dries on the back of the hand it leaves a white ring with a minute shine of mother-of-pearl in it.

A mussel on a baulk stays a mussel. It is where the young ones have settled on the roots that something rather different has happened. The root puts out finer roots to either side of its tenant, and these run round the margins of the shell and meet at the hinge. There is always a good current here. The root thickens handsomely. So does the mussel, though the space left it is increasingly long and narrow. A foot further up there is no shell to speak of, only two ribbons of pearl laid along a groove in the wood, and inside the groove a fine grey purse which opens and shuts. There are hundreds of these purses in a limb. The big gulp one hears with one's ear to the bark is the work of perhaps half a dozen of them, letting go together.

This can be seen to advantage on a wet day on the shed side of the middle tree. An old pruning cut there has never closed. Inside the roll of bark is what looks like the pleated bosom of a very clean shirt. Rain gathers on the bottom pleat until it is too heavy, when the pleat turns over and passes the drop *up* to the next. It is quite a business getting one drop in. Sometimes it is passed back two or three times. Hold a hat over the cut and the pleats go on working for a minute or so, then presently lie still, with the last drop showing about halfway up like a lost stud.

The first man to see this must have had a notion of what was coming. On the underside of the drawer with the chalk in it there is a pencil drawing of the orchard, made when the drawer still came out. The rows are ruled; each tree is a circle with a dot in it. Around one circle the man has drawn another, freehand, and led a line from it to the margin, where he has written *flesh all the way to tip*. One can imagine him up the ladder with his knife, trying a succession of smaller and smaller twigs, and at last coming down and putting the ladder away. There are two little rusty scallops on the wall where its hooks used to be.

There is flesh all the way across the orchard now. In February it is mostly down below, snug between the baulks. By the time the mint flowers it has come up into the crosswise layers of leaf, in which it lies spread out and very thin, like the white of an egg run under a plate. This is what follows a walker. The weight of a boot squeezes it sideways; it goes easily in the direction in which the leaves are laid, meets the grain of the next layer, and comes back by a slightly longer way. A child jumping twice in the same place can occasionally get a little lift out of it. Three jumps will not do. By then the purses in the nearest tree have taken up the slack and the ground is as hard as a doorstep.

Whether there is still much sense in counting the mussels is doubtful. Certainly at the lifted corner one can count to eleven without difficulty. After eleven there is a pair that share a skirt, and beyond these a blue length like a closed penknife which has seven hinges along its back. Put a finger lightly on any one of the hinges and all seven close. So does the pink mouth on the dog. A little later, well out of sight around the shed, the round leaves turn over with a sound like a pack of cards being squared up. The heron knows more or less where to stand. It is wrong only about the size of what it has heard.

Once in recent years the belt came off. It was off from a Thursday afternoon until the electricity man happened down the lane on Monday. What struck him, opening the gate, was the smell of pears. There was none on the trees; he looked, being fond of a pear. The smell was coming from the slot in the shed floor. He got down on hands and knees with his torch and saw, some distance below the slack belt, a pale, softly dimpled surface rising toward him in the dark. A bead of juice stood in each dimple. As he watched, the beads ran together, the surface tipped, and a good pint went quietly over its edge into the water. Then it began to fill again.

He put the belt back. There was a brief commotion in the mint and a quantity of white stuff came out of the spout, followed by the usual clear water. He stayed to wash his hands in it. All the way up the lane, and for a good bit of the afternoon afterward, his shirt cuffs smelt of ripe William pears.

The glazed brick has a shallow hollow in it now. What makes the noise is not quite the branch but a strip of exposed pearl on the side of it, kept very smooth by use. For several hours after it has passed the brick there is a narrow pink line along the pearl. The line widens in the damp of evening. Two soft lips turn out, and between them, tasting the air a good foot above the ground, hangs the little root hair, still making its letter.
