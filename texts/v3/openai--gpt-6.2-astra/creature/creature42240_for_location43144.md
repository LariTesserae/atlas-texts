A handprint in Measures has a gap across the heel. The skin there is drawn up into a shallow fold. Close the hand on a file or a stair rail and the fold shuts tight; spread the fingers on a roof and two damp, exceedingly tender ridges come into contact with the stone. The people feel through these much as they hear through their ears. A freight bearing going west and a freight bearing coming east give much the same click to a visitor. To a person with a hand down there is the click itself and then a little tickle running out towards one thumb or the other. It is difficult to get them to understand that anybody could mistake the two.

In most respects they look quite ordinary. They are rather short, which a tall visitor may attribute to the height of the connecting bridges until it occurs to him who built the bridges. Their hair, where it grows, grows thick and straight. There are fair people and dark people, and people who have never found out which they are. An amber work bulb is a poor light by which to settle a question of complexion. They take a good deal of trouble over the way they look, all the same. The fashion in the numbered courts at present is for a row of little pleats running from shoulder to cuff. You can admire the whole sleeve in the time it takes to put your hand through somebody’s arm.

They do not go slowly on account of the engines. If anything, a minute is a rather awkward length of time for a human being to wait for a wheel. In Number Three there is a game the attendants play with six nuts and a leather cup in the interval between readings. It is possible to lose a week’s lamp allowance before the chalk mark comes round. A forewoman tried making a second mark halfway round the rim, to give the idle attendant something useful to do. The game is now played with three nuts.

At home the principal article of furniture is a long seat cut out of the wall, with a channel behind it for things people are inclined to lose. A bedroom above will often have its seat cut from the very same upright of basalt. If the two households are on visiting terms, a person on the lower seat can lift a heel and inquire whether the people on the upper one would like company. There is no regular language of knocks. In Court Eight two quick strokes and a drag means that an old man upstairs has dropped his luminous rule down the channel again and would somebody please recover it before he loses his place. It means this to precisely four people. One of them has moved to the station district and occasionally wakes there with his arm already halfway under the bed.

It would be a mistake to suppose that a citizen of Measures spends much time contemplating permanence. Most things that matter go off very quickly. A cake of freight yeast is good for perhaps six days after the seal is broken. A baby can wear a cap for three weeks and then quite suddenly it will not go on. There is a boy in Eight who has been taken out to the rim every year of his life to see the shell animals and has yet to take much interest in them. What he likes is the excursion coat, a great red garment with a pocket at each knee, passed along among the children of the court as they grow. Last year he had to turn the cuffs back. This year his wrists stick out. He has begun asking if there is not some other child further down the stair who would look better in a different colour.

His grandmother works on the empty strip. This is a job chiefly of walking its length on the ordinary stone alongside and picking up anything that might get blown across. Once in twenty years a crew comes out with a plate from the foundry, and she gets to attend the laying down and the taking up, but for the rest of the time what she has in her sack at the end of a shift is a split washer, the skin off a cable, perhaps a paper twist with a little salt still in it. Her hands are badly stiffened. To get the left one flat she has to lay it on the rock and press the fingers apart with the right. She can nevertheless tell, a good five minutes before her grandson can, that a loaded car is making the curve.

Sometimes on the way home the two of them sit by the western cord until one goes by. In her working life she has examined a great many of the plates sent down to the low country. She has no very clear idea what the low country is like. She knows there are workshops there in which a person can stand back from a bench and see the whole of it, legs and all, without taking out a light. The boy wants to know how far back.

“As far as the wall, I should think,” she says.

He considers this. He has seen the whole of a shell animal and once, when several people happened to turn their lamps the same way, the whole of a door. He puts his hand down beside hers. For a while he feels nothing but the warmth and a little troublesome pulse that is his own. Then there it is, a stitch, a space, another stitch a little farther off. He keeps his hand there long after she has got up and shaken out the skirts of her coat. He wants to find out how much farther he can follow it this year than last.
