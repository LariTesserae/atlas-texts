The woman who laid the square is still alive. She has a room under the third oven, a little warmer than the rooms on either side, and makes a point of telling visitors that she laid only the middle of the square. The work at the edges is her brother’s. You can tell his work, she says, by putting down a stool. It will stand on three legs wherever you put it. This is unfair to the brother, since the thing under the stool is usually alive.

Her name is Enne. She was eleven when the middle was laid. The men who split the stone had made the flags thin enough for two children to carry, and what was wanted then was somebody light who could go out on a flag on hands and knees without tipping it up out of its bed. Enne had a sliver of horn to feel for the joints. She remembers the pleasant job of feeding root fibre into a joint until the horn came back clean. All the time her father was cutting the picture of the mountain, and from where she knelt it looked to her like a man trying to get a splinter out of the sole of his foot.

She has a long space between two toes, and a white mark running up from it as far as the ankle. For years her family thought it was a paving scar. It was made by a gander. There were geese here once, seven of them, which went up and down the street in order of size and had to be lifted separately through a trap at night. At the time there were only three houses below the sill. Everybody else slept behind the ovens. The geese would not sleep unless they could see a little way down.

In Enne’s room there is a girl who has come to learn floors. She is no relation. Her father lives near enough for him to pass her a spoon, if he ties it in the corner of a cloth and swings it; to come to dinner he has to put on his knees, as they say here, meaning the padded leather things that keep an old person from being hurt on the stairs. The girl’s name is Iva. She did not particularly want to learn floors. What she wanted was Enne’s room, which has a twist in one of its timbers conveniently shaped for hanging a cradle. She said so when she came. Enne likes her very much.

It is an odd thing to learn from inside a house. All the fine work of a floor is on its underside. In the season when the new rods are supple, you can look along under the sill and see here and there the rather solemn head of a person hanging head down, with a mouthful of peeled ends. Iva does not yet go over. She sits indoors with a smooth stone in her lap, pushing up with it where Enne tells her, and feels through the stone the very distinct little double pull by which Enne lets her know that a crossing has held. They have done a patch as large as a coat this way. In it, though neither knows this yet, is a pink woollen thread from Iva’s sleeve. It runs among the willow for the length of a hand and comes out on the side toward the valley, where a bird will find it in the spring.

A number of creatures live on that side exclusively. There is a yellow-bellied fly that spends its whole adult life walking upside down. Its young are heavy things, clear as drops of broth, each hanging by a white stalk. The stalks are very good for finding a draught. When the new work is tight they hang straight down. If one trembles, there is still a place above it where the wind can get in and lift the ash off a hearth. Enne will get up in the night for the sound of ash moving. It is not quite a sound; it is more that, in the midst of all the little sounds she knows, somebody seems to have begun breathing with a much larger chest.

The flies get into the room sometimes and have to be taken out on the back of a spoon. Set on a table, they lie on their backs and walk away into the air. Very small children find this upsetting. Iva has a trick of turning the spoon slowly as she takes it to the door, so that at no point does the fly feel that the world has gone wrong beneath its feet.

When the paving beast rises, Iva goes up first with the stools. Enne comes up after in her own time. She lets the others lean over the picture and trace the high pasture and the watercourse with their fingers. There is always an argument about a little quadrangular hollow on the far side of the pasture. One family remembers a sheepfold there; another remembers a house with four beds in it. It was the place where her father mixed his grit and oil. Enne has told them. The next time the stones come clear they have the argument again. By now she is almost curious to hear who lived in the house.

What she wants to show Iva is a flag a little way off, quite bare except for six short cuts near one edge. Enne has brought up a jug. She pours a little water on the flag, and the water goes out in a sheet, hesitates, and divides into six bright threads that follow the cuts. At the edge they fall. A moment later, down where the houses are, there is the soft rapid drumming of water on a skin drawn tight across a jar.

“That one’s ours,” she says.

All over the square people are finding their own little sets of cuts. A man is on his knees with a grass stem cleaning his out. Children have gone down to listen at the jars and call up when they hear anything. Rain that would otherwise spend half a mile falling through the dark is being put away in the houses a cupful at a time. The beast, quite unconcerned, has reached the last socket but two. Folded up like this it has a surprisingly small face, with a kind of beard of wet grit under the mouth. Iva finds herself holding her jug out to it. It drinks from the jug. Nobody seems to have tried this before. Enne puts a hand under Iva’s elbow to take some of the weight, and the two of them stand quite still, feeling against the rim the small hard teeth that have been under their feet all their lives.
