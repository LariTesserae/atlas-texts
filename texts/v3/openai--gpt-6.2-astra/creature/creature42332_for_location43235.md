A shrimp coming up the seam sometimes stops with its feeler bent against something clear. It can still be seen working its little feet. Then it goes down, quite suddenly, although for another moment its shadow is on the wall. The second creature, whose shadow one would like to see, makes scarcely any. There is a place where the running lines of light are pinched together as they would be in the waist of a glass bottle.

This is usually an animal lying across the mouth of its lodging. Most of it is back in the stone. Full grown it is about the length of a thumb and so flat that the red thread of the gut, when it has eaten, shows as well from below as from above. It has four feet, set very far apart. The toes of the hind pair turn forward and those of the fore pair turn back. In a narrow crack it can stand with one pair against the roof and the other on the floor and let quite a strong current go by between its legs. Brought out onto the top of a back, it would hardly be able to advance its own length. There is no obvious head until the mouth opens. What looks like the head, a white knob rather broader than the body, is at the other end.

It does not chase the shrimp. There are places along the bacterial seam where the overhead stone comes down so low that anything following the seam must turn on its side. The animal sets its lips there. They are wide, fringed inside with soft hairs, and fit to the wall with a nicety that leaves the shrimp its accustomed feel of stone until it is a good way in. A shrimp that backs out is let go. The cost of losing one's hold in that water is considerably more than the loss of a meal.

In a lamp carried down the eastern bluff one can see a little of the underside of the shelf. There are animals there too. The white knobs show first, like pins someone has stuck in the roof of the bedding plane, all inclined the same way. It is from this end that the animal gets its air. A thousand fine white folds are packed into the knob; in slack water they come apart a little and the thing looks woolly. In the full scour they close to a point. An individual can thus have its mouth in water nearly spent of air, far back in a feeding crack, and its hind end in a stream newly mixed with the air of a chamber. A good station, with the two waters just a body length apart, is worth keeping.

The young have no such station. For a time they live in the pale collars, in the little galleries left when one growth of lime has roofed another. They are rounder than their parents. Their bodies will go round a corner that their feet, when they first grow, cannot negotiate, and there is a stage at which the little creatures spend much of their time patiently backing up. They eat the skin off the walls. One can sometimes count the young without seeing any of them: in the milky inside of a collar are several short clean windows, clear enough to see the blue stone through, and at one end of each window a new paring is taken away. There is no common size to the windows. The season in which a creature hatched has very little bearing on its size here; what matters is the wall it got.

Once in a while a nearly grown one comes out among the dancing grains. It is a much more substantial thing than could reasonably be expected to issue from the collar. For a few seconds it goes up and down with a grain lodged in the crook of each foreleg. The first useful contact is often with the lee side of the spring lip, no farther away than the breadth of a pencil. It gets one foot over. All at once the animal is still and the grains are going on without it. It tries the stone with the whole length of its lower lip, moving a little way, trying again. It may spend an afternoon going round the outside of the collar and arrive within half an inch of where it came out. But by then it is underneath. There the current sets along the rock instead of away from it, and there are ways onward.

What the shelf amounts to for such a creature is difficult to see from above. Two pools with nothing but a little rounded sill between them may be quite separate countries, while the underside of a back half a mile long may offer an unbroken walk. Nor is the noise quite what one might suppose. With its feet fast and its fringe open against the feeding wall, the animal will let the concussion of a whole chamber of falling water pass through it without drawing in a hair. But let a shrimp put down one foot on the outermost edge of its lip: the lip begins to fold.
