The first one to come out on the eastern shelf is the color of the stone. One sees it because it has a shadow on both sides. The shadow on the sunny side is quite narrow. It belongs to the living half of the creature, which is still down in the rock; what has come up into the air is a kind of ear, a long slip of translucent horn, broader at the free end than at the root. The light passes through it and comes to a focus a little short of the stone. A minute object held there would be seen in considerable detail by the animal underneath.

Nothing is held there. After a while the ear goes in.

Down in the shelf there are a great many of these creatures. They occupy the space between what is being taken away and what is being put back. At any given spot this space is very small. A crystal lets go its neighbor; another crystal has almost reached the place where the neighbor was. There is room in the interval for a membrane, and what lives in the shelf consists mostly of membrane. It has no thickness to speak of, but it can be extensive. The animal that put out the ear reaches from well under the white ridge to the near side of the grove. Its hindmost parts are in country that has not yet come up into the light.

In the cliff face one could see such an animal edge on without seeing it at all. Where it crosses the machinery of a grove it divides. There is a separate film to go over each wheel, fine little cuffs for the spindles, and a deep fold, exquisitely loose, to allow for the turning of the screw. None of these parts goes round. The screw turns in the fold as a shoulder turns in a sleeve. This close association has one effect that can be heard above ground. Now and then two neighboring films draw apart with a *tik*. The sound comes up the oil in a rib. One might listen at the leaf for a second sound to complete the first, but the films come together again in perfect silence.

Their young have rather more substance. There is a stage at which one would go comfortably into the bowl of a spoon. It looks like a little purse made of the skin inside an eggshell, closed at one end, with six or eight blunt lips at the other. Inside the purse is a single hard thing. The young one turns this thing about with its lips. It is a grain from the seam on which it was born, and must last it until it finds another seam of the same kind. By turning the grain a very little at a time it gets a succession of small differences between the charge on its inside and the charge on its outside. It can travel on these for a surprisingly long way.

It does not travel under the leaves. It goes out through the ends of the roots into bare stone, folding itself small to pass a place that has finished growing, opening to its full purse shape wherever there is work going on. Sometimes there are scores of them in the thickness of a step, each with its single grain, each going its own way. When two meet they stop. Their lips open against one another and for a time each turns the other's stone. Then they part, occasionally with their grains exchanged. There appears to be no advantage in this. An exchanged grain lasts no longer than the original one. But a young animal that has been a long while alone will turn aside a considerable distance to do it.

Most of them settle at a bend. The current is a little wider there, and there is room to spread. The purse opens along one side, the lips flatten out, and the grain stays where it is while the creature grows away from it in all directions. The dark spot sometimes visible in the heart of a polished seam is such a grain. In an old animal it may be the only part that has a fixed address. All the rest is continually arriving: under a root, round a wheel, up into the thin sun-warmed crust where, in favorable weather, it can put out an ear.

It is not the light that interests it particularly. Light of very nearly the same quality has lain on the upper surface for as long as there has been an upper surface. What it is equipped to notice is an interruption. The horn will stay up for hours if a leaf tip happens to enter its focus. One can see the tip there, upside down and golden, with every separate hair along its margin enormously distinct. The animal makes no attempt to touch it. After the tip has been taken back into the leaf, the ear often holds its position a good while longer.

On the eastern shelf the ridge has risen a little. A strip of stone no wider than a fingernail is receiving sun for the first time. Along it, at irregular intervals, the horns are coming out. Some are very short and thick, the first efforts of animals scarcely done with wandering. One is so old that its free end has divided into a fan. For a few minutes every slip in the fan holds an image of the cream-colored headland across the gap. Then the ridge rises far enough to interrupt the light, and the images run together.

In the new shade the outermost leaf of the grove begins to unfold. Its underside is a clean, soft copper. Before it has opened the width of a hand, a young ear has risen into the space beneath it, and the first hair on the very edge of the leaf hangs there in the light, large as a tree.
