On the underside of the shelf in the shelter there is a patch which the sand has polished to the color of horn. A little of it shows at the front edge. It looks like a vein in the stone, except that at the first knock from the mountain the whole vein draws back an inch.

It is the lip of an animal. The rest of it fits in the angle where the shelf meets the wall, a good place in running water and a poor one in slack. In slack water it has to come out to eat. It lets its lip down over the edge of the shelf and turns it up into a gutter to catch the fall of sand. There is a skin on each grain, too thin to give the grain a color. The animal strips this off. Clean sand issues from either end of the gutter in two twisting strings. On the floor beneath an occupied shelf there are consequently two little cones on either side of the trough, quite sharp at their tips. They last until the next running of the lake.

There are more of these animals on the ridges. An empty socket suits a young one very well. Its lower part is a bag of tough, puckered skin which will take the shape of almost any hole; it pushes the bag in empty and fills it with water. Once seated, it can turn its lip quite inside out without being pulled loose. The lip opens in three directions. Laid out on the rock it looks rather like a mitten made for a hand with two thumbs. Each thumb can reach a little way into the next socket. Much of the business of the ridge goes on within that short reach.

They pass things. A mica flake passes very quickly along a row of occupied holes, each lip closing on it and opening a little farther along. A strip of red leaf travels more slowly and gets smaller. What is curious is to see a white seed passed. There is food on the feet of the seed, and the first animal to get it cleans two of the feet quite bare. It gives the seed up with the third foot still folded under it. Four or five animals along, sometimes twenty, one of them unfolds the foot and presses it to the rock. It may hold it there through several runnings. In time a black thread rises between the two thumbs. The lip keeps a bare space round the thread until it has put down a socket of its own.

It would be easy to suppose they were planting for the shelter of the trees. Yet they plant on the sheltered side as often as on the exposed one, and make no use of the ribbons that can be seen. What they lack on a bare slope is not cover but holes. A grove lasts a long time. The sockets it leaves last longer. There are places on the second ridge where an old animal, reaching as far as its skin will stretch, is just able to touch a tree it held as a seed. It will not live in that hole. Something the size of a split pea, tucked into a pleat of its water bag, very likely will.

The little ones go out on foot. They go when the woods are down and the current at its worst, which seems an odd choice until one sees the difficulty they have with a gap. They must keep some part of the lip pressed down at all times. A finger's breadth of silt is as good as six miles to them. But at full run a ribbon lies plastered to the crest from its socket to its tip. A young animal can go the length of it on the underside, in a flat glistening passage scarcely deeper than itself, and come off on a stone it could never have reached in still water. A few are caught out when the woods lift. They stay on the ribbon, folded tight as a shirt button, sometimes for days. Nothing induces them to let go.

The one in the shelter is very old. Its bag fills a crack the length of the shelf. For a long time both ends of its lip stopped short of the walls, but the left end has grown down along a joint and can now touch the floor. There is another animal there, in the trough, so scoured by grit that one can see the dark grains traveling through its lip. Between runnings the two are usually in contact. Nothing much goes between them. Once, in the bright interval before the rush, the lower one could be seen reaching up with a small angular object which the other received and drew back under the shelf.

It was a chip of wood. The peg at the bend has begun at last to go at the heart. Its outside still bears the little flats left by the tool, but from a black place at the top of it soft splinters work loose and come hopping up the road among the stones. There is no nourishment in them that the animals can get at. They pass them nevertheless. Under the shelf, pressed end to end into the joint, there is already a pale line of wood as long as a traveler's leg. Where it ends there is open mortar, and then the other wall, still out of reach.
