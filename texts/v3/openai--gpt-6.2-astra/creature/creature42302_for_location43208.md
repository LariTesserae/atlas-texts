There is a little gray coat on the great crab's underside which is not there every time it comes round. It ends precisely at the joints of the legs, as if put on with a brush by somebody who meant the joints to work. The crab wears it for the passage across the soft mud and for some way up the stair. Where the water begins to take light the coat comes off. A piece as broad as a fingernail lifts at one corner, folds, and goes over the edge of the crab like a scrap of wet paper. Presently there are a dozen such scraps on the stone, all going the same way.

They are very thin animals. In the light one can see through two of them laid one on the other. Each has a little sandy point somewhere in it, not necessarily in the middle, and about this point the rest of the animal can gather itself until it is scarcely wider than the head of a peg. This is how they get on the crab. They wait in the mud along the broken partition. When a leg sets down among them they draw together into grains and stick in the short hair at the joint. It is only when they have worked their way up under the belly plate that they spread out. The crab takes no more notice of them than of its shells. There are places on its back that have not been bare in years, but its underside is as clean as the bowl of a spoon.

What the thin animals come to the stair for is the narrow margin where the water ends. There is food there that does not grow at the seep. Dust comes out of the cloud and settles on the tooth-coloured step. Some of it is yellow dust from trees a long way below; some is the broken scales of things with wings. The stone looks clean enough. But when a tongue of water runs a little farther up it than usual, the tongue comes back with a wrinkled edge. Two or three of the thin animals will lie under that edge all day with their upper surfaces puckered into it. By evening one can see the yellow in them.

They have no particular front. To go up the stone one makes a little hem of its uphill edge and rolls the rest of itself after it. To go down it does the same with the other edge. One that has come against a shellfish will sometimes go up the shell and partway into the opening, to be pinched there until the shellfish next requires fresh water. It leaves a clear rim of itself behind when it gets free. The rim goes on puckering for a while. There are a good many such rims in the cistern; not everything that moves here is a whole animal.

At night, when there is as little light on the pale slab as at the bottom of the trough, a worm comes out of a crack in the red gauge. It is chestnut brown, the one strong colour among the living things in the basin, and no thicker at its head than at its tail. It goes feeling along the wet line. Where it finds one of the thin animals it lays its head down flat and stays there. In a little while there is a sandy point on the stone and the worm is feeling its way along again. If disturbed it backs into its crack so exactly that it need not turn. The last bit to go in is a pale button on the end of the head. One might take this for the worn end of one of the masons' lines. Indeed there is one line too many on the gauge at night, if one feels for them very gently.

When the water goes over the sill the thin animals go too, but not far. They roll until they come to the underside of the first projecting tread, where the stone is always damp, even with the cloud below it. There they thicken and lose their clearness. For the dry part of the year there is a row of little gray husks under the nosing of that step. A mountaineer putting a hand there for a hold would feel no more than the roughness of badly finished stone.

The first water over in a later season softens the husks. Each opens along the fold by which it once rolled itself uphill, and out of it come several animals so fine that all of them together drifting through the doorway make only a momentary fault in the light on the slab. A few reach the mud. The old crab sets a foot among them, or does not; there will be another circuit. Meanwhile on the outer stair the empty husks stay fast. Cloud beads on their rims, and each holds, above the long drop and quite separate from the great warm body of the cistern, its own round drop of cold water.
