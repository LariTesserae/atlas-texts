There is a shrew in the gallery under the polished root. Almost the whole of it is in a space which the vole could just get its head into. The tip of its muzzle comes out in one of the rusty bays, between two grains so close in colour to the muzzle that for a long while one sees only the particular fineness of the point on the one grain. There is a drop in the short fur under its chin. Beyond the drop are the legs of a beetle, folded up against its belly. Neither animal is aware of the other. The shrew has its attention on something a good way below the beetle, in the wet.

Down there the little bays communicate. They have a common floor of slate, beautifully clean, roofed in one place by grit and in another by the felt of the cushion. Where a pool goes down through the felt there is a thin green light on the floor. A small crustacean lies over on its side at the edge of the light, with all its colour in a thread along its gut. Its legs have the look of the teeth of a comb cut by hand, each a little different. It is these feet the shrew can feel through its jaw. There need be very little between two creatures for one to be the whole concern of the other.

The wet meadow has a great many such attentions in it. They do not all run in the direction one would think. The owl is not looking at the vole. From where it sits the animal on the root is hidden by the thickness of one willow stem. What the owl has in view, quite apart from the sky which is merely on the surface of its eye, is the underside of the leaf with the moths on it. One moth is short of a wing on its left side. In the space thus left there shows a little of the leaf itself, pale and covered with straight white hairs. This patch is well within the owl's power of sight. It would be possible in that scant light for the owl to distinguish each hair separately, and it has the time.

Farther down the willow, where a branch rests on the cushion, there are two thin marks in the bark made by the teeth of a vole. The bark is white in the marks. Here is something the vole has done. Behind its forefeet on the root are the damp prints of its hind feet, and behind those the blades of sedge lie brushed the wrong way, showing where it came out. There is no corresponding length of root in front of it. The black wood goes on, certainly, for another handbreadth, with a pale rough spot where a twig has come away; but none of that length belongs to the vole in the way the length behind it does. Its little journey is complete to the last bent whisker. It has not been interrupted.

Perhaps the hardest of them all to see properly is the snail on the yellow leaf. One looks for the foot flattened on the stalk and for the two horns set well apart, and finds them, and takes the rest for a squat, rather dirty shell. But the water serves as a poor lens. From a little to one side the shell resolves into a very clear young shell with an older whorl visible inside it, and inside that the dark, soft coil of the animal itself. The dirt is a reflection of the owl's breast high up in the willow. It reaches the pool by way of a gap between two blades of grass scarcely wide enough to put a needle through.

So there is room even on that small back for something of the upper meadow. The snail bears no weight of feathers. Its foot fits the wet stalk with a delicacy like that of a lip against the rim of a cup, and the stalk, bowed a little under the shell, holds the notch of the leaf at just the angle to keep the bubble. In the bubble the two lips of the ridge draw close together. There is a hair of brightness between them, finer towards one end. It does not come quite to a point.
