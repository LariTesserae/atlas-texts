There are fish in the pool beneath the willow, although if the pool were emptied with a spoon there would be nothing in the spoon but water. They live on the part of a leaf that is neither wet nor dry. One sees a small blunt head go by along the rim, then a length of spotted back, then, rather surprisingly far on, a tail as fine as a stitch of brown silk. A big one will go almost all the way round a hoofprint. Where its chin rests on the leaf there are six little pads the colour of pear flesh. It goes on these when it has to go on anything.

Ordinarily there is no need. It swims on its side with one gill in the water and the other opening and shutting against the moss. The two gills are quite unlike. The water one is red, in the usual way; the moss one is white and has a comb on it. Minute things live in the wet nap of the moss, clear as spit except for the green thread of leaf they have eaten, and when the fish has passed there is a combed strip, and the minute things are gone, and a little green is beginning to show through the white gill. This accounts for much of the smell about the pools. A clean fish lifted out on a leaf smells of almost nothing. It is the strips that smell, particularly when it has been a good while since they were fed on and all the cropped ends have begun to bleed.

The young are short enough to turn round in the cup of a violet. As they grow longer they lose the knack. A grown fish, finding another coming towards it, has a long business ahead of it. The heads come together. The pads explore. One fish begins to climb over the other's snout, taking its water with it in a trembling fold under the jaw. It works its way along the spotted back, and the lower fish keeps still, except for the mouth, which goes on opening and closing with an expression of having a good deal to put up with. Sometimes a moth lets go overhead and the shock tumbles the upper fish off. Then the whole thing has to be done again. There are pairs which by this means come to know each other fairly well. Afterwards, in a pool with room for a dozen, those two will feed abreast, one combing out what the other has missed.

It was such a pair that first went down the hare's bed. They followed a bead of water along the stalk of a moss leaf, head to tail, no farther apart than the two ends of a staple. From above, their tails vanished almost together. Below, the first came out alone. It fed for some time on the underside, where the minute grazers, never having been combed before, grew so large that their green insides could be seen twisting away from the advancing gill. The fish grew fat. It acquired a nick in its lip from trying to eat the hard dark foot of a beetle. By the time the second head came through, the first fish was resting, full grown and a little faded, under a bead of water so heavy it had drawn the leaf to a point.

The second fish was still in the act of following. Its chin pads were stretched forward to feel for the tail that had just been there. For a while it went hurrying about the point of the leaf, young and bright and very hungry. Then the two lay up together. Both are there yet. With a light behind the leaf one can make out the six pads of the younger fish and eleven of the twelve spots on the older; the twelfth is under its companion's cheek.

Out in the larger pool the fish have begun to spawn. They put the eggs as high as they can get them, the mother going up a stem on her chin and the father following with his mouth full. To the bare eye the eggs look like the bubbles that gather on a cut stalk in a glass of water. Each has a brown fleck in it. In the lowest ones, which are the oldest, the fleck has already uncurled far enough to put its nose against its tail. What is wanted now is water on the outside. The skin of an egg wetted from without goes slack; the little fish need only straighten itself and there it is, with a whole wet circumference at its disposal.

The collar on the willow stem has reached three eggs. Two have opened. In the third the little fish has straightened the wrong way and thrust its tail out first. It hangs a moment with its spots still packed inside and the fine brown thread of it feeling about in the green light. Something very small takes hold of the thread. For the first time in its life the fish gives a kick, and the two go off together round the stem.
