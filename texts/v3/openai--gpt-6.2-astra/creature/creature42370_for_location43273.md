The woman on the step has a little girl between her knees now. The child came down from the house behind without being called and has been standing there for some time with one hand out. At last the woman gives her a fish. It is already scaled except for a patch under one fin. She turns it slippery side down on the child’s thigh and folds the small fingers over the fin to hold it back. The girl works at the patch with her thumbnail. A few scales stick to her leg, very handsome, each with a dark little crescent in it. By the time she has shown them to everybody on the step she has forgotten which fish was hers.

There is very little skin trouble here, considering the wet, but nearly everyone has a scar or two below the ankle. Children remember quite distinctly the stone that cut them. A boy taking a stranger home will point with his chin at an otherwise unremarkable stretch of street and turn up the sole of his foot for inspection. He knows where his father was cut, too. There are pieces of a basket plaited into flat sandals in every house, for going out on the bare rock. They are generally mislaid. Someone has put a hot dish on one or tucked one under a jar that rocks.

A newcomer might suppose the people unusually grave. There will be six of them gutting fish together and not a mouth opening. Actually a good deal is going on. One woman is doing the ferry master coming up the steps with his stomach held in, and her sister, who has not so much as looked up from the liver she is freeing with her thumb, is doing the ferry master going down again after dinner. It takes very little to do a person: the set of an elbow, the precise delay before a hand goes to the small of the back. The ferry master’s wife is there too. She supplies a hitch of the hip the others have missed, and for a minute no work can be done at all.

The deaf man who mends the skin boats is less easy to talk to than other people, though not on account of his deafness. He likes to have a thing right. Tell him a seam lets water in and he will ask at which end; tell him the end and he will want to know if you were sitting alone in the boat and on which side your basket lay. He asks half these questions with your basket already in his hand. Often enough he slings it from another loop and sends you out to try again. He has a small clientele of children whose boats he treats with the same exactitude. These are cuttle bones with holes bored in them and bits of broken pot for ballast. The children run them under the streets. You can lose one on the sweet side of the market and have it come out two days later among the shrimp, with the red pot still showing through the clear water like somebody’s tongue.

What the boat mender really likes is to be taken on the big ferry. He sits well forward and trails the ends of his fingers; where a cold thread comes up through the warm backwater he takes his hand out, dries it in his armpit and puts it back to feel the change afresh. The master never charges him. This is not altogether kindness. Coming in to a crowded landing, the master has only to look at the man’s broad back to know how much room he has on the blind side. Six inches and the man goes on dabbling. Three and he takes his hand out.

The birds have particular households too. Above the scaling woman lives a tern with one straight tail feather and one permanently awry, so that it can be picked out a long way down the coast. It will drop a shrimp on the step rather than carry one a little too large up to its young. The woman used to throw these back into the water. Now that the girl is old enough they have to be kept in a basin till evening, along with anything else that may require a home. At present the basin contains two shrimp, a drowned moth and a chip off a whitewashed wall. The moth has a landing place and the shrimp have a house. It is understood that nobody is to wash anything in it.

Late in the day the girl takes the basin down. Her grandmother goes with her, holding up the back of her shift so she won’t tread on it as she squats. The shrimp go out backwards, quicker than the eye; the child believes for a moment that they are still there. She picks up the chip and looks on the underside. Behind her the old woman waits with the fingers of one hand gathered in the wet cloth and the other hand resting lightly on the child’s head. She can feel under her palm the start of each question the little girl turns to ask. She bends down each time. The last question takes a while; the girl has to set the basin down to get both hands free. Then the grandmother takes off one of her sandals and puts it afloat in the crack, and they go down the street together, bent nearly double, following the moth on its way to sea.
