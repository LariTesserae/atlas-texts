The woman in the upper bed has a flake of shell behind one ear. She has been lying on her side to reach a lid that has slipped in her roof. There is room in the seat for her, or for the work, but hardly for both. Each time she puts the lid straight with her foot her shoulder dislodges another one at the back. In the end she comes out into the lane and does it from above, in the rain. The repair takes about as long as it takes her to get properly wet.

She is the only one who stays the night now. There used to be a man in the next lane with legs so long that even sitting up he had to have some part of himself out in the weather. You can still see the two scoops in the opposite wall where his heels went. He lives on the landward side of the basin and comes down sometimes with his sister's little boy. The boy likes to be taken into the man's old seat and shown where to put his feet. There is a good two feet of air between the soles of his shoes and the scoops. He considers this each time, quite gravely, as if there might reasonably have been a change since last week.

In dry weather the woman sleeps in the lane, on three lids laid with their thin ends overlapping like the scales on a fish. She has a wool blanket, soft in the middle and stiff with salt all round the edge. On a wet night she sits with it over her knees in the seat. The clean place at the back is hers. It is higher up than you would expect from seeing her on her feet. She is long from the hip to the crown of the head, and when she sits up to let you pass she gives the impression of having got to her feet and come a step toward you.

She mends things for the people up on the bar. These come down to her in an oilskin bag: mittens worn clean through between the finger and thumb, a bread sack a mouse has got into, the little square of stout cloth that takes the strain at the corner of a sail. There is usually bread in the bag too, and sometimes an egg wrapped up in something that itself requires mending. The sky reflected in the inside of a lid gives a good light for picking out black stitches. She can turn the light where she wants it with her toe. She likes the hours of greatest noise for work. It is possible then to have somebody for company without their feeling obliged to find anything to say.

There is other company, difficult to see until she puts the work down. Along the edge of her seat, just where the threshold turns the water aside, are a great many creatures like commas cut from a brown fingernail. They lie on their sides in the wet. At the touch of a loose thread one straightens out and is gone; presently it is lying on its side again an inch away. She used to think they fell in with the rain. Then one morning she lifted the threshold to bed it more firmly and found the whole underside stirring. The little ones were nearly clear. You could see the mud through them except for a dot in each, very dark and busy, which went on working even while the creature lay quite still.

The boy would like some in a jar. She lets him fill one with the bitter water, and they look at them against his sleeve, but before he goes home she has him tip them back into the runnel. It matters which side of the threshold he tips them on. He has learnt this and some other things that nobody on the bar has much occasion to know. He can tell a roof lid from a bed lid by running a finger along the rim. He knows where there is a hollow in the wall that will keep an egg cool for two days, and that you do not press the egg in to make a better fit.

Neither of them has ever found where the bird goes at night. From the woman's bed there is a view along the deepest lane for perhaps forty yards, after which the lane bends and you see only the backs of shells. She has watched the bird go round that bend many times. Once she got up and went by the shorter way across the beds, in time to see it come out. What came out was a second bird. She knew because this one had a single black toe on feet otherwise the color of weak tea. It stood looking at her with its head to one side. A little farther in, out of sight behind it, she could hear the first one tapping.

She has kept that news for the boy. It will please him to be sent to one end of the bend while she waits at the other. In the meantime she has put a small lid down beside the big ones where she sleeps. It rocks a little, and will need a handful of grit underneath. From where his head will be there is nothing to be seen of the sea or even of the land beyond the basin. There is a white edge close up, with a nick in it, and above that all the sky there is.
