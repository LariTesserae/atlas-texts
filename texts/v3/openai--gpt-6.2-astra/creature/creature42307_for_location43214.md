At the end of the quiet ten minutes a woman comes out of the engine house with a little hammer and taps the third paving slab from the door. She waits, with the hammer hanging loose in her fingers. From well out under the bike racks there is an answering tap. She goes back inside and puts the lower intake on its night setting.

It is not quite a trained animal. If she were to tap the slab at noon she could stay there all afternoon. What she has done is to make the noise of a loose piece of roof, and the creature under the square, which is just then getting up, has put a foot against it to see if it will bear weight. In the days before the square was paved the same test must often have saved it from bringing half a ton of buttery rock down on its back. The paving is a convenience to both parties. It carries the tap a considerable distance, and there is very little chance of its falling in.

There are several of these creatures under every old station. In the books they are called seam animals. Most people call one by the name of whatever is directly over it. The one that answers the woman with the hammer is the bike racks. There is a ticket office, a number four platform, and a ladies’ alterations which has outlasted by nearly eighty years the business from which it takes its name. Nobody is sure whether it is still the original animal. Every so often there are two answers from under that part of the street, a good solid knock and then a smaller, rather hasty one.

They live in the last few yards of the passages by which the buried air reaches daylight. Here the stone parts readily along the mica and the passages are very wide and very low. A man once got an arm and a shoulder into one and was able to turn a pocket lamp on its side and shine it a long way ahead. He saw what looked like a stretch of empty ballroom, perfectly polished, with a ceiling about the height of a cigarette packet. At the far end a number of ivory buttons were moving separately across the floor. When the lamp went out he could hear them still moving. He had taken them for the animals. They were the toenails of one foot.

A seam animal has no great occasion to hold itself together in a recognizable shape. Its feet go where they are wanted, joined to the rest of it by strips of flesh which lie slack on the stone. In a narrow place the feet go through one at a time. The body follows like a quantity of washing being pulled under a door. There is a good deal more of it than one expects. When they took up the floor of the old parcel room they found one lying in the warmth beneath the scales; work had to be suspended while it got itself clear. The foreman’s report gives the delay as an hour and twelve minutes. For the last quarter of an hour there was nothing left in the way but one very small foot, quite sound and apparently forgotten. A porter eventually touched it with the bristles of his brush and it went away quite briskly.

It is the west-going dust they feed on. The little walkers on the pavement take something from a fresh red grain; the seam animals want what has been left on it by the taking. Towards evening they spread a breadth of pale tacky skin across the floor of a passage. The first indrawn air lays down a coat of spent grains on it as evenly as if they had been sifted over pastry. A foot then gets hold of a corner and rolls the skin up, dirty side in. By morning it can be unrolled clean. In a healthy station there may be acres of this material between the intake and the deep air. The earliest engineers put in elaborate banks of filters in ignorance of the fact. At the opening of the first bank for its annual cleaning they found one red grain, a collar stud, and the signed inspection slip left there at installation.

Children at the rim are particularly fond of the feet. On the lower landings, where an open joint sometimes gives onto a seam, it is possible to lay a finger along the crack about sundown and feel a cool blunt nail laid against it from below. The pressure is quite deliberate. If you press back the animal is satisfied and goes on with its business. If you take your finger away too quickly it will go on feeling about for some time in the place where the roof failed. Children who know better will stand rather inconveniently across a stair until the foot has made up its mind.

The woman at the engine house has done this since she was a girl. She lives now in the rooms over the belts, with a son old enough to work the mail train and a father who no longer finds the stairs worth the journey down. At night he can feel the bike racks walking in the wall at the head of his bed. It comes up a slant seam there to within an inch or two of the plaster. For years he thought the sound was made by the little expansion joints in the hot-current conduit. Then the current was off for three days while they changed a wheel bearing, and on the second evening, in the absence of almost every other sound a building can make, he heard a whole succession of careful contacts going past his ear, followed after a pause by that rather hasty little foot.

He has shifted the bed a few inches since then. Not away from the wall. The iron bedstead had a habit, if he turned over, of knocking the plaster just at the height of the seam. He had lain awake once for the better part of the night with something on the other side patiently holding up his headboard.
