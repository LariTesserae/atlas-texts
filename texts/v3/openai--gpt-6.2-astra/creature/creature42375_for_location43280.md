The animal is easiest to see under the brass, though there is very little of it there. In the instant before the strip comes down, something draws back from its free end. It has the look of a wet thread pulled through a hem. Look at the stone afterwards and there is no thread on it, only the bright score and, a little way short of the score, a line that the grit has not been able to cross.

It lives on the undersides of things. A loose sett, turned over, would show what looks at first like the impression of a leaf in clay: a midrib, branches finer and finer towards the margins, the whole tinted the weak pink of water in which a red rag has been rinsed. Then some of the finer branches would lift. The pink would run out of them towards the midrib, and what remained on the stone would be nearly impossible to distinguish from the water left by your fingers. By the time you put the sett down, most of the animal would be on the underside of the next one.

There is no particular direction in which its head lies. The most substantial part of it is under the bottom step, in the warmth that comes up from the pump chamber. Here it is folded thickly, like the stock of an awning rolled away for winter. From this stock it lets out a little of itself every time the water rises. The folds go slack in succession. Farther out in the court, pale edges appear in the joints of the paving and turn over against the stone. A fine brown meal collects in them from the water passing from trough to trough. In still water they would get very little. Each chipped corner, with its narrow pour, can support a length of edge as long as a man's hand.

Under the parcel office one fold goes up the inside of the counter. It occupies part of the clean ellipse around the willow, in the space between the wood and the bark. There is just enough damp there to keep it soft. On that high edge it feeds rather differently. It lays a lip against the wet litter and takes off the very darkest layer of paste, so thinly that the leaves above do not subside. A leaf lifted by its stalk will come up intact, along with its little load of water. Beneath it the paste is combed in curves, as if someone had been there with the tip of a small brush.

For a long time one could make a fairly convincing plan of the creature simply by marking where the water went. The plan would be wrong at the dye works. There the animal goes straight up the wall, behind the green strip, and stops within a finger's breadth of the salt. In the dry hours it is a skin between the moss and the brick. When the cloth bar starts to run, it grows plump enough to lift the moss in little ranks. They rise from the bottom upwards, against the fall of water. At the top a clear blister stands out over a mortar joint, takes the impact of the drops for a minute or two, and slowly draws back in. Nothing is laid there. It is the one place in all six buildings where the animal comes out from under cover, and it comes out well after the wall is wet and goes in well before it dries.

Someone who knew about it fitted the brass. You can tell from the underside of the strip, which has been filed quite smooth, even around the hole for the screw. Without the bridge the two stones would shear past one another, not by much, but enough to cut the fold that goes to the willow. Now it slides in and out against brass. The arc on the neighboring stone gives a fair measure of the years this arrangement has held. The screw is of a kind no longer made. The slot is full of green.

Stand on the bottom step through a whole filling of the tank and you can feel the stock unroll. The warmth leaves first under one heel, then under the other. Out in the court nothing hurries. Water tips over a corner; a tuft of moss inclines a little from the wall. Presently the stone by the brass comes up, and in that hair of daylight there is the pink edge, doubled over for safety, passing on its way between the cold paving and the tree.
