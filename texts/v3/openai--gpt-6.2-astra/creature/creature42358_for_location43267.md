There is a beast on the railway which it is easy to mistake for the shadow of a hand. It is about the right size, with a heel to it and four blunt ends spread out upon the felt. Ordinarily all four ends point towards the coming train. As the shadow of the load goes over them they lift, one after another, showing a lining of close cream-coloured hairs. By the time the wagon itself comes by there is nothing on the felt but a place brushed the wrong way.

Look under the last petal of the wagon and there it is, riding with the hairs pressed flat and its heel streaming out behind. The heel proves to be a considerable part of the animal. At rest it is tucked up in pleats. On a fast stretch of runner it lets out enough of itself to trail like a ribbon several yards behind the load. There are small weights at intervals along the ribbon, of a dull amber substance, rather handsome when the train goes round a bend and they swing out over the moss. In old animals the weights have worn quite clear on the side next the ground. A magnified blade of moss goes through each like a green flame.

They have been called parcel hands, though they neither handle the parcels nor trouble much about their destination. A train may take on a dozen at an empty siding and set them all down in the middle of a field. What interests them is the bit of a journey which the land has had, as it were, to make up. In a short furrow, with two busy stations and the proper day's work between them, a hand stays pleated. On the stretch beside the fallen chimney one sometimes sees a ribbon so long that the forepart of the beast has gone into the late afternoon while the last amber weight is still swinging in the light of noon.

Their food is easier to see than their mouths. After a wagon has passed there are short lengths of black nap standing up along the runner, which settle again if nothing touches them. A parcel hand going over one gives a little hitch behind; the nap comes off and goes travelling up the ribbon, from weight to weight, till it disappears under the cream hairs. The runner is glossy where it has been cropped. On an abandoned line the nap grows long and lies in whorls. There are lengths of it in the eastern furrows that retain the whorl made by the last wheel, though wheels have not been used on any line within reach of the daylight.

A hand off the track is quite a different-looking creature. It walks on two ends and carries the other two before it, feeling the air at different heights. It is then no bigger than a young pear and has something of a pear's uneven way of standing on a flat surface. The least draught tips it forward. One may spend a quarter of an hour watching one get over the sill of a door. At the top it pauses and rocks a little, plenty of room on either side of it, the whole depth of the lit galleries beyond. This is where it likes to sing. The sound is very thin. A shut door in its travelling frame will answer it better than an open one; occasionally a bale of new doors goes the length of the railway giving out one sweet, rather battered note.

The young are born at the inspection wells. For some days beforehand a hand will haunt a well rim, going round and round its satin edge with the ribbon hanging down inside. The first sign of the litter is a row of little smoky bars in the clear sides of the weights. They are not inside the amber. They are some miles below, on the pale underside of the other field, and can be seen without the amber if one knows precisely where to look. Down there a second hand is going round a second rim. When the trains pass and the interval closes, the two ribbons lie together for about the time it takes to draw thread through cloth.

Afterwards both animals go away rather lightly, without their weights. What remains on the rim might be taken for a bracelet broken in getting it off: a dozen amber beads lying loose in a curve, each with a dark flaw down the middle. Towards evening the flaws open. A minute parcel hand walks out of each on two uncertain ends, dragging the empty bead. For a while the sound about the well is like the rolling of a few seeds in a paper packet. Then they get their beads over the lip, half into one country and half into the other, and it is quiet again. A hand keeps that first bead all its life. Whatever other weights it grows, the last one on the ribbon is hollow and makes a little knock the rest do not make.

There are not many wells still in use, and at some of them the trains no longer quite coincide. The two fields come close enough for the ribbons to stir in one another's draught, no closer. Old hands frequent these places. They bring no young away, but they go round the rim just the same, and sing at the doors on the way home. One near the foot of the fallen chimney has made the journey so often that the felt has taken its impression: two little alternating hollows all along the bank, out of scale with the notches in the couplings and going in quite another direction.

In the last of the light a brown moth sometimes rides in the hollow bead of this animal. Nothing shuts on it there. It can get out whenever it pleases. As the hand goes down the long side of the chimney the bead bumps gently behind it, amber, amber, then for one turn a most unlikely pink. From the top of the bank one can see the little flash go all the sixty yards, while down beside the rail the afternoon is still nowhere near over and the moth has settled for a good long sleep.
