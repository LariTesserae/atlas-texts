The first fish to use the gentle side are using it already. They are soles no longer than a man's thumb, mottled pink and white like the broken shells. They lie just under the crest. A grain dislodged by the wash goes skipping over one of them and leaves a short track, at the end of which the fish, without getting up, gives a little shiver and covers itself again. There are several hundred along the top yard of the bank. A diver coming up the bank sees none. Going down, with the light behind him, he sees the shadows cast by their eyes.

They came here as things thin enough to look through, with an eye on either side and a manner of swimming that would have taken them straight across the dam. The change came over them in the water. For a few days they swam tilted, like chips of wood sinking edgewise; then they wanted a bottom. Those that came down on the smooth grade were visible a long way off. Here the pieces of shell are of just the right size to admit a little fish and close over it. Even a shrimp, walking across the bank on the tips of its legs, puts a foot on a sole occasionally and gets the surprise of its life.

Soles were recorded in the first filling. There is a pressed specimen in the office at the northern works, in a drawer otherwise given over to obsolete washers. It has darkened to the colour of the paper on which it is mounted. On the paper someone wrote *west sump, alive*, and the level of the water on that date, which was below the floor on which the drawer now stands. No one thought to put down where the fish had most likely come from. This was quite well known at the time.

The big soles live farther down, where the grit grades into powder. They feed there on little red worms. It used to be possible to tell the age of a bank by the size of the largest sole brought up from it, but the banks have begun to join at their ends and a fish may now be older than the ground it is lying on. The old ones have a thick white blind side, soft as the palm of a hand. A boatman lifting one from a net takes hold of it by the narrow part before the tail. For all its width the fish can bend nearly double, and there is something startling about a face going round the edge of a body to look at the hand holding it.

There is a woman on the late shift who knows these fish well. Her business is with the water samples at the post feet. Every fortnight she takes a launch out with a case of numbered bottles and a weighted brass messenger which runs down a cord to close a bottle at depth. She can do the whole operation in the dark. In fact she generally does, for the lamp brings small fish against the hull and after them the seal, which rubs along the boat hard enough to send the spare bottles rolling. It is an old cow seal with a nick out of one nostril. The woman has known her for eleven years without ever getting a good look at the whole seal at once.

In the post fields the seal hunts standing up. Her hind flippers hang loose below her and her nose is just clear of the water. With a very slight sculling motion she keeps one post between herself and the low sun. From the launch it appears that she has gone to sleep looking up a column of steel. What she is looking at is the underside of the bright water beyond it. A sole crossing a space of bare floor, though quite invisible against the floor, throws a little moving reflection up there. The seal sinks without a splash. Some time later a scatter of shell comes ticking down on the lid of the bottle, fifty feet below the boat.

At one particular post the woman leaves the motor off and eats her supper. There is a dry landing six rungs up the ladder and, in the angle of a conduit above it, a swallow's nest. The birds have to go to a leak in a service bank for mud. Most of what they bring back is grey, but there is a course of reddish earth halfway up the nest, put in during a repair at the leak when the nearest mud was in a flower box on the south walk. The young ones put their heads out at the sound of the bottle case being shut. Their parents settle an inch beyond their reach and pick the pale flies off the backs of their own feet before feeding them.

From this landing the woman can just make out the white door of the quarters where she lived as a child. In those days one went down two steps from that door to a path on the service bank. She remembers running down it behind her father, who had a spanner sticking out of his hip pocket and kept putting a hand back to stop it jumping free. At the foot of the path they would turn and look up at the depth figures newly painted on the post. The bottom figure was level with her face. It was a considerable event, later on, to take the launch out over that point and let a bottle go, and feel the cord still running through her fingers when it had paid out the height of her father.

By the time she comes back from the post fields the windows of the quarters are lighted. Someone has put a towel over the lamp by the white door to keep the flies away. Under the launch the bank shelves off in long reaches of pink grit. The little soles have come out now; in the last light they travel a few inches at a time, leaving a pattern like the marks a spoon makes when it is drawn lightly over flour. Here and there two tracks meet. Both fish lift, pass one above the other, and come down again. By morning there is hardly a hand's breadth of the gentle side that has not been gone over in this fashion. The first wash at the reversal smooths it all away.
