The woman at the rain house is in the habit of feeling under the door with her toes before she shuts it. The little gutter fish come up over the threshold. One or two will stay in the kitchen after the water has stopped, propped on their stiff breast fins in the wet print of a heel. She takes them out on a spoon. In water they look black; in the spoon they are almost clear, with a dark thread running the length of them and a bright grain at either end of the thread. She puts them down facing up the gutter. A fish put down the other way round will back quite a distance before it thinks to turn.

She used to do the gutters in one long walk, starting before the pavement got bright. Now she does one a day. The trouble is seldom where the water stops. It may be a quarter of a mile farther up, where a single stem has fallen across the channel and the water, keeping to the underside of the stem, has gone off to make a dark patch no bigger than a sheepskin. There will be half a dozen horses standing over the patch. They like the wet plants better than the dry ones and will leave the trough with the water still running off their chins if they see her going out with her bent piece of wire.

It is hard to get an idea of the size of the horses until one stands beside her. She can reach over a mare and scratch the hollow inside the far hip. Their winter coats come off in long pale tabs, damp and crimped on the inside like the wool where a bandage has just been undone. For a week or two there is horsehair on every projecting corner in the valley. Then there is none. Down in the cracks are little grey mice with ears so thin that the daylight comes through them pink. They make chambers lined entirely with the hair. When a tuft is too big to go down a crack a mouse will leave it there, go below and pull it through a few hairs at a time. The tuft gets smaller and smaller without visibly moving. This is one of the things you can see from the white chair.

There are birds here too, though a person who crosses the valley at noon might reasonably say there are none. They spend the day out of sight below the lip. Shortly before dark they come pouring up over it, little blunt-headed birds the colour of dust, each going straight to a particular scoop in the limestone. They take a dust bath lying over on one shoulder, with the upper wing held out and trembling. All along a sill the dust goes up in separate puffs. Afterwards they walk about picking up the bees’ wings. It is the joint at the root of the wing they want; they snip it off, and what the wind has carried downhill all day gets carried a little way uphill again, stuck to the short feathers under their bills.

In the rain house there is a biscuit tin full of apricot stones. The woman gathers them when she goes down to the tree for fruit. You can tell the ones the tortoise has had. A stone from an apricot eaten in the hand retains a little roughness, a few stiff gold fibres along the seam. The tortoise turns its stone about with its tongue until it is as clean as a tooth. For years the woman thought there was only the one tortoise. Then one afternoon she sat on the sill above the basin and heard the stone drop, and almost at once another, thinner knock from under the tree. There was a small tortoise there, still the colour of the dried mud on its back. It had not got hold of an apricot. It was taking up a stone the big one had finished with, raising its head as high as it could and letting the stone fall. She stayed until her shadow reached the crack the tree grew out of. In all that time the little one never tired of doing it.
