Not all the clicks after sunset are made by the seams. If there are two quite close together, and then a third, duller sound, as of a fingernail drawn across a tooth, there is likely to be an animal in the ground. It helps to be lying down. With one ear to the stone it is possible to hear the long scrape that follows, too faint to carry through the air. By then the animal has its shoulder up against the underside of a slab and is pushing, a little at a time, into the open.

It comes out backwards. What appears first is a broad ivory plate, flush at first with the surface of the stone, then tipped up sufficiently to show a fringe of apricot-colored hairs along its lower edge. The animal works the plate to either side until it has swept the loose salt away from the opening. A second plate comes into view, overlapping the first, and another. It is some time before there is anything to suggest a head. Then two slender arms reach out from under the last plate and take hold of the cleared edge, and the head comes up between them, very small for the body, with a puckered mouth and a pair of dark pits set so far back that the animal has to lift nearly half its length clear of the ground to look about it. An old one, all the way out, is about the length of a human foot. A young one can turn round on a fallen leaf.

During the day the passage in which it lives is stopped with the plate at the end of its body. Salt settling on the plate gives it the grain and color of the adjoining stone. In very old animals it even shows a little of the local fluting. This is a better protection than any the animal could contrive for itself, and it takes some care not to spoil it. When it is out walking it carries its hind end high, like someone taking the hem of a good coat across a dirty floor. The resemblance goes no further. Under the coat there are a great many short, busy feet, all pink, and along each side, at intervals, an arm long enough to reach round and attend to any particular foot that may have picked up a splinter.

Its business above ground is chiefly with the husks. All afternoon they have been coming to rest, and a good number of them, balanced in the flutes, still hold at the folded end a little of the soft stuff on which a new tree starts its life. The animal takes a husk in its foremost arms and tips it this way and that. An empty one is put down. One with something in it is passed underneath and handed back, arm to arm, until a foot can tuck it into the fringe. It goes on doing this until there is a little skirt of husks all round the hind plate. In the last of the evening light, with the stone already grey, this procession of minute pink sails is sometimes all that can be seen of it.

The outward breath combs the ground clean near a grove. Farther out, where the air slackens, there may be a hundred husks in a single hollow, but no seam underneath in which to spend the day. The best places are a mile or two beyond the last tree, where one of the yellow lines crosses a field of shallow flutes. There are often several animals living there. Each has its own opening. Below, where the softened mineral can be pressed aside with the shoulder, their passages run together into a common low room. Husks are brought down whole and wedged, folded ends inward, into the walls. The room of a large family is lined with them. By the time the young begin to walk about they need only put out their mouths on either side to feed.

Very little of the husk is eaten by the adult. It holds the folded end to its mouth for a while, working it gently in and out, and afterward there is a round translucent spot in the dry material. The young make much larger spots. They have no plates to speak of, only a series of pale creases across the back, and when they have fed well the creases disappear altogether. They sleep side by side in the lowest part of the room. If the roof is jarred they all pucker at once, and the creases come back. One sometimes sees this happen when a slab has split away in winter and left a room open to the sky. The old ones are remarkably quick at getting under the little ones in such a case. The overlapping plates go this way and that across the floor with a passenger riding in each hollow between them. In a minute there is nothing left but the lining of the room, the husks hanging loose by their tips and trembling a little in the cold.

Some of those tips are difficult to dislodge. Pressed into a patch of the right mineral, the living part of a husk will put out a root. In the dark it stays white and makes no leaves. Its growth follows the wall of the room, taking the impression of everything it touches, so that a length of root freed from the stone may show the fine parallel wrinkles of thirty or forty husks no longer there. Eventually the root crosses one of the passages to the surface. The animal coming home finds a soft white tongue laid across its threshold. It steps over it. Night after night it steps over, though it would take very little effort to bite through the thing when it is small.

The first leaves appear beside the opening. For a season or two the tree is a convenience. There is shade for the young before their plates harden, and fallen leaves to plug the narrow passages the adults cannot get into to close. Later the root thickens, and the passage has to be enlarged, usually on the side away from the tree. At last there comes a morning when the whole hind plate will no longer fit flat against the opening. A crescent of darkness shows along its edge. The family stays on for several days, sometimes longer. Then one evening the old animals come up with the little ones already settled on their backs. They go out beyond the farthest reach of the red shade and do not return.

This accounts for a peculiarity of the ground in front of an advancing grove. In among the straight ranks of seedlings that come up where the wind lays them down, there are small knots of older trees, four or five together, growing round an empty space no wider than a plate. The trees lean a little outward. Under their roots the stone is hollow. Far out ahead of them, long past the point where even the smallest leaf would be looked for, a few husks will move in the dusk against the set of the wind, disappear one after another at the same spot, and be gone by morning.
