In the bowl of the P there is usually a little grey curl. Seen through the glass it has the look of filing swarf. There is a brighter place at each end of it, as there would be where a shaving had broken off the tool. One can look at it for quite a time before one sees that the bright places move independently. They are feet. The animal is lying on its side with its head and its tail tucked in and its feet going steadily in the space between.

There are a number of these little freshwater shrimps in the box. The one in the P is easier to see than the others because it has dark water behind it. Against the lime they are very nearly the colour of the lime, and against a washer one sees a short brown stroke which is the food going through the shrimp. It takes two or three strokes moving off in different directions to give one any idea how much life there is along the bank.

The bank continues under water, though very little of it can be made out from above. A washer resting a little out of true on the one below it leaves a room with an iron ceiling and a floor of fine brown stuff. There is generally a shrimp upside down on the ceiling. It holds on with its hind feet and works over the iron with its forefeet, drawing them in very fast to its mouth. Its feelers extend well outside the room. If another shrimp comes by, there is a little business of laying the feelers back to let it pass, after which they come out again to precisely their former length. The very small shrimps can go in at the round hole in a washer and come out almost anywhere along the bank.

What feeds them is chiefly too fine to see. Now and then a brown moss leaf comes free of a cushion and lies caught by its stalk at the waterline. By the next morning the soft part has gone out of it in little bays. A shrimp feeding there has to lie nearly flat. Its back makes a dry-looking hump through the surface and the current coming along the bank lays the loose end of the leaf over it like a coat. When it lets go it goes backwards quite a distance before it gets its feet to the floor. This is about the only occasion on which one sees a whole shrimp in the clear. It lands, rights itself, and walks the few remaining steps to cover with an almost burdensome exactness, putting down one foot after another on the iron.

Under the white flake lives an animal of quite another gait. What first appears of it is a blunt grey lip at the edge of the enamel. The lip widens, acquires two points and a pair of black specks, and the rest of the little flatworm comes after it without any discernible effort of travel. It is thin enough for the grain of the floor to show through. It will spend an hour going round the flake. Where one corner of the enamel stands clear of the iron it goes underneath, and for a while there is a head at one edge and a dwindling length of tail at the other, apparently quite unconnected.

Once in a long while the pump stops. A man who has had his ear on the rim takes it off as promptly as if something had struck him. Down in the box the white reflections lose their teeth. For a moment the feet of the shrimp in the P can be seen separately, each with its fringe of pale hairs; what looked like a single flicker is a succession of small fans opening and folding, keeping fresh water moving against its gills. They go on through the silence. So does the water coming out of the moss. At the far end of the bank a shrimp reaches up from under a rim, takes hold of a thread that has been trailing past its doorway all afternoon, and begins pulling it in.
