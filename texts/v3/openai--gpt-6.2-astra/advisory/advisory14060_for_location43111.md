If you can choose your point of arrival, come in on foot from the rim in daylight. From up there you can pick out all seven houses and the works. What you can't tell from up there is whether the street you mean to take is dry. Wear shoes with a good wet grip, carry a light rain shell, and put a warm layer in your bag even if it's a fine morning. You'll want it for the walk home: the floor of the hollow gets cold before the slopes do.

At the first crossing, kneel and look along the road. Once you've seen the grain under the green, you can read it standing up. Take one of the two streets where it runs in the direction you're going. If it's rained that day, go the long way round rather than take the cross-grain street with the little blue sign. A shower can be over before you've got your coat fastened and still govern your footing hours later.

Arrange any overnight stay before you come down. Seven houses needn't mean a spare bed, and what looks from the rim like an excellent place to pitch a tent is living wood. Bring a lunch rather than count on a shop. A knot left for a seat is a good place to eat it; the wide bare bend in the road is a poor place to leave your bag, however quiet it seems. The bark cart needs the turn. Leave the loose cream-coloured curls at the roadside, too. They're tomorrow's load, not scraps.

For a visit to the works, ask someone to take you in. Don't judge whether it's running by ear: you can stand outside a yard of cork wall and miss the sound of two steel rollers turning. The blink of the high window is a better indication. An open loading door lets you see quite a lot without stepping into anybody's way. At noon, when you can suddenly hear lunch paper being torn, let the woman have her lunch.

Leave an afternoon uncommitted, particularly if there's rain in the morning. Watch where one of the little road-edge lips sends the water, then find the next bright cup downhill. There's no need to hurry after it. You can sit for a while, or, if someone invites you in, ask to see what the light does in the three-paned bay. Ask how long it took to get the window there. That's a rather different question from asking who built the house. By late afternoon the morning's rain may just be reaching the gauge behind the works. Watch from a little way back. You may see the wagtail, but don't make a missed sighting a reason to think you came on the wrong day.

If you do stay the night, go back in the morning to a strip where you saw fresh bark lifted. Pick your spot by a drain grate or a knot; the colour alone won't help you find it again. That's perhaps the best thing to prepare for here: a town in which you can see everything in a minute, and still have good reason to walk the same short street twice.
