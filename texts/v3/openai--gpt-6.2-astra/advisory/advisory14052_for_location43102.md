Arrange to arrive and return from well inland, in daylight, at a place you can identify by the shape of the gray valley shoulders. **Do not plan on recognizing the edge.** The cloud need not roll in and obscure anything; on a brilliantly clear morning it can sit exactly where the next pace of limestone ought to be. The break far to the left tells you what the shelf looks like in section. It does not tell you where it ends in front of your boots. If you have no reliable route toward the rim with a generous margin of known ground beyond it, stay in the interior. There is plenty there worth the crossing.

Allow most of a morning each way if you mean to cross the valley and come back. Bring drinking water and food; those beautiful beads on the turf are a poor prospect for filling a bottle. Wear boots with grippy soles, a brimmed hat, glare-cutting sunglasses and sun protection. Pack a warm layer. There is little shade, and warmth on the face in direct sun is no great guide to how cold a long rest on the stone will be. The glasses are for comfort, not for telling white cloud from white rock. Carry a map and a compass known to work here, or some other tested means of retracing your route. Turn back while you can still pick out the rises you passed on the way in.

Take hearing protection you can put on before the chord grows uncomfortable. With a companion, agree on a few visible signals while you can still talk: stop, come back, look here, go home. Stay in sight and wait for acknowledgment before changing direction. On this floor, someone might kneel down to listen to a joint and look up a minute later to find that the person walking ahead never heard them stop. Don't use the loudness to judge your distance from the drop; much of what you hear is coming out of the rock at your feet.

Bring close-focusing binoculars and a small hand lens. You can spend a good while in a safe inland hollow looking at a single tuft of pewter grass at eye level, or discovering how much of a supposed patch of mica is made of shells. Leave the flakes seated. What looks like no space at all underneath one is enough room for several living snails. For the swifts, watch the silver patch across the valley rather than trying to peer straight down: the moving shadows will show you the extent of a colony whose birds you cannot resolve. Accept that you may see only one runner. Hundreds of them just out of sight is not a good reason to lean over a mile of air.

If the route permits, save some time for the lee of the highest stone back. Sit there before setting out on the return, put away the binoculars and let your ears adjust. After a morning of hearing the whole valley at once, it would be worth coming all that way to hear one little animal put down its stone.
