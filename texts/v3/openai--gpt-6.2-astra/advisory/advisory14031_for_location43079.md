Bring less than you think you need. A bottle of drinking water, shoes that hold on wet tile, and clothes that can take a splash will serve you better than a traveler’s pack. There is nowhere in the court to put a pack without costing somebody a place to stand. If you have a choice of hours, go in the morning, before the bakery wall has had all day to heat the room.

Come round by the stairs. The baskets on the roof ridge belong to people taking a breather; the roof isn’t a terrace. At the turn under the eaves, stop long enough to see the pigeon in the jar handle, then look down into the court to see whether someone is coming up. From the landing you can also learn the route across the benches. Watch where a washer puts her feet. The worn saddle at the end of a bench is as much a step as the old jar shoulder at the bottom of the stair. Keeping your shoes off the furniture, ordinarily a pretty good rule for a guest, would have you squeezing past somebody’s elbows while she was trying to work.

Ask whether you may come down during a lull in the beating. Once all nine paddles are going, don’t count on hearing an answer or on anyone hearing a second question. Watch faces: a lifted chin may be showing you a place to stand. Keep clear of the runnels even when they’re dry. That first tremendous slap is only a basin filling, but the water is allotted, and it belongs to the bench whose turn it is. Don’t pull the peg for a quick rinse. Drink from your bottle rather than a basin, and go back up for air if the heat begins to tell on you.

The best things to watch here require very little moving about. From one bench end you may see the toad back into cover at the sound of water and, a moment later, discover why that is quite a good place for a toad to have chosen. Let someone else shift the hanging linen; wait and see what happens to the light when they do. If you’re lucky enough to be there late, listen through the first apparent silence after the paddles stop. You can hear a single hem dripping then, in the very room where you couldn’t hear a person speak.

Don’t mistake the child on the low ledge for someone who needs an occupation. He has one. When the blue cloth comes down, the useful thing a stranger can do is leave him a clear stretch of floor. It takes two people moving toward each other at just the right pace to fold a length of clean cloth in a place this crowded, and he knows his end of it.
