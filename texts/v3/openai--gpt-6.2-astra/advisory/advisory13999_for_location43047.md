**Arrange to arrive in daylight, before the lower plain has warmed, with someone who knows the crown.** The dangerous thing about the height is how seldom you feel it. You can spend the morning watching your boots crush leaves and then, in one more step, have nothing between your eye and a bird flying over a river far below. Ask your guide for a place to see the plain from well back of the broken rim. Stop walking before you look. Give yourself a minute to understand what you are seeing.

The best thing to settle while you can still talk is where you will spend the noisy part of the day. Pick a particular stone shelter, make sure there is room, and mark the route back on an offline map or a sketch. A green bed forks and rejoins another; an “orchard” is not much of a landmark if half a mile of it is the same tree. Nor is a shelter near just because you can see its roof. Agree on hand signs for *stop*, *come back*, and *I need help*, and on a meeting place if you lose sight of one another. By noon a shout may not reach from one side of a clay pan to the other.

Bring the following even for a day visit:

- **Well-fitted earplugs and over-ear defenders.** Have them on before the low note in the rock becomes a tearing sound in the air. You may still feel the bass through your heels. Don't take the plugs out at a hole to find out which note belongs to it.
- **Grippy closed shoes, close-fitting eye protection, and sun cover that fastens.** There is very little shade between the beds. Pink grit that was blowing away from your face a moment ago can come straight back at it. Stow scarves, maps, and other loose things in zipped pockets.
- **Enough drinking water to get back, with a reserve.** The lushest green on the crown does not mean there is a stream there. Pack food you know you can eat rather than counting on the little yellow gourds.

Walk on sound, bare rock where you can see your footing, leaving the fringes of moss and white roots alone. Give vents room on *all* sides. There isn't a reliably safe downwind side, and the cup-sized mouth is not necessarily the mild one. If ferns begin to lie flat around your resting place, get up and move onto clear ground rather than waiting to see which way they lean next. Never put your face over an opening. For lunch, a warm slab beside a quiet stretch of green will do nicely; you needn't spread a mat.

Treat the bark fringes and lidded pans as someone's water supply. A pan takes a day to fill, and its owner may still be walking toward it when you arrive. Ask before drawing any water; if invited, use your own clean vessel, treat the water for drinking, and leave the lid and fringe as you found them. Ask, too, before occupying a shelter. It may be the destination another party agreed on that morning when everyone could still hear.

**If you stay for the birds, arrange your bed or your return route before dusk.** Watch from inland, away from the vents. Silence comes in patches: hearing a thread tap a lid beside you does not mean the next mile is quiet, or even that your own patch is done blowing. Keep the hearing protection handy. But spare some attention for that first little tapping sound. After a day in which a person at arm's length could not make you hear a word, it may be as remarkable as the white bird going fifty feet straight up.
