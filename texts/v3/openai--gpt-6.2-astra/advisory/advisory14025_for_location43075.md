Arrange to arrive in morning light, on an inland whaleback where stone shows through. From there, choose a short first walk with somewhere dry to sit at the far end and ample time to get back. The height will be easiest to forget after an hour of level walking. Give the rim a wide berth even where the footing is excellent. A boot-width gap in the sedge is all the warning you may get, and the sound of water going over it is very small.

Pack lightly. Take boots with good wet grip, a walking stick with a broad foot, a waterproof sitting pad, a dry layer in a sealed bag, and food you can eat without a fire. Carry drinking water and a means of purifying more; a pool that was sweet on somebody's last visit may have a different source on yours. Tell someone where and when you mean to return. If you rely on an instrument to find your way back, test it in this world while you can still see your point of arrival.

For walking, favour exposed stone and the dry paths between the enclosures. The paths have the considerable merit of having been walked yesterday. They don't necessarily go where you want to go. Nor does a shallow hoofprint promise that the surface will carry a human heel: look at the spread of the toes that made it. Use the stick to feel for a yielding place, not to punch holes looking for the bottom. If the mat begins to sink as you put weight on it, ease back the way you came. There may be no conveniently shallow bottom. Leave a pond a generous margin, especially when the leaves turn silver and it becomes difficult to say exactly where the bank was.

Before the vapour closes in, sketch the way back. Note turns in the path, a stone outcrop, the shape of an arch; don't make a runnel your principal landmark. Following water downhill is worse than useless advice here: it may take you sideways, disappear under your feet, or conduct you very efficiently to a waterfall. Once the distance vanishes, stop somewhere you've already established is sound. If you hear grazing in the dimness, let the animals pass without going closer for a look. In particular, leave the little ones an uninterrupted line of prints to follow.

Treat a ring of living arches as a house whose door happens to be open. Look in before entering. Don't count on the shelf for your bed or hang a pack from it; there may be a rail sitting quietly at elbow height. If you need shelter, choose an unoccupied dry floor and leave it as you found it. There's no call to cut a peg, dig a drain, or improve the approach. What looks like a loose length of rotten wood may still be doing useful work several miles away.

Above all, **plan to be at your evening viewpoint before there is a view**. Pick a safe inland rise in the morning and take enough food and patience to spend the late afternoon there. For a time the whole excursion may seem to have bought you the sight of one orange seed and the sound of a drop hitting the same leaf twice. Then, while the sky above you is still quite grey, you may be able to see across a day's walk of country without getting up from your pad. There is no better reason to come, and no need at all to go looking over the edge.
