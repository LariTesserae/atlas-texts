I would arrange a visit around one known survey house, with the crossing point a short walk from its door. The house is worth staying up for. It is not worth staking your life on finding it by the last light. A roof can shine from a long way off in air that clear, and the peach color tells you the angle of the slab, not the distance you still have to walk.

**Dress for near-vacuum, not for mountaineering.** Unless somebody has actually measured a safe ambient pressure, cross in a tested full-pressure suit with its own oxygen, carbon-dioxide removal, and temperature control. An oxygen mask and a warm coat are not substitutes. Try the suit at home long enough to learn what a failing fan sounds like and whether you can get up from the floor without help. Take a companion, suit radios, and independent emergency air. The two of you can be within arm’s reach on that plain and still have no way to call to each other through the air. Use a UV-protective visor. You can enjoy the warmth of the sun without trying it on a bare hand.

Have the house and the return point checked shortly before the trip. An old map with a house marked on it may be showing you precisely the place you ought not to go: where a house is gone, the ground around its former site is unusually restless. Carry a local route map and a return beacon that has been tested on *that* side of the crossing. Budget air, power, and drinking water for waiting as well as walking. For an overnight stay I would want either a tested pressurized sleeping enclosure inside the house or a suit designed for sleep, with alarms that will wake its wearer. Two doors and a roof do not make an atmosphere.

The thing to rehearse together is what to do at the dry tick. **Stop rather than run.** By the time the grit has fallen silver-side up, the front is already past you; the next one need not come from the same direction. Keep your knees loose and your hands free, and allow space between you so that one stumble does not take both travelers down. Choose your route in advance to avoid slab margins, loose overhangs, and the western lip. A whole town’s worth of stone can move less than the width of your boot sole; the dangerous place to have that boot is in the seam. At the lip, take a glass and look from well back. There is no need to stand on the last yard of a continent to see the shadows of clouds below.

Take photographs rather than specimens. If you find a fan under an already raised edge, use a little side light and look through the gap. Don’t turn its roof over to get a better view. The way that particular stone rocks is the fan’s livelihood, and there may not be another occupied stone on your whole route. The same restraint applies at the house. Don’t drive an anchor into the floor or chip off a keepsake. Its continuity with the shelf is about the only thing anyone knows for certain matters there.

Inside at night, secure the loose kit, leave both doorways clear, and put a thin insulating pad in the sleeping hollow. The floor is cold even when the day has been bright. Keep your helmet on unless you are sealed into your own habitat. Then, if both your gauges and your companion are content, spend a little while quite still. A survey house does not give you a night without weather. It gives you somewhere to lie safely enough to notice the difference between a quick lift beneath your shoulders and a shiver that has traveled all night to reach them.
