Arrange to arrive aboard a vessel, not on the bank itself. At the lowest tide the crest is still about eleven metres underwater. There is no summit to stand on and, from a small boat, there might be nothing to mark the place except a change in the set of the ice. I would go in the open-water season with a crew who know the inlet, and see it first through the camera of a tethered observation vehicle. That way you can stay warm and watch a whole change of tide, which is much more worth doing here than covering a great deal of ground.

Ask the crew about the currents rather than just asking when low water is. A tide table can tell you the height of water over the stones; it cannot tell you when the little red weeds on a particular ledge will stand upright. The old river groove may still be draining seaward after the flow over the crest has turned inland. Have the crew choose the launch and recovery window from local observations, with room to call it off if the ice moves in. Even aboard the boat, wear flotation and dress for cold spray and long spells of sitting still.

For the camera, take lights set well out to the sides. A lamp looking straight along the lens will give you a brilliant picture of the nearest suspended grit and very little beyond it. Ask the operator to hold off the bottom and look *across* it, preferably with a boulder in the frame. That will give your eye a fixed measure: you may see a shrimp quite at ease behind the stone while shell fragments streak past just over its head. When the grey water comes down, resist the urge to chase the last clear patch. Part of the pleasure is seeing what is there when the same ledge comes back into view. Take a hydrophone if one is available, and leave some quiet in the recording for the occasional knock of stone.

I would not make this an ordinary scuba outing. The modest depth is misleading: bitter water, near-zero visibility and a current that changes over the height of a human body are serious hazards even without an ice ceiling. A streak of daylight through a lead is not an exit you can count on. If being there in person matters, book passage in a crewed cold-water observation submersible with a proper surface-support and recovery crew; otherwise the camera is the better visit.

Before you leave, try taking two pictures of the same patch of floor a tide apart. Use one of the pale mineral seams in the valley wall to find it again, not the edge of a sand patch. A ribbon of pink stones may have appeared in the interval, startlingly clean, as though someone had just laid it down. The seam will be exactly where you left it. Those two pictures together will tell you more about the bank than a stone brought home in your pocket.
