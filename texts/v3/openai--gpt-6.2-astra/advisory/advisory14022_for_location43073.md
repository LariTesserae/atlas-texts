I’d arrive in daylight and take a room upstairs in the bathhouse quarter. Before unpacking, I’d ask the host to show me the way back with the street under water. “Which stair do you use when the springs are up?” will get you a better answer than “Does it flood here?” Flooding might mean trouble to you; to your host it might mean the milk crocks need moving.

Learn the two arrivals of water. Brown water carrying grit means rain has run off the upper plain, quite possibly without a drop falling on you. The clear rise can come days later, when you’ve had several good walks and think you know which ways stay dry. In a bathhouse, look for sand stirring on the pool floor. Ask someone who uses that pool what the stirring means for the lower streets. They may have hours yet; the important thing for a stranger is not to spend those hours walking farther down a route that won’t bring them back. Don’t wait for a roar. There’s scarcely a sound even where two channels meet.

Pack for a long walk in bright sun with frequent passages into cold shade: a brimmed hat, dark glasses, a wool layer you can pull on without emptying your bag, and a light rain shell. Wear shoes that grip wet stone and hold your heel on a sloping stair. A dry pair of socks in a waterproof bag will earn its space. I wouldn’t bother with tall wading boots. If you need those to get through a doorway, you probably want the landing above it. Clear water is excellent for seeing the sill and surprisingly poor evidence that there’s a floor just beneath your foot. Carry drinking water, too; find out where people fill their jugs rather than dipping yours in the nearest clear current.

Keep your luggage small enough to leave a hand free. A stair that serves five kitchens is likely to be both a thoroughfare and somebody’s doorstep. Ask which passages visitors may use, give way to people with full baskets, and resist the temptation to peer through openings in the paving. If you can hear a bowl being rinsed, whoever is rinsing it can probably hear your remarks. A sketch of the quarter is worth making, but draw it in levels: *up the inside stair, across the landing, out beside the parapet*. Two places that touch on a street plan can be a considerable walk apart.

Save the lower embankments for a day of their own. From a roof, that bright seam looks like a destination you could reach before lunch; you can spend the morning just getting clear of the bathhouses. Ask about conditions before setting out, take food, and settle on a turn-back time that leaves daylight to spare. From down there, look back at the strips of houses on the higher ground. Once you’ve seen the shape of the land between the old streams, the upper ways home will make much more sense.

Leave some time with nowhere to get to. Sit by a bathhouse pool on a fine day and watch the same patch of sand for a while. Later, on your way upstairs, notice which step is wet now that was dry when you came in. That small acquaintance with the place is worth acquiring, quite apart from keeping your feet dry.
