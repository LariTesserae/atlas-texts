Arrange to arrive at a staffed landing in daylight. If your crossing requires coordinates, have someone at the landing confirm the **elevation** as well as the position. A point on the right chart page might be on a gantry, in open water, or on a concrete floor deep enough that the framework of the roof is invisible. Look back at the landing before you leave it. It is worth knowing what your way home looks like from the water.

For a first visit, take passage on a local boat. Bring the sounding-chart page for the part of the sea you mean to visit and learn how the skipper identifies a bay. Ask for the time of the northern works’ reversal and, separately, when its effects are due where you are going. Those are two different appointments. A change you hear in the morning may be the reason the boat stands clear of a footing that afternoon. Don’t navigate by the post shadows; a shower you never feel can take the edge off every one of them.

Dress for spray more than for rain: a windproof shell, warm layers, shoes that grip wet grating, and a dry change in a sealed bag. Wear a flotation vest on the boat. Bring drinking water and lunch even for a short outing; the next column is not necessarily a place to go ashore. Check the return forecast with the crew. A roof keeps rain off a boat, but it does not shorten the stretch of water over which the evening wind can blow. If you take a service walk back, use the handrail and leave room for people going on shift. A wave can wet you from the soles of your feet up.

If what you want is to see the broom marks, ask about a viewing boat or a guided dive at a surveyed shallow site. Don’t try to reach a mark on the floor simply because you can see it clearly. There is little in that pale interval to tell a human eye how much water it is looking through. Leave the instrument cables alone, however tempting a handhold they seem. The shells you hear rattling may sound close; so may a pin three bays away.

I’d spend the morning at the old dividing dam and the afternoon on a permitted walk overlooking the post fields. Take binoculars for the second stop. You’ll know which rail to watch by the gulls already sitting on it. For the dam, the useful preparation is to find out which way the water has been running. Then you can look at the clean face and the bank of shell grit on the other and see thirty years in the difference. If you keep a travel notebook, sketch how far the grit still lies below the crest. Of all the things in those nine volumes, that is the one I’d want a record of when I came back.
