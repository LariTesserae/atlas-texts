Go by daylight and plan a short visit, with enough in reserve to come out slowly. Arrange for someone to wait in the meadow. Have them walk the approach with you, then settle well back from the hollow rather than pace up and down over your head. The willow can deliver an afternoon years after the fact; neither the colour of its forks nor the behaviour of the mites is a good reason to postpone going home.

Dress before you enter. You want a warm layer that still insulates when damp, a windproof outer layer that won’t snag, dry mittens in a closed pocket, and soft, broad-soled boots. Leave the ice cleats behind. Take a hood you can hear through and a small lamp you can turn very low. Pack a warm drink in a flask you can open with mittens on. There’s no need to learn by experiment where, in the width of a vole’s footprint, pear water gives way to ice.

At the entrance, look back. Learn what the opening looks like from knee height; that is probably how you will see it on the way out. Then put the lamp away for a minute and let your eyes come round to the roots. Take the route with clearance for your shoulders **and your pack**. A fold that looks soft enough to push aside may have a hundred moths pressed flat to it. If they all let go at once, stand still and give them their yard of air. It will feel like something has come down. Listen for a moment and you may still hear the deep note that sent them off.

The difficult thing about walking here is that a careful person’s usual tests are quite destructive. Don’t stamp to find firm ground, drive a stick through the moss, or haul yourself upright by a hollow root. What looks like a little bar of gravel may be tomorrow’s leaves. Move slowly enough to ease your weight back if the surface begins to tip. Stay in the roomy part rather than crawling out to see how far the edges go. In particular, **watch the deep moss from alongside it**. The beetle with its hind feet showing is not stuck, and there is no reason to help it through. Nor is there any good way to judge, from those hind feet, the journey your hand would take if you reached in after it.

Give yourself a few minutes with no task at all. From one place you might hear a vole finish a mouthful, see the green of the ceiling after the moths have moved, and make out the red legs of one mite holding itself just inside that old patch of summer. That is a full visit. Don’t promise yourself the sound of the next drop. If there is still a bright collar on the stem, you have arrived in time to see the last one land.
