Make your arrival an out-and-back walk on the ocher gravel, with the way home tested before you go out of sight of it. The traveler who sat by the egg got a numb hip. That is a useful piece of information: whatever arrangement the plateau makes for its inhabitants, you can still sit too long on a hard stone. Plan for a human day's thirst, hunger, and fatigue. Take water for the return as well as the outward walk; the river channels are excellent records of water and no use at all for filling a bottle. Wear a coat, sun protection, and boots with good grip. Check that the air is breathable at the arrival point. The height is plain enough, but there is no telling the pressure from the view.

Take a watch that travels with you, and set a turnaround time on it. Don't use the moon for a clock. Don't use it as a destination, either. That blue notch has nearly every quality one looks for in a pass: a gentle approach, firm footing, a path over the saddle, and something beautiful to see on the other side. What it lacks is the other side. Choose a stopping place well back, while you are still looking at the ground. The child's heel marks are not a safe-distance guide.

I'd go with one companion and agree on hand signals before arrival. Keep each other in sight, especially when one of you stops to listen to something. A call for help may be perfectly audible in a thimbleful of air a mile behind the person you want to reach. For navigation, keep a sketch of the gravel junctions and black beds, and look back now and then to learn the return view. “Turn at the bird with the nick in its bill” is an unusually poor direction here.

Follow the winding paths, even where you can see the next bend just a few yards across the white. Give yourself a little while to learn to see what the path makers saw. At first there is a tempting piece of bare river floor; then one black eye catches the light and suddenly there isn't room to put a boot down. Nor is an apparently old event necessarily finished with its ground. The last few feet of a grazer lying down deserve as much space as its muzzle. If the path goes the long way round, somebody who knew how to walk here thought those feet worth going round for.

Bring binoculars and leave the collecting tins at home. You can follow the grub and the bird from the gravel. Walk a little, stop, and look; walk back if you'd like to see the chick again. There's no need to prise it out of its shell to find out whether it's alive. In particular, resist the urge to straighten, free, or rescue something on the evidence of a single posture. You don't know what moving one hooked feather would do to the bird a little farther along. The delightful privilege of visiting is that you can go back for a second look at the wet grit on a newly hatched chick. A second look is quite enough to take.

Treat the slab shelters as homes, not ruins. A swept floor and tools beside the door are better evidence of occupancy than an answer to a knock would be here. Don't take the blunt adze on the theory that the owner has two better ones. If you need to rest, use clear gravel rather than the polished sill. Listen from there if you like. There is something intimate about finding the precise place where a person sharpened a tool; it doesn't follow that you're invited indoors.

Finally, settle with your companion beforehand what you'll do if you hear a child. The natural response will be to lift your head, call out, and hurry in the direction of the running prints. Stop together instead. Laughter at the height of your hand tells you something lovely and quite exact about that patch of path. It tells you nothing about who is calling from the rim now. You can bend down, hear it, and turn back.
