I would plan to watch from the next height, if that height proves stable. From there you can see something a person standing on the common mostly misses: one rim taking the sunlight, then another half a mile away, both moved by the same heave. If you do go down onto the common, go for a short visit with a way home that does not require the ground to stay as you found it.

**Bring your own pressure, air, and cooling.** Until you have measured the atmosphere, treat the place as near vacuum. Take a pressure suit with its own breathing supply, carbon dioxide removal, active cooling, and an independent emergency reserve. A respirator is not enough. The oddity of the warmth is important: a gloved hand held an inch above a plate may tell you very little about what that plate will do to a boot in ten minutes. Use heat-insulating soles and cut-resistant outer layers over the pressure garment. Bring a thermal imager to check the surface, and monitor the temperature *inside* the boots. The fresh glass needles are reason enough not to kneel, even on a cool patch.

**Scout the visit from above.** Have a probe watch the proposed landing site through several episodes of movement. Compare images taken minutes apart, not just a single excellent image. What you want to know is which edges belong to the same plate, whether the plate is tilting, and whether you can leave from its middle. Use angled lamps or a range scanner: the sun is exquisitely good at showing you one particular edge of a flake and quite bad at showing you the drop beyond it. Measure local gravity before trusting your balance on loose fragments.

If you land, choose a clear area well inside one broad plate, away from the central step, the escarpment, blister walls, and fresh fractures. **Make it a one-plate excursion.** An avenue with matching walls is not a trail; the very accuracy of the fit tells you what can happen to someone between them. Nor is a gully a route down. There has been no running water here to make the small channels join larger ones and lead anywhere.

Keep a partner or pilot on watch with a radio and a view wider than yours. Arrange pickups by your live beacon, not “beside the low ridge”; the ridge may be new and your landing place may have moved. If your means of multiverse travel is a fixed doorway, be especially conservative: stay on its side of every seam and within immediate reach of it. If it cannot remain accessible when the plate shifts, do not use it to land here. Set a departure time with ample life-support reserve. A double knock through the ankles, an unexpected tilt, or a new line of heat is reason to end the visit early, not to run toward a better viewpoint. There need not be a knock before the next movement.

Don't use a roofless blister for shelter. It offers shade you hardly need, hides you from the lookout, and consists of the parts of a collapsed structure that haven't fallen yet. If you can see the gray patch beneath a ledge, use a long lens. Don't scrape it, breathe suit exhaust over it, or turn the stone to get it into the light. Whatever the grains are, their little succession of sulfurous breaths is the one history on this mountain you could erase with a glove.

Allow some time simply to stand still, preferably back on the observing height. There will be very little sound beyond the machinery keeping you alive. Pick two points on the skyline and look away and back. It may be easier to grasp what sort of place you have visited in that second look than in the sight of anything red or glowing: the mountain has done a little more of its making, and you did not see it move.
