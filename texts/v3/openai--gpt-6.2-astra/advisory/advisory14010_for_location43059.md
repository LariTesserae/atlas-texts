Book your first night in a seam district with a lift to the plain, and ask your host to write down the name of the threshold that serves it. The stair you can see on the skyline is a poor address. Thirty miles of former seabed lie between you and it; a load of melons may get there before you have worked out how to get your luggage down the first ledge.

Bring well-fitted earplugs and put them in *before* you go up the survey stair. They should make the market traffic easier on your ears, though they won’t stop you feeling a crossing in your cheeks. If you have company, agree on a hand signal for “wait here” and a place to meet if you get separated. Don’t count on being able to call someone back from an edge. A file of swallows disappearing into apparently solid ground is a good reason to stop where you are and look.

Travel with a small pack rather than a rolling case. Wear shoes with stout soles that leave your hands free for the stair rails. On the plain, what looks like a stretch of level walking tends to end in a sharp step down, and the light is poor for judging depth. Give the standing lime fans a wide berth; they are relics, not handholds. If you want to see a district from the opposite rim, ask for a public overlook. Discovering that you can see straight into somebody’s kitchen is not quite the same as being invited in.

For your first crossing, let a cart go ahead of you. It will give you a much better idea of where to stand and when to move than any account of what happens to the intervening stone. Have your destination written large enough to show an attendant, keep your pack close, and clear the tiles on arrival. On the other side, take a moment before setting off. Your feet will report that you have gone a few paces; the light on the walls may tell you that you are in a different seam altogether.

Carry a filled drinking bottle and find out where visitors refill it. The gardens can give a newcomer quite the wrong idea about how much water there is. That silver-grey wetness overhead in the morning is already spoken for, down to the last bead. Ask your host about washing, and if rain comes, take the lift up with everyone else rather than spending the occasion indoors trying to save your clothes. In the lower streets, secure your coins and glasses before leaning over a rail. If something lands on a leaf below, tell a gardener where you saw it. Don’t climb after it or try to hurry the little snail hunters along.

Leave one noon unbooked. Go up the survey stair a little early, let the last crossing die away, then take out your earplugs. Listen close first: the pattering at the foot of the stair is very small for the number of creatures it takes to make it. Then look for the dark tree. It is worth having seen it stand quite still for a while before you see it fly. Keep the plugs in your hand; when the child below starts practising, there isn’t much of the interval left.
