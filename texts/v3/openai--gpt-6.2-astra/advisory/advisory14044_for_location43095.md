Go early if you want to see what keeps the fig tree alive. In the first hour after the washhouse opens, you can watch a bead of water gather on the cold main and work its way down to the elbow above the basin. Later in the day you could look straight at that dusty little tree and have no idea where it gets a drink. For the best light on the gravel wall, go instead toward closing. Ask when you need to be back up the stairs; the lowering of the iron leaves is the end of the working day for the shops, but it’s the beginning of an evening at home for the people who live below.

Dress for the twentieth step, not for the street. Wear light clothes that fit close, tie your hair back, and choose closed shoes with a good grip on a damp tile. Leave the big pack and anything with a dangling strap upstairs. Bring a filled water bottle. There’s a drinking tap if you need it; the pipe above the fig and the tanks in the washhouse have other jobs. If you begin to feel light-headed, don’t push on for the sake of seeing one more shop. The walk back along the yard is short, but you’ll still have the stairs to climb. If stairs are difficult for you, ask about access before you go; don’t count on riding the freight lift.

Pause on the street to let anyone coming up clear the steps. You’ll have seen most of the yard by the time you reach the bottom, which can give you the mistaken impression that you know where everything goes. Spend a minute watching a basket make its way through. A patch of empty floor outside a shop may be exactly the space someone needs to swing a load around. Ask where you can stand to watch the work. The machinery is quiet enough to talk over in an ordinary voice, and quiet enough that you shouldn’t rely on hearing something approach. Keep clear of the belts and the openings between shops, even if all that’s coming through at the moment is a handful of mattress buttons.

At the cut wall, look for the white half-pebble at a schoolchild’s thumb height. It’s a better guide to where to look than to what’s safe to touch: the stone lower down is hot. At the fig, look *behind* the basin as well as into it. Those pale circles on the tiles are the record of somebody noticing, each time a pipe was mended, that the drip had moved. Leave the basin where it is. Take your lunch scraps back up with you; nothing with wings is going to carry them off.

You needn’t plan a long visit. There is time, while a single bale is being unloaded, to see the wool come out, hear the scissors go down, and notice that the light under the awning is moving although the awning itself is quite still. If someone brings out a second chair for you at closing, check how you’ll get back to the street, then settle in. After the heat of the descent, you’ll understand why people are willing to sit a while for a little cool air.
