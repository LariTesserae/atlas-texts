Arrange to arrive on the finished side of the summit, not in the hollow. At your usual size this is a place you can visit with one eye. That is probably the best size at which to go. If your means of travel offers to make you small enough to walk in, decline. The five flakes have been waiting for somewhere to land; there is nothing to say how long a person might have to wait.

Find out beforehand whether the air on the approach is breathable and what the wind and temperature will be. Take your own air if you cannot find out, and take your own water in any case. There is not a swallow to be had up there. Have a secure station on known, finished rock, with a way back that does not require you to reach around the lip. A small close-focusing camera on an angled arm will do more for you than a great deal of climbing skill. Practice using it in a crack in a wall at home. You want to be able to look sideways without putting either your head or the instrument through the opening. If the view from safety is only a partial one, take the partial view.

Come before the hour of direct sun and stay a little after it. Keep yourself and your equipment out of the line between the sun and the mouth. From a hundred miles off your shadow would signify nothing; here it could take the afternoon away from all four clumps. In sunlight, look for the notch in the leaf shadow. Once the lip cuts off the sun, let your eyes adjust rather than turning on a lamp. The folded leaves and the little bit of pink stone will be much easier to understand if you have seen which parts of the hollow change light together and which do not. Keep your own watch for the return journey. The pink shoulder is a poor clock.

Keep bare skin away from the leaves, however cool they look. More importantly, keep even a gloved finger out. The clear space between clumps is not spare room to set something down. A touch could wipe away the old root hairs, and the tip of a warm instrument laid across two seams could interfere with the small difference in heat on which a clump lives. Bring no specimen envelope. One flake is very little metal to take home and a considerable piece of local history to remove.

If you can hear a tick from your station, stay still. There will be an inclination to put a hand below the sound, the way one catches a screw dropped from a pair of spectacles. Let it fall its own way. You may have time to look out past the opening, see the whole blue spread of peaks, and look back to find the flake scarcely farther down. The thing to watch then is not how fast it falls but what, if anything, becomes distinct beneath it.

Before leaving, try to get a view of the far end of the green curve. The upright hairs there are easier to miss than the suspended metal. They are also the best reason to make the trip: a track generally tells a traveler that something stood on a particular piece of ground. Here you can see as fine a track as any in the world, right up to the point where that inference fails.
