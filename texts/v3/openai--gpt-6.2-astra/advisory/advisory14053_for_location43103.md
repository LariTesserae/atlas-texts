Arrive by passenger train in daylight, and ask the person directing passengers at the station to show you the public crossing. A bridge is a wonderful first place to get your bearings, but a bad place to step backward for a better view. With house-high freight wheels going by, you cannot count on hearing the train over the crabs. Stay on the pedestrian side, keep your bag close, and find somewhere you can stand still. At noon, look down from there. You may be able to count the wagons more easily by the gaps in their shadow than by watching the train itself.

Pack for wet feet rather than for rain. Take shoes with a good grip on slick stone, a second pair of socks in a dry bag, and trousers you can turn up. A small pack that leaves a hand free for the stair rail will serve you better than a wheeled case. It is worth watching someone enter a house before you go in after them: the iron bar at the door may span a hollow where generations of neighbors have scraped their boots. Clean your soles, then mind the step.

For a first night, I’d take a room on an upper street, within an easy walk of the station. Visit a public bath before deciding whether you’d like to lodge farther down. Sitting on a bath bench is a good introduction to the town’s machinery: you have time to feel the slap and catch as a wheel takes its load and to notice how soon your body accepts the steady motion afterward. Some people will sleep beautifully to it. Others will be glad to go back upstairs. Use the baths people direct you to, not an inviting patch of warm water you find for yourself. Get drinking water from your host. The mixing pools are pasture, not plumbing, and a working wheel chamber is no place to explore without a keeper.

Tell your host about any shellfish allergy *before* supper. Shrimp is a freight staple here, not just something that arrives on a plate looking like a shrimp. If you can eat the local catch, ask what comes from this particular district. You can put off trying the pressed shrimp until the sixth day of a rail journey; you may not get another chance at the eel. For much the same reason, clean and dry anything you’ve dipped in one district’s water before taking it to the next. Two stations can have identical tiles and entirely different lives under them.

Leave yourself one evening without a train to catch. Ask a wheel keeper if there’s a safe spot to see the low sun come through an outlet; if the animals are on the ledge, stay where the keeper puts you and let them sleep. Then go back up while there’s light on the steps. The cloud will look solid enough to walk home across, so use street names and bridge signs rather than striking out toward a roof you recognize. Buy something for supper and find a dry place to sit a little way off the curb. By then the crabs will be coming downhill. Much of the day you’ll have been taking care not to tread on them. It’s worth being settled somewhere, with your feet up and the last freight gone, when you can finally hear what just one sounds like.
