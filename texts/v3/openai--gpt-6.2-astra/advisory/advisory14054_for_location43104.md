The first thing to arrange is a way off the lip. Have your passage set you down near the head of a footbridge, with your pack already on your back and both hands free. In most landscapes you can take a step to one side to let something pass. Here a step to one side puts you among the animals, and getting back up means finding a handhold on a wet edge. I would rather spend my first ten minutes sitting at the end of a bridge, learning to see the snails, than spend them trying not to fall on any.

Wear close-fitting shoes with soft, grippy soles, and test your footing before you put your weight on it. Warm ceramic is a great temptation to go barefoot; save that pleasure for a broad, unoccupied patch you have looked at closely. Carry little enough that you can turn around without swinging anything out over the water. Pack drinking water, food you can eat without scattering crumbs, a light rain shell, and a way to carry all your waste home. There is plenty of food here in the sense in which there is plenty of food on the far side of an aquarium glass. Nothing suggests that the pale scale is food for *you*. Nor is a plain full of water necessarily a place where a thirsty person can get a drink.

For a first visit, choose one pan. Make a sketch of the way back, noting the bridge ends and the changes of floor pattern. From the lip, the rain looks like an excellent landmark, and it is, for direction; it is a very poor measure of how far you have come. If you want to walk to it, prepare for a journey of days, with known places to rest on the way. Don't count on finding a dry bank at evening. Don't even count on evening until you know what the light in the vaults does. A watch and a return time agreed with someone at home would be worth more than a very good sense of direction.

Bring small binoculars. Much of what is worth coming for happens at distances that are easy to see across and quite wrong to cross. In particular, don't wade out for a better look when the eyelashes part. Even if the great animal takes no interest in you, the next vacant place on the pink floor already has a claimant. Likewise, a line that will bear a thousand birds is not necessarily a path for one person. The fish between their feet are using that line too. Stand cleanly apart from it and watch a diving animal come up. After a while you may be able to tell where it will surface just by the successive little openings below it.

Take clean gear in and take it out again; don't rinse it in the next pan. Leave the ribs, fasteners, scale and spillways alone. What looks like an admirable bit of plumbing from above is the roof of somewhere else. There is no soil here into which the consequences of a small careless act can conveniently disappear.

Above all, allow some time in which you are not trying to get anywhere. From a safe seat, turn off whatever you brought that hums. Wait through the feather sound and the drop that follows it. The peculiar thing will not be how quiet it is, but how much eating, breathing and work you can see going on while you wait for the next audible event. If you want a souvenir, sketch the maker's letters from a place where you can reach them without leaning out. I think what you will remember, though, is the warmth coming through the seat of your clothes, and the moment it occurs to you that even while you have been sitting perfectly still, the pans downstairs have been receiving a little heat from you.
