Arrange for someone to meet you at the pass, well before the pass’s shadow reaches the uppermost spring. In the glare, an occupied house, a covered lane and a skin of ice held up by running water can look like one continuous white slope. Wait on known rock until your guide can point out the way down. The child on the red sled knows where the rope will take her; her tracks are no use to a person on foot.

Bring close-fitting snow goggles with UV protection, and put them on *before* you look out over the notch. Pack warm layers under a waterproof shell, boots with good wet-rock grip, and dry socks and mittens in a waterproof bag. You want a small pack that leaves both hands free and doesn’t swing wide when you turn: in some places a step to one side would put you in somebody’s house by a less usual entrance. If you have come straight up from a lowland world, allow time to acclimatize rather than counting on making good time over the pass.

Ask your host to show you one good place to stand and wait. That is more useful than memorizing a route. A stair you climbed on the way to the bath may have all seven treads afloat by the time you come back. Don’t test a clear dome with your heel or poke a hole in it to let the water out; the water has to go somewhere, and there are people downhill. On the roofs, follow the load-bearing route your host shows you, not the fox. Keep smoke holes clear, and remember that people on the other side of a translucent hide patch can see your shadow as plainly as you can see a mouse’s.

Carry some drinking water of your own, but leave room in the pack for one of the little bone spoons. Accept it if it is offered. Someone who knows which ridge they are drinking from by taste has had a lifetime to learn the finger trick; there is no embarrassment in being the person the spoon was made for. Ask which seeps are used for drinking and treat the water if you are unsure of it. At the bath, find out whose turn it is—the whole place is only a body-length long—and leave the steam hole free even if it lets in a draught. A bird may be counting on it for its next breath.

For the first evening, ask to be taken to a safe lookout *before* the shadow touches the spring. Once people start moving the fish baskets, stay put; a stranger offering to take the other handle may be standing exactly where the basket needs to go. Dress warmly enough to wait through the change. The reward is the green light up the cliff, but stay for the sound afterward, too. When the slab knocks home, somebody down in the covered lane knows they can put their bowl down again. Wait for your host to come for you. For a traveler, that knock is a lovely sound; it isn’t yet a set of directions.
