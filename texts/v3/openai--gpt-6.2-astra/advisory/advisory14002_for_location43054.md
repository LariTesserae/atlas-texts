I’d book passage on a shallow-draft boat with a high deck, a heated cabin, and a skipper who knows the crossings. I’d ask to be on deck for the morning fog and over one of the shallower passes at noon. Those are two quite different appointments with the same stretch of sea.

Arrange pickup at a harbour or aboard the boat. A coordinate on the crest is not a place to stand. Bring windproof layers, warm gloves, and a flotation vest that fits over them; keep a dry change of clothes in the cabin. Even at noon, when the animals find the stone agreeably warm, it has only gained a degree or two. On a clear morning, get up high enough to see several fog banks at once. That is how you see that each belongs to a notch. Once you’re inside one, it is ordinary fog, quite capable of hiding another boat. Let the skipper use the chart and sounder rather than asking them to steer by the scenery.

For the crossing itself, a downward-looking window would be worth paying extra for. There will be a moment when you can see individual chips of flint, then a moment when the boat’s shadow runs off the edge and disappears. If heights bother you, take a seat before the crossing. Knowing that the hull is just as well supported over three kilometres of water as over nine metres may not be much help when you actually see the bottom go away.

I wouldn’t plan to swim. The deceptive thing is not the depth but the quiet at the rail. Watch the spanner, if your skipper knows the shed where it lies: the fact that it ends each stroke where it started tells you very little about the force on it in between. A swimmer has neither its weight nor its hollow in the stone. There is also no reason to put your ears into sound known to damage hearing. If you want to hear the range, ask for a hydrophone with a level-limited playback. Start with the volume down. Try picking out the double clap while you can still hear the little noises aboard the boat.

At noon, ask the crew to train an underwater camera on a sunlit patch of limestone. The useful accessory is magnification: first look at the whole pink patch, then at one animal holding on by its six feet. For a longer survey, follow a bedding plane rather than lowering the camera into the blue. At a fault, look for where the same bed comes out lower down. Finding the crabs again there would tell you more about the shape of the mountain than an afternoon of staring over its edge. Keep the camera clear of the paste, the loose flint, and the works. Treat every shed as live machinery, not a shelter; a chimney above water is no assurance of breathable air inside.

Finally, leave some time to look without adjusting anything. Through the aligned windows of a working shed you might see an oil can sitting on a sill, quite dry, with a ray passing on its far side. I think that is the sight I’d want to bring home: not the depth reading or even the enormous noise, but a small, intelligible room in the middle of it.
