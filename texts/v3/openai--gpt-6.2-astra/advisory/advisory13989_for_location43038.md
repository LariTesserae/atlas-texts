I would arrive in daylight for the sake of the night. Arrange a return point on known firm ground at the high rim, and use the afternoon to find one safe place from which to listen. A broad back of stone might do, provided someone who knows the basin can show you a sound stretch of it, outside the reach of falling limbs, with a short, clear walk back. If you cannot establish that much, stay on the rim. The white fig will make a fine landmark from there. It is not a tree to walk toward.

Take a companion, stout boots with good tread and puncture-resistant soles, long trousers, gloves, and a headlamp apiece with a spare light. Pack drinking water for the whole visit and for a delayed return. The basin has plenty of water for its inhabitants and remarkably little for a person with a bottle. Those little brown cups overhead are nurseries; leave them full. Take a dressing for a cut, a light rain shell, and a way to summon help that you have checked works on this side of the crossing.

The difficult habit to learn here is to stop *before* the next stride. A mat of stems can look continuous with the leaf mold beside it. Rock can be the roof of a gallery, and a loose slab need not settle in quite the position in which you found it. Don't use your weight to find out what is underneath. Turn back rather than cross a doubtful patch, and don't go down a crack to look for shrimp. On the way out in daylight, look back often enough to recognize the return journey. Once in place for the night, turn your lamps off to listen, and turn them on **before** either of you moves. The stars may show you the whole opposite rim without showing you the edge beside your boot.

Choose a place with some distance from the feeding pigs. The hollow *tok* is worth hearing from where you sit; there is no need to follow it to its source, especially with young ones under the lifted stones. A little patient watching will show you more than turning things over would. Look for the clean track on a trunk and follow it upward with your eyes until you find the snail at its head. Bring clean boots and gear in, and clean them again before visiting another forest. A frog that lives its whole life in a cane stump has very little world to spare.

Plan for the wind while it is still quiet. Don't settle beside a wall of cane, beneath leaning trunks, or anywhere near the dead fig and its tangle of creepers. By the time you hear the frogs falling silent in succession, the gust is nearly on you; that is no time to go blundering into the trees for shelter. And when the stars come out again, remember the limb. The wind can finish its passage across the basin in minutes and leave something enormous still on its way down. Wait for daylight if the way back is in doubt.

There is no particular prize for crossing this place. From one well-chosen spot you can hear a mile of pigs at supper, feel the interval between the frogs going quiet and the wind touching your face, and see rain falling a second time out of the forks of the trees. That would be a full visit.
