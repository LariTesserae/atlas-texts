Arrange to arrive on firm high ground, and make that your way home. The place I would aim for is the broad crown of a gray hump, well back from its undercut seaward lip. From there you can hear the young hunters whistle. Down on the shingle the adults may be loud enough that you never suspect the whistles are there.

Before going, check that the air is breathable, find out when the next high tide is due, and set the return by a watch rather than by daylight. If there is no reliable tide record, plan to stay up on the hump. The danger isn’t a wave you can see coming from the gulf. Water can come up through the stones *behind* you while the stretch in front of you still looks dry. Nor is a salt crust proof of a sound place to stand. Don’t count on being able to run across loose, wet pebbles to beat the water back.

Dress for a cool wind that keeps one side of you wet for hours: a warm layer, a windproof hood, and boots with good wet-rock grip and ankle support. Bring drinking water; leave even the apparently sweet pools to the things that live in them. Take a headlamp with a low setting, a spare light, a tested means of finding the return point in poor visibility, and ear protection you can put on *before* descending into a run. If you go with someone, agree on a few hand signals. Discovering that you can’t make yourself heard is a bad way to start a discussion about turning back.

For a first visit, I’d take close-focusing binoculars and do very little walking. What looks like a bare patch to land a boot on may be hundreds of grazers packed with their feet turned inward. Wait for the wind to wet a stone and watch the patch come apart of its own accord. Don’t turn the stone over for a better look. Give the hanging fliers room on the lee side, too. I wouldn’t pitch a tent there: a shoulder that is bare rock when you unroll your bedding may be needed by a whole fleece of animals if the wind drops overnight.

If you can choose the hour, be in position on high ground before a molting tide, with no need to cross a run on the way out. A recorder with a good windscreen would be worth more than a bag of souvenirs. Leave the translucent plates overlapping where they lie; you needn’t go down to the basin to witness what happens there. Listen instead for the stretches of valley going quiet one after another. The best moment to stay quite still will be the one when it seems there’s nothing left to hear.
