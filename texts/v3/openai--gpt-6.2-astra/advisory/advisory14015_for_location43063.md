Ask at the surface station whether you can go down with a fitter who is already making a round. Pick a quiet shift when the basin is not expected to take water, and let the fitter choose the route. Before you start, have her show you the signal to come back and the way out if you lose sight of her. The clocks flashing blue mean a change of shift; find out what a flood warning looks like. It is worth settling these things upstairs, where you can hear an answer.

Dress for standing still on a wet pavement before breakfast in winter. Wear a warm layer under a hooded waterproof, grippy waterproof boots that fit well, and gloves you can keep on while taking a photograph. Leave a dry pair of socks at the station. The rain itself needn't spoil your visit. From a safe place beside the tramway you can watch the roof let go a boat's worth of drops, wait through the dry length of the towline, and see whether anything is coming behind. Keep your hood down for the first one if you like; the sound is part of it.

Don't walk the flats just because you can read the numbers through the water. A white patch can be a skin of ice with the water already gone from underneath it. The porcelain shingle isn't much better footing. Stay on the staff route, clear of the rails, and speak to your companion at her shoulder rather than trying to call her back. Six miles of indoor space is hard to judge. A clock face you can see quite distinctly may belong to a porch too far away to be of any immediate use.

If there is time for a stop, ask for the eastern pump house after the pump has been running an hour. You'll have a better chance of seeing the pale flies get up off the grass. Look from the side; one boot laid crosswise would cover the whole width of the green. On the porch, knock the ice off before going through the second door. After a while looking out across the basin you will understand why somebody goes to the trouble of keeping geraniums there.

And if someone on break offers to show you the ravine, go with them. It helps to have seen a little of the place first: the numbered panels, the hollows cast to order, the water changing direction because somebody under a distant clock has lifted a sluice. Otherwise you might walk quite a way to see a trickle fork around a bolt and wonder why everyone is so pleased.
