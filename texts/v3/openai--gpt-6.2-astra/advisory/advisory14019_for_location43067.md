Plan on spending a couple of hours at the two steps. From there you can see the whole shelf, and if you stay for a sounding you can see something a person who walked straight out to the white instrument might miss: the pool birds taking up their positions before there is any visible reason to prefer one bank to the other. Bring a warm layer, a rain shell, your own drinking water, and something dry to sit on. Lend the place your time rather than your weight.

Clean the mud out of your soles before you climb. A seed in a boot tread and the teaspoon of earth it comes in would be quite a delivery here. Wear flat shoes with a good grip, fasten your pockets, and put a strap on anything you mean to look through. You won't need much magnification. What you may need, oddly enough, is a little light directed down at your feet. Being able to count the feathers on a distant wing can give you a wholly undeserved confidence in your sight.

From the mountain end, look for the places where the pale collars have joined into a continuous pavement. If you can't trace such a route both to the shelter and back, stay on the steps. Don't test a doubtful patch with a pole: the point might go through without telling you much about what the ground beside it will bear. In wind, watch where the water spills, and give all the ground that moves with it a wide margin. The three feet of the instrument have a means of support unavailable to yours.

If you do reach the shelter, take a moment before sitting to see where the roof is dripping *today*. Leave the mended mug on its nail. The bench is a good place to put down your pack, shut your eyes for a few seconds, then look at the nearest pool again. It is surprisingly difficult to let a pool be full of animals you cannot see. Resist the temptation to make one cough. Sooner or later one probably will, and when the silver flies lift, keep still; the birds will be using the air about your knees. Don't back into the rushes to give them room.

You needn't brace yourself against the instrument's note on the bench. Expect to feel it in your jaw, which is less alarming if you know it is coming. Watch one bead of water: for a couple of breaths you can see which end of it *was* the front. If you're standing when the note comes, stop rather than trying to correct that little shift onto your heels with a quick backward step. Wait for the water to finish running back before choosing your footing again. The newly set stretch is best seen from a distance. There is no need to knock on it to verify the sound; by then there is tender ground beyond it, and the distinction between the two is precisely the sort of thing this light conceals.

For an evening visit, leave yourself enough light to descend the mountain. Spend the last few minutes behind the steps, just outside the hollow. Don't part the roots for a better look at the amber eggs. If all you can see are the empty white cups, you've seen something worth the climb: almost everything else on the shelf shows you what happened to water, and these show you where an animal once chose to be.
