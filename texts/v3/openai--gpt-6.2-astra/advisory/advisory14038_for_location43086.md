Book a room from which you can get home on foot. Ask the landlady a specific question: “If the gates close while I’m at the fish market, can I walk back here without going down any steps?” A place advertised as two stops from the market may be an excellent place to stay, but the answer to that question may still be no.

For a first day, take very little: shoes with a good wet grip, a light rain cape that leaves both hands free, a dry shirt, and a waterproof pouch for your papers, money, and anything you need to get back to your own world. Carry any medicine you’ll need through the night. Take a card with your lodging’s district and door number written on it. You may be quite close to somebody who can help you and still be unable to make yourself heard. If the sound worries you, bring earplugs for sleeping. Keep your ears free on the quays; a horn you can feel in the parapet may be meant for people on the other side of it.

Go down the nine steps at low water, with someone who knows the street. It is worth feeling the warmth of the old road through the soles of your shoes. Look at the wheel tracks and the numbered doors. Then, when the joints between the stones begin to shine, go back up. Don’t wait for the woman to move her stool. She knows how long it takes her to finish a float, and she lives in the third doorway. You have eight or nine wet steps to climb and will probably stop halfway because you have never seen water come *out* of a road before. Come back by boat later, with a local at the oar. Those white floats are for the boat to steer by, not for a swimmer to hold on to.

At a gate station, find out where the gate opens **now**, and ask again on the return trip. Let the freight through and follow the passengers when they move. Don’t arrange to meet someone “on the other side” without naming the district, or promise to be back for supper on the strength of the outward timetable. When the afternoon rain starts and the names change on the board, stay put. This is what the spare shirt and the money are for. Buy something hot. You can learn a surprising amount of geography by standing beside a regular passenger and asking which of the places that have just vanished they would have liked to go to.

You needn’t learn a whole language of fingertip pressures before you arrive. Learn how to offer your wrist, how to decline the touch politely, and how to ask someone to repeat themselves somewhere quieter. In a crowded square, don’t assume people have stopped speaking to attend to you just because their mouths are closed. And don’t go looking for quiet by following a pipe downstairs. The story about the distant bath is a marvel of plumbing, not a set of directions to the baths.

Leave the walls alone, especially the old ones. The cream skin is food, the green is scales, and what looks like a handy projecting stone may be much less firmly attached than a brick back home. For a good look, take the last ferry past a protected wall. From a little way off, the light will reach into the cavities without your having to put a hand or a lamp inside. Wait for the angle to change and a whole course of what you took for dark masonry will look back at you.

Finally, allow yourself one day on which you needn’t get anywhere. Spend low water under the market and high water up in the gallery, and watch the dishes come in. It takes most of a day to see what the woman is making; if you leave after an hour, all you’ve seen is a string.
