Ask your friend at the kitchen door which way to come. From Bell Street, the eighteen iron steps look like the obvious shortcut. If you use a cane, have a pushchair, or are carrying something that needs both hands, the walk round the two streets may be the easier visit. Check that you’re welcome to use the yard, too. What looks like a little public square from above is also the space just outside somebody’s kitchen.

Wear closed shoes with a good grip. The woman at number eight cuts a leaf back from the foot of the steps in spring; don’t count on the gap still being there when you arrive. Keep a hand free for the rail, and make sure you can see stone where you mean to put your foot. On the crossing, give the unfinished saw cut a little room. The chips at its lip have fresh edges, and the dark strip under the pipe is wet even when most of the floor is dry.

Expect the noise before you descend. There are two banks of fans, running at slightly different speeds. The low sound that gathers and fades between them is not a lorry you need to wait for. Agree where to meet while you can still talk comfortably; if steady loud noise bothers you, bring earplugs. Put your key away before you start down. The locals look at theirs for a good reason: if it falls, there’ll be no audible *ping* to tell you where it went.

For the best look at the place, find a bare patch out of the line between the steps and the doors, and stand there a while. From that height you can see both the bees waiting at the mortar holes and, now and then, a blackbird crossing a gap under the butterbur. There’s no need to part the stalks or go round the cabinet to see if the fox is home. Leave the odd little objects where they lie, particularly the heel of bread with its chalk outline. Ask before picking currants or taking a picture toward the windows. On a shutdown Sunday, expect to see *less* wildlife, not more: without the fans, the smallest ordinary human noise carries across the stone.

You don’t need expedition gear. A light layer is worth having if you mean to stay after the sunny crossing; the air moves along the bottom even at noon. What’s most worth bringing is time for a second look from the street on your way out. Once you’ve stood on the white floor, the green roofs resolve into separate islands, and the red mark on the brick means something quite precise: you can see where you were standing, and how far above your head the proposed fill would go.
