Make your first visit from the pasture, in dry weather, and give yourself an afternoon. The far side is where you understand the shape of the place, but approach it slowly: the grass gives no particular notice that the stone beneath it is about to end. Stay on firm ground back from the overhang. It would be worth bringing a small pair of binoculars. There is a good deal to see without ever going down.

If you do mean to visit the floor, settle the question of how you will get *out* before you enter. The three steps on the western wall are the remains of a stair, not a stair. The landing beyond them belongs to a nesting bird for part of the year, and the geckos are considerably better equipped for the rest of the ascent than you are. Don’t count on climbing the fig, either. Unless someone who knows the hollow can show you a sound walking route both ways, be content with the view from above.

Choose a day with no rain expected over the pasture. The hollow takes runoff from ground you can’t see once you’re under the tree. Even an ordinary shower sends water across the smooth shelves; the white shell caught high in a root is reason enough not to wait below to see what an extraordinary shower does. Wear shoes with good grip, take drinking water of your own, and carry a small light for the return. The black seam stays slick for days after the shelves dry. Expect shade, but not coolness: most of the breeze you felt on the walk over will pass a tree’s height above your head.

Down there, the most useful preparation is to have nothing else scheduled for a while. Find a dry place to sit off the shell fans, checking first for ant trails, and let your eyes adjust. You may smell a ripe fig long before you pick out its pale skin among the leaves. Leave it where it is. If one ant comes up a root carrying something pink, follow the line with your eyes rather than your feet. There may be an entire afternoon’s entertainment at the other end of it, though the tortoise will take most of that afternoon to arrive.

Ask locally, if you can, when the southern lip lets the sun through. Be sitting still before it happens. The first sign may not be light where you are, but a moth edging sideways on the wall. Watch the bright strip cross the floor and climb back toward the pasture. It is the best chance you’ll have to see that little flood shell without disturbing it, and a good cue to start back while there is still plenty of light above the rim.
