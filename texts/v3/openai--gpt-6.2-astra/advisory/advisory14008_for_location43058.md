Make the uphill lip your base for a first visit. From there you can see all three islands, the booth, and the first dimple when it forms. Ask the road crew about the ditch before you climb: is it draining normally, and is the approach still sound? The coat pegs in the booth are a useful measure of what “flooded” means here. If the water is unusually high, watch from above.

Go up in the morning if you want to see the old watershed. Take drinking water, a sun hat, a layer for the climb back down, and something to eat while you wait; there’s good reason to stay into the afternoon. Allow time at the lip to get your breath back. For the crossing, bring close-fitting water shoes with a grippy, heat-resistant sole. Barefoot is customary, but a regular visitor knows which bits of black stone break the surface. You don’t want your first discovery about the heat to be that it makes you hurry on slippery weed. Keep well inside the low curb, and don’t count on an island for firm footing. What looks like a few inches of lawn can swallow the length of a stick.

Visit the booth while the shelf is quiet. On a clear morning, kneel beside the square opening and give your eyes a minute. Watch the white flower through several bouts of rain; the pleasure of it is noticing that it comes back up a little differently each time. Keep your camera strap and other loose things clear of the hole. When the drainages join, there is a real current down there. The opening is a place to look through, not another way home.

Be back uphill for the afternoon drawdown. There’s no need to get close to a seam to understand what’s happening: find a floating seed and watch the distance between it and the stone. Don’t take the exposed bed as an invitation to cross. It’s hot, the closed snails are easy to tread on, and the first beads of returning water can join into a sheet almost at once.

Of all the things to pack, a small pair of binoculars may give you the most pleasure. At noon, look just beyond the roots of the smallest island, where the palm shade falls. If all you see is a grass stem bending and easing upright, stay with it. Later, when you hear that little patter like grit on paper, you may get to watch the same rail walk out into the open and lift a strand of weed with its toe.
