Arrange to arrive in the quiet part of the morning, at an agreed roof, with someone expecting you there. “I can see the house” is a poor reason to wave your guide on ahead. You can see practically *every* house, including ones you could scarcely reach by supper. For a first visit, I would lodge within a short walk of the municipal ovens. That gives you a whole evening’s worth of things to see without having to find your way home across open leaf in failing light.

**Bring your own water.** Carry enough for the return journey and a delay, divided between sealed containers, and keep one on you even when you leave the rest at your lodging. A perfectly attentive host might see to your warmth, your bed and your place at table without ever thinking to offer you a drink. Explain what you need before you need it. There is no good reason to suppose that leaf grease is food for you, much less a substitute for water. Bring familiar provisions until you know whether you can eat the bread. Bring back your washing water and other waste for disposal at home unless your host has somewhere for it to go.

Take a windproof layer, dust goggles, well-fitting hearing protection and soft-soled shoes with a good grip. Leave the walking spikes behind. Those widely spaced bands are streets, but they are also what holds the town in shape. Don’t drive anything into one to secure a shelter, or helpfully trim a loose fiber from a bridge. Ask where to walk. In the northern district, have someone show you the old sill and step over it. It takes about as much effort as stepping over the feet of someone sitting in a doorway, which is rather the point.

On the first day, **be where you intend to spend the evening before you hear the furniture-dragging sound.** It is advance notice of a change across the whole plateau, not a noise you can get away from by going one street farther east. Ask your host for a place to sit and put your ear protection in early. Agree beforehand on a visible signal for “I need to leave.” If there is a view of open ground, look for the deer’s knees bending; it is much easier to pick out a deer once you know where its jaw is going to meet the slope. Watch the seams from there. You needn’t put your fingers into one to see what comes out, and they do shut again.

At supper, ask to be taught two things while you can still hear: how to thank the person beside you and how to ask them to move their foot. Leave your ankles within reach. When the room is sounding and a loaf splits open and somebody touches the side of your shoe, it will be nice to be able to answer on purpose.

Treat the exchanges as a convenience for residents, **not as a way home**. Neither you nor your equipment came up through these leaves. Don’t entrust your water, spectacles, medicine or return device to a bench. If somebody lends you an old coat to send across town, empty its pockets and ask about recent repairs. The coat can be perfectly reliable and its fastening still be this morning’s button. That is what the trays are for.

Save the rim for a settled evening and go with someone who knows the way back. There is roughly an hour of light after the note, not an extra hour each way. At the smooth depressions, locals take off their shoes; if bare feet don’t give *you* a secure purchase, take the longer way round. Sit well back from the lip, out of the birds’ landing runs. You don’t have to lean over a mile of hanging leaf to see them. Wait and one will come up into view from below, pass you in the air and finish its journey on its stomach. Listen for the last little scrape of its feet. Go home while you can still see the roofs, and don’t count on finding even those two fine tracks again in the morning.
