Find the black curb in daylight. The best visit an ordinary human can make is probably from there: stay back from the stonework, wait through the change of draft at dusk, and listen. The handwide slit is an outlet, not a doorway. Don’t put your face to it to catch the note. For those few moments the slit is carrying the dust of four quarters of a town.

If your means of travel lets you arrive inside, **arrange your return before you go**. None of the four flues is a dependable way out. Visit from a position already checked for sound footing and headroom, with a companion outside who can bring you back even if you cannot give a signal. Moving air is no assurance of breathable air. If the atmosphere and heat have not been assessed, stay at the curb; a dust mask won’t supply oxygen or protect you from furnace gases. Even for a cleared, short visit, take sealed eye protection, suitable respiratory protection for the mineral dust, and hearing protection. Drink beforehand and keep any water you carry sealed. There’s no reason for the first drop ever spilled in this room to be yours.

Go for the afternoon light rather than the evening note. Bring a small, dimmable lamp for your footing, then turn it off once you’re settled. Stand back far enough to see the whole length of the red hem. The thing worth seeing isn’t one blade opening; it’s the distance the light travels along the bed in the time it takes that first blade to fold again. If you go close to inspect it, you’ll miss that.

Stay off the apparent path between the handsome ranks. It’s bare because it takes a jet of grit strong enough to carve stone. Don’t lean on the wall, test the yellow lips with a finger, or pull a dead leaf free: what looks like litter is holding the mineral meal for the shoots beneath it. There’s no need to bruise a blade to prove the account of the orange smell.

Leave before the evening reversal. The vane’s report is impressive, but directly beneath old masonry is a poor place to hear it. Back above ground, put your lamp away and give yourself time to listen. After seeing those leaves turn one after another, you’ll know what is happening below when the curb begins to sing.
