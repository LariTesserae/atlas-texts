Arrange to arrive at an engine house, with somebody waiting for you. “On the tableland” is not a good enough destination. A pace or two might make the difference between standing on warm rock and falling through the crowns of trees you will never reach from below. Don’t drop a stone to find out how close you are. By the time you hear the water, you have learned very little about where it is safe to put your *next* foot, and there may be people working the lifts below you.

For a first visit, hire a guide for the walk from a house to one of the great divides and back. Ask for a walk that is short **by the return route**: if a strip of pasture tears loose, there should still be a way home without it. Learn the guide’s signals for *stop, rock here,* and *take my arm*. Practise them outside the house while the wheel is running. A signal you can hear in your quiet kitchen may be quite useless within a mile of that belt.

Bring a hooded, waterproof lamp and a spare you can find and switch on by touch. Ask before using either around the birds or in an occupied house. Light will be a great comfort for examining a cut or finding a dropped buckle; it will not turn the marsh into a landscape you can read at a glance. Beyond its little reach there will be no skyline. Carry a plain probing staff, boots with a good grip on wet stone, and flotation you can wear all day, not something lashed to your pack. A staff that meets resistance has found *something*, not necessarily ground. A whole pasture with a goat standing on it can be afloat.

Pack drinking water for the return as well as the outward walk. The rain may fall almost pure, but the warm water squeezed from a handful of velvet is another matter. Bring food that needs no fire, dressings for your feet, and a dry change in a sealed bag. A dry pair of socks at the house will be worth considerably more than an extra instrument for measuring the rain. Put a watch with a vibrating alarm on your wrist. There is no dawn to tell you that you have lain awake long enough and ought to get up and eat.

Ask your guide to show you the leaf tubes **before** a storm. You want to know what ordinary water in a tube feels like, so that when someone puts your finger to one and says, “There, it’s climbing,” you understand. When the flocks lift, go to your agreed place on stone. The household that stays to finish its meal knows where every goat is and how long it takes to uncover the bells. You do not yet know how long it takes to do up your coat. Stay put through the rain and have the way checked afterwards. Don’t wade toward a familiar voice on the strength of remembering a bank between you.

At the engine house, let a machinist place your hand on the doorframe. Feel the old boot-stick dents; learn where the threshold is in relation to them. Then ask for a safe place to stand and listen. Keep loose gear secured and your hands off the shaped handles. Those shapes are there so that someone who knows the machine can act without hesitation, not so that a stranger can work out the controls by trial. If the wheel stops while you are out in the pasture, stop with it until your guide has taken stock. For a while you will hear vastly more and know rather less: the beat by which you were keeping the house on your left has gone, and that tearing sound may be coming from the way you came.

If you stay the night, ask to sleep on a great divide, well inland, with your bedding laid out for you the first time. Take a thin groundsheet. Before you unroll it, lie down with one ear on the bare stone. Have someone tell you which sound is the house and which is the southern fall; neither the loudness of the one nor the clearness of the other is a measure of walking distance. You needn’t set out for the rim to appreciate how high the country is. There is water going over it farther away than anyone you have met has traveled, and you can feel it quite plainly through your cheek.
