Arrange to go up with a gardener who is making a reading, and go alone with them if you can. Thirty is a measure of the circumference, not a sensible size for a party. Ask for a late shift on a day when they expect the iron to sweat. Tell them you would like to see the shadow cross the fig, so they can allow for the descent afterward.

First ask what “above the weather” means for a human from your world. Have the air at the access hatch checked for pressure, oxygen, and whatever the compressors carry. The smell of warm linen is a lovely thing to notice once you know the air is breathable. It is not the test. If you need breathing equipment or time to acclimatize, settle that below. Ask the shift crew about exhaust conditions, too; don't go out during a change they wouldn't ordinarily work through.

Get your hearing protection fitted before the hatch opens. Learn the gardener's signals for *stay there*, *come back*, and *I need help*, and agree that if you lose sight of each other you won't go looking around the ring. Wear close-fitting layers for the cold side of the horn, boots with good grip, and nothing with a loose end. Have the crew show you the protected viewing position and how to use whatever fall restraint the keepers trust. If there isn't one on the rim, take the view from inside the hatch. The fig has grown to the shape of a handrail; that doesn't make it one. A dropped lens cap is gone, and there are people at work below. Leave most things in your pockets.

Give yourself a moment when you first look out. A mountain that far away ought to be hazy. Seeing folds in the snow as clearly as folds in a sleeve, while your feet tell you there is almost nothing under them, may make you sway. Have the gardener get you settled before you try to take it in. There is no particular need to look down the throat.

Bring a small close-focusing glass rather than a long lens. From one secure spot at branch height you can spend a good hour over a few inches of bark. Ask the gardener to show you what they came to read *before* you put a hand anywhere. A bruised place under the fig matters to the people on the compressor shift; the distribution of flowers around the hoop tells them something a whole bed of healthy onions would not. Don't pick a bulb or water a dry patch. When the hoop begins to bead, watch where the first drop goes. Then watch the first snail leave the felt. Even the ones left out on the glaze afterward don't need carrying home.

If the gardener says it is safe to touch, feel the ceramic once in daylight. That gives you time to recognize the slow knock without having to search for it. Toward evening, as the blue reaches the trunk, rest one fingertip very lightly on the branch. Keep your hearing protection on. The pleasure of staying late is that you can feel exactly when the crickets start, in a place where nobody will ever hear them.
