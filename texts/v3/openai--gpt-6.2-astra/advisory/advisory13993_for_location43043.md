Arrange the visit from the nursery side, with whoever looks after the grounds and whoever operates the steam main. Ask whether the stair and landing are still sound and whether the air below has been checked. The clean edges of the shale give you no reason to suppose the ground has finished moving. If nobody can vouch for the landing, stay up top. Ask to see the nursery wall instead: its moving shadow sets the calendar for the ferns below.

If the landing is cleared, make that your destination. Take a companion, wear shoes with good grip, and carry drinking water and two independent lights. Dress more lightly than you ordinarily would for underground; you may want your coat going down the stair and very much want it off by the time you stop. Use a light for all twenty steps. The turn is where your feet will expect another flight, and the clean zigzag on the wall is all that remains of it. The three steps on the clay look like a pleasant bench, but there is no stair back up from that bench. Leave the hollow floor, the pipe, and the tilted valve house to the people responsible for them. If you hear a new hiss or see fresh material falling, head back up and tell the operator.

For the best first look, ask when the bundle beside the steps usually starts to uncurl, and come a little before then, near noon. Once you and your companion are standing still and well back from the edge, switch off your lights for a while. Give it longer than seems necessary. The first impression will be of five patches of green at different distances in black air. It takes a little time to see that the black extends *under* four of them. Then use one modest beam to look at the fifth, where the lowest leaves lie flat on the clay. That difference is worth seeing; a bright lamp left on from the start will show you a room full of ferns, which is not quite the same sight.

If you can return, come a day or so after rain rather than during it. You won't hear a shower through the roof. What you might catch instead is one dark stocking of earth among the dry ones, and the smell of a freshly watered bed in air warm enough to make that smell seem surprising. A sketchbook is better than a specimen bag here. Draw the five bundles in their places and leave room beside each for a second date. Three weeks later, from precisely the same landing, you'll be able to see how far spring has traveled across a garden you can scarcely see at all.
