I’d arrange to be met at the western lip in full daylight. Ask the person meeting you to show you the way to the works and, just as importantly, to walk the way back with you. From the lip you can see your destination perfectly well. What you can’t see is whether the ground you’re standing on goes there.

Wear closed shoes with a good tread, a warm layer you can carry when you don’t need it, and glasses that cut the glare off the roofs. Bring drinking water. It would be easy to look down into all that dripping timber and expect to find a stream up top; what you’ll actually get on your shoes is dust. Keep your hands free. A current route plan is worth having, but take the workers’ word over the plan about which stairs and platforms are in use. The movement is slow enough that nobody needs to run from it. It’s also slow enough for a door to remain perfectly serviceable long after the place it leads to has gone away.

On your first walk, learn to read the posts while you can still see the ground they describe. The enamel is on the side toward solid footing. Notice where the path bends even though the grass ahead looks exactly like the grass under your feet. Those locked seed heads can make one meadow out of two without adding anything that will bear your weight. Don’t test them, and don’t get down on a lip to look for the matching hollows in the stone.

If you can choose the month, go in June and ask whether someone can take you down a maintenance stair. That’s the excursion I’d make time for. Halfway down, let your eyes adjust and look *up*. The field you’ve just crossed will be in flower, every blossom on the side you couldn’t see. Allow yourself a quarter of an hour with no particular program. That might be long enough to notice that something pale against the yellow ceiling is a moth and not a bit of chaff.

At the glassworks, wait for someone to take you in and wear the eye protection they give you; your outdoor glasses are for the roofs, not the furnaces. Don’t rely on hearing a cart come up behind you. Ask where you can stand out of the cableway’s path to watch a finished rack leave. You might miss the glass itself. Watch instead for a stand of distant trees turning up between the near ones, upside down and moving at a walking pace.

Set your turnaround by the cloud, not by sunset. As soon as it reaches the low branches, the deep openings start to look shallower, which is precisely the wrong impression. Make that first return with your guide. If you lose the line of posts, stop on known footing and call out rather than feeling forward with a shoe. There’s a good reason to practice the walk in daylight, beyond simply getting home: on the way back you can spare a little attention for the feel of it. The air will be cold, your hand will brush a post, and for an instant under your fingers it will still be noon.
