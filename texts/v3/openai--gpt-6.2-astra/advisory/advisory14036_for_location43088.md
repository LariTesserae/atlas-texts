I’d plan to spend the day on one of the bluffs, well back from its crumbling lip. From there you can see the willows coming out of the water on one shore while the weed lifts off the bottom on the other. Down among the rushes you’d hear the whole exchange and see only the part happening to your boots.

**Choose the way home before you descend.** Come in by firm upland and keep it within reach. Those peat ridges look like paths, but even the ones that carry mature alders have given way at times. Don’t count on walking round the mere just because you can see a continuous strip of land. A gravel hump may stay perfectly dry while your way off it fills. If you need to stay overnight, camp inland on high ground rather than on one of those tempting little islands.

Dress for cold rain and for standing still: a warm layer that will still insulate when damp, a windproof rain shell, gloves, and waterproof boots with good tread. Carry a dry change in a sealed bag, a hot drink, a headlamp, and your own drinking water. Bring a companion if you can. A walking pole will help on slippery ground, but finding a firm place for its tip tells you nothing about what the water there will do next. I’d leave the waders at home; there’s little to gain by getting into cold water over soft peat.

Watch a delivery from above before going anywhere near the margin. Pick a willow fork or a pale mark on a trunk as a water gauge. Listen for the two low notes drawing together, then watch the mark rather than the sky. The ripping of the rushes means stems are **already** taking the load; it isn’t a dependable advance warning. Nor can you follow the sound along the shore and assume you know where it will start up next. If water begins lifting through the weed near you, go back toward firm high ground. Don’t try to beat it along a ridge.

Binoculars and a small notebook would earn their weight here. You could spend a good hour matching a newly exposed fork on the far shore to a deepening hollow nearby, without ever being sure you’d found its partner. Brush mud and seeds from your boots before entering another catchment, and spare the gravel patches altogether if possible. A little community that the mere’s water has never quite reached needn’t have its first new arrival carried in on a tread.

Leave enough daylight for the return even if the evening is the loveliest part. When the rain clears, there are still deliveries on the way. Watch the snipe take the new mud from the safety of the bluff. Its weight, its feet, and its business there are all quite different from yours.
