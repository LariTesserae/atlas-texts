If the tram people admit visitors, ask them for a dry place to sit with a view of the lowest steps. That is worth much more here than a pair of waders. Go on a fair day, toward noon if you can: the patch of daylight under the grating lets you see things that are all but invisible in the amber light. If the hall is closed, or there is rain coming, leave it for another day. Half a block of dry pavement at the entrance tells you very little about what is coming down the drains.

Wear shoes with good wet grip, keep a hand free, and carry a small light for the stairs. At the turn, remember that the sockets on the wall are not a railing. Stop well above the dark concrete, even if the frogs are a step farther down than usual. There is no need to get into the water. The reflection of a pier looks much the same over a sound inch of floor as it does over the beginning of the slope to the intake. Leave the inspection walk to the fitters, and give the steam main room; don't lean against the wrapping to let someone pass. Dress for the heat rather than for a cellar. If the air makes you feel poorly, go back up.

The most useful thing to bring is time. Pick out a leaf at the lowest riser and note where its stem touches the concrete. Then forget about it for a while. Look at the brush under the grating and wait for an overhead tram: the little gray cloud that rises is part of the answer to how a frog makes a living down here. Look for the pencil date on the middle pier. A little pair of binoculars is handy for this, and for seeing the channel between the two fans without putting a shoe through a winter's worth of sorting.

The clap from behind the blue gate may startle you. Stay where you are. Look back at your leaf: what was a water-level mark a moment ago is now showing you the direction of the draw. If you are lucky enough to be there when the hum stops, stay quiet a little longer. There is a brief interval when you can hear individual grains arriving from the street, and then the frogs start down to the newly exposed shore.

Take a dated sketch rather than a specimen. Leave the frogs, grit and improbable little grass plant where they are, and clean your soles before going on to another world. If you come back, bring the sketch. It may be the last view you get of those two islands while they are still two.
