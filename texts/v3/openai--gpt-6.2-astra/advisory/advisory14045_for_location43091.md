For a first visit, arrange to meet someone at the inner wall in daylight, and have them walk you to your lodgings by the route you’ll use in the dark. Learn the last turn by touch: a ceramic jamb, a change from pavement to stairs, something you can recognize with a glove on. A stream name may be perfectly current and still belong to a different street on your map. And at the end of a cross street, the place where your hand runs out of rail may be the place where the mountain runs out too.

Bring two lamps, one of which you can use with your hands full. Ask when daylight is due to end before deciding that you have enough charge for the night. Eleven days is a long time to nurse a failing light. Keep a watch on your own sleep cycle, and ask your host which kitchen will be serving when *your* morning comes. If any part of your diet is indispensable, arrange for it ahead of time. A city with public kitchens is a better prospect for dinner than an empty shelf, but even a very good cook cannot send a child outside for a handful of greens.

The most useful clothes will be warm ones you can still move in when the outer layer is wet. Wear boots with a good grip on slick stone and soles that won’t slice through on a salt lip. Take spare dry socks, gloves, and drinking water; the clear bead coming straight out of the mountain is brine. For the first few blocks, carry your lamp low enough to throw a shadow behind a raised edge. The sound of breakers at your feet tells you that something is alive there. It tells you very little about where to put your next foot. Use the covered walks where you can, and don’t shortcut through a seep just because you can see the bottom. In water that thin, the bottom is the whole inhabited depth.

There are two places to give a wide berth to everyday work. One is a roof being repaired. You may see a roof moving sideways at the height of the house next door, with what look like wet quilts still heaped on it. Stand where the crew asks you to stand. Don’t offer to carry an animal down. The whole elaborate business is being done so that a creature need not surrender a feeding place it may have held since somebody on the crew was a baby. The other place is a weight exchange. You don’t need to understand what happens to the ingots to make a sensible decision about your luggage. If a cook on the next block is lifting cups with both hands, take the long way round with your pack.

Take the noise seriously as a matter of rest, not just as a curiosity. You might find the callers pleasant for an hour and quite unbearable by supper, especially when a patch of pavement begins to beat through both soles at once. Find a municipal silence shed on your first walk, while you’re still feeling well. Sit down in it for a while. On coming out you may be able to pick apart what seemed on arrival to be one tremendous racket: the knock under the water, the resonance of a pier, the two white neighbors on a gutter who have never once conceded the point. Save your earplugs for bed rather than shutting out voices and warnings on an unfamiliar street.

Once you know the way, ask someone to take you somewhere safe to listen across the gap. Let them name the settlements *after* you’ve listened; it’s worth having a town in your head for a minute that consists entirely of one low note. I’d save the east ward for the end of the visit, too. On the first day a thumb-wide groove is an odd thing to be taken out in the cold to see. After several days of watching water hesitate across a floor of black stone, you’ll have some notion of why people bring their children. Take off one glove.
