Plan this as a visit to the rim, not a crossing. Choose a stretch where you can trace the bedrock back uphill without losing sight of it under water or snow. Arrange your return from there, on the near side. The far rim is an unreliable landmark in sleet, and the near rim looks barely high enough to matter. Make sure you can find your way inland in poor visibility; walk the return route in daylight and mark it with a couple of bright, removable flags. If you arrive in weather that won’t let you distinguish the bank from the animals, come back another day.

Dress to stand still in wet wind. Take warm layers under a waterproof shell, insulated boots with good grip on slick rock, and a dry change of clothes and mittens in a sealed bag. Bring your drinking water. Fresh enough to freeze is not the same as fit to drink, and there is nothing here to make a fire with if you get wet. A windproof shelter and something hot in a flask will do you more good than waders. Bring binoculars: the difference between a stone being carried and a stone at rest is worth seeing without having to put your fingers under either one.

Spend the first few strokes well back from the water. You may feel the knock in your heels before you recognize it as a sound. Watch one bearer through the whole interval: the testing feet, the sudden sitting down, the birds dropping over its back. That will cure much of the temptation to walk out. The pavement looks firmest precisely when everything on it has stopped moving to survive the blow. A lane between backs is no better a foothold; it can close again before you have seen what is in the hole beneath it. Don’t try the ice, either. From above it may look attached to the bank. Look at it against a fixed point and you can see it traveling.

Give the old channel the widest berth. It may be easiest to see in bad weather, when a strip of sleet goes sideways against the prevailing wind. In clear weather, look for dimples traveling on after a flock has settled. Neither an empty surface nor a quiet interval tells you that it is safe. Something that happened elsewhere still has to pass that bend, with enough force to send an eel up the bank. Don’t go down for a closer look at a stranded one. If you can’t make out the course of the channel, stay inland rather than trying to locate its edge by experiment.

If you can choose your hour, be in position before the sleet clears, with your retreat already marked and a light handy. Find a sheltered place to sit on continuous dry rock overlooking the sill. There is no need to lean over a window in the bedrock: from a little height you can follow a stroke much farther by the successive strips of rising birds than you could by looking straight down. Later, when the red seams show, resist the quite human inclination to move toward what looks warm.

Save a little patience for the pearl light. If a bearer has lost its back, leave it the choice of a new stone; what looks to a human like an animal trapped under a slab may be the first moment it has been properly covered all day. Watch the rough new oval take its place among the polished ones. That is a better thing to have come all this way for than the opposite bank.
