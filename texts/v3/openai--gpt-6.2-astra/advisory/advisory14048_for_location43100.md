Prepare for a visit to the lip, not a journey into the basin. Sideways, your hand might fit through the opening; that is a useful measure of its size, not a suggestion. Even if you could arrive there small enough to walk down the steps, you would have nowhere to put your first foot.

For an ordinary traveler, the serious part of the expedition is outside. Wear boots and gloves suited to cold rock, a helmet, and warm layers that will still keep you warm while you are hanging still. Arrange the visit with people qualified to manage an exposed rope descent and get you back over the lip. The last survey stud is a landmark, not evidence of a sound anchor. If the slate is loose, the wind is gusting, or a safe return cannot be assured, stay on the ridge. The pale rectangle where the instrument house stood is worth seeing in its own right.

If you do go below, have your equipment and signals sorted out before you leave the summit. The first note in your teeth may startle you; you do not want that to be the moment you discover you need a free hand. Keep buckles, loose straps, and rope clear of the opening. Leave the torch off and give your eyes time. The light you want has come off another mountain and round a corner. At first you may see only velvet. Try looking for the pewter edge of a single leaf; once you have a sense of its size, the patch of green will begin to resolve into a garden. The lower benches may remain out of sight. Let them.

There is no need to make a sound to see what happens. In particular, leave the iron tongue alone. It looks like the working part of an abandoned instrument, something one might gently flick to find out whether it still works. The purple tips resting against it are your answer. Nor should you blow dust off anything, wet a leaf, or reach in for one of the white pebbles. Water is not a kindness in a hollow that has never had any.

Notice, on the way back up, where you stop feeling the note in your jaw. That may be the part of the visit you remember best: you have moved only a little way, the slate under your boots is still the same slate, and an entire garden’s means of living has passed out of your perception.
