For a first visit, arrange to meet someone at the pay-office bench late in the morning. That gives you time to hear the mill running before the noon stop, and a place to sit that is out of the way of the handcarts. Ask your guide to show you the way back to the brick ridge. In particular, have them point out where the ridge ends. The apparent meadow beyond the office is sedge over shoulder-deep water; there is nothing about the color of it, from the bench, to tell a stranger not to walk there.

Wear closed shoes with good grip, light clothes that dry readily, and bring a dry pair of socks. Use a small pack rather than a wheeled case, and keep your papers in a waterproof sleeve. The water by the bench may be no more than a puddle throughout your stay. If it starts welling up through the drain, though, ask which route is still dry rather than wading across paving you can no longer see. The level mark on the tilted bench leg tells you where the water has stood before, not where it will stop.

Visit the sheds by invitation. Bring hearing protection, tie back long hair, and put away anything that dangles before going near the drive. On the landing, let the people unloading a barge show you where to stand: the ends of the reeds reach the planks before the bow does. You can smell a bundle being opened from well out of the way. Inside, resist the impulse to reach out to a fraying edge of the wet sheet. Even if the machine were quite still, the stuff is scarcely strong enough yet to bear the weight of a drop of water.

In the lanes, follow the local habit of walking over to speak to someone rather than calling across. A doorway may be the best place to hear each other, but leave room for whoever is coming out with a tub. The beans under the steam main are somebody’s crop, and the cut grass on a muddy step has been put there deliberately. Wipe your shoes.

If you can spare the time, come back to the bench after work. At noon the pleasure is in hearing all at once how many people are close by, eating and moving chairs, who were inaudible a moment ago. In the evening you have time to notice slower things. Ask someone at the office to show you the old sill behind the herb bed. You might otherwise spend a whole visit looking out for the twice-yearly flood and miss the little run of clear water among the mint, marking where a person once stepped through a door.
