Arrange to arrive by daylight, on a keeper’s path well inside one of the basins. If you can choose where your passage home opens, choose that same path. The mountain would be an unusually bad place to follow the ordinary traveler’s rule of going up to get your bearings. From a ridge you can see the next cook fire perfectly well; what you can’t see is which ground between here and there will bear your weight, or where the air stops being air you can breathe comfortably.

Ask a keeper to walk the first stretch with you. Learn a route by its turns on firm ground, not by the sound of the nearest bell. In all that rattling water and bird noise you may scarcely hear it. Find out where to turn back on the outer slope *before* you go there. The dead grove is evidence of where the boundary used to be, not a marker of where it is today. Don’t go looking for the first breath of cold as a way of finding the line. If you do suddenly find yourself gasping on gravel, retrace your steps toward the ferns with your companion rather than pressing on for a better view.

Dress for a warm marsh. Take light clothes that dry quickly, deeply treaded shoes that stay on your feet in mud, a walking staff, and a dry bag with a change of socks and a warm layer. Keep the load small enough that you can recover from a slip. Carry drinking water and familiar food; neither the cheek-warm pools nor the bread-scented fruit come with any assurance that they suit a human visitor from elsewhere. If staying overnight, arrange a dry sleeping place in advance. Nine little houses with nine people’s work to do are not a chain of inns. Bring your own supper and ask what, if anything, would be useful to carry up.

Give the drains much more room than their size seems to warrant. On level ground a trickle disappearing into reeds looks like something you could step across. Here the far side of a clump of reeds may be sky. The keeper with the snail basket knows that lip by the soles of her feet; her being there is not an invitation to crawl out beside her. If seeing the red river matters to you, tell her so and let her decide whether there is a place to see it that day. Don’t back up to frame a photograph anywhere near running water.

Leave yourself an afternoon with nothing to cross off a list. Sit on firm ground a little way back from a fruit tree and wait for what looks like a white stripe on the forest floor to become an animal. Ask before going under a bell or touching a leg, and leave the roots to the person who tends them. One of the best things to bring might be a shallow bowl and some beans to shell: a reason to sit on a doorstep with your host, doing a useful small job until the skins stir. By dusk you could know something about the distance to the next basin that you would never learn by walking to its edge.
