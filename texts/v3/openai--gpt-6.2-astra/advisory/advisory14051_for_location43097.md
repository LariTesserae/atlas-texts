Arrive after rain, and allow an hour for finding the town. While the whole street is wet, its paving stone looks much like any other. Wait for the others to dry. When you see the damp crescent, you have found the place; there is no need to put your weight on the stone to make sure.

Arrange to arrive at the inhabitants’ scale, with your clothes and provisions reduced along with you. If your means of travel cannot do that, stay on the street. Lifting the stone for a look would take the daylight off one end of town and put the whole sky over the other. From below, the narrow strip of grey light is quite sufficient.

Have somebody meet you. The most useful thing your host can show you on the first day is how to stand at a door. Let them put your heel in the right place on the threshold and show you how little pressure it takes to raise the latch. Wait there until the door opens. Knocking harder because you haven’t heard an answer is a habit to get out of. So is bouncing your knee when sitting down. You can carry on quite a conversation with the next house without knowing a word of it.

Bring a warm close-fitting layer, a dry change in a waterproof wrap, and shoes with soft, grippy soles that come off easily. Keep the parcel small enough to carry against your chest. There are places, particularly on the way around the old square, where a person with a pack on can get through facing forward but cannot turn to see who is behind them. Take food that doesn’t shed crumbs, and bring drinking water until you know where guests’ jars are filled. There is water almost everywhere; that does not make it all yours to dip into. If you’re handed a jar, look for the flat on its belly before setting it down. A sill that looks too narrow to hold it has probably held that particular jar for years.

For the first walk, follow in your host’s exact footsteps. The three steps will bear a person climbing them; there’s no reason to worry about that. But don’t lift one to see how it’s made. Much of what looks like a good shortcut is the top of something you’d take considerable care of if you could see it from below. At the square, keep to the narrow way around the bowl. Give anyone fitting a handle room to finish before you pass between their hands and the light. Down at the retaining wall, watch for the clean thread in the milky skin. It will help you make out the limpet. A remarkable thing about seeing it is that you can stand quite still, see no movement at all, and yet see exactly where the animal has been.

Expect to be tired by the noise before you realize you are tired. Bring soft ear protection for the first night, but don’t spend the day trying to hear every stray sound through the surf. Sit indoors with your shoes off. If invited, rest a foot against the wall as your neighbor does. It takes much less effort to notice a movement through the sole than to strain for a voice. The quiet behind the quarry is worth a visit, though it may not be the rest you imagined. Arrange the walk back before you go.

Leave yourself at least one night with no plans for the morning. Your bed may be cool even in the dry room, and for a long time the water will seem to be hitting just above your head. If a single hard knock comes through your back, you needn’t get up to see what has come loose. After all those roofs have taken their share of the fall, there is still enough water getting through to turn a pebble. If the person in the next bed stirs at all, it will be to settle a little more comfortably.
