Aim to arrive late in the morning and stay into the afternoon. That gives you a chance of seeing the cloud withdraw from the garden and, later, of seeing the carriage make its pass. Wear a shirt with sleeves you can roll up, a light waterproof for the approach, and shoes that grip wet tile. Come with clean soles and use the scraper at the upper door. A fleck of blue wool on your boot as you leave is one thing; a weed seed you bring in might take somebody quite a while to put right.

At the door, take a moment to find the top step. If the cloud is in, you may see the tops of the beans quite distinctly without being able to see where to put your foot. Sit and let the red path come back into view. When you do walk down, keep to the tiles and give anyone pushing a barrow the right of way well up the slope. There’s practically nowhere to pass at the bottom. Don’t tuck the loose blanket hem back under the meadow, however strong the impulse. It’s easier to tell where the cloth ought to go than where the water goes.

Leave the yellow sill out of your plans as a place to sit or set anything down. In particular, don’t lean out to see the carriage underneath. Stay back from the fresh lime work and watch along the wall from the path. What’s worth seeing is small enough to miss if you’re trying to get a better view: the traveling bead of slurry comes to a standstill, and you know, without seeing the skip or hearing anybody call, that a load has arrived. If you want the scale of the work to sink in, bring a sketchbook and put a date on the drawing. You’d do better to come back in a year than to watch the edge for an hour.

Save some time at the end for the top step, with your bag out of the way of the door. The first motion underfoot may make you want to get up. Look at the old lamp bowl instead. The step and the water are answering the same drum, and by the time you’ve picked out the strip of sky between the duckweed, the motion will be over. It’s a good place to spend another few minutes after you think you’ve seen everything.
