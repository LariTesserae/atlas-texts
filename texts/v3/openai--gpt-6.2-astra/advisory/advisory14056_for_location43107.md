Arrange your arrival on broad, dry ground well back from the eastern lip, with enough daylight to walk the same ground twice. The first time, find a place to sit and look into the lake. The second time, walk back to your point of return while you can still see your footing. You will want to stay until the western shadow reaches you; that is a poor time to discover how much two black headlands look alike. If the crossing is your own to set, fix the return to the rock, not to a raft of round leaves or to the apparent shoreline.

Check that the air at rim height is fit for you before stepping out. Take your own drinking water and food, shoes with good wet-rock grip, a windproof layer, a lamp, and a dry bag. The warmth of the shallows will make the extra layer seem unnecessary until you have been sitting still in damp clothes for an hour. Of the things you might bring purely for pleasure, I would choose polarized glasses and a small pair of binoculars. The glasses will let you look past some of the reflected sky. The binoculars are for the ferns across the gap and for that one blade of grass. There is no need to get nearer either of them.

In particular, do not try to reproduce the view straight down between your feet. The slight inward tilt explains why the water stays; it tells you nothing about how much weight the thin rock will bear. Approach the water at an inland cove instead. Look for a broad shelf you can actually see joining the shore. A bright crease between two islands is not a footpath, however continuous it looks. Once the sun moves, you may lose sight of it all at once.

I would make the first visit a shore visit. This is water in which you can see a leaf vein and still fail to judge where to put a foot. The soft-looking meadow has cutting flakes inside it; pushing through the stems would be hard on your skin and worse for a growth that has taken a season to close around a single flake. Nor is the shimmer over a deep seam a reliable indication of a comfortable place to bathe. If you want to see the leaf undersides, wait where a floating bed comes close to rock rather than pulling it toward you. Bring clean, dry gear in, and take nothing living out.

Allow a generous part of the visit for one cove. Sit down a little before the shadow comes across, put the glasses away when the glare goes, and give your eyes time to find the bottom again. What looked all afternoon like a dark patch beside your seat may resolve into the far slope of a valley. You may hear the leaves touch before you can pick out which two are touching. Do not plan your departure around seeing the white fish; it may cost you days and owes you no appearance. A good place to end the visit is beside one of the stems at the waterline. You can see the air bead and the water drop from solid ground, close enough to tell them apart, without disturbing either.
