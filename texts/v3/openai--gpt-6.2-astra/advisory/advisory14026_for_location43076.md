Arrange to arrive at the upper end of the shelf, on known solid ground, with a way back that you can find in cloud. Don’t count on recognizing the arrival point by the view. The little snow peak is a good thing to look at and a poor thing to steer by: for much of the morning the cloud makes six miles of lava and the mountain beyond look like parts of one continuous walk. If you can choose your return point, make it the same as your arrival point. There’s no need to cross the shelf to have seen it.

Dress for standing still in sleet. You’ll probably spend more time watching than walking, and there’s very little shelter from the wind. Wear warm layers under a waterproof jacket and trousers, boots with good wet-rock grip and soles stiff enough for sharp lava, and gloves you can use with binoculars on. Keep a spare dry layer and mittens in a sealed bag. Bring your own drinking water, food, a hot drink, a headlamp and an emergency shelter. Go with someone, preferably someone who knows the current safe ground. Carry a local map and compass rather than assuming your usual navigation or communications satellites exist here.

The important distinction underfoot is between *rock* and *a roof*. The lovely continuous wrinkles tell you what the lava did when it was liquid, not what supports it now. Stay on an established route and well back from collapse holes and the nose of the flow. Snow can conceal a gap, clear ice can look like wet stone, and a loose flake may fit its bed perfectly until you put your weight on one end. If the cloud closes in, don’t hunt for a view by walking toward the light at the rim.

Give the long steaming crack a wide berth. The whistle would be an almost irresistible invitation to put an ear to the stone; resist it. A quiet vent can discharge scalding water or steam, and the ground beside it may be both thin and hot. In still weather volcanic gases can collect in hollows without giving you useful warning by smell. Check any local volcanic advisories before you go, stay away from low spots near the vents, and don’t use a warm seep to thaw your hands or fill a bottle. There can be ice within a few yards of dangerously hot water.

For the birds, take binoculars and time rather than a longer walk. Pick a secure place back from the sedge and watch one patch through a change of light. What looks like a few flattened blades may resolve into a pillar of midges, then a line of little bills working underneath. The birds have very few places to feed. If they lift because of you, they have to fit onto a strip already occupied. Clean your boots before arriving, keep off the vegetation, and leave the larvae, feathers and mineral rims where they are.

Set your turnaround by the weather, not by dusk. The first spread of silver water across your outward route is reason to head back while you can still distinguish its surface from the stone. Don’t wade after a lost trail: shallow runoff here ends in holes. If you want the last light on the pool, watch it from beside your return point. That is probably the best way to experience this place anyway—to stand somewhere dependable long enough for what seemed a bare, nearly motionless shelf to disclose how much is moving on it.
