Arrive by way of the old ferry slip if you can, and have somebody meet you there. “By the red paint, on the lip” is a better arrangement than “at my flat, you can see it from the slip.” From up there your host can point out the oyster bank, the school and the turn you want to take at the end of the bank. Once you get down among the trees you may be glad to know what order those things come in.

Take shoes with tough soles that still let you feel the ground. There is a difference between walking across a ripple and walking along one, and on the dance floor it will be useful to feel that difference. At a shell bed, though, go round. If a branch of the copper figs throws shade across the path, that’s a good place to stop and drink the water you brought. It doesn’t mean the soil off the path is soft or that there’s drinking water anywhere nearby. Those extravagant trees are living on rain stored well below your feet.

For a visit to a flat, get two sets of directions: one to the public entrance on the clay, and one from there to the door. **Allow time for the indoor part of the journey.** The block you stepped over on your way to the entrance is quite genuinely the block you now have to walk the length of. Look for the bicycles and prams, leave the approach clear, and don’t try to find a quicker way in by feeling along the cloudy glass. If you’re carrying luggage, a light pack will be easier than little wheels on fluted clay. Ask your host which exit you want on the way back, too. It needn’t be the one nearest their kitchen.

Dress for two kinds of noon. A hat that seems a foolish thing to have packed while you’re out in the brown street light may be just what you want sitting on a school landing among the melons. Give your eyes a moment when you come back out, particularly before crossing uneven ground. Don’t take the duskiness of the street as a sign you’re late for lunch. The children setting off for the sea wall are a better guide.

If you want to see the new land, ask that day where you can go. A map made before the last dry year might be perfectly good for finding the ferry slip and quite wrong about the shore. Stay on the finished street or the wall unless someone who knows that particular stretch takes you farther. Black ground with things still closing on it has only just ceased to be seabed. The woman with the pudding bowl knows where she can put her feet, and has a reason for being there. You can watch an anemone open quite well from the wall.

Above all, leave an evening free. When you smell warm pennies you still have time to fetch a stool; when you smell mint, look where the gardeners are working before you set it down. They need room to swing a paddle, and the people coming home need a doorway clear of vines. Ask to sit beside someone. It’s a good occasion to get to know a neighbor whom you might otherwise have to walk three indoor streets to call on.

For the few minutes when the light comes out again, I’d go back up to the slip. By then you’ll have some feeling in your legs for a distance your eyes still insist is no more than a step. Afterward, if there’s dancing, go down and join in. Nobody said you had to manage the first dance without looking at your feet.
