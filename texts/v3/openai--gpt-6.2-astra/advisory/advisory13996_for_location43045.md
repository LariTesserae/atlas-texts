For a first visit, I’d plan an out-and-back walk along the stone backs nearest the shelf, and save time to sit at the shelf again toward evening. The basin is easy to see across and hard to judge a distance in. An open line of sight to a patch of rushes doesn’t mean there’s a dry walk to it.

**Carry your drinking water, and bring your own shade.** Take enough water for the return even if the walk takes longer than you expect. The pans are a little salty, and the fact that fish prefer a certain trickle doesn’t tell you it’s fit to drink. Clear drops from the seam aren’t a guarantee either. A brimmed hat, light covering clothes, sunscreen and polarized glasses will do more for your comfort than you might expect from the cool look of all that water. Pack a little portable shade if you mean to stay through noon; the one bush is already doing important work for the toad. A sitting pad will let you rest on the dry rock without having to sit directly on its hot surface.

Check how much rain has fallen over the past few days, not just whether it will rain today. From the shelf, pick out the higher, continuous backs of stone and note the positions of the low saddles on your return route. Carry a compass and, if available, a map or position recorder that actually works in this world. Mark your starting point. Don’t navigate by a shoreline that the afternoon wind can move, or by the sound of frogs: the two stands you can distinguish so well at noon may both be silent by the time you turn back.

Wear closed shoes with good wet-rock grip. Favor bare, high stone over salty crust, slick seams and the thin soil that supports the basin’s few plants. If water starts washing over a low part of your route, turn back while the way is still clear. The concern isn’t necessarily deep water. Even a few inches of glare can hide the place where firm stone ends and a mud bowl begins. During the season when the pans join, I’d watch from the shelf rather than try to follow the old dry route by memory.

Bring binoculars and something to sit on rather than gear for wading. From firm ground you can watch the sandpipers work along the strip the wind uncovers without walking over their feeding ground. Give the rushes and seep fans room, and clean mud and seeds from your footwear **before arriving and after leaving**; these little refuges needn’t exchange passengers by way of another universe. A light wind layer and a headlamp are worth carrying if you stay late.

The best preparation may be to leave an hour unassigned. A hollow that seems empty at noon can turn out to have held a dozen fish all day; you didn’t need to get closer, only to let the angle of the light change. Be back on known ground before dusk, then sit within earshot of the shelf. If you leave as soon as the insects quiet down, you’ll miss the moment when you can hear the drip again.
