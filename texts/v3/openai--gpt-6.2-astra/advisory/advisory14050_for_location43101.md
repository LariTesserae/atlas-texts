Plan this as a visit to the threshold. The best place for a human is on the first step, *if* it can be confirmed as bare, stable rock and reached safely. There is no path between the dishes. From the step, though, you can watch a whole bed change without putting a boot on a single animal.

The summit approach is the first reason to turn back. Wind that rolls stones uphill can knock you down before you reach the shelter of the notch. Go in daylight, with someone who knows the ridge, and identify a sheltered retreat while you can still see it. Take a helmet, eye protection, gloves, and a windproof outer layer; secure anything that could blow loose. If the last stretch is exposed to the full wind, wait for another day. Make sure you can get *out* as well as in. The outward trip takes you straight from stillness into the blast.

**Still air is not necessarily breathable air.** The falling dust tells you how little wind reaches the room, not how much oxygen there is. Gas is coming through the floor, and its smell is no measure of a safe dose. Have a qualified survey team check the atmosphere remotely, including at the height of a seated visitor’s face. Measure the heat there too: a bearable air temperature need not mean a rock surface cool enough to sit on. Unless the gases have been identified and conditions can be monitored throughout the visit, stay outside and use a camera. A dust mask won’t make an unknown volcanic atmosphere safe. Make the same choice if the tilted roof and its support have not been assessed; don’t test the slab by pulling on it.

For a cleared threshold visit, take two independent hands-free lights. Use a low, angled beam to distinguish fixed stone from rolled lips *before* moving a foot. Don’t use your toe to settle a doubtful case. Keep your pack outside in a sheltered spot, and mind your head: what looks like a patch of rough ceiling may be sleepers hooked one into the next. Leave the dishes seated over their cracks. Lifting one both disturbs the creatures on its rim and uncovers the gas beneath it. Take nothing home, not even a pinch of apparently lifeless grit.

The old walls are something to look *toward*, not a place to seek shelter. Besides being at the hotter end, they do their most important work by standing just as they are. Don’t reset a slipped stone or help a young dish turn over. For the evening drift, a low-light camera set back from the notch and out of the cooling current is a better guest than a person blocking the opening. It also lets you leave the ridge in daylight rather than betting the descent on one white line that is about to disappear.

Give yourself something small to watch. Pick a nick in one rim and count the grazers that can fit their narrow heads into it. Listen for the two knocks, then look back at the nick. The pleasure of the place is that while you were listening, someone else may have made room there.
