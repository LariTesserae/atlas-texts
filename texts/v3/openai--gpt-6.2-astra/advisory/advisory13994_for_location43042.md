Settle the question of size before you go down to the willow. The fact that the hole will admit your wrist tells you nothing about whether it will admit *you*. If your means of travel can bring you there at the size of a resident, and light enough to walk on their pavement, arrange to arrive on the firm lip of the opening at low water. Arrange to regain your ordinary size well clear of the tree. If you cannot do that, visit from the bank. A snowy morning is your best chance of seeing the market from outside. Stand back far enough not to cut off the light reflected from the opposite bank, and don't put a finger in to see whether the floor really gives.

For a visit inside, dress for cold that comes through the soles of your feet. Take a close-fitting warm layer, a dry wrap in a closed pouch, your own drinking water, and very little else. You want soft, thin-soled shoes: enough between you and a wet street to keep your feet useful, but not so much that you can't feel a comb mark. Fasten everything close. A loose scarf trailing behind you could undo a considerable length of gutter before you felt the first tug.

Have someone meet you at the opening. Ask them to show you the marks with your own feet while there is still a little light. One groove leads down to water; three go up to the dry beds; a crosswise scoring closes the mouth of a blind alley. Learn what a *fading* mark feels like, too. There is a difference between a smooth stretch of street and a stretch you no longer know how to read. If you lose the way, stop and speak rather than feeling about with your hands. The pale seam that would make such a convenient guide along the curb may be eggs.

Give the market time. On a snow morning you might spend most of its silver half hour letting your eyes adjust, and see the pink of the floor clearly only just before it goes. Afterwards the shape of the place will come to you in other ways: the swing of your guide's steps at a knot, the pause while somebody brings a barrow round end first, the sudden little weight of cold against one ankle. Don't linger in a gutter because it happens to be empty. If a run cuts you off, wait on the street for it to pass. Don't be tempted to drink a bead from the wall or rinse anything in a settling yard. People will be picking through what comes down.

Follow the residents' lead about quiet. In particular, at the deep double knock from overhead, stand still with everyone else. Wait until work starts again before asking what it was. There is no need to spend the whole visit in fear of ducks, but this is a place where the people below and the animal above can feel one another through the same piece of wood.

If invited to see the seventh clearing, understand that you are being shown a hope, not a room. Let a worker indicate where you can stand without loading the reed frame. Don't tighten a wet binding, test the tip for spring, or slip anything between the green faces for a better look. A good question would be how they tell, from one week to the next, that the root has taken a little more of the curve. They may be able to show you a difference you would otherwise never see.

Agree on a time to leave while the river is still low; from inside, darkness won't tell you how late it is. Take out all you brought, and check the hems of your clothes with your guide before crossing back. The best thing to carry home is the memory of standing at the end of a proposed street, with its drains already laid and its pavement still to come, and learning that even the first hair of space in it was occupied before you got there.
