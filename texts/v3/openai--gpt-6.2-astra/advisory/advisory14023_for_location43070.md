**Plan the first visit as a stay on one ridge, not a journey through the city.** Arrange to arrive in daylight on broad, bare paving well back from a lip, and to leave from the same ridge. Your return should work even if no bridge forms that day. The thing you have come to see is visible perfectly well without crossing: for a few minutes you can look across a mile of air and see a crack in a stone as clearly as if you were about to put your hand on it.

Find out whether a human can breathe the ridge air before exposing yourself to it. The brown weather is a long way below and tells you little about the pressure where you will stand. If there are no reliable measurements, use independent breathing equipment. Even in breathable air, bring close-fitting goggles and a particulate respirator. Pack water, food, a shaded lamp, and enough insulation for an unexpected night, including something to put *between you and the paving*. Keep the load compact enough to turn around in a cart-wide street without swinging part of it over the edge. You want both hands free and nothing trailing.

On the first street, look at your next few footfalls before you look at the world. Favor the shelf side, without counting on a shelf full of loose chips to catch you. The lips are deposits of powder, not curbs. Stop on sound paving to enjoy the view; looking into that much depth while walking in a crosswind asks rather a lot of an ordinary human sense of balance. Leave the drifts alone. It would be very tempting to make one of those beautiful upright columns just to see it happen. You would still have to get your eyes open and find your way back to firm stone.

Watch the midday bridge from several paces back. **The animal's crossing is evidence of a passage the size and shape of the animal.** It is not evidence of a passage for a person. The near half of the abutment can be within reach while the other half is still a speck; you have no assurance that the space at shoulder height agrees with the space at your boots. Don't test it with a finger. If traveling with someone, agree beforehand that neither of you will follow the animal, so the decision doesn't have to be made in the few astonishing seconds when stepping across seems the most natural thing in the world.

Give as much thought to what may arrive *above* you as to what may disappear underfoot. A blind wall in the lee of the wind looks like a good lunch stop. A shallow saddle looks like a place intended for your hips. Neither was made with you in mind. Before lingering anywhere, look for polished undersides, fresh violet scars, stone powdered even under cover. Those are records of contact. The grit you hear behind a closed wall is reason enough not to count the room as shelter. Watch the evening square from outside the flight of steps. The warm corner belongs to creatures that can spend the rest of the day inside the pores of a rock; don't brush the wall clean for a better look at them.

For a longer stay, first observe a proposed resting place through a complete cycle from elsewhere, and retain a means of leaving if the next cycle differs. A grazing track overhead can be years old. Yesterday's clearances are useful knowledge, not a lease. Take photographs rather than making marks, and frame them widely enough to include the ridge you are standing on: a distant street may move, and a familiar bend occurs almost everywhere.

If the little animal is in the sheltered seat when you return, leave it the seat. Of all the apparent amenities of the city, those six hollows are the one place where you can be fairly sure you know who uses it, for what, and how well it fits.
