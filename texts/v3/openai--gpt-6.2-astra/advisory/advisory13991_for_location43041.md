Go down to the flats before you go anywhere near the steps. From above, the most natural thing in the world would be to put a hand on the bottom step and bend for a better look. From below, you can see whose ceiling you would have put your weight on.

If you are going in at your ordinary size, prepare for a visit spent almost entirely on the salt. Take a warm layer, drinking water, a pad to sit on, and, if you need it, a little glass for seeing detail at a distance. Find a patch of dry ground from which you can see the opening without coming between it and the bright flats. Sit down slowly. Watch the light at the back of the ward as you settle; if a doorway goes dim, move aside. A lens will let you see the two worn places in a shell sill. It needn't be trained on the people sitting there.

Don't put your face up to the opening to say hello. Even the breath you take before speaking would be quite a wind to someone carrying a currant. Rest a hand where it can be seen, well out on the salt, and wait to be noticed. Someone may come to the rail; someone may raise a hand without putting down their sewing. That's a greeting, and may be the whole visit. Don't go round to the top afterward and try the crack with a twig.

If your means of travel lets you arrive small, arrange your size *before* approaching, and arrange to recover it well clear of the masonry. Come in by a way someone shows you. Wear warm, close-fitting clothes, soft soles, and nothing tall on your back. The clearance at the garden crossings was measured for a cart, not a stranger with a bedroll tied on end. Bring your own water in a stoppered flask. What looks like a bit of old yarn trailing from a jar is part of the water supply; take care not to catch it under your heel. And don't offer to fill a jar from a full-sized bottle. Water is scarce there, but the trouble they have gone to with the wool should tell you something about the force of a single drop.

You won't need to tiptoe theatrically. People live and work here; it's just that they have found ways to do both without making much noise. Let someone come close enough to talk rather than calling down a street. Step over the lizards where everyone else does. At the seventh tread, sit on the seat. Don't calculate how much sooner the tail would move if you gave it a little nudge. If you have only an hour before you must go home, spend it there and miss the stair.

Leave some time, if you can, for the place beyond the last fork of the currant rail. Ask before joining anyone there. It may take a while for your eyes, accustomed by then to picking out moths in a gable and roots in a net, to stop looking for another small thing to examine. That is a good moment to turn around. The people who left that bit unbuilt have given you a view in the other direction: from under a market step, the whole sky.
