Arrange to be met at the reeds, not at an address. Someone who lives here knows where a lane cord comes up within reach of the lip, and which of the bells below it is a public room. Neither fact is obvious from above. Go in daylight. The ranges would be worth a long look, but take it sitting down, well back from the outside edge. A wet apron of new white stone offers nothing to catch hold of if you slip.

The useful measure of fitness here is not how long you can hold your breath in a bath. It is whether you can swim a short stretch, find a dark opening, and turn back calmly if it isn't the opening you wanted. Practice in ordinary water before you go. Take a well-fitting mask, soft grippy water shoes, and a close-fitting bag without trailing straps. Keep a towel and warm, windproof clothes in a dry bag at the rim. Ask about the elevation before committing to the swim; a climb that leaves you short of breath calls for a rest on land, not a dive. If you aren't a comfortable swimmer, make the lip and the reeds your visit. There is a great deal to see from there.

For a first outing, choose one occupied room on the uppermost street and visit it with your escort. Before going under, have them show you the signal for “back to the surface.” Slip in rather than jump. Use the lane cord lightly, and keep track of the open water above you. A cane under a rail is a water main's equivalent here, not a second handhold. If you hear the little sucking noise of a bad join, remember where it was and tell an air keeper; don't pull at it to show your companion.

At the house, let your host demonstrate the knock. Slap the water floor from below and wait to be welcomed. If there's a delay, go up *outside* for a breath rather than waiting politely until you have to barge in. Once on the shelf, ask where to put your shoes and whether there's air enough for you to stay awhile. At the end of a long cane run it may take some time to replace what an unexpected guest breathes. Don't light anything. In particular, don't take the sight of a dry ceiling in an abandoned house as a promise of breathable air. The old quarter is somewhere to hear stories about on a shelf in the new one.

Bring drinking water of your own; clear, warm pool water needn't be potable. If you hope to stay overnight, arrange the bed beforehand. What your host is offering is not chiefly the width of the shelf but a night's share of the air. A small gift that will stand immersion would travel better than anything wrapped in paper.

Leave time for a slow visit. The plait running through the meadow may be one family's way to work; ask before following it. If everyone stops and looks down through the leaves, stop with them rather than swimming closer. It may be years before that back passes under them again. And if a couple takes you to feel the sill of a house that isn't there yet, let them guide your fingers. The thing to feel is the place where yesterday's weave has become today's stone, not how deep a mark your thumb can make in it.
