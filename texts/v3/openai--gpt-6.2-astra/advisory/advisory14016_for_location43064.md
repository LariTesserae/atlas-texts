Arrange to arrive at the notch already the size of someone who lives below. The test is not whether you can squeeze through the opening; an ant managed that. You want to be able to walk down the seven steps, put both feet on the landing, and turn right with your pack on. Make sure you can leave at that size, too. Stay small until you are well clear of the weighhouse stair. If your means of travel doesn't allow for that, find a fair place in the square to lie on your stomach and enjoy the view. Don't try the hollow step with your heel.

Bring drinking water in a soft, well-stoppered flask scaled with you. There is nothing to fill it from in the gardens, and the handsome green leaves are no better evidence of water than the green hinges are. Bring a little food that won't shed crumbs. Check the folds of your pack before you go down: what counts as a remarkably small hitchhiker in the square counts as an animal whose fall people will draw pictures of for generations below. Wear soles that grip polished stone, and stow things in cloth rather than hanging them from rigid clips. The townspeople have had ample time to discover what works loose here.

The piece of equipment I'd least like to forget is well-fitted hearing protection. Put it on before the descent. You won't necessarily get the warning of a cart coming up behind you; you'll be inside the thing it makes a noise *of*. Even with your ears protected, expect to feel the blows through your feet. If the first one catches you on the stairs, stop and steady yourself rather than trying to hurry down through the noise. Arrange a few hand signals with your companion in advance, especially “wait here” and “I want to go back.” At market, watch a transaction before attempting one. You can learn quite a bit about the price of something by watching fingers and baskets, and very little by shouting.

Take the town flight first. Find someone willing to show you the gardens before you take the other one. There are places where the distinction between a path and a livelihood might not be apparent to a stranger, particularly at noon, when a misplaced boot could press a root into the soft seam. Don't test the mineral with your thumb or cut a stalk to see how fast it closes. If you'd like something to take home, ask whether anyone sells the plaited grass. Having seen a gate swinging on it will make a short length of that plait a considerably better keepsake than a wilted fan.

Allow plenty of time for what looks from outside like almost no distance at all. The bends around the fossil are real bends; the high openings in the house fronts are not shortcuts to the upper streets. And resist the feeling, looking across the saucer, that the bright flake is the one obvious destination for a traveler. The bare slope already does something rather wonderful without anybody going out on it. Stand in a town doorway with permission and hold out your hand: the light by which you can see the lines in your palm has come off all that empty stone.

Ask a resident when to start back if you want light on the divided stair. Carry a small shaded lamp for the return, so that the instant the sun leaves the tread needn't be the instant you have to go. I'd spend the last of the day near the town end of the garden road, out of the paddlers' way. After hours of being unable to hear your own voice, there's a temptation to use the first quiet to say something. Leave a little of it unused. It may be your only chance to hear what a gate with a grass hinge sounds like when somebody comes home.
