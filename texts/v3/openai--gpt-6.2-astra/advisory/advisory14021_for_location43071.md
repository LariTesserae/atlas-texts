I would arrange to arrive on a dry ridge within sight of the way out, and make the first visit on foot, with one companion and no intention of spending the night. Half a mile along that ribbon of water sounds like an easy walk until you have to get back. From the far side of the cane stand you may be able to see precisely where you want to go and still have no way of getting your shoulders there. Go around while you can still see the near end. If going around means trampling a wood flat, let that be the end of the walk.

Wear clean, soft-soled shoes with enough tread for a wet root, and light clothes that cover your skin. Take your own water, a small lamp, a watch, and a means of returning that does not depend on finding a patch of bare earth. If your crossing equipment can test the air before you commit to breathing it, use it. The lizard is a recommendation for the silvery ground cover, not a guarantee about the air. Once that is settled, the thing to watch in yourself is heat. The light will feel like shade, but there is no promise of a breeze anywhere in the valley. Drink and turn back by the watch rather than waiting for the light to change. Neither the salt overhead nor the drops coming off the leaves belong in your canteen.

Take a picture looking back at the crossing before you leave it. Include the roof: a fork in a bright seam above a particular stand of canes will be more useful than a hundred nearly identical ridges at your feet. Look back again at each turn. Don't tie things to stems, cut blazes or test the crust with a stick. What feels like the soft verge of a path is the surface of a living thing. It may take your weight beautifully; that doesn't mean it will take a tent peg. Walk where the ridges are broad and firm, give the pale shoot buds room, and resist the very considerable temptation to take your shoes off in the root hairs. Stay out from under loose-looking crystal, and don't follow the green writing into the narrowing space at the rim. A lovely view of the end of the country is quite close enough.

I would stop at the gap in one of the enclosures. It would be easy, especially when hot and tired, to decide that anything so exactly fitted to a human body had been made for one's own use. But a gap wide enough for shoulders tells you something about the shoulders of the maker, not about your welcome. Leave the silver cover undented and the lizard its whole afternoon. Bring nothing in on muddy soles; take no cutting, pinch of salt or handful of the soft stuff home. Pack out even the water you used to rinse your hands.

Allow some time in the middle of the visit simply to stand still. At first you will hear a drop fall and wait for the next drop. Stay a little longer and you may hear the much smaller sound of *the same water* arriving farther down. That seems to me worth the journey: to come away knowing the shape of a plant overhead by ear. You needn't settle whether the valley is a day old or has held that pose for centuries. With a little care, the person who comes after you can have the pleasure of not knowing either.
