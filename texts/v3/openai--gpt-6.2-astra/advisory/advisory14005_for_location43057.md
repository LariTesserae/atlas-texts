I would make the first visit from the lower summit, in full daylight, with the few yards of wet stone between me and the water left uncrossed. Bring binoculars. What you will want a better look at is small: the white roots of the rushes, the rim of a limpet, the bead of air in the bitten place on the leaf. You need not get within arm’s reach of the lake to see those things.

If anyone in the valley goes up there now, ask them to show you where they stand and how they get back down. Ask about the mist in particular. Go with someone, and leave your route and a return time with someone else. Wear boots with tough soles and good wet-rock grip, gloves you can scramble in, and a waterproof outer layer over something warm. Carry a little first-aid kit with dressings for cuts, and your own drinking water. Do not count on catching your weight with a bare palm. On an ordinary mountain a handful of loose stones is poor support; here a perfectly firm handhold may be worse.

Put hearing protection on before you enter the cleft. A useful companion here is one whose sleeve you can see, not one whose voice you know well. Agree on a few gestures down in the quiet: *stop there*, *look at me*, *we’re going back*. Make the last one a decision either person can take without discussion. When the first deep blow comes, you may feel it despite the ear protection and turn to ask whether your companion felt it too. It is worth choosing a place to stand where you can turn round without first having to look after your feet.

Spend some time watching the **stone**, rather than the face of the water. It is the only way to notice how far apart two places are that get drenched a moment apart. Don’t take the farthest splash you have seen as a limit, or assume that standing uphill of a wet patch puts you out of its way. If the only footing you can reach for a better view involves a slab shifting under your boot, settle for the poorer view. A hollow with daylight at the back of it is particularly hard to see for what it is. By the time you have recognized its floor as a place you could stand, you have used up part of the time it will be dry.

Leave the landing to the people who know it. The smooth notch is beautiful evidence of a very precise piece of knowledge: someone knew not just where to kneel, but when to have their knee there already. Seeing the three walls resolve into a landing is not the same as acquiring that knowledge. Leave the pot at home. Leave the limpets under their film of water and the rushes in their seam; neither the long roots nor the neatly laid courses are handholds.

Set your time to turn back while you can still see the descent. The clear band where warm water meets evening mist may give you a view all the way to the flowering trees without giving you a view of the next six feet of path. If it matters to you to watch the leaf come round at evening, arrange another visit with someone who knows a safe way to stay late. On the first one I would take the sight of it turning over, far back in the brown-green water, and go down content. If it does come free while you are there, there is no need to rescue it. Of all the things in that cleft, the leaf has found a place to dry.
