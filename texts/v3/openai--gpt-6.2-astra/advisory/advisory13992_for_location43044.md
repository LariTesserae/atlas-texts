Plan to spend the better part of a day on the landing. Ask at the works whether visitors are allowed up the tower and when the stair has to be clear for the pump crew. Wear shoes with a good grip and a layer you can sit still in. The warmth you see through the round window is on the other side of the door. A pocket notebook and a dark scarf are worth bringing; held beside your cheek, the scarf will cut the reflection of the stair in the glass.

The three enamel plates are old directions. Don’t try the latch beneath them, even if you can see the yellow rail perfectly well and it looks an easy walk to the windows. There is water against the door all the way up to the riveted roof. Beyond it are sharp ceramic edges, curtains of roots, and presses that are still connected to the pumps. The ten days in April when the motor is off are an inspection interval, not an opening season. For an ordinary traveler the round window is the place to visit, not an obstacle on the way there.

Go up in the morning. Find the broken yellow leaf first and sketch its angle against something fixed, perhaps a corner of the lowest step. Then give someone else a turn at the glass. It’s worth having that drawing when you come back: the leaf may have lain down in your absence, and the difference between the two views will give you a better feel for the water than watching hard for a minute and seeing nothing move. At noon look for the blue lines of the window wire spread across the white treads. If you can manage only one visit, choose about two, when the light comes in under the broad leaves. Look into the mouth of the oilcan then. It would be easy to go home remembering a room full of one enormous crop and never know about the little brown fans.

If you particularly want to hear the press, ask the crew which day it’s due. Otherwise I wouldn’t choose a date on that account. Nineteen days is a long time to arrange a journey around a tick, and there are things you can see on any afternoon that the factory took years to do. Follow one of the old sheets from its brass clip down to where its root fringe meets the next. That was once a finished article, ready to be carried downstairs and laid over somebody’s skylight.

Leave a little time for the cloud to lift. From up there the station roof is small enough that you may need someone who knows the town to point it out. Find the pane that doesn’t quite match the others. Then, on your way home, if the station is open, go in and stand beneath it. After a day spent looking through a window at the place where they made shade, it would be good to see what the light is like on the receiving end.
