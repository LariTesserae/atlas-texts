Arrange to meet someone on the bank. A person standing on the rim may be only a few yards from the common room and still have no way to hail anyone in it. Agree on the place and the hour beforehand, and choose a way in well away from the western lip. Leave dry clothes, a windproof layer and something warm to drink there. It will be good to have someone on the bank when you come out, too.

**The rooms contain water, not air.** If you have access to a breathing adaptation, try it in cold fresh water before you go, and find out how much warning you get before it wears off. Being able to breathe there need not mean being able to keep warm there. Otherwise, visit as a properly equipped cold-water diver, with a qualified dive buddy, an altitude-appropriate dive plan and a conservative reserve of gas. Fasten hoses and gauges close so they don’t trail through the willow. Your town guide may know every root in the tarn without knowing anything about getting an air-breather safely to the surface. If you aren’t trained for the dive, ask to meet in the shallows in summer. There is quite a lot of the town to be understood by looking along a single sheet of roots.

Learn a handful of signs on shore, with your gloves on: *yes, no, wait, I’m cold, I need to go up,* and *thank you*. Agree on an attention signal that doesn’t depend on either of you hearing it. A guide who has swum a little way ahead and turned to look back may be perfectly visible to you in the sun while you have all but vanished against the dark weed. For a first visit, stay alongside. A private word spoken against the bone below the ear is a lovely prospect, but you may have a hood in the way; don’t take it or your breathing gear off to be polite.

Ask to be shown a street before anybody takes you on a shortcut. You can learn the swelling and the ribs and the fork by touch, and it is worth knowing the way home even in daylight. Swim just clear of the rail rather than pulling yourself along it over the shrimps. Keep your fins up, use small strokes, and let the hurdle carriers have the width they need. Stay in open water with a clear ascent above you; an invitation to a common room can quite reasonably be answered by visiting at the sill. Don’t plan to sleep in a drift net. For a human visitor it is an entanglement, not a bed.

Bring gear that has been cleaned, decontaminated for freshwater use, and dried before arrival. A scrap of weed in a buckle is a considerable import into a world the size of this tarn. Take nothing living away, and don’t tidy anything. A fork of antler that looks to have worked loose may be holding the shape of somebody’s future wall. The pike under the northern sill is doing a job by lying still; give it room to keep doing it.

Leave the western court out of the itinerary this year. The open doors don’t mean that the householders have gone out for the afternoon. They mean that, for once, nothing that grows across a doorway has to move. Besides, the polished shelf is at a spillway with a forty-foot fall. Ask your guide where the draw of the outlet begins and stay well back on the town side of it. There will be other years to visit the west rooms.

Save some of your visit for staying in one place. Ask where you may rest a gloved hand on the wood and feel the note of the falls. Wait for your own bubbles to go up. At first you may see only a dark sill with a pale scratch along it; after a little while you may see the snail at the end of the scratch, making it longer. That is a good use of the time. There is no need to see all seven courts. Nobody who lives here expects to have all seven at once.
