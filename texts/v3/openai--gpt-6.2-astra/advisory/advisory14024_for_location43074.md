Bring a folding mat, a hand lens, and something warm enough to lie still in. The mat is for your hip and elbow, not for the ground over the room. Settle yourself downhill of the peeled turf, with your weight well back from its lip. It is worth finding a comfortable position before you look in. There are things here that take longer to happen than it takes an elbow to go numb.

Unless you have a reliable means of becoming small, plan to visit from the sill. This is not much of a deprivation. With one eye level with the clay you can see all the way to the little shell lintel. Breathe sideways, back into the field. An ordinary sigh directed into the opening would be weather in there. Take off any scarf with a fringe, particularly if you mean to stay until evening. You do not want to sit up and discover that the last few minutes of daylight are coming away with you.

Go first while there is still some sun on the track. Find the red mite, then look *past* it; otherwise the brightness of the far wall can hold your eye for most of the visit. The dark marks behind its feet will show you what is newly firm. Leave your lamp off. Nobody knows from looking whether lamplight sets too, and there is little enough space between the walls already.

A good place to rest your attention is the underside of a root. Pick a bead that is a little fuller than its neighbors and stay with it. When it falls, resist the impulse to move the lens closer. The interesting part is at the foot of the water, and in the time it takes to get a better view you can miss the coming out of a whole population. Nor is there any need to hurry things along with a drop from your bottle. What looks like a rather dry floor has tenants, even in the stretches between drinks.

If your manner of travel does let you enter at the mite’s scale, keep the opening in sight and make your return while the sun is still on it. Walk on dry clay, clear of the line beneath either root. A bead that looks handsome from the track is a considerable body of water when it is standing beside you. Give the chalk chips and the small holes a wide berth. At the shell doorway remember that the carter comes out backwards, cannot see you, and has a load nearly the width of the opening. Wait off to one side. Do not steady the lintel unless asked; its tick may be the only notice anyone on this side gets of the next load.

Watch the evening from outside. This is when a place that has seemed to consist of separate little occupations briefly shows its paths. Follow one bright strand with your eye rather than trying to keep account of them all. The one that goes between the seed leaves is easy to lose and worth finding. When the light has hardened, put the lens away; nothing is gained by peeling off a sample of the wall.

Before getting up, have one last look at the track itself. After an afternoon spent waiting for the smallest visible things to move, a grain rolling downhill is quite a sight. Leave the turf at the angle you found it. The impulse to tuck it neatly back is a kind one, but that untidy corner is where tomorrow gets in.
