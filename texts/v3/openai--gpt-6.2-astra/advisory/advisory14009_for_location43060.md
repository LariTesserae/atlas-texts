Plan for a visit to a desert ledge, not a journey into a cave. The useful place to stand is on firm ground just outside the saltbush, where you can see under the lip without putting your head beneath it. Arrange a route back and a check-in time before leaving for the ledge. Bring water for the return as well as the approach, a hat, sun cover, and shoes with a good grip on sand and stone. If you can choose your hour, come in the cooler part of the day. Don’t save the hollow as your place to recover from the heat: the slab overhead is warm, and there is scarcely a breath of air beneath it.

Give your eyes time. Stand facing into the shade for a few minutes without looking back across the bright plain. At first you might take the thin brown line near the mouth for a whole stone rather than the one edge of a stone that catches light. Watch for the point of bedrock on the left; once you can make it out, you may begin to see the rise of sand around it. A small adjustable lamp is worth bringing. If you need it, shine it low across the floor from outside. There is no need to walk in to find out where the floor rises or the roof drops.

Leave the crust untested. A grain striking it is about the lightest touch it gets; a boot would break the skin and leave a mark where little wind comes to smooth it out. Keep clear of the branches, too. There is no reason to crawl toward the reddish patch. The matching band above it tells you that the roof still sheds stone, and the dark gap that looks like a way farther in is, at the back, barely wide enough for a hand.

Allow yourself a few quiet minutes at the mouth. You may hear one grain land, or you may hear nothing. From there you can feel wind on your back while looking at a curl of salt so frail it could hardly have lasted out on the plain. That short distance is worth coming to see.
