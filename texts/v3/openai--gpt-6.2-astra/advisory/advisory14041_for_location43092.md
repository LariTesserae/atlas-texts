I would go in the open season, with a companion and a shallow-draft boat for two, and make my first excursion from a landing I had already looked at on both the rise and the fall of the tide. I would plan to sleep ashore. The old banks are just where, after a few hours in a boat, you will badly want a bank to be. Not one of them promises a place to stand up and get dry.

Take paddles and a light, straight sounding pole marked in handbreadths. Learn its feel near the landing, where you can see the bottom. The first resistance is leaf, and it can be remarkably convincing; the shoe may have a long, slanting journey yet to make before it knocks. Use the pole to feel ahead, not to put your weight on. A cut can begin in one boat length, and a stone sill can bring the floor back just as abruptly. A propeller would be a nuisance in the long beds. I would wear flotation and dress for immersion, however pleasant the air was, and carry drinking water of my own. A sweet-water catchment on an old plan is a piece of history, not a promise about the water in your flask.

Spend the first tide watching a gate opening from beside it. Look at the set of a blade there and then at the mud only a few yards away. You can learn more about getting a boat home from those two observations than from a view across a square mile of smooth surface. Note when the stream eases *at that opening*. Do not commit yourself to a passage on the assumption that, because the level has nearly stopped rising, the water has nearly stopped moving. Carry a compass, a waterproof sketch of the way back, and a watch. Draw the little correcting bends in the banks. If you draw the country as a neat grid, you will erase some of the few marks by which you can tell one pan from its neighbour. Allow time to return the long way rather than fight a gate stream or wait for it in the dark.

I would not choose winter for a first visit. Shore-to-shore ice sounds like the restoration of the land, but the gates are still working underneath it, and snow conceals both the cuts and the new cracks. The dark and light rectangles tell you something about the light reaching the leaves, not what weight the ice will carry. Without a locally checked route and people equipped for ice rescue, watch from solid shore.

For the visit itself, the one extra thing I would pack is a viewing box with a clear bottom. Rest it on the surface from the boat at the turn of the tide. There is no need to rake up a sample to see how the place was made; a willow stem with bark still on it is better left holding its few inches of country together. Nor would I spend the clear interval looking for fish in the deep cut. Watch the edge instead, where the leaves stop and a whole fall’s growth lies folded below.

Then put the box away while there is still a little swell. Choose a place with long grass on one side and short grass on the other, and give it a few minutes. The upward movement will run along the lost ditch and falter on the crown. That is about the best view you are likely to get of the old coast: from the height of a seated person, for the length of a wave.
