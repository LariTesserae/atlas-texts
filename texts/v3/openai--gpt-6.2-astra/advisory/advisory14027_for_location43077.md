I would arrange to be met at the upper goods platform. Ask for the name of the person who is coming and which stair they will come up, rather than the number of the house where you are staying. On a damp afternoon there may be two perfectly legible numbers at that address, and one of them belongs to a house that is gone. While you wait, have a good look at the valley. This may be your best chance to understand its shape, though it will do surprisingly little to help you find your way about.

Wear warm layers, thin gloves you can grip a rail through, and shoes with soft, well-treaded soles. Keep your luggage light enough to carry in one hand on the industrial stairs. When a cloud comes up under the town, the footing can change before you feel a drop on your face. At the corners, take a scrap of felt from the cup and fold it over the rail. The old people have worked out how to get old here; there is no particular merit in acquiring the young people's hands.

Bring drinking water. The silver thing with bridges over it is a provision for a river, not a river, and the little bit of water under one bridge is already doing a great deal of work. If you visit the pool, sit a little way back from the tin edge. It takes a while to see that the birds aren't merely bobbing their heads. Follow one drink round the circle. While you're sitting still, look up through the bridge as well as down: some of what seems to be daylight coming through the iron is daylight coming through a moth.

For getting about, ask two questions: “How do you go today?” and “How do you come back if the washing's in?” Learn at least one way home by the public stairs. A balcony close enough to take a spool from is not necessarily a balcony you can return to after supper. Don't untie a line to get a tall bag past it, or offer to take down a neighbour's washing as a surprise kindness. If the route to the baker's does take you through somebody's bedroom, pause at the threshold and let your companion introduce you. Being on the way to somewhere else doesn't make it any less a bedroom.

You needn't brace yourself for a great racket from the works. You may find yourself speaking softly there, simply because someone quite far away can answer in an ordinary voice. If the tea leans in its glass, leave it on the table a moment and watch what the others do. To them it is an engine coming into engagement, not the beginning of a fall. The converse is worth remembering on the eastern roof: just because you can't hear six acres of town being lowered doesn't mean the work has stopped. Leave the straps to the crews and the broom work to people who know the roof.

Above all, don't plan the visit around seeing the two streets meet. Eleven years is a long time to describe a place as nearly ready. Ask when the light is good on the underside of the school, and go then. You can stand beside the little felt-bound step, both feet on the finished street, and see where the children have polished the copper. A woman may still be catching the sun above you after you've buttoned your coat against the evening. She's not waiting to move in. She lives there already.
