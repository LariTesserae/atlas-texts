Arrange to see the field with the works attendant, from the inspection hatch. There is standing room below, but standing room is not the same as a place to put your feet. The surveyor found three places for the legs of a stool. You needn't try to improve on that.

Find out where you may stand while the works are running. Have your hearing protection fitted before the hatch opens, and agree on a hand signal for “I've had enough”; you won't want to take a plug out to explain. Keep loose sleeves, straps and spectacles secured, and keep your weight on the works side of the sill. The pleasant smell of warm oil tells you nothing about whether the air below is fit to breathe. If the hatch offers a poor view that day, accept the poor view rather than leaning in for a better one.

A small close-focusing glass is worth bringing. Let your eyes get used to the caged light first. What looks at first like a bright line drawn across a black gap may be the only visible part of a whole slab. Turn a little and the line may disappear; the edge hasn't gone anywhere. Look for the curl of blue cotton. It is a better warning about reaching in than any notice the foreman could put up. Leave the lighting to the attendant, and leave the rule and test pencil upstairs. There's nothing to be learned by lifting another animal that the electrician hasn't learned already.

Try spending most of the visit looking at one hand's breadth of stone. At first you'll hear tremendous activity and see almost none, which can make you feel you're looking in the wrong place. Pick out a smoke-brown lens and watch its rim rather than its body. Then look for a milky one on the underside of the same slab. If the angle is good, you may find the two colours meeting at the edge. Even a patch that seems merely dusty is worth the glass. There is no bare spot down there on which you could safely rest a fingertip, either for your sake or for the sake of what lives under it.

Make it a short visit. Hearing protection won't altogether stop the feeling in your teeth. For a last look, sight across to the surveyor's bob. You don't have to wait for it to do anything: the thing to see is the thread of orange *under* the brass point. Then let the attendant close the hatch. If you go up to the canteen afterward, sit still a moment before you move your chair. You'll know now what that little sound in its legs is.
