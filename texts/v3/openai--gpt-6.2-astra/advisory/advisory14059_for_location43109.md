For a first visit to Olt, arrange to arrive at an oven court, and arrange to leave from that same court if you possibly can. Ask for a bed there. “In Olt” is not a sufficient address. Two people can live on the same street, use the same mark to warn their neighbours of a loose paving stone, and never in their lives have had occasion to meet.

Pack for the shady side of the oven. You want wool, a windproof outer layer, gloves you can put on with cold fingers, and boots with a good grip on wet stone. Carry dry socks, drinking water and a day’s bread even for a walk within the city. Take something to protect your eyes and skin from the sun; the pleasant warmth of it is a poor measure of exposure. Give yourself an easy first day to find out how well you breathe. A level walk at great height can be hard work without ever offering the satisfaction of a view down. If altitude is a problem for you, establish before you come that you can leave by the way you arrived. The southern edge is not a practical route to lower ground.

I would spend the first wet morning at a bridge, in the company of somebody who uses it. Go while the bed is still dry. Have them show you the white notch and what the pebble looks like against it from where you stand. A leaning line is about half an hour’s notice, not half an hour in which you are invited to potter about under the bridge. A true line farther along does not cancel the warning at the first. The woman with the seedlings knows something much more particular than “there will be a flood today.” She knows which bit of grit will get wet. Stay on the paving and let her have the channel to herself.

The great temptation will be to cross just to see the laundry. It is hard to imagine, looking at a shirt pegged out to dry, that the bank on which it hangs is any less accessible than the one you are standing on. Before you set out, ask yourself where you will sleep **on that bank**. Take your bread, your warm clothes, any medicine you depend on, and the name of somebody who expects you. Let your host know where you meant to go. If you have a means of returning to your own world, be quite sure whether it travels with you or is waiting at the oven court. Ankle-deep water is quite enough to change the journey home. There need be no dangerous current, no damage to the bridge, no particular mishap at all. The far end need only cease to be there.

For ordinary dry travel, get directions in walking time, not street names. Learn the paving marks. Ask which court has water and which merely has a conduit passing beneath it. Do not count on buying supper at the next oven unless you know when you will reach it. And do not take a great empty yard for waste ground where nobody will mind what you do. Somebody has been keeping those slabs level, possibly for longer than anybody remembers what level slabs were needed there for.

Come with clean boots and cuffs, and ask before going from one planted court into another. Here a sleeve does some of the work a bee would do elsewhere. Don’t introduce insects, seeds or a pet in the hope of supplying something Olt appears to lack. If you are invited to help with the flour flowers, take the other end of the rope and let your partner set the pace. The bare feet are not ceremonial. You are meant to notice a stem before you put your weight on it.

Finally, leave yourself a morning with nothing to accomplish. Sit on the warm side of a court and listen until you can distinguish somebody setting a kerb from somebody shaking out a shirt. At first those sounds may make the place feel terribly empty. After a while they are company. If the river comes, you can watch a different afternoon for a little; if it doesn’t, there is still a loaf making its way home over the roofs, and it is worth being there to see whose hands reach up for it next.
