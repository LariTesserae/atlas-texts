Pack for a long walk, even if all you mean to do is visit someone in town. From the road, the white parapet makes a good landmark for the climb; once you are up among the houses, it tells you rather little about how far you still have to go. Ask which court you want and where to turn. A host may be able to stand outside and wave to you quite some time before you can shake hands.

Wear shoes you trust on both rough stone and wet pavement. A sole should be thick enough that you can put your weight on a sharp corner, but flexible enough to feel the little footholds cut into a slope. Take a spare pair of socks, something against the sun, and a layer for when you stop climbing. Pack lightly enough to carry everything yourself. There is no sign of a way to send a bag on ahead, and it would be a poor introduction to have someone carry a second load across town because you mistook the last stretch for a short one.

Stay on the paved route, especially where it seems to take a foolish turn. The clear sheet of water between you and the next group of roofs may be only an inch deep, but that does not tell you how wide the next joint in the rock is, or what footing you will find when you step down. Nor is a patch of green necessarily solid ground. There is a place here where what looks from the street like a low bush is the crown of a tree, with room for its whole trunk beneath it. Allow time to go the long way. Some of the bends that inconvenience a visitor save a reed cutter a hard carry every working day.

At the first basin, ask someone to show you where to fill your bottle and where to wash your hands. It should take only a moment, and you will know what to look for at the next court. The water has several uses in a particular order; its clarity will not distinguish them for you. Bring any water treatment you ordinarily need when traveling between worlds. Keep grit, soap, and the soles of your shoes out of the drinking branch. Leave the slotted stones alone, however tempting it is to put a finger across a slot to see where the water goes.

Arrange a meeting by *place*, not just by sight: “at the basin in your court” is better than “when I see you by the wall.” Take a small light if you expect to walk back late. The high slabs hold daylight after the valley has lost it, but you want enough light for the notches at your feet, not merely enough to make out the roof you are going toward.

Finally, leave an afternoon unplanned. Ask if there is room beside someone doing their mending, and sit down. The stone will still be warm. If you are used to judging a town by traffic and noise, it may take a while to notice how much is going on: a bowl touching stone out of sight, someone parting the reeds, a child on the far pavement who disappears behind a grove and comes out just where you thought. Stay long enough to see the child reach the next court. By then you will have a much better idea of what your host means by “near.”
