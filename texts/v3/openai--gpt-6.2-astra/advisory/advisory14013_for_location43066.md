Plan to visit the lip, not the blind end. At human size the country under the flake is a place to look into from the trail. If your means of travel lets you go small, make your first arrival on bare rock *outside* the opening, in daylight. You want to see how much room there is before you put yourself and all your luggage into it.

For the walk up, dress for a windy, snow-bearing pass and bring your own water, a hand lens, and a light for the descent. The black brow may be bare when the approaches are under snow. Don't count on the vent for shelter: its warmth is quite sufficient for a fly and of almost no use to a walker. Look for the white patch from the trail, then approach from the side so that you don't put a boot on the roof. Don't lift the flake for a better look. It is still joined to the mountain at one end; you couldn't put it back the way it was.

Late afternoon offers the best visit. Come while the mill is running and stay until it stops, provided you have enough light left to get down. The tingling through your sole is worth attending to: it gives you some idea of what morning is like for the animals beneath it. Watch the salt at the lip. A grain that seems to dance of its own accord and then suddenly settles will tell you when the machinery on the far side of the mountain has gone quiet.

If you do go in small, leave the pack outside. Take sealed water, a dim light, and clothes that let you lie flat without anything catching overhead. Stay well short of any gap you can enter only by exhaling. The matching knobs and sockets make the floor look like a puzzle you could find a way through, but the narrowing rooms have no other exit. Keep the blade of daylight in sight. Avoid the loose, chattering crystal, the fresh glass points, and the spider's three taut lines; the apparently generous headroom by the spider is already occupied. Don't brush against a dry fly for balance. What looks like a bit of litter may be a whole afternoon's work for the mites on it.

Check the warmth underfoot as the sun moves. At noon the danger is being too near the seam, not too far from it. If the beetles next to you begin to shift, ease out ahead of them rather than waiting to see where they go. A half-hour is a leisurely pace for the crowd and a long time to spend pinned by it. Don't offer them a drop of water: there is nowhere here that a drop belongs, and at their size it is a considerable object.

For the last part of the visit, sit still just outside. After the roar cuts off it may take a little while to notice that the place isn't silent. There is the dry rub of one shell on another, perhaps a single snip from above, and all the while the cold edge of the chamber is moving inward. Leave before the stripe of light goes. The beetles know exactly where to spend the night; you should already have picked somewhere else.
