I would arrange to arrive at the western lip shortly after daylight, with a return passage that stays open there, and make the lip itself the first destination. It needn’t be a poor second to a walk across the top. From there you can watch a snowstorm in profile and see the whole course of a drainage working. Down in one of the hollows you might hear nothing but your own particular *ka-lung* and have no idea what an extraordinary thing you were standing in the middle of.

Prepare for a cold day spent mostly standing still: warm layers under a windproof, waterproof shell, gloves you can work binoculars in, and boots with a good wet-weather tread. Carry a dry change sealed against immersion, your own water, and enough food to wait out a spell of bad visibility. A clear sky over the point of arrival is not a forecast for the far side. Bring a companion, and mark the return point with a beacon you can home on without seeing it. There are almost no landmarks at human scale, and the most conspicuous one on the skyline may presently fold its legs and go fishing.

**Make sure of your footing at the arrival point before committing your weight to the mountain.** A leaf as thick as a shoulder is not necessarily a floor; it may have water moving freely beneath it and another leaf beneath that. Nothing in the account establishes a reliable test for how much weight the mat will carry. If the crossing can set you down on verified rock or let you observe from its threshold, take that option. I would not plan a traverse on the theory that the brown stuff is land and the green stuff is water. Those are two ways for a leaf to be wet. In a shower the first warning that the smooth kind has become slippery may be that you can suddenly see your face in it.

Give yourself time to learn the ordinary noises. The knocking is not something pursuing you, nor is the silence after a long run of knocks an all-clear. Watch a dark band travel and see what the ground does in its wake. The little white chimneys would be especially tempting to bend over; look through the binoculars instead. Those openings take things in as well as letting vapor out. Don’t put a hand into a seam, pry up a leaf to see the fish, or plant a pole in front of a traveling crease. Much of what looks like unoccupied space here is the roof of somebody’s world.

For the evening joining, choose your viewpoint **before** the birds come out. Stay well up and back from the hollows, clear of loose ice, with no hollow between you and your way home. If you can’t find such a perch, leave in daylight. The birds are worth watching as much for what they don’t do as for what they do: even built for this place, even with food within reach, they keep their feet back from the edge. Don’t try to touch the snow coming through or cross to the other hollow. A common surface is not a promise of a return journey, and the birds coming out have just left a great deal of slack in the skin. After a loud parting, let the gum and the moths be; the birds that went hungry the first time are still having supper.

The thing I’d most want to bring home is a pair of photographs taken from exactly the same spot: one in the afternoon, with a bird making its little forked prints in the distant snow, and one toward nightfall, with the prints close enough to see and the bird nowhere about. For that, the most useful preparations are a long lens, warm feet, and the willingness to spend a whole day going almost nowhere.
