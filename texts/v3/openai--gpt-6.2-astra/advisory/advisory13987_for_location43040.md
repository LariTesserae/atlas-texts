If you have a choice of arrivals, come in on the western tram, in daylight, with a seat well inside the car. It is easier to get used to the underside of the east ward sitting down. Look at the aprons first, then at the drainpipe they are hanging from, and only then let your eye go down to the snow. It may take a little while before your stomach consents to the arrangement. There is no need to get over this on the first crossing.

Pack for a long walk in cool shade: a windproof coat, an extra warm layer, and shoes that grip wet stone. Carry water. There is a great deal of bare country between quite ordinary addresses, and a street already laid out in white paint may not yet have a house where you can stop to ask the way. Get a current tram timetable and ask your host which stop they actually use. Go by the municipal clocks for your appointments. In the shadow of the middle tooth you can finish your tea in what looks like exactly the light you began it in, and still have missed the last car home.

For a first outing, go to the viaduct with someone who knows the steps. The dates on the pier are worth reading on the way down; they give you a better notion of the pace of things than staring at the rock in hopes of catching it grow. Stay on the iron. What rings like good stone at your shoulder is hardly stone at all a little lower down. If you come in early winter, ask at the school when the southern star can be seen. Let the children have their turn at the pore. It is quite all right if what you see looks like a signal lamp. Consider how far away a signal lamp of that size would have to be.

Leave one cool morning free for the pipe gardens. You needn't follow the fitters about or know when they start work. Find a place to sit within earshot of the boiler house and listen for the first frog; after a while you will be able to follow the opening of the valves with your eyes shut. Bring something dry to sit on. An hour spent still is a useful part of the visit here, especially if you have been looking at the north face all morning without seeing a single goat. Behind the depot, ask a watercress picker to show you the old runnel. Step round it, even where you could cover the whole stream with one boot.

Save a dusk for the ride east. Get your return journey settled beforehand so that when the hares come out you aren't watching the time. At the terminus, stay in the car until you have looked to see which door gives onto the ticket hall. The driver opens the other one for a view, not a platform. A tuft of grass is no assurance that the ground beneath it will bear you, nor is the word of a magpie.

From a little way back inside the car you can see the whole puddle. If the sky happens to clear, resist the impulse to look up for the mountain; you have a perfectly good view of it where you are. Then go out on the florist's side. After all that evening air it is pleasant to stand in a ticket hall with a ceiling over you and hear somebody upstairs setting the table.
