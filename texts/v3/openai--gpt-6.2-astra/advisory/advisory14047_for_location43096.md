Go in February, with someone who will stay on the lane. In September, if the gate won’t swing, leave it be. Don’t climb it. The post is on land and the orchard is afloat; getting over the gate settles only half the question of getting in. Even in February, look down at the join before you step across. If you would have to jump, spend the morning by the post instead. Much of what is worth a visit can be heard from there.

For a visit on foot I’d wear grippy shoes that can get wet, close-fitting clothes, and a properly fitted life jacket. I’d leave the heavy pack and long scarf behind and carry my own drinking water. The danger under the leaves isn’t just getting wet. There’s a yard of fine root between the timbers and the open water at the torn corner. A swimmer who went through might have a very hard time finding the same piece of daylight again. A life jacket is worth wearing for a fall at the edge; it doesn’t make the rent a good place to go and look down. Agree on a return time with the person at the gate, and make sure you can signal each other over the washhouse noise.

Once on, give yourself a moment to get used to the footing. The most unnerving thing will be taking a perfectly successful step and *then* feeling the floor give behind you. There’s no need to run from that sensation. Take short, easy steps, and if the layers start to part underfoot, go back the way you came. Don’t take hold of a red cord to steady yourself. It’s the growing tip of a pear tree, not a handrail. Keep clear of the turning round leaves and the place where the mint stands up again: the surface looks whole there precisely where a stout stream of water goes through it.

Give the shed a miss inside. You can see quite enough from outside to enjoy the oddity of a brick building that goes round with its garden. The belt is live, the floor is out of level and the current comes in overhead. In particular, resist any kindly impulse to put the watercourse back in order. At present the pump gives the same water a short outing in the sun. If you got it to the culvert again, it would begin lowering the water under the very thing you were standing on.

I’d spend most of my visit quite near the entrance, listening. You don’t need to put your weight on a limb to hear the difference between one tree and the next. Pick a place where a trunk cuts off one edge of the white tile and a safe crossing is still in sight. Have a sandwich. Look again. That little discrepancy in the view will tell you more about where you are than a walk to the far edge. Remember on the way out that the gap you crossed belongs to the bank, not to a particular row of trees; check it afresh rather than stepping off where you remember stepping on.

Don’t count on hearing the branch squeak against the brick. That would be an extraordinary piece of luck on a single visit. But on the lane afterward, stand still for a minute with your feet on the earth. It should be possible even with your eyes shut to pick out the shed by the one steady splash in all that rolling and gulping. It’s a nice thing to take away: the sound of twelve feet of daylight in an orchard full of hidden water.
