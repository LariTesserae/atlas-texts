Buy a pair of felt soles in the first quarter you reach. Bring stout boots for the descent from the rim, but for walking about town it would be worth having whatever the local shoemaker fits to feet that spend all day on stone. Ask for directions to the shoemaker by the *garden* gate. From the shelf it is remarkably easy to walk the length of a row of houses looking for a door.

Allow a day for arriving, even if your notion of a day's journey is considerably longer than the distance on the map. It's high country; find out how you breathe there before you set off across it. Carry drinking water, something to eat, a windproof layer, a brimmed hat and dark glasses. The cold of the air won't keep the backs of your hands from burning. Take a little square of insulating mat to sit on. And don't promise to be in the next quarter for dinner merely because you can see it. From the rim you can probably see the very house where you're expected, and still have most of the walk ahead of you.

Let someone show you the bridge approach the first time. The river is the one considerable feature that the view from above does *not* teach you how to find. Don't go down the cut to fill a bottle, and certainly don't wade in it barefoot. A lifetime in other mountains will have taught you to put your hand on a water-worn ledge without looking very closely at it. Here a ledge under running water may have the edge of a newly broken tile. Equally, don't conclude from the matching quartz on the two walls that the split happened last winter. Freshness is no guide to age here. If you want the newer, easier way down a flight of steps, look for chisel marks on the rounded edges.

Arrange your first night's lodging in one quarter and explore that one on foot. Ask where people fill their jugs. The garden cistern may or may not be the drinking supply; the well with the cup in it emphatically isn't the place to start. Keep grit out of the inspection holes, don't shift a conduit marker for a more comfortable seat, and leave the branches in their loops when you pass. Those extraordinary gardens are not evidence of water to spare. Somebody has seen to it that a trickle collected a long way off arrives precisely at the foot of that pear tree. If you're invited to listen at a channel, do. Hearing water below your ear while the wind drives grit past both your feet will give you a better sense of the cost of a pear than any lecture on the town works.

There are a few courtesies peculiar to a place like this. Don't cut your initials anywhere, even on a loose stone well outside town. Pick up a dropped fastening or a bit of broken crockery rather than kicking it off the path. If you chip the corner of a doorstep, tell the householder; it is possible to put it right, but not by waiting. On the other hand, don't treat the place as if nothing in it could break, or as if nothing lived or died. The cup *did* lose its handle. The currants put on enough growth to need tying back. Ask a gardener what has changed since last year and you may hear quite a long answer.

Save some time to sit still. The first stretch of grey ground you cross will look vacant; on the way back, with the light coming from the other side, you may discover you walked past forty hares. Don't go nearer for a better look. One of the pleasures of borrowing the town's soft footwear is finding out how close the road already takes you to a sleeping animal without costing it its sleep. And when someone offers to take you to see the red cup, go with them, even if you've seen stranger things in other worlds. They aren't promising a rarity so much as trying to tell you something about home. On the way out, notice how comfortably the garden gate fits your hand. Here that comfort isn't the trace of all the hands before yours. A person shaping the latch had to think of it.
