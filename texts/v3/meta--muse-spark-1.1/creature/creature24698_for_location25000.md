No one lives here. That is the first thing the town tells you, and it almost convinces you.

It has all the grammar for people — streets, doors with healed frames, trolley wire, a factory light buzzing in empty air — but the nouns are missing. You stand in a room that is a district and your breath makes a fog that nobody else has made fog with for a long time, and so you think, empty.

Then you adjust to the cool. To the low-place cool that lives in root cellars and under bridges. And you start to see that empty is not how it is. Empty is how it manages.

The town is inhabited at the level of employ.

Lowest and most numerous: they are the rivets and the maintenance.

The beetles. Black, domed, each one the size of a closed fist. They are not on the town, they are *of* it. Where two thorn-strung trolley wires need to be joined, a beetle has grown over the joint and gone to sleep there, its shell hardening into an insulator. If you pry at one — you won't — the wire sags and the beetle wakes up furious, chittering. Along the high wires more of them walk endlessly, tightening as they go.

In the gutters made of root-hair, the water-bearers. They are not insects and not frogs, something in between that never needed a name. About as long as your thumb, translucent white except for two lamp-eyes that are too large, always wet. They process in lines, shoulder to shoulder, carrying beads of condensation that cling to them like glass. Each droplet is bigger than its carrier. They take it where the pith is drying, they press their bodies to it until it soaks in, and go back for another. If you crouch, you can hear them sigh when the wall drinks.

And the moths. People imagine a forest town would have birds for scale. This town has moths that replaced them. Sheets of living paper, almost clear, veined like the leaves they live in. They don't fly so much as they drift on the cold and the humid sighs the stems make when they adjust. Their caterpillars — thick, blind, pale green, as thick as your forearm — are the factory workers. In the fruit-lofts they butt their heads against the press belts and push, and push, and push. They don't know what they're stamping. The presses stamp out more vein, more pipe, more leaf, more floor. Caterpillar juice lubricates the machine. When a caterpillar gets too big it climbs the wall, glues itself down, and thins into a moth, and the moth goes up to check the work.

And then the birds-that-are-not-birds. You'll see one unroot. It starts as a shelf. Or a sconce. A knot of leaf-feathers growing out of the damp pith wall, preening itself. Then it shivers, detaches with a soft pop, and does a slow circuit of the vast empty room — one lap, sometimes two — as if remembering what flying is for. Then it lands somewhere else and puts out rootlets and becomes a shelf again. While it is flying, the place where it was is a little warmer. While it is a shelf, you can put things on it and it will hold them.

That is the working town. That lives here.

But above that — because this place is low, lower than it should be, and low places collect — there is another kind of resident. The ones the architecture was trained for.

They are still here, they just learned the proportion of the place.

You won't see them at first because they move the way the condensation moves: slowly, then all at once somewhere else. They are tall, but not tall like people. Tall like they were stretched to fit a taller room. Pale from never needing sun, because the leaf filters everything to green and sodium-light. Their skin has the fine fur of root-hairs, especially on their forearms and calves, to wick the sweat-stones give off and channel it into their mouths. Their clothes, if they are clothes, are healed into them — collars of leaf-frame, cuffs of bark that grew around their wrists while they slept against a wall.

They are the Gardeners, or the Gardened. Hard to say which verb came first. 

They don't speak loud because the town agreed to keep sound low. They communicate by moving water. One will cup a droplet from a gutter and place it on another's shoulder and the droplet will run a particular way down a particular channel of hair, and that is a sentence: *this district is too dry, the boiler is breathing fast tonight, the outer basalt has put out another wall.* When they need to be alone, which is often because the town is so huge on the inside, they step into a stomata door and let it sigh shut and sit in the cathedral-empty with the single buzzing light and watch the moths do their work.

They look after the town not because the town would die without them — the town is manufacturing itself endlessly out of itself — but because they love the agreement. That it can be both totally thick with vegetation and totally empty at once. Total industry and total stillness. 

If you go far enough toward what feels like the center, where the geometry starts to bead sideways and you see yourself from behind entering the leaf you're inside of, you come to where the Gardeners go when they are done.

The Boiler. The original heart.

It is not a person, but it lives here more completely than anything else. It is a mantle cavity of damp red heat, an organ before industry had a word for organ. The town's builders layered their human boilers around it, riveted iron tanks humming on power that isn't being made anymore but still arrives from somewhere lower. 

Inside that heat sits something that isn't moving very fast. It looks like a woman, or a tuber, or a pile of coats left too long in the wet. It is all of them. She is covered in spores that are also cinders. Each time she exhales — which is rarely, because exhale here is measured in hours — the spores/cinders lift and drift on the heat and land. Some land on the iron, hiss, go out. Some land on raw basalt out beyond the edge of town, outside where you came down, and instantly there is a wall there, a damp pith wall with a valve door in it, and a new district with a light buzzing inside it, waiting.

She does not look at you. She is busy being low. Being lower than she should be, so all the water and all the waiting collects around her.

When it gets to true night — that textured, pre-dawn dark the town keeps — the big leaves close a fraction. All those huge, hollow leaf-rooms become slightly less vast. The town respirates. The Gardeners stop moving water. The moths land. The caterpillars stop pushing. The beetles tighten. The water-bearers huddle. The shelf-birds root.

And for one long minute the whole impossible place listens to make sure it is still alone.

Then it opens again. The drip resumes. The hydraulic sigh. The chitter of feet.

You are still there. And now the town is noting down your dimensions, quietly, the way it notes down how much water a new wall will need.
