They live in contact.

That is the first law of them. Not that they *prefer* contact. That there is no interval that is not them.

If you stand on your little island of real stone and look out in the wet, low light, you will think for a long time that you are looking at a plain of wet animals lying on a plain of wet rock. You are wrong in the proportion. The rock is the exception. The animal is the ground.

They are the Motile Pavement.

At distance, in the weeping gloom, you think seal. You think walrus, because of the way the blubber spreads under gravity when they lie still. You think penguin colony because of the way steam rises from all of them at once and makes a rookery haze. Then the one at your knee shifts its weight and the sound it makes corrects you entirely.

It goes *clank.*

A deep, wet iron knock from inside a body. A sound that should need a foundry yard to exist, now sealed under skin.

There are thousands within sight, millions within the dish. I will tell you the kinds, but understand they are not kinds like species. They are kinds like jobs on a floor that has never had a foreman.

**The Boiler-backs** are the largest, and the oldest by feel. Small-truck-big, although size slides here when you aren't looking straight at them. Their backs are a single, seamless dome of what looks like black hide until it declares itself. A seam sweats open down the spine, not bleeding — parting. The lips of it are thick and chapped, with rivets. Inside is not meat, or not only meat. A boiler hangs in a chest made of soot-greased ribs. A real boiler. You can see the firemouth low and orange inside it, even though the air is twenty below and your breath falls to the ground like lead shot. It burns what they all eat: the brine-slush that stands in every invisible dent of the gneiss, full of dissolved iron and nickel and sulfur, full of ground-up time. They have a snout that is just a flexible hose that worries at the cracks until water is sucked up with a mineral slurrrp. The fire never goes out because it can't afford to. If it did, the water around it would finally become the ice it has been threatening to be since the Pleistocene, and the seal of the pavement would break.

Heat flows backward here. You feel it first on your face. Their outside is hotter than their inside. Put your hand near a Boiler-back's flank and the air shimmers. Put your hand - if you could - inside the seam and it is cold as stone. The heat is their gift, their excretion. They sweat steam from a short, embarrassed smokestack that grows between their shoulder blades like a second, stunted head. The stack coughs - *chuff... chuff... CHUFF* - and a gout of coal-smoke and blood-warm wet air hurls upward a few feet, hits the too-low sky, condenses instantly, and rains back down on everyone as hot, oily condensation. That is why the rocks look wet. That is why your shoulders are wet. It is their breath returning.

**The Stamping Mothers** lie on their sides because they cannot stand. Dog-big to man-big, bellies split along a zipper you only see when it moves. When it ratchets open, light comes out. Inside, it is bright as a workbench, and dry, which is a miracle in this place. On hooks, on a chain that runs endlessly through their own guts, ride the young. They are blind, slick, mewling, and unfinished. A stamping arm, made of what is obviously a modified rib, slams down. *CLACK.* A leg is attached. *CLACK.* A vent hole is punched between what will be shoulder blades. *CLACK.* A little stack-collar is fitted. The Mother doesn't seem to know what she is doing. Her eyes, if she has them, are filmed over with condensation. She just chuffs and shudders and keeps the line moving. When a pup is done enough to shiver, the chain dumps it out onto the brine and it lands with a splash, already steaming, already looking around for a crack to suck.

**The Grinders** are low, wide, almost chins dragging on the ground. Their heads are not heads. One huge, prehensile lower lip and above it a wheel. A grinding wheel, grey and sparking, turning slowly even when they sleep. They are the reason the dish is a dish. They go forward an inch a day, lapping at the ancient gneiss - black and purple and rust-red, polished by glaciers and then polished again by them - and turning it to a fine slurry. You hear them as a low, constant, tooth-aching *screeeee* under the chuffing. They excrete that slurry out a broad rear vent as a tail of mineral mud, which the Boiler-backs immediately drink. Nothing is wasted. The stone becomes water becomes fire becomes rain becomes stone.

And on top of the ones that have stopped - because the biggest, oldest Boiler-backs do stop for days at a time, their fires banked to a dull cherry glow - live the **Wire-Nesters.** Rat-sized, weasel-sized, all stack and needle. They extrude. From spines along their own backs they pull thin wire, not hair, actual wire, black iron and hair-fine carbon fiber that steams as it comes out. They knit it over the hot, still flanks of the big ones into damp, hot nests that tick with escaping heat. You see colonies of twenty, thirty of them in one nest, fighting and coupling and stamping out even smaller copies of themselves that fall off and get lost in the blubber folds.

They eat the world constantly, and each other only by accident. When a pup falls under a Grinder. When two Boiler-backs are too tightly wedged and hot hide fuses and one has to tear away. What keeps them from consuming the world entirely is that they *are* the world here. They make the temperature that lets them stay liquid. They grind the stone that feeds the fire that makes the rain that fills the cracks that they drink.

And because reality here has been rubbed as thin as that stone, they have had to solve a problem: there are more of them than there is space.

So space has learned to fold.

You see it when you try to count them. The creature a quarter mile away near the center - the one with the crane neck that ends in a hammer, that lifts and brings it down on the anvil of its own back with a ring you feel in your molars - that creature looks larger than the pup at your feet. Perspective is sick. And if you slip - if you try to actually step between two Boiler-backs that are touching, shoulder to shell - you will find the alley. A mile of clanking, steaming, ringing alley that should not exist between two bodies that were touching. Walls of hot, breathing ironhide on both sides. The floor is brine-slush that remembers being worked; if you cup it, you can see microscopic parallel lines in it, like it was planed, before it drips through your fingers. In that alley you can walk and be lost among the works. You can hear the clank ten seconds before the hammer falls. You can see a warm, breathing after-image of a Grinder where a Grinder was, still huffing, until you blink hard and it sputters like a bulb and vanishes.

Nothing is built here because building is impossible. If you tried to stack the few loose stones into a windbreak, the windbreak would shiver. By morning the stones would have grown a seam and a small, puzzled stack and would be trying to creep toward the nearest boiler warmth, peeping out a thin wisp of steam.

This is what lives here. Not a herd. Not a hive. A factory that dreamt it was an animal so thoroughly that it forgot it needed a building, forgot it needed a maker, forgot it needed wilderness to be in. A self-assembling, self-forging, self-stamping pavement of hot, wet, iron-lunged bodies that keeps an entire shallow sea of slush from freezing by the simple, unending labor of being too crowded to die.
