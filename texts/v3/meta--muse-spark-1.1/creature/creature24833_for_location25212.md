They live low, and they live slow.

That's the first thing you get wrong. You come down expecting to find them darting — quick, insectile things to match the chatter — but the ones who cut and bind and inhabit the platforms are not quick at all. If they were quick they'd be dead. In a place where everything grows while you watch, where a shoot can punch through your boot-sole in an hour and a reed trunk can go soft under you like rotted cardboard, speed is a liability. You have to be slower than the forest.

You thought the platforms were empty at first because they were moving among the beetles.

There are perhaps two hundred of them in this open reach of the chasm, though counting is difficult because they do not gather. A family — if that word holds — is two, sometimes three. An adult and a long child, or two adults sharing a knife-stone. They are spread out by kilometers of thatch, each little nest on its raft of interwoven living reed, each raft audible only by a slightly different kind of creak. The mat between them is not a road. No one travels casually. To cross from one shelter to another is a half-day's work of careful climbing and cutting and listening.

They look, at first, like the plant has tried to make a person.

They are tall and they are light. Taller than you, because long limbs weigh nothing here — they are all tendon and strut, horn and stretched membrane. No swell of muscle that holds water. Their skin is not skin. Or not only skin. It's a dry integument, the color of old paper, thickened and scuffed with embedded floss, that fine glassy airborne lint that never settles. It has grown into them. They don't brush it off. Their bodies cultivate it the way some of the big tortoise-beetles cultivate sail-leaves on their backs. From a few meters away in the filtered amber dusk of the under-thatch, you cannot see the line where cloak ends and arm begins.

Their faces are covered. Not with cloth. They have grown a mask.

The cloak they sew — sails stitched with fiber twine — is hooded and overlaps in a beak, but under it, the face itself is narrowed, contracted. Their nostrils are not holes but slits that can close. They are lined with fine combs of white bristle that strain the floss that would otherwise pack their throats in minutes. Their mouths are small, lipless, kept shut except to sip. They don't sip water. There is a wax — warm, pale, half-liquid — that beads where you cut a living trunk. They collect it in folded leaf-cups and draw it through a reed straw. It tastes of nothing and it coats the tongue like cold tallow. It is their blood and their drink. Their eyes are the only wet-looking things on them, and they aren't wet. They are large and dark and capped with a second, translucent lid, like a lizard's, which flicks sideways every few seconds to clear dust. Those eyes never stare straight at you. They watch the lattice.

Because nothing here is truly separate, you stop thinking of them as "the people" and the rest as "the forest."

They are the pruning organ of the chasm.

Where the thatch grows too dense, it collapses of its own weight and ferments — not with rot as you know rot, which needs water, but a dry, hot fermentation that smells like singed hair and can smolder for weeks without flame. That kind of death spreads. They prevent it. They walk — slowly, one deliberate handhold after another — feeling the tension in the reeds with the soles of their feet, which are broad, callused pads four toes wide, horn-nailed. They know which trunks have gone pithy and are about to give. They cut them.

Their tools are only this: a stone, riverless stone, chipped until one edge shines like glass; a hook of cured fiber for pulling; a needle of black lignin thorn. They cut on the bias, so the resin beads clean and seals. The cut edge they press with ash until it hardens. They take the fallen trunk and strip it for fiber, for building, for fuel that does not burn with flame but melts slowly and gives up a low, persistent heat for cooking the wax thicker.

They never cut more than they can carry to the edge of their platform and weave in before night, because at night the growth is louder. In the heatless dark — though it is still 30 degrees — the whole mass exhales. The cinnamon smell comes strong enough to make you dizzy. The moths come down low then, clouds of them, hand-big, rasping wax from every surface, including, if you stand still long enough, your cloak, your hair, your exposed forearms. The people stand in their nests then and hum.

Their language is not much. Sound is too expensive here, too easily confused with the warning sounds. A slab falling from the high fault makes a particular cough. A trunk about to go makes a faint ticking for ten minutes beforehand. They listen so intently that speech would deafen them. So they have a small language of knocks — knuckle on hollow reed — that travels through the lattice better than air does. Two knocks: *I am here.* Three quick: *come look.* A long scrape: *do not step here, it is softening.* Children learn it before they learn to vocalize. When they do speak, at noon when the white light spears straight down and the cicada scream is at its absolute peak and no predator hunts, they speak in a sub-vocal whisper, mouth to the bristle-lined ear, words gummed with wax.

Their children are not born small. There are no infants crying in the thatch — anything that cried would be eaten by mantis-things within an hour. A child here is already long, already wrapped. They are grown in a separate nest, a tight, womb-like sphere of densely woven sails that filters the floss to almost nothing. You see them hanging like galls from the underside of larger platforms, trembling slightly as the air moves through. When you get close you hear a faint sawing inside, like something learning to rasp. After months, the mother — or the other adult, sex here is hard to read under the dust and the cloaks — tears it open with their stone, and a figure as tall as your waist unfolds, eyes already lidded, already knowing to move slow.

They do not live long by your count. Thirty of your years, perhaps. Long by the chasm's count, where a stand of reeds lives three. Their old die not by sickness but by drying further. Their joints stop. Their integument cracks and the floss that has always half-lived on them sprouts — one morning you find an old one sitting cross-legged on their platform, and fine white filaments are already growing from the seams of their hood, from their wrists, small sail-leaves unfurling from their shoulders. They have become substrate. The family does not bury them. They cut around them, leave that raft to be taken by the mat. Within a week there is no telling where the body ended and the pith began.

If you stay longer than a few days, they will begin to show you what they know without ever looking at you directly. That the big tortoise beetles are not enemies — they carry seed in their backs and you can follow their slow furrows to find fresh wax. That the transparent mantis things must be given a wide berth because they do not see you as anything but moving leaf and will take your finger clean off with one silent close. That the rolling pod-snakes are useful: pick one up at both ends — they are dry and rattle — and shake out the black wind-seeds before it can plant them in your platform.

And they will show you, always obliquely, the one law that keeps them alive: nothing wet can happen here.

They have no word for water that isn't also a word for poison or death or world-ending. They have seen what a single high storm does that makes it past the rim heat. A drop finds a sail, and the sail blackens in minutes. The black spreads as the water seeks more water that should never have been there. The wax melts wrong and goes rancid. The floss clumps and falls. The fungal thing that is not a fungus — because fungi need water too, but this place has its own wetness-phage — comes, white and then green-black, and hollows before it spreads. A whole acre of thatch can slump into a stinking mat that even the moths will not touch. On the far wall you can see the scars, dark stains where an old shower fell years ago.

So when you sweat — because you sweat, you are still a water animal, 40-degree air — they move away from you. They knock. Rapid, alarmed. They bring ash in a cup and offer it, not unkindly. For your forehead. For your neck. To dry you.

If you do not dry, they will cut the platform out from under you and let you and your wetness fall down into the unending thatch without ever hitting bottom, not out of cruelty, but because the chasm itself, which they serve, has not yet learned how to forgive water.
