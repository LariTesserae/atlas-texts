They live pressed.

That is the first wrongness about them. Nothing that high up should live pressed together. Up at four thousand meters everything thins and scatters — lichen to dots, wind to a scream, air to nothing useful. But here they have turned the throat inside out and made it dense.

The swifts are the bulk of it. The heat of it.

They are not the swifts of the low valleys, those sickle-thin things that never land. These are mountain things. They have gotten heavy. The cold and the height and ten thousand generations of never having to go far have made them soft and sooted, the color of wet basalt, of old smoke. Their wings are too long for the chamber so they keep them furled brutally tight to their sides, like closed knives. Their feet are wrong — not dainty swift feet, but raptor feet, long-toed and hook-clawed, because flat rock is the only ground they have ever known. They hang from it. Their heads are naked. Pink-gray, wrinkled skin stretched over too-large skulls, with sparse black bristles that catch condensation. When their eyes are closed, and they are closed almost always, the whole wall looks like it has grown a bark of damp, breathing stone.

There are thousands. Shingled, layered, belly to back, one wing tucked over a neighbor's head. They cover the bowl of the shaft from the lip where the light dies down to the deepest curve where the dark is total. They breathe together. You can see it if you stay long enough for your own lungs to stop hurting — the whole wall of them swelling, just a fraction, and releasing. The chamber stays warm because they are there. Each one is a small furnace, a core of hollow-boned heat that never leaves. That is their first job.

Their second job is to be ground.

Because under and over and between every bird, moving with a slowness that only looks slow until you realize how much work it is doing, is the beetle.

Thumb-big, thumb-heavy. Domed and black and polished until their backs hold the dark like the pools do. They are flightless. Their elytra have sealed shut over millions of years and become varnish. They walk on legs that are too long and too careful, and their mandibles are pale at the tips from wear. They never stop.

A beetle will spend its whole life on the territory of two or three birds. It climbs the naked pink of a head, braced, and picks through the bristles. It cleans the fold where wing meets body. It eats mites, and dead skin, and salt. The bird leans into it, still asleep, and makes a low sound deep in its throat, not quite a purr. Elsewhere, another beetle carries a fleck of shed down and tucks it, with other flecks, into a crevice in the rock. Nothing is wasted. The guano that streaks the walls white feeds the black tar-lichen and the one bleach-transparent fern and the tuft of alpine grass that has no right to be alive. The grass feeds nothing. It just stands there because it can.

Most of the beetles, though, are not grooming. Most of them are on the water.

The mountain sweats. The cold cloud from below hits the warm, used breath of birds and beetles and rock and turns instantly to beads. Without help that water would sheet down the smooth basalt and bead away into nothing. So they catch it.

You have to watch for a long time to see how deliberate it is. Around each pitted cup, each palm-sized bath and each basin-sized mirror, there is a low, uneven crescent of pebbles. Not fallen there — placed. Quartz chips carried from where the wind blows them in the mouth, black glass shards pried from the wall itself, each one set by mandibles, nudged a millimeter, nudged again. A dew wall. A plumbing system. The condensation hits the pebbles, climbs them by surface tension, runs, gathers, and is guided down in a single fat drop that falls, *tik*, into the pool it guards. Some beetles stand sentry at these drops. If a drop goes astray, they move a stone. If a bird drinks too deep and dislodges the rim, they rebuild before it has lifted its head.

The birds know not to step on the work. They lower their long, naked necks with an absurd, courtly delicacy, beaks barely dimpling the black surface, and drink. Then they settle back and close their eyes and resume being heat.

And behind all this, in the back beyond seeing where even the air-going-thin light never reaches, is the third thing that lives there.

It looks at first like the wall is moldy. But the mold is woven. A dense felt of silk, yellowed-white, stretched wall to wall between blunt pillars of rock, tunneled and chambered. Inside it, things move. Larvae. Pale as grubs, as long as your finger, each one fat with stored guano and lichen paste that the beetles pack into the silk for them. They breathe. You can see the whole back of the chamber breathe, separately from the birds, a slow pulsing that belongs to something softer. They are the beetles' young and the chamber's future and its past. The birds tolerate their silk because the silk holds heat. The beetles tend it because it is theirs.

They are all waiting.

Every day, there is an hour — not set by light, because light barely gets in, but by pressure dropping or cloud getting too cold or some old signal in bird blood — when they all open their eyes at once. Ten thousand naked heads. Ten thousand glossy black backs. The chittering that was a low, continuous rug of sound stops like a held breath. Wings loosen an inch. Claws flex on rock. Far back in the dark, one of the swifts gives a single high, looping, questioning whistle, and it echoes down the shaft and out the top and is torn away by the wind.

They don't fly. Not then. There is nowhere to go in the middle of a cloud sea. They just look up at the ragged oval mouth and taste the new air.

What they are waiting for is the moment when the wind tips over the lip just wrong and the whole white outside pours in, just for a second, not as wind but as light. A hard spear of wet sun falls straight down the throat and for one instant every drop on every pebble wall, every wet eye, every varnished beetle back, every black mirror pool, every transparent fern frond, catches fire. The whole packed, warm mouth of the mountain glitters like it is full of stars.

Then it is dark again, and they sigh all together, and close their eyes, and the beetles go back to nudging quartz a millimeter to the left.
