They don't live here the way people live down below. Nothing lives here alone.

The first and real resident is the kelp itself.

It owns this place. It had to learn how to be an ocean when there was only a puddle. The ancestor was a green scum in a riverbed that got stranded when the mountains died and the Table filled. It had a hundred million years of flat, cold, clear, furious water to figure it out in, and it did. 

Its holdfasts are not roots. They are fists. A single plant throws a knotted, iron-black mass as wide as a truck over the polished basalt and cements itself with its own acid into pits and striations older than bone. You can take a cutting torch to one and not free it. You have to unbolt the rock it stands on. From that fist a stipe rises, as thick through as your chest, not buoyant but drowning-tough, wrapped in its own dead skin so the surge can't sand it through. It goes up thirty meters, shivering, straining, creaking — you can hear it through your helmet, that low rope-creak of living wood — and then it gives up to the sun and bursts.

Up there is a second world. Fronds the size of barn doors, olive and smoked-glass amber, lapped one over the other so thick that down on the bottom the light becomes a false forest noon. They are never still. When the wind-trains come screaming over the rim with nothing to stop them for a thousand kilometers, they pull the whole canopy sideways. The fronds crack. That's the main part of the loud. Not a slap. A deep, wet, concussive *boom* as a ton of kelp snaps taut and collides with the next ton, and the next, all the way to the horizon. The whole sea claps.

That is the house everything else rents a room in.

In the alleys between the stipes lives the snow. That's what the divers call them — clouds of glass shrimp, each one as long as your thumb and almost invisible except for its eyes, two black dots. There are so many of them that when they turn the water goes white. They live for a few months, molt, eat each other's molt, glow a little when bruised, die. Everything else eats them. When a school moves through the Frame's lights at night it sounds like static.

The fish that eat them have learned to be the water.

The plateau trout — no relation to real trout except in the shape of desperation — are black on the back, hammered silver on the belly to steal the sun-net. A single one is nothing, forearm-long, mean-mouthed, always hungry. Ten thousand of them are one animal. They move in sheets and walls, turning without a signal you can hear, because if any one of them pauses in a surge that is trying to pour the whole sea off a cliff, it dies. So they became a decision. You will be working a cut-line with your chainsaw stalled in kelp jelly and this wall will come down the alley toward you, silent compared to everything else, and part itself around your body and your air hose and close again on the other side without ever breaking rhythm. Your gloves will be silver with their slime for an hour after.

On the bottom, where the light finally hits and trembles, live the ones who hold on for real. The crawlers. They look like someone took a bulldog and covered it in chipped basalt armor and gave it a mouth on its chin. They are the size of dogs, sometimes bigger, and they walk — not swim — on six stubby, clawed legs against currents that would take you to the cataracts. Their heads are flat and wide, made for scraping. The sound they make carries through your boots and your teeth: a long, grinding *shhhhk, shhhhk* as they rasp biofilm and colonial moss-animals off the polished floor. They don't care about you. You learn to step over them. If you knock one loose by accident, you chase it down and put it back. It will die otherwise, rolling until it falls off the world.

And then the grazers. Everyone calls them seals because what else do you call something fat, breathing air, with whiskers, that eats kelp all day? But they are not seals. They're something older that never left the Table after it became sea. Flat heads like shovels, eyes on top, no hind flippers, just big, hand-like front flippers that pull them through fronds and back feet like a frog's for kicking. They hang in the canopy like cows in kelp-trees upside down, tearing off the young growth with incisor teeth and letting out baritone moans that vibrate your chest plate. The calves cling to their backs, bald and wide-eyed. The adults let you touch them. They have to, or you can't herd them. The prod-men use a low sonic bleat that imitates a calf in distress — another layer of loud — and the whole herd, moaning in irritation, turns as one toward the milking pens where their oil is let and tagged.

None of that is what *you* mean when you ask who lives here.

We do. For a while.

There are maybe two hundred of us up here at high summer, half that when the autumn gales start to take the platforms apart. We don't live on the Table. We live *in* the sea on the Table, wedded to air.

If you come here you are bolted. Everything is bolted. The platforms — the Pulpmill, Seven, the Airhouse — are ancient forests of riveted steel, pitted gray-black, standing on legs that end in feet that are buried in the original stone. They have cables as thick as your body running off them at angles into potholes, into history. The wind and the water run over and under them, so they hum. The big Lister diesels in the Airhouse never stop. If they stop, everyone tethered below dies in twenty minutes.

So we wear the suit. Vulcanized canvas over wool that never quite dries, lead boots that barely work, brass helmets with three little pitted windows that leak constantly around the seal no matter what you do. The hose is your life. The bright yellow ribbed hose that carries your air and your heat and your comms line, which is useless anyway because the outside is too loud for comms. The generators thump, the kelp booms, the birds scream, the bottom-crawlers grind, the water itself hisses as it tries to drain off the planet. You can't hear anything in the helmet except your own breathing and the blood in your ears. So you learn to talk with your hands.

The old divers here have a sign that has nothing to do with any navy manual: they press both palms flat to their helmet, then out toward the edge. It means: *The sea is leaning. Hold.*

Because it does lean. When a storm comes over the rim — and it comes with no sky to warn you, just a dark line out past the kelp that suddenly has white teeth — the whole volume of the Table tilts. You can feel it in your air hose, the pull strengthening, your tether going from slack to singing. Silt erupts from the potholes like smoke. The canopy overhead vanishes into foam. The trout wall is gone. You do what your fathers did: you find the nearest holdfast that has been there longer than people and you wrap your arms around it and you cling to the smooth, indifferent rock while the mountain tries to throw its sea off through the clouds.

If you hold, you stay a resident.

Up on deck after, in the sluice of cold water and torn fronds, the Airhouse crew are standing in the airlock light, steam coming off their suits, bleeding from little cuts from bottle-brush weed that clings to everything. They look out. Because it's open — God, it's so open you can forget to breathe. In every direction, miles, it's all green-gold thrashing, the lights of the other rigs swinging on their cables like stars that have lost their place, the kelp forests swaying back to noon, the silver walls of trout reforming, and on every side, much too close and much too far, that thin shining line where the water goes skinny over black stone and simply — stops. The Cataracts. White, silent from here, endless falling.

Terns come down from the high air like thrown spears, screaming their heads off, and steal the offal from the chutes. The seal-things moan. A crawler finds its place again and starts to scrape.

Everyone gets back to work.
