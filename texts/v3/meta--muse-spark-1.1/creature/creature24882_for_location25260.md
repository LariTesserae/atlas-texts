Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing you understand, and you believe it for a long time because the roar steals your ability to count. The turbines are too loud, the water too fast. You can't hold still enough to see anything smaller than a building shudder.

Then you stay a little longer — caught in that warm down-welling at the center of town — and you realize that the white fur on the concrete isn't fur.

It's where they start.

**The Fur** is alive. A bacterial felt, but not bacterial as you know it. It doesn't photosynthesize, it doesn't feed on vent chemistry. It eats noise. Or rather, it eats the iron that only precipitates out of the water when it's hammered by a specific frequency. Where a pipe rattles at the right pitch, the dissolved iron in this impossibly heavy warm water crashes out of solution and the Fur blooms to harvest it. So it grows thickest in the loudest places: on the seams of the boilers, under the street lamps where the glass buzzes, on the underside of the grate where the entire ocean is trying to get in. If you put your hand close — not touching — you feel it pull heat, not cold. It's warm as a scalp. And if you watch for ten minutes, which is hard, it moves. A ripple passes sideways against the current. A bald spot fills in.

Where the Fur is thickest, it knits together and makes the second thing.

You think it's rust at first. Those smooth, dark second-skins of rust that don't flake off the I-beams. They shiver. Not with the current — against it. You see one near the tram line peel itself away from its beam a millimeter at a time, holding on with a hundred little white root-feet made of Fur. It's a sheet the size of a coat. A plate. It detaches and becomes something else in the water, and you finally understand why no rust ever falls.

They are **Mantles**. They're not animals the way a crab is an animal. They're a behavior of the rust. When a sheet gets heavy enough with the Fur underneath, it gets up and walks.

It doesn't swim. Swimming is suicide here. It clings. It has no front or back — just a slightly thicker leading edge that tastes vibration. It crawls along a building face against a current that would gut a submarine, hand-over-hand with cilia made of iron oxide that are invisible until they catch the amber light. It crawls toward louder, toward newer iron, toward where it can graze and get bigger. When two Mantles meet on a wall, they don't fight. They overlap for a second and go still, and in that moment of contact a perfect bubble of silence blooms around them. Rule two. Sound is architecture. Their bodies cancel the city's roar when they touch, and in that pocket the Fur underneath can flower and sporulate, a white puff that is instantly torn away by the water.

They are the maintenance crew. They replaced the need for painters. There are hundreds of them, maybe thousands if you count the small ones like dinner plates clinging inside the factory intakes, all slowly eating the town and excreting it back as itself. That's why nothing decays.

When a Mantle gets too big — bigger than a door — it can't cling anymore. The down-welling catches it. It peels off the tenement in the high water and tumbles, cartwheeling end over end, and you think it's dead. It's not. It's pupating.

It falls to the grate.

Nothing you have seen prepares you for what's under the grate. Because the buildings are anchored, but below them is not a floor. It's a throat. Open dark where the warm comes from, and a forest of vertical turbine shafts going down out of sight, turning forever. And caught in the slow sink at the center, pinned just above that throat, is a cloud of the big ones.

They used to be Mantles. Now they are **Kites**.

Stripped by pressure and heat, they've lost all their rust and become only Fur, but Fur grown thick, grown into muscle. White, translucent, vast things, ten, fifteen feet across, that hold their shape in the current like jellyfish holding shape in reverse — they don't pulse, they stiffen. They are sails. They live in the down-welling and ride it. They have learned to use the heaviest water as ballast. They fill bladders with the cold ocean that leaks in from cracks and become neutral just for long enough to climb a few meters up the side of the factory, then they vent and sink again, grazing the turbine housings as they fall, pruning the Fur off the machines with radula made of braided cable-wire they've incorporated.

They are silent. That's how you know where one is, even when you can't see it. They drag a cancel-bubble with them — a phone-booth of near-silence that moves. Inside that bubble the deafening town just... stops. And your own heart is suddenly deafening. If you're lucky, caught out in open water between tenements where the cross-currents would tenderize you, you can tuck in behind a Kite and hide in its quiet. They don't mind. Sometimes you'll find a pale, boiled crab inside there too, or one of those blind vent fish, huddled, borrowing the silence because its own lateral line can't make sense of anything else.

And if you stay in the Kite's silence long enough, you hear the last thing that lives here.

Not a thing. A place.

The town itself.

Because when all those bubbles of silence drift — off the walls where Mantles kiss, behind the Kites as they fall, in the standing vortices that spin up between buildings — sometimes two bubbles collide. And when silence meets silence, it doesn't make louder silence. It makes hearing.

For a second, maybe two seconds, in that interference, the roar is perfectly shaped into something like a voice. Not a human voice. An industrial one. The sum of all doors banging open-shut-open-shut a hundred times a minute, all turbine shafts turning out of sync, all tram cables singing. It says, without words, *I am still running. I am still empty. I am still correct.*

And all the Fur tightens. All the Mantles pause on their walls. All the Kites flare.

And then the currents tear the bubble apart and the deafening comes back like a hammer.

That's what lives in Sump Town. A lawn that eats sound, coats that walk the walls to mow it, sails that harvest the coats, and a city that learned to use all three to hear itself think, once every few hours, in the dark below the bottom of the ocean.
