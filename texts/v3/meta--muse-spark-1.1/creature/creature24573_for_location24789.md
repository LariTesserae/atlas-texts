Nothing lives here quickly.

That is the first rule, and everything that lives here obeys it.

If you come down off the rim into the warm, held air, your first inventory will say: nothing. No tracks. No burrows. No birds. No buzz. You will see the seven or nine dark shrubs and you will think they are waiting the way driftwood waits — shaped by something that left. You will not see them move, because you will not stay long enough to.

You have to stay longer than your own wanting to see something move.

About an hour, if you sit on the hard clay in the bottom — on one of the great plates that is still warm — your eyes will start to give the darkness a texture. The low dark-gray light is not empty. It is full of the dust that never settles. And when your ears give up on hearing anything else, you begin to hear the place breathing, which is not breathing.

The shrubs are where to start, because they are the most obvious lie.

They are not shrubs.

Up close they are not wood at all, not really. The black, wiry branches are too hard, too even, too cool to the touch where you expect them to be warm like the air. They have no bark. No rings. No sap. The thorn-scales are not leaves reduced; they are shutters. If you hold your breath and look at one for a long time — longer than you think you should look at anything so still — you will see that a few, very few of those shutters are closed differently than the others. They are shut around a bead of air that is warmer than the rest of the shrub.

What lives here lives inside the shape of a shrub.

It built the shape because the shape works. A low tangle of dark basalt-colored fingers breaks up the radiative loss at night, if there is a night. It combs what little sifts down from the lid of dust — not water, never water, but a faint mineral snow: salts exhaled from below, nitrates, a soot of organics that have been drifting for years in that high haze. The branches are not for photosynthesis — there is almost no light to photosynthesize with. They are antennae. Condensers.

The inhabitant itself is not in the branches. The branches are the roof of it. The thing itself lives half in the sand, half out of it, centered under that tangle like the knot of a net.

Call it a keeper, because it keeps.

Its body is low and broad and flat, no higher than the top side of a basalt pebble. If you brushed the sand away at the base of one of those dark tangles — and the sand there is strangely loose, not packed like everywhere else — you would uncover a disc the size of a dinner plate, the size of a shield. It is dark, damp-dark even though there is no water, the color of wet clay that never dries. Its back is paved with the same smooth basalt pebbles that litter the slope, but these are not stuck on. They *are* it. It secretes them, draws the silica and the iron from the deep chemistry and grows them as a back, as armor against the dryness. When one flakes off, it becomes one of the innocent pebbles with the dust-tail behind it. When you find that almost-perfect hand-knife flake at the foot of the largest tangle — thick where the thumb would go, fine where it would cut — you are holding something it shed. Not a tool. A scale. A molting.

Turn it over and its underside is the secret. It is pale, almost translucent, with a mouth that is not a mouth. No teeth. No tongue. A soft, broad, segmented lip that presses flat against the hard pan and seals. It does not eat. It dissolves. It lays itself over the microscopic salt frost and the clay and very slowly makes a chemistry of it, releasing a trace of water as a byproduct — not enough to bead, enough to keep its own membrane from cracking. It resorbs the water as quickly as it makes it. Nothing wasted. The endolithic bacteria that live *inside* the clay — faint green-black freckles in the mineral — do most of the real work. The keeper farms them with its warmth. It holds the day-heat pool over its patch of pan and keeps them five degrees warmer than the air. In return, they fix what little nitrogen falls.

It does not move. Not in the sense you mean movement.

Once every few years, when the charge in the air changes — though there is no storm, the overcast holds a static that comes and goes on a cycle too slow for any human calendar — the keepers open all their shutters at once. The mid-slope goes a shade darker for a minute. And then, very quickly for something so slow, they contract.

A keeper can move an inch in a night, dragging its disc of pebble-back across the flour-soft sand on ten thousand hair cilia you cannot see. It leaves behind a furrow so shallow and so quickly softened by the settling of the dust-tails that by morning you would call it nothing. It is moving toward the center. All of them are.

They are all moving toward the center at the speed of lichen, generation by generation. At the rim, there is room. In the middle, the clay plates fit without touching. In the middle is where they go to meet.

If you stayed years — if you became part of the established balance — you would see that the bottom flat is not empty. You would see that the pattern of cracks between the clay plates is not random. It is a packing problem. Each large plate holds one keeper under it. The old ones. The ones who have reached the bottom and have flattened out until they are almost the plate itself, two feet across, three. Their pebble-backs are now indistinguishable from the basalt that has worked itself up from below. Their shrub-tangles above them died back long ago, because they no longer need to comb the dust. They are fed by the slow migration of ions under the clay.

They touch each other, plate to plate, but never quite. There is always a hairline gap, wide enough for a knife. That gap is how they speak. When one side of the bowl cools a fraction more than the other — because your body sat on it, for instance, stealing heat — the clay ticks. A faint, infinitesimal tick that travels through the hard-fired pan. The keepers feel it as a change in pressure on their soft undersides. It is enough.

They are not many. Seven now, maybe nine on the slope, and six or seven in the bottom. They are not growing and not dying, which is not the same as being immortal. Once in a very long time, a plate buckles. A keeper in the middle cracks its own back to relieve the pressure of its own age. Its minerals flood the bacteria underneath in a glut. The bacteria bloom, blacken, then go dormant. The empty plate sits a little higher than the others. After a century, sand finds it again. After two, a new wire-thin sprout of black branches pushes up through its dust-tail to begin combing.

They do not need light, or water, or wind, or sound. They need only what this low place kept when it forgot everything else: a constant, dry warmth to hold over, and time so abundant it stops being time at all.

If you sit in the exact center, as you are sitting now, with your back to everything and your weight on one of those warm, hard plates, you are not sitting on the ground.

You are sitting on the back of the oldest one. And it, having gone without company for longer than you have been alive, registers you only as a small, brief, interesting surplus of heat that will be gone by morning, and which it will keep politely, without comment, until then.
