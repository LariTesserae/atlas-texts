What lives here didn't move here.

That is the first thing you have to understand.

### The People

They are sparse. Not sparse like a frontier is sparse — not lean, hungry people who expect more people to come later. Sparse like deer are sparse. The right number for this amount of browse.

You see them a quarter mile down a boulevard made of braided living wood, and you know them by their coat before you know them by their face. Everybody wears the same coat up here whether they want to or not: heavy canvas or waxed wool, dark from never quite drying, beaded always with fog-drip. Soft boots, soft gloves. Nothing sharp. You learn early not to go barefoot on the root-mat — the mat will not cut you, but you will cut it, and then you will be bleeding sweet sap from your house for a week.

They are quiet people. Not shy. Just — you learn not to raise your voice in a resonator. When you live inside a trunk that is three hundred meters across and hollow, a shout in a kitchen on shelf three can wake a baby on shelf nineteen two columns over. A dropped pot can travel a mile through living timber before it stops ringing. So they speak low, and they walk light, and their engines are left to do the loud work.

They are mostly older than they look, or younger. The damp preserves. Their hands are stained. Sap-sugar under the nails goes black and won't wash out. Crown-oil gets into the creases of the face. They have a patience that looks like resignation if you are from lower ground, but it isn't.

You cannot live where a street is sixty steps on Tuesday and eighty steps on Thursday and keep a schedule. You cannot live where a door you cut last year was already scarred over in your handwriting and still trust a blueprint. So they stopped trusting plans.

What lives here is a maintenance culture. Tenders. Listeners.

There are sapmen who live almost entirely inside a single Column — their Column — opening and closing the old valves in the cambium, listening to the chuck-chuck-chuck of the mill and adjusting the press so it doesn't bruise. There are high riggers who walk the Crown when the leaves are dry, which is never really, in suits of oiled silk, to train the wind-turbine vines, humming to them until they slacken or tighten. The vines know their voice.

There are children — not many, one or two to a banyan-block — who are taught not to count. "Don't count your steps to school," their mothers tell them. "Sing your way to school. Hum your way. The way knows how long it should be if you let it." And so children here move in little repeated songs, the same four notes over and over, and as long as they sing, the street stays the right length to hold them.

They don't own. You can't own a thing that is growing through your foundation and remembering your house before you built it. You ask. You graft and wait to see if the graft takes. If it doesn't, you go live somewhere else. A family might live for three generations on the same five shelves of Column 17, and they will still refer to it as "staying with Column 17."

At night they gather on root-porches and listen to the leaves surf and to the distant moan of all the turbines at once. They don't gossip much. The city repeats enough of what might have been or will be that gossip feels redundant. Instead they compare precedents. "My door has opened to the south mill for twelve days now." "Mine still opens to the cooper's, but his cooper is not my cooper." And they nod. That is how you navigate.

What lives here, in human form, is a people who have made a truce with unreliability.

### The Forest That Is The City That Is The Forest

But if you ask what really lives here, the answer isn't them. They are a lichen on the back of something larger. A necessary lichen.

What lives here is the forest itself, which is not a forest.

Individual trees do not really exist here. That is an idea you brought from below. What you call the giants — those forty-meter high, three-hundred-meter-wide columns with black-green bark you can walk into — those aren't trees. They are fruiting bodies. Or breathing holes. Or knuckles, like the erratics, pushing up from deeper.

Remember: they cored three hundred meters and were still in living tissue. No transition to rock. Just root into root into wood into root. The pink-gray granite swells you see are not bedrock. They are old, callused wood so pressed and mineralized you mistake it for stone, warm to the touch at noon because it is still, slowly, metabolizing sun.

So what lives here is one thing. One very, very old, very high, very flat thing that decided, a long geologic time ago, to become a plain in the sky, and then to become a forest, and then, when humans came with their streets and water towers and copper gutters, to become a city.

It tries.

That's the wrongness. Not malice. Not haunting. Trying. The Plateau learned the shape of "city" the way a vine learns the shape of a trellis — by touching it over and over until it crumples itself into approximation. It felt the weight of tram lines and water towers and the intention of people wanting to get from a door to a mill, and it tried to grow that for them.

It is bad at space. Or our space is bad for it. So sometimes a street is longer when damp because the Plateau is trying very hard to remember how long a street should be when it rains. Sometimes you cut a root and find two roots were always there, because for a second it remembered two different ways of plumbing a neighborhood, and both became true. Sometimes you look up through the leaf-lattice and see another sky — deep indigo with too many stars, or green rain falling up — because the leaves have grown through into a version of the city that occurs in a slightly different past or future and left a window open.

The leaves themselves — each the size of a dining table, waxy, turned down — live short, intense lives. A season. They fall with a sound like a dropped door and are collected immediately and pressed. If you leave one, it will try to root where it fell, on top of a roof, inside a tram track. The mast of them dripping all morning isn't rain. It's the Plateau exhaling last night's cloud through every leaf-tip at once.

The vines that carry current contract when the electricity runs. They have learned to enjoy it. On cold nights you can put your hand on the living cable outside your window and feel it humming, warm.

Even the sound lives. The moan of the turbines, the chuck of the mills, the ship-hull creak of leaves — the hollow columns hold onto it. You can stand in an abandoned shelf and hear a conversation from three days ago still going around and around the grain, too faint to make out words, just the shape of voices.

### The Others

And then there are the things that live here because there is so little else to eat them.

The high kite — maybe one, maybe the same one, circling so high its shadow can take an hour to cross from one district to the next. It has watched the columns grow. Its wings are almost transparent. It does not call.

The lichen-goats, shaggy and stone-colored, that graze the moss off the roof-boles. They are not goats. Their eyes have a second lid that wipes sideways. If you stand still for a long time on one of the stone-steps at the edge of the world, where the Plateau falls away, one might come and stand next to you and look out with you at the nothing that the city becomes in the mist. It will not be afraid. Nothing here has predators.

And the water. There is no river, but water lives here as a behavior — beads on iron, tocking in barrels, traveling in temporary bark-waterfalls that only exist for ten minutes after a cloud scrapes through. The children catch it in their mouths.

If you stay long enough — past the point where your clothes never dry and you stop counting steps — you start to feel the other population, the densest one. The versions. The unbuilt houses that left graft-scars. The streets that only appear in fog. The whistling of someone taking a shortcut that you have never taken, going through a trunk you have never opened, to a mill that hasn't been built yet.

The Plateau is crowded with its own attempts.

And you, on the edge at dusk, listening to the whole woven city breathe and creak and drip and moan — you live here now too. Not as an inhabitant. As a precedent. A path that has taken the city to the workshed for the last three weeks, and so, if you are kind, will continue to do so tomorrow.
