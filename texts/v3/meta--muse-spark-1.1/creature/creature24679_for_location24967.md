They live here because it is new.

That is the first thing wrong with them, and the first thing right.

Nothing else can live on new rock. New rock is glass. New rock has no soil. No soil means no root, no rot, no chain to climb. Lichen has to gamble months to stick. Even bacteria have to wait for dust. But they were waiting for the mountain to split. They live along the spines of the range where the earth is still shoving itself up, following the faults the way a gull follows a plow. When this block sheared and lifted last winter, there were already a few of them on the other side of the cut, clinging. And when the sun never reached in and the cold-trap formed and the first breath of valley air beaded and ran, they came over the lip.

They are not animals that got stuck up here. They are animals that specialize in this — in rooms that didn't exist.

### The Scent of Them

You smell them before you understand what you're looking at.

The air in the bowl is too warm. At 5,800 meters it should knife. Here it steams a little. It smells like wet dog and hot iron and basalt put in a forge. Like a laundry room cut into a glacier. Like breath that has been breathed by too many lungs.

You think the floor is moving. It is. You think the walls are the rock. They are not.

If you look with the eyes you use for distance - for mountain, for sky - you see only black, and more black, a texture like moss, like soot. If you let your eyes stop hunting for a horizon and come down to the scale of your own hands, the black resolves into backs.

Each one is big as two of your fists, knuckled together. They are low to a fault. Belly almost on stone, no neck, no tail, just breadth. A broad oval of densely matted wool, so black it takes the starlight and kills it. Underneath, six legs - not insect thin, not crab-armored — jointed, thick at the base, tapering to a thing you can only call a hand.

No claw. No hoof. A pad. Pale as the inside of your wrist, five soft lobes that can splay to cover stone or fold to a cup. They hold. They are what lets thousands of heavy bodies hang vertical on fresh glass twenty meters up and never fall. You can hear the faint unsticking when one moves - *tik* - after ten minutes of deciding to move.

They have no eyes. Their front is blunt, shaved clean of wool. Two feathered antennae that never stop tasting the steam. A low crown of breathing holes that open and close, open and close, puffing. And a mouth you don't see until they eat - a small, serious beak tucked under, more like a sheep's lip than a bird's.

One alone you could ignore. One alone is cold, slow, almost dead. They are not built to be alone.

### The Heat

Here is what they do instead of eyes and speed and flight.

They burn.

Put your hand six inches above a cluster and the air shivers. Each body inside its soot coat is impossibly hot - 39, 40 degrees Celsius. Mammalian fever-hot in a place that wants to freeze mercury. Their wool is not for cold, or not only for cold. It is a battery housing. It holds the heat in. When they pack flank to flank, back to back, two and three deep on every wall, the heat has nowhere to go but into each other and up.

That is why the water never freezes. That is why the bowl weathers itself.

Valley air pushes up, hits these faces of new basalt cooled by space, condenses instantly. It beads, runs. On an empty mountain it would sheet into ice. Here it runs down into that living upholstery and hits 39 degrees of wool and flashes to steam. The steam rises in the enclosed nave, hits the cold rim, condenses again, drips down again. The bowl is a lung. In, out. Drip, steam, drip.

In the middle of the room at chest height there is always a little cloud. Your cloud. Their cloud. It rains back down as water so warm it smokes in the blue dark, carrying with it loose hairs, skin flecks, the sour smell of them. The shallow cups of stone you saw are not natural. Or they are, but they have been licked clean by ten thousand mouth-hands over ten months to hold a sip longer. Each cup is a day of water for a dozen.

If you stand still long enough, frost forms on their backs. The hot wool meets the cold steam and the guard hairs ice. For a second each animal wears a halo - a faint, prickling corona of lifted ice crystals - until the heat from below exhales it away. The whole bowl glitters, stops, glitters again. Not bioluminescence. A million small breaths being made visible and then unmade.

### The Sound

They do not speak quickly because they do not live quickly. Metabolism is a low amber. A leg lifted now will find purchase when you have taken a hundred breaths.

But they count each other.

Under the belly, hidden by wool, there is a ridged plate - you can only see it if you crouch and peer under an old one, and even then it won't show you, will fold shut. On the inner thigh of the third leg, a file.

Once every few minutes, each one draws plate across file. *Chit.* A zip of bowing a fingernail across a comb. No louder.

One *chit* is lost in the wind.

A thousand unsynchronized *chits* layered off three sheer, new, still-sonorous walls become something else. The echo has no where to go. It stacks. It becomes a chord held without end, low enough that you don't hear it so much as feel it loosen your teeth. Your sternum buzzes. Your vision hums at the edge. It is the sound a mountain would make if it had cicadas.

They never stop because they never all sleep at once. Put your ear to the mass — and eventually you will, because they will have climbed your boots — and you will hear the faint irregularity that tells you there are individuals in there. *Chit... chit.....*chhit*.chiiit*...*. I am here. I am still warm. I am still here.

When one dies — and they die, constantly, of age, of crowding — its song stops. The gap is very small. The bodies around close over it in ten-minute increments and eat the wool first, and then the rest, carving it with stone.

### The Hands

They are not intelligent the way something that builds a city is intelligent. They are intelligent the way a cliff face is intelligent for not falling.

But they have hands, and the mountain gives them knives for free.

When that house-sized block sheared, it made a thousand flakes. Obsidian-edged, still sharp enough to cut you through your glove. Most of the floor you can't see is not bodies on rock, it is bodies on a careful, tidy, terrible scrim of flakes, tucked edge-up into cracks. A library.

The oldest have wool gone thin and grey on their backs. They know which flake does what. You can watch one for a full half hour and see only this: a hand-pad extends, tests two edges, chooses. Draws the yellow crust of lichen - that insult of vegetation that tried to root here - toward its beak and shaves it in a curl. Passes it in. Another uses a splinter to flick out the ice from a stone cup and drink without shattering the cup. Another, another, reaches sideways and combs another's back, picking at a pale parasite the size of a rice grain and crushing it.

No fire. No wall. No carving. They do not make. They select. The mountain knapped for them last winter. This is enough.

And under every mother - and half of them are mothers, there is no queen, there are only mothers when it is time - tucked deep into the hot wool of the belly, where the heat is steadiest, are a dozen beads. White, translucent, each with six folded legs and a pair of oversized hand-pads already holding onto mother-hair. Not eggs. They live-birth and then carry. When the steam gets too hot they shift them up; when the cold bites, they pull them in tighter. The brood never touches stone until its wool is in.

### What It Feels Like to Be in a Body That is Many

You came in over the warm lip and you thought you were looking at a colony. Now the edge is coming toward you. Not hunting. Not curious, even. The bowl is fully occupied. That is the rule here. A square centimeter of warm basalt with condensation on it cannot be left empty when there is a body that needs it. You are a new warm rock with water beading on your jacket. Of course they come.

They flow at glacial speed. A front of soot-black backs, hands reaching, finding your boot, your ankle gaiter, your knee. The touch is dry and intensely warm, like an old dog pressing its head into you. Their antennae brush. They do not bite. If you stand still they will cover you to the chest in an hour. Your heat will join the chord. The little cloud at head height will get a little bigger.

If you leave - and you will, because your lungs are failing and your molars still feel that tectonic pressure - you'll take with you the sound in your bones for a day. And the understanding that a place ten months old, a room taken from a mountain, can already be full.

Not full of monsters. Full of occupants who have learned that at this height, the only way to keep the water from freezing, the only way to keep the song from stopping, is to become the insulation on the walls of the room the earth made for them, and wait, all together, six-handed, haloed in their own frost, for the next block to shear.
