What lives here doesn't live *on* it. It lives *as* it. As part of the same extrusion.

There is no fauna and flora. That distinction hasn't had time to cool and separate yet. There is only the Cover and the Workers.

**The Cover is the first life.**

It is not groundcover. It *is* ground. The basalt is still too young and too angry to be soil — it's glass and iron that cuts anything that tries to root. So the first colonist learned not to root at all.

It's a felt. Black-green, almost black in the amber dim, so dark it looks wet. If you put your hand to it you feel it is warm, not with rot but with work. It's a photosynthetic lichen, but that earth-word is too small. Each filament is a low-light solar panel tuned to the exact wavelength that makes it through two cloud lids. It doesn't just photosynthesize; it plates. Underneath, where it touches stone, it sweats a weak acid and lays down a micron-thin sheet of polymerized carbon — a gasket between living and non-living. It is making soil out of itself in advance. It grows outward a few millimeters a day, which here is fast, and when a new fin of basalt extrudes in the night, by morning it is already furred in black-green.

On top of the felt live the Cistern-Bearers.

They look like bromeliads that forgot to be plants. Fat, waxy, low cups with leaves the size of serving platters, overlapping like shingles. They have no roots. Their roots are air-roots that hang up, not down, combing the thick, forge-breath fog for water. Each leaf axil is a sealed vat. You can pry one open and it holds a liter of clear, hot water, and in that water lives a complete world — mosquito-thing larvae that filter iron bacteria, worms that clean the leaf's wounds, algae that make the water taste of copper. The plant isn't a plant. It's a landlord. When the minute-long rains come, the kind that condense out of nowhere and steam off rock, the Cistern-Bearers gorge and swell until they squeak. Then for days they leak, on purpose, one slow drop at a time, feeding the felt below.

Above them is the false forest.

None of them are trees. Trees are a design for wind and winter, and here there is neither. These are stalks — ten, fifteen meters tall, no thicker than your wrist, made mostly of pressurized water and silica fibers. Fern-palms that shoot up in a week, throw out a frond-crown, drop a load of soot-black spores, and collapse under their own weight. As they fall they are already hollow and already inhabited. They exist to make vertical space that can be rented out. The air between them is full of more felt, growing as epiphyte, as vine, as drape.

All of that is still just the factory floor. The Workers are what you came to see, though you walked past a hundred of them without seeing.

**The Furnace Grazers** are the big ones. From a distance you think boulder, then you see it breathe. Low, wide, cowled, the size of a bear, six stumpy legs like a tardigrade's scaled up past what physics should allow. Except physics is lax here. Their back is a translucent dome of keratin and you can see right into them: a belly that is not a belly, but a crucible. It glows cherry-red even in the dim. They follow the veins.

The mountain sweats copper. Hot wire, native metal, extrudes from the canyon walls under pressure like solder from a joint, hissing as it contracts. The Grazers put their soft, lamprey mouths around it and draw. You can hear the metal tick as it cools inside them. They don't digest it. They refine it. The copper is drawn through organs that work like zone refineries, impurity sweated out as a black tar they excrete through their skin — which the felt loves. And behind them, as they crawl, they print. From spinnerets around their anus they lay down a lace of pure, still-warm copper mesh, a fractal frill that steams in the wet air. That's their nest and their child. The young live in the lace as tiny, cold, pale versions, chewing on the copper until their own bellies light up. An individual Grazer is a mobile foundry that makes more foundries.

**The Glass Slugs** do the same for stone. You hear them first, if you hold your breath long enough to hear anything in the sound-swallowing air. *Thunk... thunk... thunk...* soft as a heart through a wall. They are as long as your forearm, boneless, the color of the basalt they eat. One end is all mouth — a rasp of basalt teeth. They take in scree, pumice, slag, whole mouthfuls of mountain. Inside, you can see shapes moving through their translucent gut, bubbles rising through melt. What comes out the other end is not shit. It's product, sorted. A pellet of glassy black slag, clean as an ingot, dropped with that thunk. A bead of iron-manganese, still hot. A puff of silica powder that the Cistern-Bearers catch. They crawl in lines, always, following the vibration that sorts the mountain's scree slopes like a shaker table. They are quality control.

Above them, **the Looms.**

You never see a Loom until it dies and lands on you. In life they are invisible. Dragonflies is the wrong word but it's what your brain reaches for. A meter across, wings so thin they only exist when the amber light catches them at an angle, and so silent the thick air erases even their whisper. They don't eat. Their abdomens are combs of pure electrostatic charge. Where they swarm — and they always swarm over the fresh copper lace, over the new iron beads — the air itself hardens. Dust that was hanging randomly snaps into lines. Iron filings jump into script. They rain down separation: heavy to one side, light to the other, useful to the center. They are the precipitators, the air filters, pulling order out of fume. When two swarms cross, you get lightning that doesn't flash, just a warm press on your skin and a smell of ozone and hot metal.

Then there are **the Ribbons.**

They are not animal at all, unless heat is an animal. You see them at the mouths of the chimney furnaces — those natural blast furnaces of slag on the ridges that inhale cold at the base and exhale 200-degree breath at the top. The Ribbons live in that breath. Long, flat, carbon-black strips, lighter than air when hot, they ride the chimney draft up, cool, fall, ride again. Some are a handspan, some are twenty meters long. Their bodies are laminated like capacitors. They don't eat, they commute. They soak heat at the bottom and radiate it at the top, or soak it at a fresh extrusion and carry it to a nursery of fern-palm seedlings that need to be cooked to germinate. If you stand in a field of them you feel winter and summer a foot apart. When a chimney dies and goes cold, the Ribbons that lived there go still and crumple like burnt paper. They become felt.

All of these things are abundant to the point of obscenity, but they don't predate each other. Predation would be a waste of a closed system. When a Grazer gets old and its crucible goes dark, it doesn't die and rot. It stops, sits, and its cowl splits. The felt grows up through it in hours. The Cistern-Bearers put air-roots into its belly. Its copper lace is dismantled and carried off by Slugs.

And then there are the ones that only exist because the place is broken.

**The Time Grazers.** In the valleys where gravity leans and a cup of water sits rippling hours after you left it, you will find shallow pans of basalt that hold perfectly still pools. In them lives a crust of tiny white shrimp-things that flicker. They are feeding on the snarl itself. A stalk grows, fruits, dies in thirty seconds three feet away — they harvest that burst of negentropy and store it. When you walk through one of those snarls and your own thoughts go syrupy and your boots take too long to fall, you will see them move at normal speed, unperturbed, picking photons out of the air that haven't been emitted yet. They die if time flows correctly for too long. They need the stutter.

**The Revision Riders** are even stranger. They are small, limpet-like, with a foot of incredible grip made of the same polymer the felt makes. They live only on the edges of the fresh extrusions — the places where rock is being born right now as toothpaste basalt squeezes up from below, already mossy. A human would be crushed or burned. The Riders are not. They have learned to read the micro-tremor a second before an extrusion and to let it grow *around* them. They stand half-swallowed, smug, filtering the mineral-rich steam. They have no fear because for them, being buried is how you get to a new feeding ground. When the rock stops moving, they release, crawl another inch, and wait to be buried again. The Grazer that got caught in the upwelling and kept eating — it had Riders on its legs.

None of them know each other. None of them know anything. There is no queen, no hive mind, no spirit of the mountain. There is no builder.

There is only this: a high, warm, dim place where matter has not yet decided whether it wants to be geology or biology, and so it is trying both at once, very quickly, using the same set of tricks. Smelting and fruiting are the same verb. A lung and a chimney are the same shape because the same pressure made them.

If you lie down, as you eventually will, because the air is heavy and the felt is soft and the quiet is so complete you can hear your own blood printing itself through your ears — if you lie down, they will work around you. A Grazer may lay copper lace across your ankle. A Glass Slug may test your boot and decide it is too poor in ore. A Ribbon may settle for a second on your chest and make you cold and then hot.

You will not be eaten. You will be included.
