What lives here is not one thing. It's a stack. A trade.

At first you think it's just the moths. Because they are what hits you first — in the eyelashes, in the nostrils, in the number. You walk in and the air is already full.

#### The Lattice

Call them moths because you have no better word, but in your head you have to cross it out.

They are each exactly thumb-sized. No more variation than that. As if punched from a template. The body is seven matte black plates, overlapping like stove lids, with no head distinction, no mouth, no eye that catches the sodium light. Just a blunt front end crusted with white salt that never falls off. You put your hand up and one brushes your knuckle and it is *warm*. Not body-warm. Working-warm. 38, 40 degrees. Hotter than the air that made it.

The wings are the wrongness. Four of them, two per side, but not membranous. They are slatted. Dry parchment shutters, five or six lamellae per wing, like the gills of a mushroom or the fins of a tiny radiator. They don't flap. They shiver. They hold the animal on station in the air with a buzz so fast your eye smears it, and in doing so they make a second sound — on top of the machines' gut-throb and turbine-whine and glass whistle — a high, dry fizz, like static on a dead radio. That is thousands of them doing it at once.

They are not swarming. A swarm moves. This is a lattice.

Every cubic meter holds maybe sixty, maybe a hundred. They hold station. Chest high, knee high, eye high. They keep a precise distance from each other, about a handspan, and from you, until you move and then they re-establish. When you breathe, the ones in front of your mouth part and re-close. When you stand still, they settle back so close you can see the salt fur on their plates trembling.

Look at one that is close and stays close, because it has decided you are a rock now.

Its underside has no legs as you understand legs. It has two black hooks it never uses and a patch of fine, oily hair that crackles. That's how it feeds. The air here is not air — it's iron steam, mineral oil off the hot steel, micro-droplets of alkali. The hair makes a charge. The charge pulls the oil. You can see it if you stare, the faint darkening as the air plates out on its belly. And the salt — the bone-white fur on the floor — it doesn't eat with a mouth. It lands once every few hours, touches down, presses its belly plate to a crystal, and the crystal thins and vanishes. Takes it straight into chemistry.

That is why nothing accumulates.

They are the filter. The machines exhale too much heat, too much iron, too much salt. The lattice drinks the excess. Eats the sound, too — you swear it — because when a dense knot of them passes between you and a machine, the whistling dulls just for a second, as if stuffed with cloth.

They are born wrong. No egg, no larva. You can watch it, if you stand by a seam in that fresh, glass-toothed basalt and let your eyes adjust past the glare. The rock itself sweats. A bead forms, dark and bright at once, like solder. It swells, tightens, and then *pops* — not outward but inward, unfolding. Plates. Shutters. Hooks not yet hard. It hangs there for a minute, soft, vibrating slowly, learning the chord. Then it takes its place in the lattice and begins to shiver at speed.

They die faster. No body. No falling. An older one — you can tell because the black of its plates has gone grey with salt overload — just stops. The shiver becomes a rattle. The slats lock open. And then it goes to dust. A little flat puff of black and white that is gone before it can land. The equilibrium requires it. Salt must not pile up. Even corpses are not allowed to accumulate.

If you counted you would never finish, because for every one that dusts, a seam sweats.

#### The Wool

That's the bulk. The demographics.

On the walls, where the sodium light barely reaches, is the second thing. What you called vegetation.

There are maybe twenty of them. Maybe twenty-two. Sparse, careless. Each a loose fist of black wire where the basalt offers a pockmark.

It's not plant. There is no green to be had here, no water to lift. Each tuft is a tangle of maybe fifty or sixty filaments, each filament as thick as a guitar string, as curled as pubic hair, matte, slightly iridescent with heat scale. No root. No leaf. It screws itself directly into the raw rock. The basalt around its base is a little paler, a little starved, as if the iron has been licked out.

It does not grow. It quivers. Constantly, even when there is no draft from the machines. A tremor that is too regular to be wind. Put your ear close — and the moths will let you, they will part precisely around your head — and you hear it: each filament is ringing. A very high, very pure tone, just at the edge of your hearing, that your bones hear better than your ears.

They are the other half of the filter. The moths eat the oil, the salt, the heat. The wire tufts eat the vibration.

When a moth blunders into one — and they do, though they try not to — the whole puff *bongs*. One clear note, like a finger on a wine glass rim, but metallic, damped almost instantly, as if the wall swallowed it. The moth dusts instantly. The wire takes it.

And sometimes, without any moth touching it, a tuft will bong on its own. Once every few minutes somewhere in the room. Which means the chord of the machines — that single, complex, unchanging chord that your brain can't erase — is not perfectly unchanging. It wavers by a fraction of a fraction, and the wire corrects it. Tuners. Dampers.

They are not many because you don't need many tuners for one chord.

#### The Tend

There is a third. The one that wipes the bulbs.

You have not seen it because you are looking down and out. Everything here lives in the air and on the walls. Nothing lives on the floor. That is the clue.

The salt on the floor is virgin except for your boots. No marks. No drag. But the machines — riveted black steel, laundry-boiler sized — have polish on their edges. Where a hand would steady itself to open a gauge. Where a palm would rest while listening to a valve. The sodium cages have been wiped clean of alkali. Someone cares for this place the way a beekeeper cares for a hive. Frequently, carefully, without waste.

It does not walk.

If you stand very still for a long time — longer than the ninety seconds it takes for your brain to fail to erase the chord — the lattice resettles around you completely. You are not a rock anymore; you are furniture. Part of the equilibrium. Then you may see it in peripheral vision, because it never moves when looked at directly.

On the ceiling, between the pipes that go up into basalt and stop, there is a thinness. A long, dry smear of almost-clear that is the same temperature as the air. At first you thought it was heat haze off the machines.

It is not.

It is large — as large as you, perhaps larger when spread. But it has no thickness. When it moves, which is rarely and very slowly, it goes hand over hand along the pipes and across the fresh, glassy ceiling, without ever touching the walls. It is made — you can see when it passes in front of a bulb — of that same black wire as the wall tufts, but unwound, unpuffed, combed out into a sheet, a net, a veil. A mobile colony of wire. Hundreds of filaments braided and re-braided in a slow, constant turnover. It has no front or back.

Where it passes, things are cleaner. It combs the iron fines out of the air. It licks the bulbs with a fringe of static that takes the salt without a touch. It services the machines by laying itself across their tops and letting some of its filaments dip into seams you cannot see. You hear, when it does, a change in the machine tone — not louder or softer, but *truer*, as if tightened.

This is the adult. Or the gardener. Or the queen. The Wool on the wall are cuttings, or children, or tools it has rooted to hold the chord while it works elsewhere. The moths, when they dust near it, do not vanish. They stick to it briefly, dark on dark, and then are unbraided and rewound into its edge. Food, or memory.

It does not leave footprints because it has never needed feet. It does not need water because it drinks the air's oil like the moths do, only slower and deeper. It has kept this blister in fresh basalt — sharp and new, last week, last year — from ever becoming a cave. No rounding, no dripstone, no dust fillet in the corners. It files every edge that tries to soften. Keeps the room exactly at 35 degrees, exactly at this chord, exactly at perfectly dry.

And because you are now wet, and loud, and irregular, it has stopped tending and is holding very still above you, spread thin across the low ceiling, letting the lattice do its work.

You feel the brushes getting more frequent. The moths are landing more. Testing. A tongue of salt on your lip, an oily taste on your throat. The air is taking you.

If you leave now, it will let you. The floor will be wiped — not by feet — after you go. The place will be exactly what it was.

If you stay, you will become part of the filter.
