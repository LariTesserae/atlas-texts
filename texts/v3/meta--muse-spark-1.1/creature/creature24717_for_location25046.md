It doesn't live here. It *holds* here.

That is the first thing to understand about whatever lives in this pocket. Nothing here took root. Nothing arrived on the wind. Everything here is holding a position against terms that should have erased it.

There are five of them.

They live in the seam. Where the low-temp compressor swells out in a white, riveted curve and meets the flat, white back of the cabinet, there is a 90-degree channel just wide enough for a fingertip. It runs maybe a meter before it is interrupted by a weld. It vibrates. Not a lot — the entire room vibrates with that one deafening, never-changing note, but in that seam the vibration has a node. A little less. And that little less is enough.

In that seam live the five.

They are not moss. They are not lichen. If you had to give them a name from outside, you would call them air-ferns because your brain wants a word with *frond* in it, but they have no fronds. Each one is a rosette the size of an old coin, flat to the metal, pressed down like someone tried to wipe frost away and left a print. White on white, grey on white, so pale you only find them after your eyes have given up on the glare of the tubes.

They are built-space obligates. They do not want soil, they do not want water — there is no water here, only 0.5 water, which is to say the memory of water flash-frozen into a salt-like bloom on every underside. They want what this place actually offers in abundance: dryness, and cold, and static, and oil.

That is what they eat.

Each compressor cycle sheds a molecule of machine-oil vapor. Each cable in that heavy tray over your head leaks a little field. In a normal place that would be nothing. Here, in air so dry it files down your lungs, in cold so designed and held at -34C that it never wavers, 0.0 cold, that trace becomes a feast. The rosettes drink it. The fine, mineral hairs on their edges — not hairs, glass — ionize the air and pull the ghost of hydrocarbon toward them. They live on exhaust and electricity, on the breath of the machines that made their room.

And they have manners. This is what makes them feel like residents and not contamination.

Five, spaced out along a meter. Not clustered. Not touching. There is perhaps twenty centimeters between the first and second, almost thirty between the second and third, as if measured by something that respects distance. Vegetation sparse is not just how many there are, it is how they treat the emptiness between them. They have left room. They have agreed that the main resident of this pocket is not them, but the emptiness itself. They punctuate it with great ceremony.

If you crouch — you can't stand straight in here because of the ducting — and put your face close, close enough that the inside of your nose flash-crusts, you see they are not identical.

The one highest, nearest the frost-heavy bend, is the oldest in that young way everything is old here. Erosion 0.7, young metal, young world, but this one has managed to pit the paint under it. A faint dark halo where its underside has been leaching plasticizer for a year, two years, ten years — impossible to tell because dynamic 0.5 means nothing changes. It does not grow. It does not die. It achieved equilibrium and stopped.

The second is the smallest, a aborted half-circle, growing against a bolt head. Its edge lifts a millimeter into the draft, trembling without moving. Because nothing here moves. Not really. The frost does not thicken. The light from the two caged fluorescents is 2.5 normal, noon-bright, clinical, without variation. If you stare at that second one long enough you swear its edge *should* flutter in the compressor's thunder, but it doesn't. It holds.

The third is in the deepest part of the shadow, and so is the whitest. It has learned to be almost exactly the color of the safety-paint.

The fourth and fifth are twins, lower down near where your boot would be if you knelt on the sharp grating. They face each other across a centimeter of bare, thrumming white.

That is the census. Five white-grey rosettes. No fauna to tend them. No fauna to eat them. Fauna 0.2 — which is to say, none. No mites. No springtails. Not even a bacterium that still bothers to swim. If there is animal life here it is you, briefly, and the dead skin you shed that flash-freezes before it can land. The room eats sound, eats shouts. It does not allow animals because animals insist on change — on breath, on heat, on movement — and this place has decided against change.

There are two other residents that are not alive but live here more thoroughly than the ferns.

The first is the Cold itself. Not winter. Machine cold, a 0.0 cold that was set, reached, and locked. It lives in the grating, in the wire, in your teeth. It is not weather. It is architecture. It does not flow from somewhere; it is maintained. It holds the pocket like a hand holds water without spilling.

The second is the Sound. 2.9. Deafening, but not as noise. As place. The piston on the left that is not pistoning but *being* piston. The transformer on the right that presses 60 hertz into your molars. The outer skin of the tower high above, taking real wind and conducting it down through every strut until the whole pocket rings like it is inside a bell that was struck once at the beginning of time and never stopped. You don't hear it after ten seconds. You become located by it. It tells you, with pressure: you are *here*, between these things. It is the only thing in the room that feels like it wants to be bigger, and it gets its wish —

— because of the last resident, which is the emptiness.

Weirdness 1.9, strange but not impossible: when you look straight at the floor, you see grating, and 40 centimeters below that, plate. When you look out of the corner of your eye, that same grating falls away. The frosted depth goes down and down past machines that shouldn't fit, into a depth that is cold and bright and empty. The pocket feels bigger when you are not policing its edges. That peripheral depth is a kind of life too. It lives in the corner of your vision. It moves away when you try to see it properly, which is exactly what the five ferns do. They are not growing into the room. They are growing into the idea that the room is larger than its making.

That is who lives here. Five small, static, white mouths eating oil and static, holding apart from each other with politeness. A designed cold that never sleeps. A deafening note that never decays. And a little extra space that only exists when you aren't looking directly at it, keeping them all company so the sparseness doesn't get lonely.
