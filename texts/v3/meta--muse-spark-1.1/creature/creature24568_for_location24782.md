Nothing lives there the way a fish lives in a lake.

That's the first thing it teaches you. If you come up here expecting to find something swimming, you will see only the suggestions - the kelp-strokes, the panic-fish that the turbulence assembles and then forgets. Those aren't residents. Those are drafts. Typos the water makes while it tries to learn how to be an ocean.

What lives there is longer, slower, and it doesn't need to be fed.

Start with the edge of its body. You thought that was weather.

For three meters out from the rim, where the air gives up and becomes hanging rain — the beads that form out of nothing and never fall, that orbit the main mass like moons — look closer. Not all of them are water.

Some of them are clear and slightly wrong. They have too much surface. They hang a little too still in the roar that should be tearing everything apart. They are about the size of the first joint of your thumb, and when you breathe out, they lean *into* your breath instead of away from it.

They are the young. Or the eggs. Or the lungs.

The locals, if you could call them that, are called Hangers by nobody, because nobody stays long enough to name them. Each one is a sphere of water holding a sphere of vacuum holding a sphere of water again, like a pearl made of a mistake in pressure. The column makes them constantly when one of those deep low detonations goes off — the boom that you feel in your molars — a cavitation bubble that should collapse and doesn't. Something lives in the failure to collapse.

Inside each bead there is a flicker, a thread, too straight to be organic and too alive to be mineral. A black filament, the same as the tall calligraphy strokes in the main column. In the Hangers it is folded tight, like a fern fiddlehead. In the main column it is unfolded, twenty meters long, hanging in the churn without being torn apart because it isn't *in* the water - it is in the *shear* between waters moving opposite ways. It lives on differential.

It is one thing.

All those black strokes you saw? They are not individuals. They are the arms of a single distributed animal, let yourself think of it as kelp, but it is really more like a root system made of current. It has no center in the column. Its center is in the beads. The beads are its seeds and its brain and its anchors. When a bead is pulled back into the main mass, its filament unspools and becomes one of those long strokes. It will spend hours to days holding, stretched between two layers of water that want to go different directions, straining, eating the energy of their disagreement. You can see it shiver. That shivering is work. That is how it feeds — not on light, not on plankton, there is no light or plankton here, but on friction itself.

Then it frays. That spray of fibers you saw? That's not death. That's reproduction. The filament divides along its length into a hundred smaller filaments until each one is small enough that the water can't hold it anymore and it beads up, and a droplet is ejected — not down, not up, but sideways, into the suspended halo — and there it becomes a new Hanger, hanging in mid-air that is also sea, learning how to stay still.

That is why the rain never touches the river stones. The Hangers don't allow it. The spiral of dry stones between the three leaning figures is not sacred to people. It is a bald patch, an area where the Hangers have learned to not be, because the figures are shaped like cupped hands, and cupped hands make a little pocket of air that is too still. The Hangers need the roar. They need the shear. Stillness kills them more completely than heat would kill you.

When you threw — or thought about throwing — a river stone into the column, what threw it back wasn't the water. It was this animal. The filament-network contracting all at once. A reflex. A clearing of the throat. Anything solid, anything that tries to root or claim or sink or be a bottom for it — it punches out. It cannot tolerate a bottom. Its whole biology depends on there never being a bottom.

And so it tends the void.

If you stay long enough to go truly deaf and your eyes stop trying to cry, you will see the other one. The one that is actually living *in* the impossible water, not just in the argument about it.

Deep down, where the blue goes black and the column stops being a column and becomes a throat into somewhere else, there is a knot that never moves. While everything else churns at insane speeds, it holds. It is not big. At first you think it is a shadow or a trick of compressed water, a darker spinning thing maybe two meters across. It doesn't assemble out of turbulence. The turbulence assembles *around* it.

It has the color of old bruises and it has no face, but you think of it as her anyway, because the three stone figures are leaning toward her with their empty hands. She is made of that same filament-stuff, but braided, supercoiled, dense enough that light can't get through it right. She looks like kelp and like scar tissue and like a closed fist.

She is the Weight. The first seed that came up when the mountain was new and the water pushed up where it shouldn't. She found this high table and decided to hold it, because if she lets go the whole pillar will finally remember gravity and fall — either down onto the scree and boil away, or down into that endless elsewhere and leave the pit dry. So she holds. The booming and the shrieking and the roar you hear — most of that is her. The water shearing against her grip. The vacuum detonations are her knuckles popping.

The fish that flash into existence are her dreams, briefly, of companions. She makes them out of eddy and loneliness and then she can't keep them together because she has forgotten what a fish is supposed to be for.

She will not be there long, on geological time. The basalt is cooling. The air is winning. One winter soon — not this one, but one that's coming — the top will skim with ice, the Hangers will not be able to hang, the shear will stop, and she will finally lose her grip. Then this room will be just a room again, a deep dry pit full of black glass and three faceless figures holding out empty hands toward a dry spot.

But right now she lives there, up high where nothing should. Deaf, unrooted, furiously busy remaining herself.
