They are not one thing. Not yet.

At first, when you stand on the Top and realize the ground is breathing, you think insect. You think crab. You think a tortoise that has been stepped on and taught to be quiet for a few million years. Then you get down on your knees and the categories fall off, because the air is warm and the stone is warm and the thing under your hand is warm in a different way.

What lives here is the ground. Or a second ground that has learned how to wear the first one as a color.

Call them pavers, for lack of anything better. There is no local name because no one local in the human sense has ever stayed long enough to need one.

Each one is about as wide as a large tortoise, but much lower. If a tortoise were pressed by the sky until its dome became a shallow arch of shingles. The back is not shell in the turtle sense. It's plates. Twelve, fourteen, sometimes twenty overlapping plates, each as thin as a fingernail and as broad as your palm, loose-jointed over one another like slates on a roof. The plates are not grown to be hard. They are grown to be the exact color of the peneplain at that exact spot — not just stone-colored, but *that* stone's color, pale bone with a faint pink of salt beneath, so that from two meters up you truly cannot see the animal, only stone that seems oddly domed. The plates are dusty. If you breathe on them close up, the dust doesn't blow off; it is the plate, exuded, fine rock-flour cemented with wax.

They have no head that you would recognize. The front is only slightly narrower. Underneath, where the damp lives, is the animal.

Turn one over — and you shouldn't, because it will take it a day to right itself and that day will be a hard one, and because the smell that comes up is the intimate, hurt smell of wet limestone — and you see why the Top is never fully dry. The underside is a broad, circular foot, cream-yellow and slick, ringed with a frill of fine cilia that hold water like a rudder holds air. In the center of the foot is a mouth that isn't a mouth but a low, soft rasp, a radial tongue of chitinous grit that works the stone. They do not chew. They lap. They sand. When the afternoon cloud lowers and the bacterial varnish blooms black across the grooves for an hour, when the salt efflorescence deliquesces into a brief brine, that mouth extends a millimeter and kisses the rock and takes it.

That is what they eat: the day. The Top makes a living of almost nothing — a few hours of mist, a microns-thick skin of cyanobacteria and lichen that rehydrates and photosynthesizes frantically in the damp hour, then precipitates salt as it dries. The pavers graze it off. They graze the stone itself, pitting it over centuries into a soft honeycomb that holds more water next time. Underneath a big one, the rock is never bone-pale. It's dark, pitted, and cool to the touch, and it glistens even at noon.

They have no eyes. Or rather, the seams between their plates are eyes. Each seam can open a fraction, exposing a strip of light-sensitive epithelium that doesn't form images, only gradients. They feel light as a direction and a weight. In the morning, when the sun is still honest and overhead hasn't happened yet, the gradient is simple, and so they all turn. You come back from making water behind that stunted white tree and you swear the field has shifted, though you didn't see it move. Every dome has rotated perhaps five degrees. Every seam points east. Not roughly. Geometrically. There is magnetite in the oldest plate, the apical one. They are compasses because the Top is old enough that its magnetic field has wandered, and they remembered.

In the afternoon, when the cloud comes and the world goes soft and gray and beading, they turn again, even more slowly. The seams gape. The plates lift like blinds. The arch flattens. They become bowls. The water that condenses on millions of arched backs runs inward instead of off, into that fur of cilia, into the reservoir under the foot. You can hear it then if you put your ear close enough, not a drinking but a wicking, a movement of water up a hundred thousand microscopic hairs.

They move. You think they don't until you realize the one that was by your left boot this morning is now over your bootlace. They move by peristalsis of that great foot, a slow hydraulic creep over their own slime. A meter a day if they're in a hurry. Most days they don't hurry. There is nowhere to go where there isn't more stone and more mist and another paver already dimpling it. They move away from their own grazed shadow.

And they build, because water wants to be kept.

They are all the same animal, there are no castes, but an old one that has lived long enough to have thickened plates starts to do a different thing. Instead of grazing, it grazes and shits building material. It scrapes stone flour and half-digested bacterial mat and sheds a plate, chews that plate into paste with its own mucus, carries the paste a body-length, and plasters it. Then again. And again. For months. Other old ones join. They don't cooperate. They accrete around the same place because water stays longer there, and so the place becomes more attractive.

This is the tower. From a distance it looks like termite work, but up close it is clearly made of the animals themselves — you can see the ghosts of plates in the wall, the layered secretion like cold honey. It's hollow, open at the top, the inside always weeping. It's a lung, a condenser. At night the stone cools faster than the air inside the chimney, and water beads continuously even when the mist has left. It runs down and pools at the foot where the young, the smallest, palest pavers crowd so thick you can't see the ground, translucent as rice paper, drinking through their entire bodies.

When a tower dies — when the wind finally takes the lip off or the base dissolves — the ring that remains is the only place on the Top where the rule breaks. The pitted stone underneath has been kept damp so long it has become soil, a centimeter of black-green that is not lichen but true moss, true leaf. It lives only there, in those fallen circles. It has no other strategy. It lives because the pavers made a well and then forgot it. In its turf live things even smaller than the pavers — blind white springtails, a mite that glitters red — the only real fauna upon the fauna, passengers on the car.

They die rarely, and slowly. A paver that has been underneath others too long goes pale and stops moving. Its foot dries. Its plates fall free and become ground again. Nothing eats it. The salt takes it. There is no stink of death here. There is only a thinning.

At dusk, as you described, the whole plain closes. This is worth kneeling for again. Because it is not on command. There is no suddenness. One by one by millions, the plates slide shut. You hear, finally, the only sound they have ever made: a faint, subsonic creak, like a house settling far away, plate edge against plate edge. The field that was bowls becomes stone again. Under each dome, in the dark space between foot and rock, a few drops of water are held, cupped, saved from the warm night that would take them. Under each dome, that mouth rests against its own small garden of wet stone, waiting for it to grow back.

If you stand too long with your boots in it, they will come onto you. Not from hunger. From worse — from indifference. To them, a boot is just another swell in the peneplain, a little higher, a little shadier, a little more likely to gather dew. They will flow onto it, and over it, and after a while you will understand that you are not being attacked or even noticed. You are being incorporated. Weathered. Made into Top.
