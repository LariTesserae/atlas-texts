They don't live here now. That's what you think at first, because the cold is biting too deep into your shins and the dripping is too loud for anything to think in.

Then you realize the houses are dry.

In a place where water appears out of the air itself, where every surface sweats, where your own shoulders are beaded in five minutes — the inside of those little houses is dry. The little triangular doorways are set just proud of the wet. The floors inside are black basalt dust, fine as ash, and not a single bead of water has fallen there. The lintel stones are lipped outward, just a little too deliberately. The clay chinking is thumbed into a tiny gutter that runs around each roof. Someone still knows how to keep water out.

That's who lives here. The ones who know how.

You don't see them right away because you are looking for movement and they have made stillness into a trade. You are a hot, loud, dripping thing that fell through their sky. When you came down through the throat-hole you brought noise and change and a warm column of air, and everything that lives here stopped and hid and held its breath until you learn how to be still again.

If you stay — if you let the ache climb from your ankles to your knees and you stop trying to hear over the plip-plok-plik — you start to hear the other drips. The ones that aren't drips.

In a thousand metronomes, one is off.

*Plip. Plok. Plik. Plip. Pat. Plok.*

A soft, bare foot coming out of a doorway and onto a wet ledge. Not a splash. They know never to splash. The water on the floor has to stay exactly that deep.

Then another. And another. They come out low and quick, because the doors are only a foot high and because staying tall is a good way to get hit by cold water. They are the color of the thing you thought was a fish. Not white, not translucent quite — more like the calcite that heals over sharp edges. A soft, thick white with a faint greenish light inside it, like the phosphor in the seams has gotten into their skin. Blind white. Wet-white.

They are small. You knew they were small, but small doesn't prepare you. A forearm long if they stretched, but they never stretch. They are folded. Curled in on themselves even when they walk, shoulders up to ears, heads tucked, knees bent high. As if they were designed to live in a place you can't stand up straight in. No sharpness to them either — that's the second rule. In a place where nothing sharp is allowed to remain, they have no elbows, no chins. Their hands are broad and spade-like, the fingers fused halfway and then tipped with blunt keratin, good for scooping clay. Their feet are wider still, round as lily pads, spreading their weight so they don't sink into the black pool. The soles are grooved, concentric rings worn smooth and then worn smooth again, and those grooves are what you saw in the thresholds. A thousand years of going in and out the same doorway in the wet, polishing the stone.

They don't have eyes. Not really. There are pits where eyes should be, lidded over with that same calcite skin, because what would you look at here? The light never changes. The room never changes. Sight would be a cruelty in a room this crowded and this loud. Instead their whole head is an ear, a smooth dome that tenses and turns toward each sound. That is why they froze when you came. You were a landslide of sound.

Now, when you have gone quiet long enough that the room starts to forget you, they go back to work.

One will crouch at the edge of a hut and run its wide hands along the little clay gutter, clearing it with a thumb. The water sheets off its back — it doesn't seem to mind being wet, but it minds very much where the wet goes. Another will pick up the three smooth stones from its shelf — always three — and resort them by size, and by how cold they are, and by some third metric you don't have an organ for. Another will go down to the ankle-deep mirror, belly almost touching it, and wait.

That's for the other thing that lives here. The pale thumb-long thing that makes the V-wake. They don't farm them. They tend them. The pale thing is blind too, and even shyer, and it swims the permanent pool eating whatever falls down the throat-hole when the upper world leaks — a mite, a root-hair, a fleck of skin from your shin you didn't know you lost. The little folded ones stroke the water and coo, not with a voice — they have no voice, the room is too loud for voices — but with a low tap of their broad hands on stone: *tok. tok-tok.* Come here. It's safe. The pale things come and nudge their palms and leave a faint oil behind, and that oil is what made the dark smudge in the hearth-place. No fire. Just that pale fat, rubbed on stone to keep the dry inside dry, and to keep their white skin from cracking.

They live absurdly close. The alleys are not alleys, they're the spaces between neighbors who want to touch. You thought it felt crowded when it was empty — now you understand. Two hundred huts and each with three or four inside, all folded together like fingers, all listening to the same ceiling. They build not because they need more houses — the old ones never fall — but because there is nothing else to do in equilibrium except make a little more dryness for someone else to curl into. A new family buds off when a hut gets too warm from too many bodies, and they go find a ledge that sweats a little less and start piling stone.

They don't die often. Nothing sharp, nothing changing, nothing to catch. When one does, they don't bury it. There is nowhere to bury. They carry it, very carefully, to the deepest part of the pool where your feet made ripples, and lay it flat. The water there is cold enough to bite. The calcite comes. It grows over the soft white skin like healed skin over a broken stone, lipping it, softening it, until it is just another smooth, bellied lump in the wall. Another place to put a house.

You shift, because your knees are going numb, and a single drop hits the pool wrong — *PLOK!* — loud as a gunshot in your molars. Every domed white head snaps toward you at once. Every doorway that had emptied is suddenly full again, broad hands braced on either side, listening to the huge, hot, temporary thing that leaked into their blister.

You are too big. You are too warm. You are a leak in their perfect budget of water. They will wait until you pull yourself back up through the throat-hole, as all big things eventually do.

Then they will come out with their spade hands and smooth over your footprints in the black dust, and clear the gutters, and re-sort their three stones, and go on keeping the floor exactly as deep as it has always been.

*Plip. Plok. Plik.*

Plok.
