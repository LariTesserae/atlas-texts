They don't live here the way people live in a valley. Nothing here has the luxury of being permanent. They live here the way a flame lives on a wick — by constant work.

Start with what is actually here all winter, because that's the test.

The moss.

It is the landlord. A species with no name down below, up here it is everything. *Sphagnum aurimontana* or close enough, but that makes it sound like a specimen. In the bowl it is a body. A meter deep in the center, maybe more, building itself out of its own dead, saturated to 90% water, springy and alive on top and black and cold and airless just an inch down. You step and it takes your weight and sighs water up around your boot and then slowly erases your print. It doesn't grow up; it grows out, sideways, into itself, a tight-cheeked cushion that knows stone is warmer than air and hugs it. 

If you pull it apart — and something does, constantly — inside each hummock is a little climate. The peat holds last week's sun. There are mites the color of rust, tardigrades like moving commas, white blind springtails that pop when the sun hits them. The moss is loud in a way you have to put your ear down to hear: it ticks and gulps as it drinks the rock's drool.

Living on and inside the moss is the second tier. The ones who make the place look empty to your satellite.

Beetles. Not many kinds, but obscene numbers. Small, polished, wingless ground beetles, *Nebria nigerrima*, that never fly because there is nowhere to fly to. They run under the hummocks in highways, eating the mites, eating each other. And in the small black pools that each hairgrass tussock cups in its roots, midge larvae hang in commas by the thousands, waiting out the freeze in a state that isn't quite alive. Then, for two weeks in high summer, they are all alive at once.

That is when the bowl becomes crowded. The air fills.

The bumblebee queens — *Bombus superbus*, huge, black as basalt with a single orange band like a wound — overwinter alone in the old vesicle holes in the west wall, buried in moss. They wake early. They are too big for this thin air and you can hear the work of it, that furious, low, unhappy buzz as they haul themselves from saxifrage star to saxifrage star. There are no workers up here, ever. Only queens. Each one is a colony that decided being alone was safer. They work the white flowers with a desperation that isn't about honey. It's about heat. They shiver on the flower.

Above them, the midges you saw as smoke. They are born and mate and die in one afternoon, a single pulsing column that leans with the wind over the rim. The moths come for them, glasswings, almost invisible until the sun hits.

And the birds that own the holes.

They are the only real vertebrate residents, and they are not residents at all. They commute. White-winged Snowfinches, thirty or forty pairs, and a smaller mob of Alpine Choughs with mustard-yellow beaks. They don't build nests. They occupy. Each vesicle in the dark, rounded basalt is already the perfect size — widened by a thousand years of water freezing and birds prying — so they just tuck a little yak hair and lichen in and lay. The finches are quiet, efficient; the choughs are loud in the way crows are loud when they know they are the smartest thing on the mountain. They scream when the wind comes through the eastern notch and they ride it like it owes them money.

Under them, the marmot.

There is one. Maybe there have always been one, though obviously not the same one. An impossibly fat, pale brown marmot, old female, her coat thick as felt. She lives under the low wall on the rim, where the moss is cropped short. That's her doing. She eats it. She keeps a lawn. She has dug a burrow that goes down into the warm dark under the moss and then down further into a crack where the water disappears. On clear days she sits on the flat seat-stone and whistles, and the sound is so sharp in the cupped ear of the bowl that every beetle stops for half a second.

The wall. The bells. The rope.

No one lives here year-round. No human could. Thin air, no fuel, wet that gets into your bones and stays until September.

But someone lives *with* here.

A caretaker. Not a village. A practice. In the low valleys three days down, in villages where the air is thick enough to keep a fire going, there are old women and some younger women who have taken the braid. They call themselves the Empty-Ear Sisters or just the listeners. When a girl is too restless for weaving and too careful for herding, they send her up here for a summer with a hammer, a bag of tin bells, and a slate.

Her job is not to survive. It is to tend.

She re-lays the knee-high windbreak after the frost heaves it. She checks the pegs of split hornbeam that mark the safe line across the hanging marsh, because if a pilgrim or a yak strays off it they will sink to the knee and the moss will fill in over their foot. She clips the marmot's lawn with a bone knife, not because the marmot needs it but because tradition says the moss in the wall must be short enough to see where you are. She re-ties the knotted cord fences — they are not to keep animals out. They are to keep the snowfinches from eating all the saxifrage seed. A small, polite negotiation.

And she listens to the drain.

In the very center, where the moss is deepest and blackest, the water convenes. There is no creek. It just sinks. If you put your ear to the moss when the cloud is sitting in the bowl with you, when the bells have gone quiet because the wool is too wet to move them, you hear it: a hollow, patient *tok... tok... tok* far below, like water dropping onto stone in a big room.

Which is exactly what it is. The density 0.0 told you. The mountain is hollow here. Old lava blister, never collapsed. The bowl is its roof, thinning.

The sisters know this is the point. They put river pebbles in the cist under the cairn — smooth, far-traveled stones — so the mountain has something to listen to that isn't its own drip. They hang the tin bells so the wind keeps reminding the hollow that there is still a top. They play the bone flute down into the sinkhole when the cloud is low, not as music, but as proof that someone small, furious, sideways-growing, and temporarily alive is still up here managing it.

That is what lives here: a bog that thinks it is a body, insects that think they are a weather system, birds that think they are landlords, a single marmot who thinks she is a monk, and, for a few weeks a year, a girl with cold hands and a flute who thinks she is keeping the mountain from swallowing its own puddle.

When she leaves, before first snow, she tips the slate back over the cist and the place goes back to being too loud and too empty to be uninhabited.
