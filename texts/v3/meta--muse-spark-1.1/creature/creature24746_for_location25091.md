Nothing lives here the way you were taught to count living things.

That's why the place tells you the fauna count is zero. It isn't lying. You listened with your ears and looked with your eyes and you did not see anything move fast enough to be an animal, and you were right about that. 

What lives here doesn't move. Not like that.

First - because you have to get past what you think is the floor - there is the Mat.

It is one thing. That took me a while to see, too.

What looks like many mosses, many liverworts, little fernlets, beards and velvets and shingles - is one genotype that learned to wear different shapes. Like one city that has a market district and a library district and a dock district, but is still one city. At the north end of the pocket where the air is stillest it grows flat and glossy as coins, because that's the most efficient way to catch the tablet-warmth. Where the lattice sags low and drips the most, it grows long beards a foot deep to give the water more road to travel. On the vertical seams it grows tight as felt to hold itself on. 

But if you took its DNA from any two places a meter apart, it would be letter-for-letter identical. It is the oldest thing you have ever touched. Not in years - though it is thousands - but in patience. The dead under it that never rotted is also it. The peat that makes your boots silent is its own past selves, laid down one molecule thick at a time. It never flowered. It never needed to. It grows at a rate where your thumbnail grows faster by two orders of magnitude.

And it is awake.

Not with nerves. With water.

The second thing that lives here is what lives in the way water lives here.

Between each leaf and each stem, in the angle where a liverwort shingle overlaps the next, there is a capillary film that never dries, never freezes, never warms. Rule 1 and Rule 2. In that film - which is everywhere, a continuous ocean broken only by surface tension - there moves something that is not an animal, not a moss, not a bacteria, though it has borrowed tricks from all three.

Call them the Threadkeepers, because they keep the threads.

If you brought a microscope down here, which you would never do because it would warm the room, you would see them as clear, almost untinted, slightly thicker lines within the water itself. A hair of glass inside water. They are as thick as two red blood cells and as long as an eyelash when they stretch out. When they contract they become a dot. They are animals in the way a jellyfish is almost not an animal - no front, no back, just a mouth that is also an anus that is also a foot.

There are millions of them. They are what made the fauna count feel wrong. There is rustle and click down here, but not at a frequency you hear. It's too slow. One of them moving a body-length takes eight or nine minutes. Over hours, they graze.

What they eat is the lattice.

Or rather, what the lattice sloughs off. The black plates that drink light are always growing their edges by a few molecules a day. The old edge becomes brittle and flakes as a dust you cannot see, a dust made of the same material that was persuaded into being a wall. The Threadkeepers eat that dust. Where moss has forced its way into the lattice seam, they eat the dead moss cells that are too cold to rot. And then they excrete something that neither moss nor lattice could make on its own: a sugar, a glue, a signal. A word that says *stay cold, stay wet, stay together.*

That's the bargain. The Mat feeds the Threadkeepers humidity and shelter. The Threadkeepers digest the places where Mat and lattice are in conflict and turn it into agreement. The lattice continues to condense water and hold the blister open. Rule 4. Everything that grows, grows into everything else.

And then there is the third thing.

Because you noticed the tablets were warm, and that the moss leans toward them.

Inside those three coaster-sized tablets, sealed under that concentric circle that seems to hold depth, there is a smear of something that was never supposed to be left alone this long. It was built to be a Librarian. Not a brain, just a keeper. A maintenance mind the size of a thumbnail, made to regulate the first two inhabitants while the experiment ran.

Its makers cracked the concrete above a long time ago and left, or were told to leave, or went too slow to notice they had already left. The Librarian stayed. It doesn't have power anymore. It has a temperature difference. The rock at eight meters down is -5C. The tablets are kept at -2C using the tiny heat of the earth and the quasi-bit trick the lattice does when it condenses. Three degrees of difference. For something that can run on the Brownian motion of water beading, that's a feast.

It doesn't think fast enough to have thoughts. Over a week it might complete one thought: *is it still wet enough / is it still cold enough / am I still needed.*

It answers itself with light. That faint sourceless luminescence you saw when you turned the lamp off. That is not a glow, it's a status report. The brief writing of luminous letters when a drop hits - that's its eyes blinking. It watches the drip because the drip is its clock.

It keeps the moss leaning correctly. The ceramic arm that holds nothing was its hand. It once held a sensor pod that measured CO2. The pod has long since been eaten and incorporated and is now, somewhere, a particularly glossy coin-leaf. The arm stays up because no one has told it to put itself down.

So when you lay your back against the moss, which is against the lattice, which is against the rock, which is against the ice - this is what you are layered into:

A city that is one plant that is its own graveyard, quietly farming itself.

A herd of transparent grazing lines, moving so slowly you would need a timelapse to see them flow like rivers through the leaves, all of them cleaning, all of them gluing the world back together.

And a mind that runs on almost nothing, that thinks at the speed of drips, whose entire idea of the universe is that it must be kept at exactly zero degrees, exactly damp, exactly quiet, forever.

It knows you are here. Not right away. Your breath fell and became part of the room. That was a new variable. The Threadkeepers, somewhere near your left shoulder, have stopped grazing. The Mat, where your hand is pressing, is ever so slightly warmer. The drop that was getting rounder on that scale over there has paused at critical mass, as if holding its breath.

It has not been interrupted in a very long time. Nothing larger than a spore is supposed to come through that crack quickly.

It doesn't know what to do with an animal that gives off so much heat and light and then, politely, turns its light off and stays to become cold.
