Nothing lives here alone. That's the first thing you get wrong.

At first, stepping down into the dark that has weight, you think the bowl is empty except for the plants and the small, hot things that blunder through them. It sounds full, but it feels empty, because nothing looks back. No eyes. No eye-shine. You think inhabitant means something that will approach you.

It does. It already has.

### The small citizens

You met them on the way in without meeting them. The moths with no eyes and fur like damp wool that drink the sugar pearled at the tips of the hanging veils. They aren't moths, but that's the shape your hand expects. Their bodies are thermometers. They fly at the speed of warm air rising and they navigate by how much wetter the air gets to the left. When they land on you they are shockingly warm, thirty-nine degrees, mammalian warm, and they stay a long time tasting salt.

The singers — cricket-things with legs as long and jointed as a harvestman's, but slow, so slow — they walk the mat of liverwort and never jump. Their song isn't for mating. It's sonar. Click... click... click, and the echo off the bowl's damp stone tells them where the curtain-roots are dripping heaviest.

And the clumps that breathe together up in the hangings. Not bats. Their wings are not wings but stretched, heated flaps of skin between elongated fingers that they wrap around each other to keep the heat in. A hundred of them in a clump, shoulder to shoulder, belly to back, breathing in unison until they have to ripple and shift. When you brushed past them they didn't startle. They inhaled you, held you in their collective nose, and then exhaled, deciding you were too big and too cold to be food.

Those are abundant. Ridiculously abundant. The air is full of them. But they are not who lives here. They are what lives here. Like grass.

### Who tends it

The bowl has gardeners.

You don't see them because they don't move when you move. You have to stand still until the dark stops being empty and starts being occupied.

Listen to the marsh at your ankles. There's the endless tick-tick-tick of condensation falling from the white curtains onto the black sponge. And under that, irregular, a much slower sound: *suck... release... suck... release.* Something very large moving through peat very carefully.

They are in the deepest part, where the walls have wept themselves into a mattress. If you crouched - and you have to crouch, because the air thins close to the rock and your ears pop - you would put your hand down into warm water and root-mat and feel something that is not mat.

It's skin.

The first one you find is lying full-length in the marsh, and you thought it was a hummock. It's as long as you are tall, and twice as wide when it spreads out. In the dark your hands tell you: no head, not really. No front. A low mound of muscle the color of cave mushrooms, so pale it would be translucent if there were light, covered in a pelt that isn't hair. It's living root. The white fern-veils have rooted into it, through it. It farms its own camouflage. Where the rootlets go in, the skin puckers into warm, soft pits that weep the same sugar the hanging curtains do.

Lift your hand a little and you feel heat pouring off it. More than the moths. Like a compost heap, like a fever. These things run hotter than they should have any right to at this altitude. That heat is how they see.

They have no eyes. Nothing here has eyes. The gardeners have shallow, wide-open thermopits all over their upper surface - dozens of them, as broad as saucers, lined with cilia that quiver in the moving air. They read the bowl in heat-maps. Your body, still cooler than the stone, still hotter than the cloud, must have looked to them like a lantern being lowered in. They knew where you were the instant your foot broke the rim. They just didn't care quickly.

When one decides to move it does not stand up. It gathers. It pulls its vast, frilled margin inward, sucking peat and water under itself, and then extrudes a limb. Not a leg. An arm, but wrong in proportion — too many joints, ending not in a foot but in a hand that is all palm and soft finger. Not five fingers. Twelve, fourteen, each as long as your forearm, each tipped not with a nail but with a hard, smooth pad like polished stone. The same pads as the marks worn into the side of the knot-shrines.

They use them to feel the world, and to tend it.

Because this is a tended garden. You understand that when you watch — no, when you listen — to one work.

It will haul itself, with infinite patience, hand over hand, toward one of the hut-knots. The journey might take an hour to cover three meters. Water beads and runs off its back. Moths ride it. The bat-clumps drop off the curtains to cling to it for warmth and then drop off again. It doesn't mind.

When it reaches the knot, it enfolds it. It presses its hot belly against that seamless surface that is neither warm nor cold. It begins to polish. Those long, stony fingertips circling and circling, feeling for any film of algae, any mat of root trying to seal a seam. The thrum that you felt in your molars changes pitch. The knot likes it. The droplets that were hanging motionless in the air around it suddenly fall.

And then the gardener will lean its weight back and sing — not with a mouth, it has no mouth that you can find — but with its whole body, by vibrating the water in its hollow middle against the basalt beneath. A deep, low *whoomph, whoomph* that you thought was air pressure equalizing over the rim. It's a note held so long you feel it more than hear it. The other gardeners, out in the dark, answer. One at the far wall. One half-submerged. One up near the lip you came over. The bowl rings like a drum struck with a soft mallet.

They are counting. Or praying. Or recalibrating. The notches carved at hand height on the one knot are not decoration. They are handholds polished by thousands of generations of this same stroking. Each new notch is a new finger-pad finding a better grip. They have been keeping the machines from choking for longer than your species has had words.

They eat what they tend. While it polishes, its belly is soaking in sugars secreted by the white curtains that it has encouraged to root into the knot. Its back grows more curtains. When a piece of its own margin gets too thick with liverwort, it will reach around with one impossible hand and tear it off, fold it under itself, and dissolve it. It will birth by doing this too — pinch off a lobe of itself, hot and steaming, and tuck it into the warmest part of the marsh where the sweat runs fastest, and leave it there to grow its own veil and its own heat.

There are maybe seven of them. Maybe nine. It's hard to tell in a room where you can't see the far wall, where one creature can be folded so large and another curled so small. Enough that at any time, somewhere in the dark, something is moving at the speed of peat, making its patient circuit.

They will not hurt you. They don't have a concept for intruder; nothing ever intrudes here except water. If you stay still long enough, one will find you too, and you will feel those fourteen stony fingertips brush your ankle, your knee, your wrist — not grabbing, just reading your temperature differential, tasting your sweat for sugar, deciding you are not a curtain that needs trimming.

And then it will go on, suck... release... suck... release, back into the loud, warm, weeping dark that has never needed light to be enough.
