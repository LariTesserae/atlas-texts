They live here because they fell correctly.

Not everyone who dropped when the road dropped made it. The first ones down thought they were still on the surface — thought this was still an accident with edges and insurance and a rescue coming. They left. The ones who stayed are the ones who understood, after the first night, that the air down here was already a different country and they were already breathing it.

You don't see them at first. That's part of it.

The Hollow should be loud — a hundred meters of tin and grate and people stacked shoulder to shoulder should be loud — and it isn't. So you think it's empty. Then your eyes adjust past the amber bulbs and you realize the quiet is *them*. They move the way water moves in here: not flowing away, looping.

They are narrow people. The heat made them that way, and the architecture finished the job. You can't be broad in a place where a rooftop is a floor is a ceiling. You turn sideways to pass. You learn to carry everything against your chest. Their shoulders are hunched, not from age but from the low catwalks. Their hands are always a little ahead of them, feeling.

Look at their hands.

Everyone's hands here are the same story. The basalt is young, glassy — it doesn't want to be touched. So the pads of their fingers are mapped with fine white scars. Not big cuts. The little ticking flakes that pop off the cooling rock at night do that. But the palms are soft, permanently soft, because nothing here ever fully dries. The skin prunes and stays pruned. It goes a little translucent. Under the amber light you can see the salt in them — the white bloom that crusts on everything black crusts on them too. At their hairlines, at the seams of their shirts, in the creases of their wrists. They sweat and it never evaporates, it just gathers more of itself.

They don't have dry clothes. No one does. Their shirts cling and then almost dry and then cling again when the 2 a.m. weather comes. So they stopped trying to be dry. The older ones wear almost nothing — cut-off coveralls, vests made from shade cloth, nothing that can hold water. Their lips are always cracked anyway, even with all that mist. You see them lick the condensation off the underside of a pipe without thinking about it.

They are quiet because the room forces it. Sound doesn't get interrupted here — no rats in the walls, no birds to cover you — if you shout on the middle deck, eighty people hear your throat click. So they speak in low, short trades. Not whispers. Whispers carry too. Just...plat. Flat words dropped straight down.

*"hold"*
*"up a bit"*
*"good — leave it"*

They work at night. Not because it's cooler — it's never cooler — but because that's when the water comes. At 2 a.m. when the desert above finally exhales cold air down the throat and the whole dense stack starts to bead and glitter, that's when you hear them move in earnest. The whole town pings with the tick of condensation on tin and with wrenches turning. That interior rain for twenty minutes is when they fill drums, when they wash, when they check the joints. Then the engine hisses louder and throws it all back into the air as hot mist and they go back to bracing.

That's what they do. They brace.

There is no mayor. There is no plan. There is just the third rule, which they never say out loud because they are living it: *You do not build a house, you build a shelf and hope someone else builds under you.* So a man will spend three days welding a perfect, level platform out of rebar and grate just slightly out from his own front door, for no reason that makes sense until a week later a woman and her kid have bolted a frame under it and now his floor is their roof and both structures are twice as strong because they lean on each other. Then someone else runs an extension cord across both and now they're on the same line. It is a town that only knows how to be added to.

The deepest ones are different.

Down near the sump, where the light is only furnace-glow from the cutting saws and the single-piston pump that never stops — *hiss... hiss... hiss* — the air is the thickest and the stone is still settling. Down there live the Keepers. They are older, or they just look older because they've been damp longer. Their skin has gone the color of the vegetation — not green, because green is expensive, but that black-green, that rust-plate color of the lichen mats. They don't scrape the mats off. The mats don't grow on them, but close. The white rootlets that hang without soil sometimes brush their heads as they pass and they duck without irritation, the way you would duck for a friend's laundry line.

The Keepers listen to two things: the drip and the tick. The drip tells them how much water they have in the loop. The tick tells them how much rock they are gaining. Because the walls are still popping, spalling little glassy births of mineral at night. They collect those flakes in tin cups and when the saw isn't running they crush them into paste for patching. Their ears are the best in the Hollow. In a place without animals, they became the animals that listen.

Children here learn early not to look up expecting sky. They learn to navigate by sound-shadow — the way the hiss gets muffled if there's a new tarp three tiers up, the way a footstep on grate three houses over tells you who is home. They play a game where they stay perfectly still during the interior rain and try to get hit by as few drops as possible. No one ever wins. They are small and fast and they look like they've been carved to fit the exact space left over between two adult elbows. Because they have been.

They are not sick. They are not sad. They think the surface is the strange place — too bright, too dry, too much wind that doesn't come back. They think you are strange for not knowing that if you rust, you rust from the inside out.

If you stay long enough — ten feet, thirty feet, down onto the main mesh deck where you can touch two opposite dwellings without stretching — one of them will offer you a dented cup of water that came off their own ceiling an hour ago, that was breathed out by the rock, that was drunk yesterday, that will be drunk tomorrow. They will watch you drink it to see if you understand the loop.

What lives here is not a population. It's a single wet, stacked, quietly adding, salt-bloomed, soft-palmed organism that learned how to make shelves out of itself so it wouldn't fall any further.
