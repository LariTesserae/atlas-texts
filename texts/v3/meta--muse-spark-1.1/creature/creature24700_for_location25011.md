They live by moving earth.

At first you think nothing lives here. That's what the newness does. The clay is still cold and clean, breaking under your thumbnail in plates, and it looks too raw to have been claimed. A room with no tenants. Then your eyes learn the scale.

First — the Builders.

They are amber. Not the amber of jewelry, but the amber of fresh sap, legs translucent when the light catches them. Atta. Leafcutters. You have to stop breathing so loud to see them correctly, because from standing up they are just a shiver on the ground, a fizz of motion.

Crouch and the shiver resolves into a nation.

The small ones, the minims, no bigger than a grain of rice, live almost entirely inside. You will rarely see them unless you put your eye to a doorway and wait. They ride on the leaf confetti and they clean. They lick. The air in the mounds is all fungus-breath and they tend it like nurses.

The medias are the river. Endless, patient. They make the avenues. You think at first that each one is deciding for herself, but after a minute you see it's one decision made by many bodies. A green sail of hog plum flickers and goes down a hole. Another follows the exact ghost of its path, antennae kissing the ground where the other stepped. They carry squares so big relative to themselves they have to walk backwards to balance, and they carry them without pause from the outside forest to the deep inside. Leaves go down. That is the law.

The majors, the soldiers, live like statues. They stand at corners where two swept roads meet, or at the rim of a plaza, heads like polished chestnuts, mandibles open just enough to be a threat. You can watch one for ten minutes and not see it move except for the slow sweep of an antenna. They are not aggressive here — the city is too new, too hidden for war. They are architecture that can bite.

And underneath all of them, under the towers that never get higher than your knee, is the one who really lives here.

The Fungus.

You have to get your face close to smell it, close enough that the ants will climb onto your knuckles to investigate the salt on you. Inside the granular towers, behind the labyrinth of round doors, the chambers pulse a faint, sour-sweet heat. White. Not mushroom white, but lung white, brain white. A cottony mass that fills the room wall to wall, riddled with tunnels the ants have chewed through it. This is the city inside the city. The reason for every leaf. They don't eat the leaves — they feed them to this. They masticate the hog plum into a paste and plaister it onto the white, and the white digests the forest for them. If you are still enough you can see it growing. The hyphae swelling. In the dimmest chamber, it glows, not with light but with the idea of light — so pale against the dark clay it seems lit from within. She is the queen's other body. Without her, there is no city.

Above her, below the fig, lives the old Queen herself. You will never see her. She is deeper than your eyes can go, in the oldest, darkest, dampest room where the clay is still stained black from the forest floor before the collapse. She is the size of your thumb joint, wing-scars on her back, blind, egg-swollen, attended constantly. She does not move. She is a heart. Every ant you see is an extension of her breathing. She has been alive longer than this room. She rode the nuptial flight in May, fell out of the hot air, tore off her own wings, and dug. When the ceiling fell in it wasn't a disaster — it was an expansion.

Then the Tenants, because a city that rich cannot stay unsquat.

The bronze frogs. They are not visitors, they are citizens of a different tax bracket. Eleutherodactylus, each one the size of your thumbnail, skin like wet leaf-litter, eyes like black glass beads. They sit one to a tower, in the shadow, throat pulsing but making no sound. Sound is forbidden here, vibration is news. They have learned silence. They drink. When a root hair overhead swells with condensation until it can no longer hold, they tilt their heads back and let the drop fall into their mouth. They eat the flies that would otherwise lay maggots in the fungus. The ants walk over their feet and do not mind them. A pact so old it has no beginning.

The polished beetles patrol the outer ring where the ants dump their waste — the exhausted fungus and dead sisters, carried out like trash to a midden you can smell from two feet away, sharp and ammoniac. The beetles live in that decay and roll it into balls and bury it, gardening the gardeners.

Through it all moves the shoelace. The blindsnake, white as the fungus, no thicker than a headphone wire. It has no eyes because it needs none. It swims through the service tunnels of loose earth, tongue flicking, eating ant larvae and the ant's own parasites. The workers will mob it if they meet it head-on, but usually they pass each other in the dark like trains.

High above them all, slung between two living thigh-thick roots with her knees tucked, lives the weaver. A nephila or near enough, tiger-striped, as big as your palm if you splay it. She has understood urban planning. Her web is not across the roads but above them, so the highways run clear underneath and she catches only what belongs to the air — the lone mosquito levitating in its own shaft of green light, the gnat, the stray winged male at season's end. She keeps the sky clean.

And smaller still: the mites that ride the ants' necks like cowboys, the bacteria in the clay that make that cold iron smell, the green algae that films the root where the light hits longest.

What lives here then is not a thing but a bargain. The fig holds the roof up, and drinks the waste water. The fungus eats the forest. The ants farm the fungus and carry the earth up, grain by grain, in their mandibles, which is why the floor is so strange and terrace-formed — it is all spoil, all foundation. The frogs keep the flies down. The snake keeps the sick brood down. The spider keeps the gnats down.

It is sparse on purpose. You can count the presences. One frog per tower. One gnat per light column. No crowding. That's how you know it is healthy. There is space left in the avenues, whole empty plazas of swept clay waiting.

It is waiting to be bigger.
