You don't see them at first.

From the rim, the Cut looks like failed construction. Like debris that fell in and got stuck. You have to watch for a while to understand that the debris is moving - not settling, but being continually moved.

Then you see a hand.

It doesn't look like a hand that lives in a house. It looks like a hand that lives in a glove that is always wet. Pale to the point of being translucent at the knuckles. Nails short and black-rimmed and filed flat by scraping stone. No rings, no ornament — ornament would hold water. The fingers are swollen at the second joint from cold and from the work of wringing. Everything here is wrung: thatch, clothes, hair, skins.

They are people. All of them.

But they are not bog people. Bog people are tall and ruddy even when they're starving. These are smaller. Not dwarfish small — made small, the way a plant in a crack makes itself small. A generation or two of living with a thirty-foot sky made of wet black glass will do that to a back, to a neck. They stoop without stooping, shoulders rolled forward around the lungs to keep heat in. Children look elderly. The old look ageless, because their skin has been damp so long it never had a chance to dry and wrinkle properly.

Their hair never really dries either. It is kept cut brutally short with that flat scraping stone, to keep it from holding water against the scalp. The white fungus that grows on the joints of the houses also tries to grow on them — in the crease behind the ear, between fingers — and so each morning, everyone scrapes. You hear it before you see it: a soft, rhythmic scritch-scritch, family after family sitting in their doorholes running a smooth shard over their own skin and each other's. They do it without comment, the way you would brush your teeth.

Their clothes are the same color as the rock. That is not a choice. Alder smoke and peat smoke and standing water dye everything the same black-brown in a week. Wool that has not been dry in five years weighs twice what wool should weigh. So they wear as little of it as they can bear, and what they wear is cut tight to the body, patched with skins that are themselves always supple with damp. No loose cloaks. Nothing that drips when it doesn't have to. A child who goes wading in the sheet-water gets cuffed not for being careless but for bringing more water into the house.

Their eyes are the only thing that hasn't learned to conserve. Large, you think at first, until you realize it's that the lids are always pulled wide, to catch what little vertical light falls down the wound. The light in the Cut never comes straight. It hits one sweating basalt wall and bounces to the other, and then to a roof, and then to water, and by the time it reaches a face at the bottom it is secondhand. So they stare upward a lot, not hopefully, just habitually, tracking the brightness. Their irises are washed grey-green, like the bog pools. When one of those shutters opens and a face looks out at the sky, what you see is not wonder. It's a quick, practiced squint — checking the cloud color for rain — and then that face is gone, because a face in open air is a surface that loses heat and makes vapor.

They don't have jobs, they have maintenance. You cannot live in the Cut the way you live in a village. You keep a place from disappearing.

There is the Bail. That's everyone, in rotation. Two people with the carved buckets, always moving, ankle-deep, throwing the sheet-water up and out to the bog on ropes. If they stop for an hour, the lowest houses take water over their thresholds.

There is the Scrape. The children and the old ones do that best, with small hands. Fungus, algae, the birch seedlings that try to root in the thatch. Anything green that is not in a peat box is an enemy, because green means it is holding water against wood that has to bear weight.

There is the Wedge. The men and women who watch the walls. Because the rock is young, it is still breaking. A crack will open with no noise at all — just a fresh dark line where there wasn't one — and water will bead there faster. That is good and bad. Good because it is new water and new flake-stone. They collect the flakes as they pop off, some no bigger than a fingernail, some as big as a hand, and they tuck them immediately under a sagging post or into the foundation of acousin's cousin's floor. The Cut eats itself and they eat the crumbs to keep it from closing.

They are very quiet, not because they are solemn but because condensation is a tax. You can see your breath all year. If you talk too much in your house, water runs down your ceiling all night. So their language has become low and consonantal, lots of clicks and pointed sounds you can make with a small mouth. They signal more than they speak — a tug on a rope means *bucket going up*, two tugs *bucket full, mind your head*, a palm flat on wet wood means *quiet, crack opening*.

At night, or what they call night — when the strip of sky goes from pewter to deep blue — they stack themselves. There is not enough dry flat to lie down alone. Families lie in shifts, in hammocks of plaited reed strung so tight they creak like bows. The babies sleep highest, up near the overhanging nests, where the air moves a little. They are born very pale and they do not cry loudly; a loud cry makes damp and cold air hit their lungs and they learn fast. The mothers chew the few onions to a paste and press it into those tiny mouths, not for nutrition, they believe, but to give the child something sharp enough to remember to swallow.

They do not believe the Cut was made by people. Or by gods. They believe it is a wound the flat country gave itself, because it was tired of holding all that water. And that they fell in. And that their job, for as long as the wound stays open — which will not be geologically long at all, a few thousand years, a blink — is to keep it from healing over while they are still inside it.

If you stand on the rim long enough, one of them will see you. They will not wave. They will watch you the way a person in a very crowded boat watches someone on the shore. Not with envy. With a kind of quiet, practical incomprehension. You are standing where water is supposed to be, and you are dry.

Then their bucket will be full, and they will turn back to the endless work of throwing the Cut out of itself, one ankle-deep sheet at a time.
