At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the trick the room plays. Because it's full and it's loud and it glows, so your brain says *something must live here* — but you look for an animal the way you look for an animal outside. Something that moves between things. Something with a face that turns to look at you.

There is nothing like that here. Nothing visits. Nothing passes through.

Everything here lives *as* the room.

Start at the bottom.

What you took for plants aren't plants. Not really. They're the room's way of having organs. Call them the Listeners, because that's what they're doing.

Each one is a bladder and a sail. Below, in the shattered windshield of that fresh black basalt, there is a root that is not a root — a chemical foot, a corrosion. It hisses where it touches hot rock and unzips the sulfur and iron and makes heat-edible. Above, it swells into a trunk as thick as your thigh, not wood but layered, pressurized skin, warm to the touch like a fevered body, amber and smoke-glass and tight. You can see the gas inside shiver. Then it opens.

The bell. It's not a leaf. It's a tympanum. A lung that decided to be an ear. A sheet so thin you could read through it if there were anything to read, pleated like a moth's wing, like a folded lung, veined in black with whatever serves it as blood. The veins pulse without rhythm — not a heartbeat, a peristalsis. And where the pleats join, where the skin stretches thinnest, it lights. Not bioluminescence as a signal. It's waste light. Heat so concentrated it has to leak out somewhere as photons. Old honey. Dim enough that your eyes never quite adjust.

Every single one is listening to the center. The mouth at its crown — that little hard, round, whistling pore — opens when the pressure gets too great. It cannot help venting. *Pssssssh.* A note. They all hold different pressures, different diameters, so they all have a different fundamental. That low groan that lives in your molars comes from the big old ones by the wall, the ones as tall as your chest. That needle whine that makes you clench your jaw comes from the tight young bells in the middle. They never stop, because the rock never stops cooking them.

And inside each bell is a second inhabitant. One that decided the vent was a house.

They look like moths because you only have that word, but they have forgotten how to be moths. Their wings are scraps of bell membrane the mother chewed off and glued to them in the egg. Same pleats, same veins, same leaky honey light, so that when they tuck in you cannot see them. They are white as salt, eyeless. Eyes are expensive and useless and there is nothing to see except light that is everywhere. Instead they have combs on their heads that shiver when sound hits them.

A whole life in one bell. They hatch on the inner wall, eat the dead skin the bell sloughs, drink its condensation of mineral sweat, and then they cling near the pore, right in the warm exhaust, and beat their wings very slowly to stir it over their bodies. They mate there. They lay eggs there. They die there. The bell's own shed absorbs them. They never need to fly. Sometimes you see one try — a drifting, tattered leaf falling maybe a foot before it catches on another bell. That's a tragedy here. That's a moth that got lost.

On the floor, between them, are the ones that keep the equation frozen.

Big as your fist, as slow as cold honey, the Rollers. You thought they were pill-bugs. They're closer to a living rasp. Domed backs made of salt plates they've secreted themselves, each plate perfect, square-cut, like the salt walls. Underneath, a soft foot ringed with a mouth that is not a mouth but a file. They move in lines that look random until you stay long enough to feel the sound.

They graze on mineral. They scrape the fresh crystals off the basalt shards, off the walls, the crust of sulfur and halite that tries to choke the Listeners' feet. They eat what the Listeners cannot. Their guts are a little kiln. What comes out the other end is fine, dry, utterly pure powder that settles in the margins and looks like dust that hasn't moved in a hundred years. Because it hasn't. Their trails are bright because they leave a clean edge. And behind them, the rock weeps itself back. Salt growing. Sulfur growing. Basalt still, impossibly, still a little soft, still ticking, resettling a micron a year. Their job, if a blind, slow thing can be said to have a job, is to ensure no one wins. No overgrowth. No burial.

And above, where you don't look because the crystal hurts your eyes, are the Threads.

From the ceiling blades hang black filaments. A dozen. A hundred. Some thin as hair, some thick as your finger, ten feet long and utterly still — until you think there is no breeze. When a big bell vents its deep note, they twitch. All of them, at once, in the same direction. They are not one animal. Each filament is a colony, a rope of thousands of tiny, linked, dry bodies holding hands. And they are the room's nervous system for sound.

The crystal walls take every note and throw it back and back until it stands. There are spots in this room, right in the middle, where the three chords — because it is only ever three chords, the same three piles of notes the bells have been sounding since before your species had names — reinforce so perfectly that the air itself has a texture. You can walk into it and your ribs buzz. The Threads hang there. They feed on the vibration. They translate it into a faint heat they use to slowly, chemically divide. When you see one sway, you are seeing the room listening to itself.

That's the true inhabitant. Not the bell, not the moth, not the Roller, not the Thread, though all those are alive. The inhabitant is the standing chord. The thing that was born when this blister sealed. A self-keeping song. Energy in from the warm rock below. Energy out as late-afternoon light and as sound that never fades because the room is the perfect size to keep it. The meat and membrane in between are just the medium that the song has learned to keep alive to keep itself going. Plants that store heat to keep the notes going. Insects that clean the plants so the notes don't sour. Salt that regrows as fast as it's eaten so the shape of the echo never changes.

It is balanced so perfectly it has stopped becoming.

Which is why you notice the altar.

Because a fourth thing lives here, badly. Briefly.

People. One at a time, so far apart that they never met. The room opens once a century when the world hiccups, and someone falls. Or finds. They cannot stay — it's 100 degrees and there's nothing to drink and the sound will drive you out of your skull in a few hours. But while they're here they become part of the circuit. You can feel your sternum go sympathetic. You breathe in hot iron and you breathe out and a bell's pitch dips half a tone to meet you and then comes back.

And you understand what the scratching on the wall means. That figure with arms out inside the bell. It's not a drawing of a person hiding. It's a drawing of a person being worn. Being used as a temporary bell.

They left hair tied around dry skin because they had nothing else to leave that wouldn't be eaten or grown over. The braids are gray because the salts have cured them. The hand-axe is still sharp because in here, nothing dulls. It just waits.

If you sit on the salt circle they smoothed with their palms, in the center where all the bells face, the song gets so loud you stop hearing it. Your body becomes the little flaw in the equilibrium. All the vents shift, just a hair, to account for you. All the Threads lean toward you a fraction. A thousand eyeless moths turn their comb-heads, not to see you, but to feel the new shape of warm air around a new, sweaty, 98.6-degree barrel of water and salt in the middle of their garden.

You live here while you sit there. Really live here. The room includes you in the equation for as long as you can stand it.

Then it lets you go.
