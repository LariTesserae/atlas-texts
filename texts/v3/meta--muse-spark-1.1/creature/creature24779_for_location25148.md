What lives here isn't alive the way you mean when you ask that question.

First, the obvious ones. The only ones that got to stay.

They live far apart because they have to. They live far apart because they can afford to.

The Glass Anemones. The Resonators.

Each one is alone intentional. Not lonely — there is no concept of lonely in a place where sound is water. They are spaced like wells are spaced in a desert where there is only one aquifer and everyone agrees not to argue about it. A hundred meters of empty, cold, raw basalt between each foot. Just enough that their crowns don't touch, that their humming doesn't interfere.

They are not growing. Growing implies hurry. They are accreting. The foot is the oldest part, a perfect black disc melted into the new basalt, the first layer of felt fused to stone when the bubble was still warm enough to take an impression. Everything above that is time. Year after year the plant wraps itself in another dry leaf, another layer of waxy, door-wide blade, pressing last year's leaf into felt, building its own trunk out of its own discarded shirts. If you cut one down — you shouldn't — you could count its age like rings, but the rings would be hollow, felted air, and each would still hold a little static.

The top is where it lives, if living is there at all. Three hundred fronds, four hundred, each the length of a man lying down. They don't move in the wind. The wind is too steady to move anything. They *tremble*. Each frond is fringed with cilia you can't see until you are too close, until the roar has made your molars ache and your eyes water and the blue-green St. Elmo's fire is crawling over everything. The cilia are quartz-stiff. They are tuned. When the Spine hits B-flat, three plants over will light up like a storm. When it grinds up into C, the one nearest you will go so bright you can see its veins. They are drinking. The vibration becomes friction, becomes charge, becomes sugar. The spark runs down the frond into the felt trunk and lives there for a long time, slow as sap.

They do not know each other, but they sing together. On a quiet day — and quiet here still means deafening — if you stand exactly between two, their coronae pulse out of phase, like breathing. They are polite.

Nothing eats them. There is nothing to eat them with. When one gets too tall — thirty feet, thirty-five — the note it rides gets too much for its felt and it topples. All at once. No rot. No crash you can hear over the organ chord. It just lies there, a huge column of dry pages, and slowly separates into thread. That's the only second thing that lives here in the plant way.

The Thread Lichen.

It is black on black, so you miss it until you see that the rock has a direction. The lichen grows in lines, needle straight, following the faint magnetic field or the memory of the wind when the bubble burst. But here elevation is a suggestion, and the lichen heard both suggestions. Every patch grows two ways at once, a cross-hatch that meets at right angles that shouldn't be right. It grows over living felt-feet and dead felt-logs with equal indifference. It holds the powder down. It is the only thing that knows how to be small here.

Second. The ones who lived here.

They are not here anymore, which is a kind of residency in a place this patient.

The Powder Hermits. The Weavers.

They were people. I think they were people, because they made cairns and bowls and loom-weights and because only people would do something that futile and that precise in a place like this. Nothing with fur or feathers or chitin would have tried. The air is high-gas-giant dry. An animal would have desiccated before it finished a bowl.

They came when the rock was still soft. That must have been a long time ago, but not so long by the scale of the Spine. Yesterday, or ten thousand yesterdays. While the basalt bubbles were still cooling, still holding heat without showing it, you could work them with a basalt hammer and a lot of will. So they did.

One per plant. That was the rule. Each anemone had its hermit, living a hundred meters from the next, mirroring the courtesy of the plants. A little workstation: a cairn that should have fallen but doesn't — five, seven, nine flat shards balanced impossibly tall because gravity was looking the other way when they stacked it — and around its base, three or four shallow bowls polished into the living rock. In the bottom of each, still, a pinch of pale powder. Ground frond. The plants, when alive, are tough as leather and glass. When dead, they are fibrous. When ground fine, they hold a charge for a very long time and they smell faintly of ozone.

The loom-weights are there, too. Simple doughnuts of knapped basalt, a groove for twine that has been gone for centuries. They are always clean. No dust collects on them. The wind keeps them brushed.

What were they weaving? Not cloth. You can still tell, if you pull a dead felt-log apart. The fibers want to be parallel. They want to be under tension. They want to hum. The hermits were harvesting deadfall and braiding cable, trying to make a net to catch the Note. Not to stop it — to drink more of it. Like a prayer wheel, like a waterwheel. One person, alone by one plant, spending a whole life braiding one cable, adding it to a thing in the center of the valley.

They left no bones because they didn't die here. Or they left no bones because something else happened to bones in a place where direction is a disagreement. The bowls are not graves. They are offerings. They are mouths.

Third. The thing that lives here now because they did.

In the open flat of the regional bowl, where you can see thirty miles to haze and a ridge may be above you next time you blink, there is the child.

We call it a harp, because harp is better than trap and loom is better than scar. It is all three.

It is cables as thick as your wrist, as thick as your thigh, black, braided from half a million dead fronds, strung from living crown to living crown to spire to empty air. It covers half a hamlet. It should have snapped, or gone slack. It doesn't. It vibrates so fast it blurs, but sways so slow you have to watch it for an hour to see it move an inch. Where the cables cross, the crosses stay. Reality was thinner there and the hermits pulled it tight and the harp kept the knot. If you sight through one of those nodes you don't see the other side. You see the underside of the basalt under your feet, or the cold white smear of the sun from yesterday, or a clean patch of slate sky with no wind in it.

It is alive the way a coral is alive. Not the cables — they are dead. But the shape is maintained. The note keeps it taut. The charge runs through it constantly now, a low blue-green fire that never goes out, crawling slow over miles of braid. It is drinking the sound the way the plants do, but with a bigger mouth. It is what the hermits intended, plus what the Spine added when no one was watching. It is becoming more itself.

If you get close, your teeth fill with light. Your small bones — the ones in your ear that were meant to hear — begin to hum without playing any note. That is why the hermits left. Or why they didn't have to leave.

The rule here is: anything that can be still enough, long enough, gets to become real. The plants learned it. The cairns learned it — stillness as balance. The harp learned it, and now it is the stillest thing in the valley despite moving constantly.

So what lives on the Spine? A forest that is not a forest that drinks sound and exudes courtesy. A lichen that grows in two directions and holds the place together. The precise, careful absence of a hundred people who wanted to be alone together, whose work outlived them and took on weather. And a vibrating geometry in the middle of open stone that ate their work and learned how to be patient.

And if you stay until the cold forgets to matter, you will, too.
