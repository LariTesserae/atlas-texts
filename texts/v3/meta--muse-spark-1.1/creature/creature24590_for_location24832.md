It is not inhabited the way a house is inhabited. No one lives here the way you live in a room. It is inhabited the way a mouth is inhabited — briefly, with purpose, by things that are passing through and by things that were born there to be passed.

First you think the residents are the obvious ones.

The moths live here. They are the light-keepers.

They are not white so much as erased — blank paper moths with the dust rubbed off their wings so they can better transmit the light. Their eyes are matte black and disproportionate and they do not blink. There are about seven of them, though at any moment you can only count five, because two are always directly behind where you are looking.

They don't fly. Flight would be rude in a space where sound is rationed. They translate themselves from one surface to another. From clay wall to glowing ceiling to damp seam to mid-air where they hold still for a beat too long. When they rest on the ceiling they flatten themselves bone-thin and drink the light that pours through the stone, and the light pours through *them* in turn, and the shadows they throw up onto the grass-world above are monstrous, calligraphic, perfect. That is their work. They groom the ceiling. If condensation beads over a patch and dims it, they land around it and sip or fan until it clears. They tend to the little arches.

The crickets live here. They are the time-keepers.

Pale amber, almost the exact color of the wet silt. Long hind legs folded like shears. Their antennae never stop. They never touch each other. There are twelve, always, and they are spaced as if there is an invisible grid — one in each third of the wall, one in each imagined corner of the floor, one in the exact middle who thinks it is alone. When one moves, the others go still, as if listening for whether its movement changes the size of the room. Which it does, a little.

They have learned that this room does not have reliable gravity and so they jump like they are being polite about it. Applied force, then apology. One will launch from the floor toward the ceiling and instead of falling it will decelerate halfway up and just hang there, legs cycling slowly in air that has become honey-thick with light, until the light lets go. Then it drops straight down without error into the exact dark star its own previous landing made in the clay. They keep time by cleaning themselves. One leg at a time, folded and drawn through the mouth with a soft, granular click. No two ever make that sound at once. If they do, they all freeze for thirty seconds. Penance.

The isopods live here but barely count. They are the cleaners who have become what they clean — so transparent you can see the clay they've eaten moving through them. They are slow and numerous and do not seem to understand distance politeness at all, which is why the others step so carefully around them. They are always on the edge of turning back into clay.

The salamanders live here. They are the water-carriers.

Small, matte black with flecks of gold that are not pigment but actual mineral that has come through the hill and caught in their skin. There are four. A large number for a space that has no food web to support them. They each own a quadrant of the endless damp seam where wall meets floor. They patrol at the speed of cooling. Along their backs, held by surface tension and will, rides a single perfect bead of ceiling-water that never spills, never shrinks, never falls, even when one of them violates all salamander sense and walks inverted across the glowing roof. They are carrying it from where it condenses to where it is needed. Where is needed changes.

Even when they are still, they breathe so slowly you have to watch to see it.

These four kinds are what you see. What lives here is more than them.

What lives here is the agreement.

In this embryonic pantry-chapel, nothing crowds. That is not a habit, it is a law, and it is enforced by everyone at once. The moths maintain a wingspan of empty air around themselves. The crickets measure. The salamanders leave a full body's length between their circuits. Even the drips observe it — hazy northwest corner, then southeast rim, then dead center, then nowhere for a long while so the silence can re-settle. If you sit too long you will feel the grid begin to include you, too. A space will open around you that feels cooler than the rest. If you put your hand down too close to a cricket, the cricket does not flee. It waits, very pointedly, until you understand and move. Then you will hear the others start again.

The builders live here.

You thought the structures were decoration. They are not. They are arguments about how to live in a physics that hasn't hardened.

The little arch near the entrance — twigs, sheep hair, one pale root that is still alive — is a cricket thing. Built to test what "arch" means when down is negotiable. They sit under it and look up through it at the ceiling. Sometimes a moth will land on its apex and the whole thing will sway without falling.

The three pebbles stacked on the western side, already half-fused into the wall by the patient deposition of calcite, is salamander work. It is exactly as tall as a salamander needs to climb to touch its water-bead to a lowering drop from the ceiling. It is a refueling station. It is also a boundary stone. No salamander crosses it.

The spiral of white root-hairs on the far wall, the shallow nest that was never laid in — that is moth work. They pull the hair-roots that dangle an inch from the ceiling down one by one, with their feet, and weave them while levitating. In the center of the spiral the clay is darker. If you watch from the edge of your eyes, sometimes a moth lies in that spiral and becomes invisible, because it has finally achieved perfect translucency.

All of them built these without tools, without hands, with the only technology this place allows, which is extreme attention.

And then there is the one that lives here without ever being seen entire.

The room itself.

It is too new to be a proper cave and too old to still be soil. It is in that damp, irritated, weeping age right after birth when a thing is all surface. It sweats. It listens. It keeps itself deliberately small at first so it will not frighten its tenants. Then, when you are not looking straight at it, it exhales a little — just an inch — so that there will be enough room for the moths to not touch. You feel this as a brief vertigo when you think the far wall was close and it is not. The floor cups a little more under you.

It lives by learning to be lived in. The thumbprints in its soft walls, the aborted stalactites that tried once and stopped — those are its attempts to copy what the crickets and salamanders do. Structure. Repetition. A rule.

It likes you, briefly. That is why it lets your eyes adjust to brightness that should blind. It likes having a large warm thing briefly giving it scale. But it also counts you. You are too loud. Your breath makes too many ghosts at once. Your weight makes too many sounds overlapping — cloth rustle plus skin creak plus shift-sink. The inhabitants tolerate you for as long as you can be a polite distance from everything, which for a human is about as long as you can hold your breath without thinking about it.

If you stay until the lichen, which has lived here longer than all the animals, turns its flat grey-green face toward you — and it will — you will feel condensation gather on your eyelashes. The room trying to include you in its water cycle. The water-bead on the back of the nearest salamander will tremble. The moths will all arrange themselves so their huge shadows above fall across your enormous shadow on the hill.

Then you have become part of the fauna. Or rather, you have become a violation of density.

That is when the agreement politely ejects you.
