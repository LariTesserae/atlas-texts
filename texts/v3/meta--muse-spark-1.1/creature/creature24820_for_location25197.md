They do not live here the way people live in a house. They live here the way lichen lives on a stone. The stone was here first, and will be longer.

There aren't many of them.

If you stand for a while at the bottom of the iron stairs and let your eyes adjust to the 0.6 light — the amber workbulbs swinging, the columns of street-light from the grates, the slow bruise of the lantern leaves — you will count the dwellings before you count the people. Ten, twelve at most, scattered the way you would scatter houses if you had a hundred meters of vault and a shared, unspoken agreement to not crowd each other. Or to not crowd what lives between you.

The first time you come, you might think it's abandoned. Because it's loud. 2.5 loud. You have to raise your voice to be heard over the engine's clank-thump-sigh and the frogs sawing in every bromeliad cup and the bats stitching the air and the tick, tick, tick of condensation on tin, and so you assume no one could live in that. You assume what lives here is the fauna, which is weather.

Then you see that the walkway you are on has been swept. Not recently, not neurotically — but the seed husks and the bolt-beetles and the little amber pools that collect in the low places between the mangrove-knees have been pushed aside with a broom made from bound aerial roots.

That is how you meet them. By their maintenance.

There are maybe eighteen now. Maybe fewer this year. The number inclines and declines. They are old for how young some of them look. Pale in a way that surface people are not pale — not white, but green-under, like something grown under a leaf. The damp does that. It never gets you truly wet, but it never lets you be dry. Their hair, when they have it, is always a little clumped. Their knees and knuckles are soft. Their lungs make a faint sound when they breathe. They wear clothes that the vault can make: trousers of pressed leaf-veneer that is supple as canvas until it dries, shirts of twisted fig-fiber that never quite stops smelling like hot butter when you sweat, boots cut down and re-soled with belt rubber from the factory. Their skin is freckled with a faint, flat lichen that they do not pick at. It glows a little if you slap it, like the lantern leaves.

None of them were born very far from here. Some were born *here*, in one of those huts that is a tree that is a column that is a house, on a mattress of woven root that has been hollowed and lined with old transit uniforms. Their mothers taught them not to shout. You can't live at 2.5 if you shout. You go deaf, or you go mean.

So they sign.

A hand up, palm out — bat cloud coming through, tuck in. Two fingers tapping the throat — frog chorus rising, machine about to cough, wait. A flat hand patted in a slow circle over the chest — all is balanced, leave it be. Their whole language down here is small and low and built for distance. When they do use voices, it's in that little square where three root-pillars leave a gap, and it's short. "Belt's off." "North drain's got a nest." "Leave the middle alone."

Because there is a middle. That big polite emptiness in the center of the vault where nothing stands, where even the moths that are the size of dinner plates will not go. Where the sound cuts out like stepping into snow. The houses are arranged around it like chairs around a fire that is cold. The inhabitants do not cross it unless they have to, and then they do it quickly, without swinging a lantern. One of them told a surface kid once that it was where the vault listens. They don't worship it. They just leave it more empty than empty.

Work is what tethers them, but not as a job.

Down at the south end, the factory that should have seized in the sixties sighs and turns. Boiler fed with litter bricks, looms pressing leaf into sheet, grinders making oil. The fig has learned to fruit directly into the hoppers — glossy fruits that fall with a thud and split and are collected. Some fruits have come out wrong. Weirdness 2.4 wrong. Perfect, threaded machine screws, warm to the touch, rattling in the oil pan. They use them. It seems wasteful not to.

An older man — you would call him the Boiler Man if you needed a name, but he doesn't use one — has a wrench that ends in a pruning hook. The bearing on the main flywheel weeps a clear sap that smokes. He lets it. He will come out every few hours, not on a schedule but when the change in pitch tells him, and he will adjust, prune, wipe, tap. Then he goes back to his hut, which is also a little workshop where he makes jars. Not for honey. He makes jars for the jars to go in.

A woman two root-pillars over presses veneer. She has figured out how to coax the conveyor-belt-leaf that only grows on vibrating iron to grow in a flat sheet. She does not sell it. She laminates her walls with it when the rain comes hard down the overflow channel and fills the low pockets for twenty minutes and then is gone. She has a child, or she watches a child. It is hard to tell which children belong to whom here, because they are all taught to slap the lantern leaves as they pass to light the way to the latrine and to never pull a root that is holding a ceiling.

The child is one of maybe three. Small, with enormous pupils for the dim, with frog-voice mimicry already perfect. She keeps tadpoles in an old hard hat. She knows not to keep the black swifts that never see the sky.

None of them own much of the hundred-meter-long room. They own about six square meters each and share the rest. That is why it feels sparse. Not because it failed to fill, but because it learned not to. Anything denser would tip. The fungal mat would go sour. The bats would hit walls. The engine would starve. The compromise they have reached — human, fig, beetle, bat, boiler — is a narrow one, weathered into stability over decades of being left alone with a brick propping the door.

In truth, the humans are not the dominant residents. The fig that decided to be architecture is more resident. The mat that drinks every temporary pool completely is more resident. The frogs, thousands of thumbnails inflating in unison in every cup where wall meets root, are more resident. But if you asked, in their low shared sign, who lives here, they would include themselves without hesitation. They have been here long enough to be part of the hum.

They will not follow you up the iron stairs when you leave. They will watch from a doorway cut into a living trunk fitted with an industrial jamb that creaks as the tree grows around it, a hand raised, palm out — not goodbye, but tuck in, bat cloud coming — and then they will go back inside and leave a lot of space around themselves, in the warm, soft, ticking air that has translated the street noise into something that sounds, improbably, like a town breathing.
