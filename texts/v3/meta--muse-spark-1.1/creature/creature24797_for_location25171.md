They do not live *in* the fissure. They *are* the fissure's way of staying open.

That is the first thing you understand when you stay past the noise of arrival. Nothing here is separate enough to be counted alone.

Start from the bottom of the food chain, because there is no bottom that is soil.

#### The stone-eaters

The rock-lice came first, or they were always going to come. They are not lice. They are isopods that forgot what an ocean was. Each one the size of your thumbnail, the color of old ash, the top of its carapace flattened until it is almost two-dimensional, to slip under a nominally closed crack with a molecule to spare. They have no eyes. They have combs for mouths.

They do not drink. Water would kill them here - it would freeze their gut and split them. They eat the town. They graze the bloom of orange rust that grows on every cut, the black snow of iron filings under every lathe, the skin-flake of basalt that spalls off when the glass ticks and cracks at night. Their guts are full of magnetite and slow bacteria that can wring energy out of oxidation itself. Leave your boots off for an hour and you will find them polishing them. In the deep cold they move fast because if they stop they lose the heat of their own friction. The floor of the town is quietly crawling with them, and no one minds. They are the street cleaners.

#### The light-eaters

Where there are lice, there are moths. They did not evolve here. Nothing with wings that large evolves at 7,000 meters. They came up from lower, following light. Soot-moths — *Hadena fuliginosa* would be a polite lie, but call them that.

They are big. The span of a man's hand. Their wings are not soft; they are a felt of asbestos-like fibers that have trapped so much tool-smoke and diesel particulate that they are blacker than the rock. When a worklight buzzes on at 05:30 they come in a silent snowstorm and land on every hot thing. They drink oil. Really drink it — long, coiled black proboscises unrolled to sip the film of cutting oil that coats every bench, the spill of low kerosene, the weeping seals of the generator. They need it. Their larvae need water the air will not give them, so they get their hydrogen from hydrocarbon. Crush one — which no one does — and it smells like a garage. The people brush them away like you would brush away an affectionate cat and then leave them alone, because they are the weather vane for carbon monoxide and bad air.

#### The things that need edges

The choughs were here before the people. Alpine choughs, *Pyrrhocorax graculus*, but stunted, their yellow beaks burned black by frost, their lungs grown absurdly large. They scream once in the morning and are otherwise silent to save air. They nest in the exact junction where yellow rebar meets black glass, stealing wire insulation for lining. They eat moths in mid-air inside the fissure — a flicker, a snap, a puff of sooty wing.

Then the bats came down from above instead of up from below. Feather-legged cave bats that shouldn't be able to survive without standing water, but they have learned to lick condensate off cold steel and to get all of their moisture from the moths they eat. Their legs are feathered all the way to the claws not for warmth but to mop up oil-mist. They roost by the hundred in the hollow of an old concrete mixer, in junction boxes, behind a fuel drum, hanging like old, pensive fruit. They are not shy. You will find one asleep in your tool chest if you left it open.

All of their guano is property. A boy with a putty knife and a bucket makes his rounds at dusk and before anyone can kick it away it is gone, taken to the nitrate pit where it is steeped and boiled down into something that can be used to blue steel and, in a pinch, blow a new bolt-hole.

#### The cavy

And then the only thing anyone deliberately brought here.

Rock-cavies — *Kerodon poltus*, if you want a name. They look like someone took a guinea pig and packed it down under a hydraulic press until it gave up on having legs. Squat, barrel-chested, nostrils that can close. Their fur is tight and oily and the exact dirty yellow of the newer scaffolding. They breathe like this town does: shallow, fast, patient. Eight or ten to a hutch, hutches made from welded bed frames stacked six high, each hutch vibrating faintly with their collective heat. They eat almost nothing — a pellet made of ground lichen trucked in, of moth frass, of shredded cardboard from the once-a-year airdrop. They give back heat, which is the real currency at -15C, and skins, which become the curtains and the bellows and the patches on elbows, and a kind of social weight. A person with no cavy is a person who has not been trusted yet. The old women — and they are all old early — keep them on their chests under their overalls when they are working at the bench, for the warmth and for the company.

#### The people

They are human. They are human enough that you can still see where the design is failing.

They are short, not because this is a mountain people, but because everything tall was cut off by the ceiling. Crouching has become anatomy: thick thighs, broad hands, necks that have learned to work with the head tipped slightly down to avoid a low Spar truss. Their chests are too big, their cheeks a permanent dusky red, even under the grease. The grease is part of them now. You cannot wash cutting oil out not because it stains, but because the skin has started to expect it. It seals the cracks that open at the knuckles in dry air that has never known sweat. The youngest still have white hands for a month after they arrive — none are born here, not anymore, the air is too thin for a pregnancy to finish — and then they too turn black up to the second knuckle, a glove they cannot take off.

They all wear the same thing because it is the only thing that works: quilted overalls that were once blue and now are black, steel toes held together with wire, a dust mask that is also black, goggles with the rubber perishing into the skin. They take them off only in sleep. They are quiet, but not from reverence. At this pressure, shouting is expensive and sounds terrible — the thin air shreds consonants. So they have a language of taps. Tap your spanner on the rail twice: *hold that*. Three times: *look out, it’s moving*. Drag a chain across a catwalk: *engine going off for the night*. 

They are not miners. There is nothing to mine. They are not researchers. There is nothing to research that would not be destroyed by being noticed. They are maintainers. The first crew was probably left to keep a relay station alive that died before they did. The second crew came to fix the first. At some point the job became the place.

Each person tends about two square meters of town. That is a bench with a lathe bolted directly into the glass — you can feel the planet's low shiver through the bed — a rack of drifts and punches, a small orange coke forge made out of a brake drum, a bin of stock that is all off-cuts of itself. Their work all day, every day, is to make fasteners. Pins, cotter pins, rivets, wedges, shims, little dog-bone brackets with two holes that have no name but without which a catwalk falls down. The fissure is always settling. You hear it at night: *CRACK* like a rifle shot, and in the morning your neighbor's platform is an inch lower and your own door won't open. No one curses. They come out with pry bars and chain hoists and bottle jacks and all together they re-level the world. By noon, the bracket you made yesterday is holding up someone else's house. By evening, you need a new one.

Ingots do come. Once a year, if the helicopter can get that high, a dull aluminum bundle is left on the ice a hundred meters down-slope and dragged up by hand, hand-over-hand on the old knotted cable, cursing quietly. That aluminum has to last until next year. Nothing leaves. The product is the continued presence of the product.

Why they stay dense is not sentiment. Density is insulation. Ten bodies in a thirty-meter-long crevice and the diesel generator running low and the cavies breathing and the forges going mean the air in the middle of the fissure might hit -2C instead of -15C. A moth that needs a bulb, a bat that needs a moth, a person who needs the bat's shit to keep the steel from rusting through, a cavy that needs the person's chest. Take one away and the seam closes.

At night the generator clicks off to save fuel and the caged bulbs go out one by one. The soot-moths fold up and disappear. The bats stop twitching. The people zip themselves into their bolted-together tool chests, into nests of cavy skin and fiberglass, shoulder to shoulder, goggles up on foreheads leaving pale rings, and breathe shallowly in unison until the cold gets cellular and personal. The rock ticks and ticks.

If you stood on the lip and looked down after lights-out, you would see nothing. No glow, no smoke, the air too thin to hold it. Just the black glass, and a dark line you could step over without noticing. That is how they survive. The mountain preserves them because it does not know they are there.
