Nothing here lives *in* the Crown the way you live in a place. That implies you can leave.

The first residents you meet are the ones you already heard.

The soot-handed monkeys — they're not monkeys, not quite, but your brain wants them to be. Up here they are huge, old, twice the weight they should be because they never have to come down. Their fur is not fur but a dense, woolly felt that grows moss in it, and the moss flowers. Tiny white orchids, the same as on the basalt steps. They move so slowly because every branch under them is a highway of other lives. They put a hand down and wait for the beetles to move. Then they put the next hand down a foot ahead, into the exact groove worn a hand's depth into the living mat. That groove is older than any one monkey. There are troops of them at different heights, never mixing, each following the same slow circuit around the bowl. When one dies up there, it does not fall. The mat grows over it in hours, draws it in. You can see the shape underneath for years.

Under your false floor — the bouncy peat-floor of root and humus and moss a foot thick that you walk on — there is the true resident. 

If you cut a window in the mat with a knife, which takes effort because the roots bleed a clear, hot sap, you see black water that never moves. Or seems not to. Warm as blood, dark with tannin and stone-dust. And in it, things that are not fish. Axolotl-things the length of your arm, with no eyes and six feathered gills that wave even when they're still. Their skin is the exact color of the iron rock, pitted. They hang just under the surface, mouths open, filtering the drips. All day they hang. You would think they were dead. At dusk, when the spires exhale and the real rain comes for six minutes, they all sink at once and walk on the bottom with little, human-looking hands, turning the mud, planting something. They don't have mouths on their faces in the way you expect — the mouth is on the underside, wide and soft, that kisses the stone and leaves a pale patch where it's drunk minerals.

But those aren't the ones who made the path.

The steps coming up — twenty, thirty feet a riser — are not stairs for giants. They're terraces. They're for slowing water. Whatever lives here doesn't climb. It *holds*. It taught the basalt to hold.

The spires are alive, but not like an animal is alive.

In the daylight, you think they are posts, machines. You come up close to one and you realize the black material is not reflecting you. It's showing you a little bit after you are. Your hand passes, then its shadow passes. The cold that pours out of the top isn't wind. It's the place breathing out what it doesn't need to keep the cloud-belly close. The faint movement under the surface — that ink in water — is faster when it has rained. It rushes. It has seen you.

The dust around their base that rearranges itself? Kneel. Don't touch it at first. Watch it.

It is not dust. They are mites, or seeds, or spores, each no bigger than a grain of flour, each black glass and pale root. When you disturb the circle, they start over, laying down a new pattern of lines from scratch. The pattern is never the same, but it is always a map of where the water is standing under the peat closest to you. If you stand in the circle too long, they climb your boots. Not biting. Tasting the salt. Trying to map you into the Crown.

That fallen spire that cracked — the one where your arm hairs stood up — put your ear near it, not your hand.

There is water dripping inside that is not dripping inside this space. The hollow is deeper than the spire is wide because it doesn't end in the spire. It goes down into the warm stone and then somewhere else, where it is still cold enough that clouds are born. It's a throat. The others are throats too, but they are not broken, so they don't let you hear.

And for weeks you can tell yourself this is a forest that grew around dead machines that still run. That the figs leaning toward the spires are just phototroping toward a cool spot. That the white roots that go in as root and come out the other side as living black glass threads with leaves that aren't leaves are just strange epiphytes.

Then you see one being made.

It doesn't happen fast. Like everything else here, it happens slow enough you can miss it if you blink on the scale of months. 

Deep in the center of the spiral, where the mat is so thick it covers water thirty feet down, there is a place where the monkeys never go. The beetles land there and die immediately and are not rotted. The four-winged hummingbirds avoid it. In the middle of a pool that has no bottom that you can find, peat knots itself. Root over root over root, pulling itself out of the water, felting, hardening. For days it just looks like a hummock. Then the water that sweats out of the air starts to avoid it. Then it turns black. Not rots — cures. The white roots from the surrounding figs all lean so hard they snap, reaching. They enter it. They do not come out as root.

A new spire, twenty inches tall, with an open top that is already breathing out January air in this eternal hot August. The mat around it is already clear in a perfect circle. Dust-mites gathering.

The Crown is not a forest with spires in it.

The Crown is a thing that learned that hot things rise.

Water rises. It made throats to catch it and rain it back down again before it could escape over the lip.

Heat rises. It made a lid of wet air to keep it.

Life rose out of the lowlands — spores, seeds, monkeys, insects, crawlers in the mud — up the giant steps that were never steps, up into this warm bowl. And the Crown, which is patient, which is the whole choked, layered, felted mat and the black water under it and the cold throats stuck through it like pins, is teaching it how to stay. Slowing it. Turning its roots into glass. Turning its dead into floor. Turning its movement into a groove.

It doesn't need you to live here. It is trying to make you into someone who can.

If you stay through the dusk exhale — when all hundred spires sound that low note in your molars and the rain comes down hard for six minutes — you'll feel the whole fifteen-mile table inhale afterward. The mat tightens under your feet, just a fraction. The water under it sighs. The fig leaves that are not leaves anymore all turn, together, a degree.

You are standing on something that is not ground. You are standing on its back.
