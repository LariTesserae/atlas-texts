What lives here doesn't live here the way you live somewhere.

At first you think it's just the film. That's how it gets you.

You put your hand out to steady yourself on the low ceiling and your palm comes away black at the edges, slick. Not oil. Not slime. It's a skin, a living varnish, one cell thick and then another and then another, built up over decades until it has a velvet to it. It tastes the water as it runs. You can see, if you hold still enough that the beetles leave your knee alone, the iridescence on it — purple and gasoline-green — and the way it tightens around a rivet head, the way it pulls back, white at the margin, where the condensation is too acid. It doesn't move. It grows. It is waiting to become wall.

Then the wall itself corrects you. The wall lives here.

Not alive like you think. Alive like a very old dog still trying to do the job you trained it to do when it was young. The metaplasm was told a century ago: *Be solid. Be sealed. Heal if cut.* It is still obeying. That faint amber pulse deep in the black, like a coal breathing under ash — that's its thought. Stress, repair, stress, repair. When a heat-shunt beetle dies with its legs in a sweating pore, you can watch the wall take it personally. A clear filament extrudes, blind and patient, and stitches over the little black body. Laps it. Makes it part of the hull. The wall has eaten ten thousand generations of beetles and kept some little archive of their shape in its pitting. That is erosion. That is how it weathers.

But the beetles own the surface. That's what lives here, really.

You called them cicadas because your brain wants a word and it was too loud for a better one. They are not singing. They are dumping heat. Each one is the size of the last joint of your thumb, carapace so black it reflects nothing, but edged in a fine copper lattice where the heat exchangers unfold. They press themselves flat to the weeping plates and drink, and then they have to shiver the water back off or cook. So they lift those terrible wings — not wings, radiators — and they buzz. One buzz is nothing. A thousand buzzes layered legs-to-legs on every vertical inch of a chapel closet is a white scream that takes your voice out of your skull. They are stupid and perfect. They crowd so thick in the corners that the wall underneath disappears. When you move, they don't fly. There is nowhere to fly. They part like a magnetic fluid, make a beetle-shaped hole where your hand was, and close again over your sweat. Two are on your knee. You didn't feel them arrive.

And because there are beetles, there has to be something that lives off beetles.

The mites. Bright, bright silver, too fast, like mercury spilled and given legs. They don't eat the beetles. They eat what the beetles don't — the dead skin of the biofilm, the mineral beards on the bolt heads, the skin your suit is shedding into the air right now. They run the highways between the beetles' legs. If you watch one still patch, you see traffic rules.

And then, smaller than that, because this is a caught breath and nothing here can afford to waste a fall, there are the ones in the water itself.

They look like shrimp. No bigger than rice grains. Clear bodies, with a dark speck of whatever they're carrying in their guts. They are not supposed to be animals at all — they're sanitation drones that forgot how to go home. They skate on the surface tension of the sheet of water running across the outer plate, they ride the falling beads down like elevators, they catch in the grate under your boots and right themselves and keep going. Each one is cleaning. That is their whole religion. Pick up a fleck of calcium, a flake of rust, a spore that didn't land right, hold it in your belly until you dissolve. They make the ticking, gulping sound when water goes down through the double grates. That isn't plumbing. That's them swallowing.

And above them, where you have to duck, where the two skins almost kiss and the thermal shear is strongest — that wrongness when you lift your hand and the air gets thick — is what lives the longest.

The bats. You saw them right away and dismissed them because they were too still. They hang folded, not from claws but fused at the spine to the inner hull, a dozen pretzeled kites of warm membrane, the color of old bruise and wet paper. They don't have eyes. They have mouths too large, ventral mouths that open and close and open. The air here is too wet, too full of metal and skin and diagnostic. So they breathe it. With every slow pulse — one, two, three, slower than you — they draw the whole pocket through themselves, sieve it, and exhale that absurd, sweet, wrong smell of cinnamon. It's not smell. It's code. They are telling the wall, telling the city far below, telling each other: *still wet, still warm, still balanced, still occupied.* They are the gardeners.

And in the one crack where there is enough false soil — calcium and beetle husk and five decades of that biofilm dying and compressing — that's where the other two live, the ones that tricked permission.

The fern that isn't a fern. Three fronds, black-green and too stiff, each leaflet printed with a photovoltaic grid, trembling every time a cold drop from the outer plate detonations onto it. It doesn't photosynthesize much; there is almost no light. It drinks the infrared leaking off the inner wall, drinks the shiver-heat off the beetles. It roots in rust.

And beside it, the wire-lichen, white as a nerve, hair-thin, spread in a mat. It doesn't want sun. It wants pH.

Together, that's what lives here. Not a single resident. A compact. A continent folded into a closet. The film that covers everything, the wall that tries to heal everything, the beetles that drink heat, the mites that run between their legs, the shrimp that clean their water, the bats that breathe their air, and two plants that stole in and learned to shiver on cue.

You are too big and too dry and too loud to live here. In three minutes the sweat starts to run into your eyes and the buzz gets inside your teeth and the cinnamon code gets sweet in a way that makes you feel sick. It was never for you.

You are the brief weather in a place that already has its people.
