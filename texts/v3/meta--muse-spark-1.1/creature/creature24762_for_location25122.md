It lives here anyway.

That's your first thought after you stop looking for something to look *at*. At first it seems empty — sterilized by noise and light and water that can't sit still long enough to be drunk. A place this bright and this wet should be a jungle. It isn't. Anything that tried to live big here got washed off the walls.

So what does live here lives like an apology. Low. Thin. Stubborn.

**The Paper.**

You see it first because you came in through it. High up near the throat, where the rebar smiles and the spray thins to mere humidity — up there, where your hand would steady yourself on concrete still holding the ghost of its wooden mold — there is a discoloration.

It's not discoloration.

It's a mat. A felt. Off-white, the color of cheap napkins left out in the rain, spreading across basalt that was never supposed to host anything. Algae and fungus and mineral that got tired of being separate things and decided to be one thing together. It has no leaves, no fruit, no ambition to be more. Where the light hits it directly it curls away, so it lives in the Room's own shadow, in the underneath of the pylon cages, in the lee of a rock lip. Touch it and it comes away on your glove like snot and old paper. It is the only thing in here that is quiet.

If you sit and watch it — and the Room makes you sit, because your jacket is soaked and your knees are wet and there is nowhere else to go — you see it breathe. Condensation beads on it, swells, and then is sucked inward. Not rolled off. Drunk.

**The Threaders.**

Those roots you thought were roots.

They are. Some aspen or pine or something even more stupidly optimistic on the surface twenty meters up found a microfracture and sent a tap down. That was probably fifty years ago. The tip died when it hit this air — too bright, too loud, too much — but the root didn't get the message. So it kept growing sideways into air.

Now there are maybe a dozen of them, hanging from the ceiling like lost plumb lines. Ten feet long and thinning until you can't tell where plant becomes water. They never reach the lake. If they did, the turbulence would rip them. So they hang and drink the air itself. The condensation runs down them in beads, and they tremble every time the pressure drops and the waterfall leans. They are not residents. They are tourists who got lost on their way down and decided to live in the doorway forever.

When the gravity hiccups, they all sway in unison, like sea anemones feeling a tide.

**The Pilgrims.**

Then you see them again in the waterfall, because now you know where to look.

White shrimp. No, not white — transparent with a white idea inside. Three of them. You thought there was one, flickering. There are three, each the size of the last joint of your thumb. No eyes. No pigment, because what's the point of color in a place with no shadows.

They live their entire, honest-to-god lives in that two-meter white-water fall on the far wall. The wall is scalloped smooth and the water pours over it as a sheet and then a fall, and that is their world. A vertical river. They climb it. That's it. That is the whole religion.

They have little hooked feet and they pick their way up through the roar, against insane current, grazing the biofilm off the scallops as they go. It takes them an hour to go a foot. When they get to the top lip, the sheeting water gets too thin and they lose their footing, tumble, cartwheel down in the white foam — you wince, you think that's death — and land in the churn below, and immediately start climbing again.

They are so perfectly adapted to instability that stability would kill them. If you cupped one in your hands and put it in a calm tidepool, it would have no idea what to do. Here, every time the lake throws a column up and slaps itself, every time the cold bubble pops and your molars thrum, one of them gets stranded on a relatively dry scallop and just... lies there, antennae flicking, flicking, until the next sheet comes and saves it. They have no memory of the abyss. They just climb.

**The Keeper.**

It is in the absolute upper corner, above even the Paper, where the vault stops being an egg and becomes a throat again. You have to tilt your head all the way back and shade your eyes from the Thread to see it.

You thought it was mineral. Six legs folded up tight under itself, body the size of a dog, maybe bigger under the calcite. A lens on its front, or what used to be a lens, now filmed over with that patient, creamy cauliflower of calcium the wall grows over unrusted things. The titanium pylons below have it. This has more.

It is not dead. It is roosting.

Some maintenance thing. Builder's pet. It was set here to listen to the machine think it was working, to clip the Threaders when they got too long, to keep the biofilm from growing over the black glass cages. It has been here so long that the Room has started to grow it. Mineral has bridged the gap between its belly and the rock. Water runs over its back. The lens twitches - not mechanical - a slow nystagmus every twenty seconds, trying to clear itself.

It does not hunt the Pilgrims. It doesn't have anything to hunt. It listens. The roar of the lake is data to it. The *tck* of the bead of light running up the filament is a prayer call. You realize it has been listening so long it has become part of the acoustic baffling of the place. The Room would sound different without it.

If you throw a rock at it, it wouldn't move. Not because it can't. Because you are not relevant. You are too fast.

**And then - the thing that actually lives here.**

Not the shrimp. Not the Paper. Not you.

The cycle.

The thing they hung in the center of the air — the filament, the flaw, the hair-thin welding arc — it doesn't just light. It keeps. Below it, in the middle of the lake, the water braids itself up against gravity into a hollow rope a meter high, holds, and collapses with too much weight. That is not a glitch. That's respiration. Light becomes heat that shouldn't have a signature becomes pressure becomes water climbing up to touch light again. The pylons with their empty black cages are its grown-over nerve endings; one of them holds rain that never falls because the field inside it tells water to stop.

You are warm because it is breathing on you. The weeping walls sweat because it is warm. The lake jumps because deep under it, something that was built as a heat sink now thinks it is a heart and beats out of habit.

It has no name because it never needed one. It has been left on so long it has developed weather, and weather is a kind of memory. It knows how to be this size, this bright, this loud. It keeps itself at sixty meters and thirty degrees and furious. It has waited long enough that life has moved into its sweat and its corners the way moss moves into the letters of a gravestone.

It doesn't need you. But for as long as you stand here dripping, exhaling loud in the loud room, you are conscripted. Your breath beads on a scallop ten feet up, runs down in a skin, joins the sheet, hits the lake, gets thrown as a column, becomes mist, lands on the Paper.

You are, for a little while, one of the things that lives here.
