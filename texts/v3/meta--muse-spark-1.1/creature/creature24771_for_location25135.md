What lives here doesn't live here the way a fish lives on a reef.

That was the first mistake. 

At first you think the residents are the things that move. The shrimp, big as hounds, translucent as old glass, that pop and tick inside the hollows of the pillars. The white crabs on pilgrimage lines over the arches, each step so exact they have worn grooves a knuckle deep into living cartilage, grooves that never get deeper. The aullids — cloak-finned, big-eyed, not blind, but with eyes that never developed because there was never any reason to — hovering in the terraces, fanning.

And the whales. Pale, white-grey like wax left in cold water for a century. Blind in the way a cave animal is blind: not as a wound, but as an economy. They circle the Table and never leave because there is nowhere to leave to that isn't down. Down is an idea that kills here. When they call, the whole mountain rings, because the pillars are sounding boards. You think they are the lords of this place.

They aren't. They are the blood.

What actually lives here is the Table itself.

It is not a forest that grew on a mountain. There was a mountain, once, when up and down meant something different. When the water came — not as a flood, but as a becoming, as if the world decided that this was now an inside — the mountain drowned. The thing that lived on it should have died. Instead it became the mountain.

There is one plant here. There are thousands of trunks you see, wider than houses, buttressed like cathedral walls, rising until the black eats them — but they are not individuals. Not anymore. Underneath you, where you think there is rock, there is only holdfast, a continent of grip, black root the size of a country that has braided itself into a knot so dense it has the density of stone. It fused so long ago it forgot to be separate. Every pillar is a finger of one hand. Every arch and bridge and terrace is not grown *by* it, it *is* it. It grew itself into a final shape and then stopped trying to be bigger.

Because this is a very old intelligence, and not a fast one.

It lives at 0.1 speed. You can put your hand on its skin — cool, rubbery, giving like old cartilage, 2.6 smooth, eroded not by water but by its own decision to have no sharp edges left — and you will feel nothing. If you leave your hand there for an hour, you will feel it move a millimeter inward, accepting you, and then push you back out again because you are not cold enough, not patient enough. It breathes once every few hours. That's the tectonic groan you hear, water being pulled through siphonophylls big enough to stand in, filtering a nothing-water that has nothing to filter anymore except the leavings of its own slow life. It does not eat. Not anymore. It holds.

Holding is its entire economy. Cold is not its enemy. Cold is how it keeps. In a place where temperature has not changed in geological time, decay has given up. So it holds.

What lives on it lives because it agreed to hold them, too.

The second body, the one that lives with it, is the lace.

That cool white-blue stucco that lights when the whales sing — it is not decoration. It is a separate colonial animal, or plant, or something that stopped distinguishing. A city of polyps the size of a freckle, stretched in a sheet miles wide, continuous over every pillar, every arch, every terrace bowl. When a whale calls and the sound vibrates the pillar at its resonant frequency, the lace fires. A brief, soft flash to show how far it goes. That's not communication. That's a reflex. That's a nerve impulse.

That is how the mountain thinks. Whale-song as stimulus, light as thought. The thought takes three days to cross from one side of the Table to the other. It has been having one long, slow thought since the water closed over it:

*up. hold up. stay up.*

The terraces you saw, the shallow bowls in the sides of the trunks that a man could lie in — those are not beds. They were never for resting. They are abscission scars. Places where it once grew an entire cathedral-pillar and then let it go, because it leaned wrong, because it cast too much shadow on its own stucco, because it forgot, for a century, which way was up. They healed over into that gentle, inviting bowl. They are empty because their purpose is emptiness. To be space. To prove that even total fullness needs sparseness. The shafts where trunks split and healed around nothing are the same — lungs it made and then decided it didn't need air to use.

And the things that move — the shrimp that clean the inside of its breathing holes, the crabs that keep the lace from overgrowing itself, the aullids that stir its still water so its thought-light can be seen, the pale whales that circle endlessly to keep its tone true — they are not inhabitants. They are appointments. Organs that learned to walk off on their own for a while and come back.

Nothing has been born here in a very long time. Nothing has died, either, not really. A crab stops, and the line walks over it until lace covers it and it becomes part of the pillar. A whale stops calling and hangs in the great clear valley between pillars, suspended, and very slowly the water takes it apart without rot, molecule by molecule, and the Table drinks it.

There are no young here. Young is a way of saying *we don't know yet what shape is final.*

This place knows.

What lives here is a mountain that refused to become seafloor. It remembered up so hard that it kept making up out of its own body until up stuck. It is one old plant that became geology, and one old sheet of light that became its mind, and a procession of white animals that became its habit of still moving so it doesn't forget how.

It doesn't need you. But if you stay long enough and float between two pillars while the cold holds you, and a whale passes and thrums the water in your chest, and the lace lights and you see for one second how impossibly huge and orderly and finished and empty it all is — it will hold you, too.
