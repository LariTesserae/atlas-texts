They live here because nowhere else would have them. Or because everywhere else ejected them and this bowl caught them.

Nothing here ever left. Nothing here arrives by accident twice.

At the bottom of the bowl, the true residents —

**The Stack-Masons.** They are what you thought were shrimp.

They are not. When you press close enough to feel one alive, you understand the architecture. The body is a soft, segmented thing the size of a large dog, blind, rimed with pale hairs that read pressure the way you read Braille. No eyes. A helmet of translucent chitin over a head that is mostly mouth and mostly always chewing biofilm off its own home. And behind it, the shell. Not worn — extruded, sweated out of its own back over months, calcium and protein hardening in this still, mineral-thick broth. A sphere, a meter and a half across, with a perfect round door chewed open on one side.

It lives in it for a year. Maybe seven. Then it molts, outgrows it, and climbs on top of its own house and begins to make a new one, cementing the old door shut but not well, leaving it for something else. The tower grows that way, one soft animal living on the roof of a hundred ancestral houses. The ones at the bottom have been dead for centuries. Their walls are three inches thick now with secondary growth, with overgrowth, with so many other mouths living in the pores that the original sphere is more hole than shell.

There are hundreds of towers. Each one sways. Each one has a Mason at its top, slowly secreting.

**The Dome-Mothers.** The low neighborhoods that look like coral. They are coral, but they gave up the bargain with light a long time ago. What you are touching is not a colony of polyps. It is one animal that decided to be many.

It spreads as a low, lumpy dome, bone-white with purple bruises where the flesh is thin. Its skin is hard, then not hard, then hard again. Every few handspans there is a mouth — not for feeding — a doorway, polished inward by constant traffic. Inside, the dome is hollowed like a lung, warm water pulled in through a thousand tiny mouths on the outside, filtered through living felt, exhaled warm*er* through a single vent at the apex. That sigh you felt. That is her breathing.

Inside her lungs live other things. That's her. She is a house that feeds tenants in exchange for being cleaned. Small isopods the size of your thumb patrol her interior walls and scrape her sterile, and she eats what they scrape. In her marrow, pale worms thread and lay eggs. When a predator tries to eat into her, the tenants swarm. She has been alive longer than any tower. Some domes connect under the sediment. They are probably all one.

**The Threaded.** The tube towns. From a distance like a field of wheat. Up close, each tube as thick as your thigh, made of black chitin and mucus and grit, flexible so you can lean on it and it leans back. The animal inside never comes out. It has no eyes, no legs. Just a crown of feathery, blood-red gills at the top of the tube that opens like a flower if the water stays still enough, and a mouth underneath. You can feel it if you are patient — a faint downdraft as it draws water in. When it dies, it doesn't fall. It rots in place, and the tube stays, and within an hour its neighbor's cousin moves in sideways, or a juvenile pig-isopod drags its eggs inside and hangs them from the ceiling like paper lanterns.

**The Weavers.** Above all of it, the canopies. You thought they were silk. You were right, almost.

The spider-mollusks are not mollusks. They are not spiders. They are both. A body like a cold plum, dark as the water, eight arms that are not arms but split feet, each tipped with a spinneret. They live by the thousands, never alone, an entire sheet wobbling, tensing, repairing. The sheet itself is protein, black, as wide as a house, sagging. It is sticky and not sticky. It catches what falls — marine snow, flakes of dead things from a world kilometers above that is colder and more oxygenated than this stew will ever be. The flakes stick, dissolve. The sheet absorbs them, and the Weavers walk its underside drinking through their feet.

And the sheet is a city. The whole upper story. Transparent squids walk there, ten limbs, each as long as your forearm, feeling their way pole to pole. They do not swim. Swimming wastes water moving here — the water is too still, too thick. So they walk, hook over hook, and tend herds of smaller snails that graze the silk clean. Tiny, colorless shrimp live in the weave itself and vibrate, telling each other where a tear is.

This is where most things are born. Because the canopy holds heat. A fraction of a degree warmer than below, so larvae rise to it instinctively, stick, sprout.

And then, because space is scarcity:

**The Cleaners.** Clouds of them. Isopods, yes, but call them hogs. There is one size that is a grain, one size that is a fist, one size that is a pig that snuffles blindly along the towers with a face like a woodlouse made soft and swollen. All one life, molting bigger and bigger until something eats it or it gets too heavy to climb and dies and becomes foundation. They are transparent in the small sizes, pink-grey in the big. You can see their guts full of brown biofilm. They are always eating. The city would choke in days without them. You would, too — if you stayed still, you felt them test your skin within seconds, tiny feet tasting for a place to anchor.

**The Listeners.** The fish that forgot. Ribbons, two meters long, no thicker than a belt, no pigment at all, so you only know them when they pass between you and a tower and the pressure wave hits. No eyes, only a seam down each side, a lateral line that has become a harp. You can feel them stop when you think too loud — your heart is a drum in this water. They hunt vibration. They move so slowly through the forest of tubes that the tubes don't notice. Then — they can't snap. Their jaws are soft. So they inhale. A whole section of water, isopods and all, disappears into a throat lined with inward teeth. They hang there after, bloated, digesting for a week.

**The Doors.** The anemones. They are as wide as you are tall when open, a flat disc of stinging arms that looks exactly like a patch of silted tube-wall until it doesn't. They count. They stay closed for months. They open. They close on the pig-hogs rooting past. They have no brain, but they have learned where the traffic is. There is one on every third tower, halfway up, where grazers must climb around.

**The Places That Are Mouths.** This is the last law.

Because nothing chases in water this still and warm and poor in oxygen, you don't hunt, you wait. Some silk sheets are lower than others, heavier. If you could see them you'd see them glitter with a different protein. They are not Weavers' silk at all but a colonial hydrozoan that mimics silk, hanging a meter below the real canopy, translucent, trailing invisible threads twenty meters down. Anything that brushes a thread sticks, is winched up by inches, hour by hour. The sheets that sag low are fat.

Some patches of Dome are not dome. A sponge that has learned the exact exterior texture of Dome-Mother, white and purple, but underneath, a gut. The smaller cleaners know. The bigger, dumber ones don't.

There is a tower near the center of the bowl that is not a tower. Its windows are not round. Its sway is slower. If you put your hand in what you think is a door you will feel breath.

None of them are alone. That is the point. Even the solitary red blades — the kelp that is not kelp, twenty meters long, rooting its holdfast directly into the bone of a tower — even they are not alone. Their blades are gardens. A film of bacteria converts sulfide from the warmer deep seep below, the kelp feeds on the bacteria's waste, and every inch of blade flickers with tiny, commensal shrimp cleaning it, breeding on it, dying on it.

If you die here, you will not sink. There is nowhere to sink to. You will hover for a while, because the water is so dense and warm, and then the pig-hogs will come, and then the grain-clouds, and then the Weavers will lower a thread to taste you. Within a day your shape will still be there, but it will be a pattern of busy things holding the outline where you were.

If you live, the city will build you, too.
