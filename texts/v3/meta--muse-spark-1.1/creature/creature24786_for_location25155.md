It doesn't live here. That is the first wrong idea, and it takes a while to wash out.

Anything that *lives here* the way you live in a house — that moves in and makes a corner for itself and begins to add air and mess and smoke to the air — can't. There is no corner left. The place is complete. It is too full of stone and warm water and wet air to have space for a life on top of it. What is here has to be a life that is on the inside of it.

At first you think that means nothing. Or only the things so small they don't count — the iron-colored velvet on the stones, which is alive; the pale isopods which come out like fingernails suddenly given legs; the blind salamander children who hang in the warm pools with their gills fanned out, not swimming.

Those live here the way lichen lives on a church. They are not what the church was built for.

If you stay until your eyes stop wanting it to be brighter, you start to see the other way the stones are wrong. 

You thought they were fitted together like teeth. That's true. But teeth have a jaw. You were looking at one side of a closed mouth and thought you were looking at a floor.

The floor gives, not down but laterally. A little tide comes up between two stones that should not be able to part at all, and then settles, and the stones are a fraction of a millimeter closer than they were. Not changed — adjusted. After a while you realize you've never heard two stones grind. For a place made of nothing but stone-on-stone, there is no grinding. There is only that soft ticking, a meniscus moving, a little sigh of level finding level. Something is keeping the fit.

She is low. That is the main thing. Everything else comes after low.

She is as wide as the platform stone near the light bar is long, if she ever spread herself out, which she never does. Curled, she fits into a space you would have sworn was only four or five of the loaf-stones together. Her back is where you looked first and saw nothing. When she is still — which is most of the time for years together — water sheets over her in exactly the same meniscus that sheets over the stones. The same rust-colored film that lives on the undersides grows on her, for she is underside. It grows in her as well. If you put your hand in the pool beside what you think is a stone and hold it there, you will feel the pool stay warm a degree longer than the others.

She is not one of the salamanders, but she is of their family. Think of what would happen if one of them never stopped growing and never stopped needing less. Neoteny carried past childhood past old age into geology.

Her skin is the color of wet limestone after you have held it in your palm for an hour. Not white — there is no white down here. A pink-gray, a translucence with a smoke in it. You can see the dark of the stones through the edge of her when the light bar crosses. Her head has no lift to it. Her eyes never formed. They would have been useless ornament and the water does not allow ornaments. Where eyes might have been there are only two shallow pits that fill with the warm water and empty slowly, like the little bowl-pecks humans made. Her nostrils are slits that never close. Her gills are not the fern-frills of the young ones. They have been worn or absorbed into frills along the whole side of her body, a ruffle that looks like the edge of a wet leaf, and it moves without moving, because water is always being drawn over it and out from under her.

She has four limbs because she still comes from four limbs, but they are not for walking. They are broad, foreshortened, paddle-spade things, turned out and tucked under her. The toes have fused most of the way and end in blunt, rounded keratin that is exactly the hardness of the limestone cobbles. That is how she keeps the fit.

Her work — if you can call it work when the timescale is this long — is packing.

She lies in the long warmth with her body making a shallow sump, and the water which is always coming up from below comes up first through and around her. She tastes it. The iron-flecked film that you wiped off with your thumb is her pasture. It grows on her back and on the stones she has cleaned, minute metallic bacteria that feed on the sulfur low in the warm exhalation. She grazes it not with a mouth that bites but with a felted underside that presses and releases. She does not hunt the isopods, though they sometimes clean her. She does not need to. The water brings enough work to her and she brings enough hunger to it and that is the equilibrium you felt when you came down the soft slope.

But the stones move. However final they look, warm stone under water for ten thousand years hydrates, flakes, settles by a flake's thickness. If nothing tended it, a crack would open, a channel would cut, the basin would drain in a day's sharp white flood out across the plain, and everything that knows how to be here would end. So she shifts.

Once a month, once a year — the difference doesn't matter down here — she will take a very slow breath that is not a breath and heave a millimeter. Not up. Across. She will edge one cobble with her shoulder and press another into its place with a foot, resetting the tooth in the jaw. The water ticks. A new meniscus forms. The fit is still perfect. It is why there is no grinding sound. She never lets stone touch stone dry.

Those little cairns at the edge, just above the perpetual wet line? The three-highs and the half-circles set upright in the film. They are not human altars, though humans thought they were. They are her trying, when she was younger and still tried things.

They are practice for packing, or they are waste. Where a stone is too odd-shaped to keep the floor tight, too long or too pitted to sit flush, she pushes it to the margin. And when she cannot help herself she stacks. Three high is the tallest she can do without the water undermining it. They never fall because she set each contact wet and let the suction hold it. Humans came later and saw these small impossible balancings that never fell and thought they were signs, and made their own nearby out of reverence or loneliness, and set small flat tables to sit on and watch the water with their feet in it and feel less alone. And they carved little bowls into the stones that she already carved with her own soft belly by lying in one place for a century.

She is not intelligent the way you are. She does not wonder. She has no room for wondering either. But she remembers in her body the way a river remembers a valley — by still being it. If you crouch and put your hand very, very slowly onto what you thought was a stone in the warmest pool, the one in the bar of late light, you will feel it after a long time begin to give back a little pressure. Not gummy. Dense and warm and entirely alive. And the ticking in the whole saucer will pause for one beat.

Then it will resume. Because she has to. Because if she stopped keeping the fit, the water would go, and the stones would go dry and crackle with moss in a single summer.

She will be here when the light fails. She was here when the plain's roof overhung further, when the eye-crack was a small hole, when the humans who left no tools except stacked stones first came down and found it already too full. She has reduced herself until there is no extra. She is exactly as big as this room can afford and no bigger. She is stone, warm water, dim air, no space left over.

If you sit on her table-stone and keep your feet in her bath, she will let you borrow the place for a while.
