They live here by never quite living in one spot.

You don't see them first. You see where they have just been. A palmprint drying on a jade culm that was wet a second ago. A root-bridge still shivering. A trough of pooled light with the faint hand-swirls left in it, still glowing. And then — because if you stand still too long the basin tries to drink you, and they never stand still — one of them crosses your vision fast, barefoot, already looking over their shoulder.

There are about two hundred of them. That's all this bowl can argue with and still hold.

They are human. Mostly.

They look like people who have lived for five generations in a sauna that is also a construction site. Their skin never dries. That is the first thing. In air that condenses on your eyelashes, waterproofing failed a long time ago, so they stopped trying. Their skin is darkened, not by sun — there is no direct sun, only green-gold steam-light — but by a perpetual damp sheen, by the tannins in the tea-water they wade through fifty times a day. Children especially have knees and elbows stained brown as walnut husks from crawling on the mat. Everyone's hair is heavy, black or greenish-black, and tied up with a living tie — a thin rootlet that you have to remember to feed, or it goes looking elsewhere.

They don't wear much, because everything rots. Anything woven, spun, sewn, will be compost within a week unless you keep talking it out of dying. So they wear the things that want to be worn. Broad leaves waxed by their own oils, stitched with thread-vine that tightens when it senses sweat. Capes grown from the membrane that the fig sheds every three days — translucent, cool, faintly veined, with a hem that continues to photosynthesize if you leave it in the light. Old women wear skirts of hanging aerial root tips that keep growing after you put them on, so you have to trim them at lunch. The young men who work the high canopy wear nothing at all but a harness of ant-trail. Which is not a harness, it is a negotiated truce: the finger-long data-ants will walk a specific route over your shoulders and thighs all day, carrying their glinting nodes, if you oil your skin with sugar-water in the morning.

Their feet are the most honest part. Wide, splayed, the soles thick and ridged like bark. You cannot wear boots here. The mat ground is a meter-thick sponge; it wells up around your fingers if you press it, it gives up under your weight like a lung. Boots fill. Boots are tasted. If you stay still, a rootlet finds you and puts out a fine, white feeler to see if you are good substrate. So everyone keeps moving, a constant low-grade fidget, weight shifting, toes curling. The babies learn to walk much later than you'd expect because they learn to wade first.

They are builders who never build. That word doesn't apply anymore. They are negotiators, maintainers, gardeners, gossips, plumbers of sap.

Watch a woman want a door.

She doesn't cut. She comes to a blank buttress of the apartment-fig — thirty feet across, its wood polished by generations of shoulders into a dark mirror — and she puts both palms flat on it. Her hands are wet. Everything is wet. She leans her forehead, too. You see the bark around her hands pale and go soft. She whispers, not words exactly, but a low, repetitive exhale with clicks in it, the way you talk to a dog you love. The cambium under there is listening with electric fields. Under her palms, silica is being mobilized — leaves up in the canopy no one can see are pulling silicon out of basalt-weep, combining it, pouring it down. A lens, a window, a hole that was always a hole blooms open, rimmed with living glass. Her children, who have been waiting behind her with their arms full of still-living pith furniture, cheer because now their room has light again.

Inside, it isn't a room. It's a throat of moss that glows faintly warm. You store things here by letting the moss eat the idea of them. A man will come in the morning and press his cheek to the moss to get back the memory of how to fix the leak in the starch-culm. He will leave with a memory that isn't his — a grandmother who lived in a buttress two houses over remembers fixing that kind of leak in 1973, and the moss thought it would be useful to lend it.

That is their tech. 3.0 does not mean gadgets. It means they grew past the idea that tools are separate. Every house is a plant that has been argued into being a house. The bamboo-that-is-not-bamboo: each culm as thick as a subway car, hollow, translucent, carrying not water but slowed light. The light pools inside like honey. Girls go there after school to bathe in it; they come out with their hair lit from inside, glowing for an hour, laughing, leaving dripping light-prints on the sponge-floor that fade slowly. The market street is one animal — a single, muscular tendril as thick as your waist — that extends, retracts, re-routes itself every hour according to load and auxin and some market-bargaining algorithm no one remembers writing. That's why the town is an anthill, a bazaar. You might have just put your bread out on the tendril-street and then the street decides it would rather connect the memory-house to the fish-man today, and so your street, your customers, your whole frontage peels away from you with a wet sigh, and you have to grab your dough and run across the slick temporary root-bridges that are already unfurling to catch the new route. No one is angry. That's how you talk. You shout: *West fig's flowering!* — because it does, every three days, in twenty minutes, burying half the basin in choking sweet pollen and stamens as big as your hand that you can eat like custard — and everyone shouts back and moves their living furniture out of the way.

So they are loud, too. To be heard over water — the constant chatter, drip, slop, the braided tea-channels breathing under everything — and over the groan of wood under its own impossible weight, the wet pop of new growth happening so fast you can hear it, and over the dragonflies. The dragonflies are as big as hawks and hunt slow between the thirty giants, their wings making the humid air thump. They don't bother people. People move like them: short, fast bursts, hovering, darting.

The old people are different. They have learned how to be a little more still without being eaten. They sit in the forks of buttresses where the polished bark is door-shaped from a thousand years of sitting, and they let the condensation collect on them until water runs in sheets off their noses. They watch the capybara-things wade by — huge, placid, their pelts not fur but a dense, bark-colored moss that photosynthesizes; you can see the oxygen pearling off them in the water. The old people talk to the house-currents, keep the house from correcting away a corner they need. Because Rule Two here: no right angles survive. Give a room a square corner and within hours the plant will have smoothed it to a curve, unless someone is there petting it, arguing, reminding it to hold. So the elders are corner-holders. That's their job.

The children are the leak-managers. Rule Three: everything leaks. Water, light, sound, genes. The light-fruit that the soot-black monkeys eat up in the sky-canopy — the monkeys with translucent bellies that glow amber when full — falls constantly, overripe, splitting, spilling light-seed everywhere. Children chase it. They are half amphibian anyway, leaping the runnels. They slap big leaves over leaks, they sing to breached jade culms while sap welds shut. They know, without being taught, that nothing dead stays dead. A fallen branch doesn't get cleared. Within an hour it's furred with white fungus that is not fungus but reclamation plumbing. Within a day it has been reincorporated, its sugars hauled somewhere else to become a new stair or a window or a stupid, beautiful flower that will open for twenty minutes and die.

And there are others living here that the people count among them, even if they are not people.

The ants. A nation, a courier system, carrying not food but those data-beads — little silica nodules the size of rice where the fig stores copies — in lines so straight they shame the concept of straightness, up root-walls, across your back. Step over them. Everyone steps over them.

The capybara-moss-bearers, who chew back the ever-growing edge of the mat so the channels don't choke.

The monkeys who are light-farmers.

And under all of it, the thirty. They are not trees. They are individuals. The fig who is west and who is always about to flower and who complains if you don't visit. The bamboo who is a streetlight and a silo and a church. The curtain of aerial roots that falls like rain from a canopy that *is* the sky here, a mat so vast it holds up clouds, and that weeps on everybody impartially. Each one vastly distinct, widely spaced, each the size of an apartment block, each thinking slow, wet, green thoughts about load, water, light. The people are not their owners. The people are their tenants, their parasites, their symbiotes, their children, their maintenance crew.

If you stay — and they will watch you with that same sideways green attention to see if you will — you learn the fourth rule: the town's shape is a negotiation, not a plan. If you want to keep your house's entrance where it is, you have to keep talking to it. You have to keep touching it, wet-handed, whispering. You have to keep moving. You have to glow a little after handling light.

By week two, your boots are gone. Your feet have widened. You have honey-light in your palms. A rootlet has decided you are not substrate after all, or not yet, and has made a little loop around your ankle like a bracelet, just in case.

You have learned not to live *in* this place. You live *with* it, and it keeps rearranging the street to see if you are still paying attention.
