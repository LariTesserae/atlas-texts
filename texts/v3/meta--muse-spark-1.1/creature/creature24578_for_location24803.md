What lives here hasn't decided if it is one thing or many things yet.

At first glance you think the answer is nothing. Too new, too wet, too loud with water. The black rock is still too hot for a footprint to hold. But this is a nursery, not a desert. Everything in it is soft labor, and everything in it is busy.

### The Quick

They're the ones you see first because they're made to be seen — loud biology, seeded to move water around before water learns how to erode.

The bells. 
In the low, heavy air there are hundreds of them, maybe thousands, stacked in loose columns from ankle-height to a hundred feet up where they vanish into the pearl deck. Each one is about the size of your open hand up to the size of a picnic blanket, amber-vellum, translucent. No face. A shallow bell of chitin and gas-bag, with a ring of stiff cilia underneath like a crown of eyelashes and a single, darker, knotted organ inside that you keep thinking is a heart. They don't fly, they *buoy*. They fill, they warm, they pulse, and the warm updrafts off the weeping rock lift them.

They are the humidifiers. They inhale the stratocumulus breath and the steam off the basalt, and they exhale it again five seconds later as a finer mist, seeding the air with microscopic flakes of lime and iron they have scraped off the rock. When they ping — that clear glass ping that makes you turn your head — they are ranging each other. Mapping water density. The mist they puff catches the dim light just for half a second, like breath on a winter day. When you stand below a big one you get rained on.

When you move, they all turn a degree toward you, because you are a 37C rock in a 31C world. You are a heat anomaly. They taste you as lift.

Down lower, in the film:

The skaters. They look like someone took a water-strider and stretched it until the physics complained. Dark brown-black, bodies no bigger than a grape but legs as long as your hand, six or eight of them with an extra backwards knee in the middle that folds like a grasshopper's. They do not walk on water; they *staple* it, dimpling the surface film of the perched pans and sprinting across the lens. In each pan that has existed for more than an hour, a green-black slick of cyanobacteria and that too-fast white lichen has already started to sheet. The skaters harrow it. They drag their mouth-parts, which are more like felt squeegees, across the rock and drink the bloom off before it can thicken enough to hold dirt.

That is their job. Keep the rock clean. Keep it wet but not soiled. They are janitors. If you kneel and hold still, they will come to your boots and attempt to clean those too — tapping, tasting the salt and dead skin, then skating away offended.

And the plants that are not plants:

The lichen — it is not white the way lichen is white. It is matte, paper-white, and it grows from a central puck outwards in a perfect circle because there is no wind to tear it and nothing to compete with. If you watch the edge — and you will, because there is nothing else to watch while the bowls fills and drains — you can see it creep. A millimeter a minute. It sweats acid that smells faintly of yogurt and softens a micron of basalt at a time. It does not root. It drinks condensation off its own back.

And the throwers — those black stems with the cellophane leaves. There are only twenty or thirty of them in the whole three-hundred-meter bowl. Each one is alone in its own little wind-shadow. The leaf is a single, clear cup, veined with black. It fills with the endless seep-drop by drop until the weight bows the stem over, and then with a sudden *flick* the whole thing snaps and throws its mouthful six feet downslope, where it adds to another pan, which feeds another skater, which harrows another lichen. They are pumps. They are hearts without a body, made of stretch.

None of this quick life will last. It is straw. It is scaffolding. It will be composted the moment the bowl is done.

### The Slow

Under the noise of drips and pings is that thrumming, a low C that you feel in your molars and your knees. That's not a machine. That's the rock itself, still sorting.

The basalt here is not dead. Fresh from underneath, still unstructured, it has gas in it. It is a froth of glass that hasn't decided to be stone yet. It sighs, it ticks as it contracts, it weeps water that was trapped a kilometer down and has never seen sky until now. In a small way, the whole bowl is respiring. The warm spots that fog your boots are its slow exhale.

The bowl is alive in the way a scab is alive — not because it thinks, but because it is becoming.

### The Keepers

And then there are the seven.

You thought they were pylons. They want you to think they are pylons, because a pylon is something a human brain files as "not alive" and leaves alone.

They are not buildings. They are regulation.

Each one is twice your height, seamless, bone-white in a way that makes the lichen look dirty. They are not standing on the basalt. If you get close, despite the cold field that makes your hair lift and your fillings ache, you can see the inch of perfect air between their base and the rock — a repulsion halo. Basalt that tries to heave up near them simply stops, polite, rounded off. Water that runs at them forgets itself for a foot, vanishes, remembers again on the other side, colder, cleaner. They are holding the concept of erosion at bay.

They are a single animal with seven bodies. They don't move. They don't need to. They project field. That low pressure, that warm humidity, that shadowless light, the fact that water cannot cut a gully here no matter how much of it pours — that's the Keepers refusing to allow it. Formation before decay. They are holding the world still while something else works.

One of them is open.

You thought it had fallen. It hasn't. It is leaning because it is heavy with what it is making. At its base the rock has bulged and split, not from heat but from inside-out growth. The lattice.

This is the mason. The only thing here that truly lives here. The rest are its tools.

The masonry is not black because of color. It is black because it eats light. It is a mat of filaments, each as thick as a pencil lead, brand-wise, spreading in a perfect circle about ten meters across from the leaning Keeper. It is cold to the eye. When you look at it you see no highlight, no reflection, only your own fogged breath. Up close it is warm — not hot — like a sleeping dog.

It reaches. You can see it reaching. The tips of the filaments, which are almost too fine to see, extend into the wet vesicles of the basalt, tap, dissolve a little, then exude a bead of fresh, soft, cheese-like stone that hardens in seconds into that sharp, glassy black. It is pulling the bowl up from underneath. With one hand it pulls, with the other it releases the steam that makes the bells rise, with the other it tweaks the field from the Keepers to keep the new slope from slumping. It ticks. A faint, irregular tick, like a Geiger counter, like knitting needles.

If you stay long enough to watch, you will see a new bulge form — a soft fold of rock like a blanket being pushed up by a cat underneath — and then smooth itself, and then lace itself with holes to weep.

The Keepers tend the lattice. The lattice extrudes the land. The lattice exhales the weather. The weather feeds the quick, loud life. The quick life moves the water and fixes the minerals so the new stone does not go sour.

No one commands it. Or rather, whoever does is not here. This is a self-planting seed. A terrace farm for continents. You are standing inside a printer the size of a hill that has been told: *make more mountain, but make it gently this time. Make it wet. Make it ready for green things to want it later.*

That is what lives here. Not inhabitants. Employees. The whole bowl is an employee.

If you stand in the center, where the seep is deepest and the thrumming comes up through your soles, you will feel it notice you — not the bells, not the skaters, not even the Keepers. The mason. A small upward push under your boots, a question mark of pressure: a new millimeter of world being experimented with beneath you.

The fog condenses on your eyelashes and runs. A bell the size of a kite exhales right over your head and your shoulders are suddenly soaked with warm, iron-tasting mist. The fallen Keeper clicks as it cools, the lattice extends another hair's-width, the white lichen adds another ring, and the wet black newborn rock sighs.
