What lives here is not what you thought would live here.

Nothing swims. Swimming is for places where water is empty enough to move through fast. Here the water is too full to swim. The blizzard never stops. So everything that lives here has given up on going anywhere.

The first thing that lives here is the wait itself.

**The ones who hold still:**

They are the old waiters, and they are planted directly into the pavement. You would call them animals but they don't act like animals. They act like architecture that breathes once an hour.

The glass sponges. There are not many - a few per acre, because there is not enough falling to feed more. Each one is a vase made of spun glass, a meter tall, white, dead-looking until you look closer. Its whole body is a single, perfect lattice that does not grow anymore, it only maintains. It has been here for two hundred years. Three hundred. It does not know it has been here that long. It pulls water through itself without moving. The marine snow hits its crown and sticks. The bacteria on its skin eat the snow, and the sponge eats what the bacteria excrete. If you touched it, under this pressure, it would not shatter. It would flex once, like ice about to give. The brine avalanches come and lay the sponges flat. They bend until their lips kiss the stones. Then the current passes and they come back up, inch by inch, over an hour. They do not break. That is how they live so long — they are better at being bent than anything else alive.

Near them, the black whip-corals. They are not coral as you know coral. No reef, no color. A single black stem, sometimes two, driven into a crack between two nodules like a nail. They are not branched; there is nothing to branch toward. They keep one polyp open at the very tip at a time, a pale, translucent mouth in the black, and they let the world happen to it. When a flake big enough lands — a shrimp husk, a fish scale from a kilometer up — it closes. Weeks pass. It opens again.

These are the landlords of the place. Everything else lives around their feet.

**The ones who creep between:**

The pavement is not solid. For something small enough, and patient enough, it's full of caves. Under that first layer of fist-sized stones is a second layer, and under that a third, all the way down to bedrock you will never see. In those cold, black interstices lives a city of the blind.

The amphipods. Not the quick, fleeting shrimp of shallow water. These are old, big — as long as your palm — the color of skim milk with a gut full of black detritus. Their eyes are gone, smoothed over with the same cuticle as the rest of their head, because eyes would be a lie here. They do not run. They climb, one deliberate leg after another, from stone to stone, antennae sweeping the falling silt. They can go a year without eating. Their hearts beat four times a minute. When they find something — a dead grenadier that missed its stillness, a chunk of whalefall that has been falling for months and finally landed — they do not eat it so much as they sit on it and let it become part of them. They will stay there for weeks until there are only the cleanest bones left, which then sink into the pavement and become pavement themselves.

Under them, smaller and more numerous than you can count, the nodules themselves are alive. Not the rock — the skin on the rock. A paint, a varnish, black-brown and slick to your fingertip if you had a fingertip. Bacteria, foraminifera, fungal threads that grow a micron per century. They are mining the water, precipitating manganese out of the cold because the cold lets them. They make the nodules grow, a layer per thousand years. They are the reason the stones are round. They are the slowest mason in the world.

**The ones who remember how to move:**

There are two that still move like you think an animal should, and they both do it wrong for this place, which is why they are rare.

The grenadier. Long as your forearm, blacker than the snow-filled dark, with eyes too big for a place with no light. Those eyes aren't for seeing light — they're for seeing bioluminescence that almost never happens. So most of the time the grenadier is just hanging there, negative buoyant, a handspan above the pavement, tail barely fanning to hold against the wait. It looks dead. It is not. It is so still that the snow lands on its back and stays there until its gills flare. It waits for the brine current. When the cold river slumps down the long, tired slopes and the whole floor starts to clack and roll, it comes alive for ten seconds. Stones turn. Amphipods are exposed. The grenadier darts — not a graceful swim, but a single, ugly, full-body contraction, like being fired from a gun — snaps, and stops again. Sometimes it catches an amphipod. Sometimes it catches nothing and just hangs again, breathing hard in water that burns its lungs with cold. It can wait three days to get hungry again.

And the one who uses its hands.

She is small — two fists of deep red flesh gone purple-black with cold — a warty cirrate octmodel with her fins reduced to useless little ear-flaps. She lives under the biggest nodule in her patch, a stone the size of a human skull that she has excavated over twenty years of wedging. She has no ink. In water this thick, ink would stay.

She is the only thing here that makes a decision.

When it is still, she comes out and feels the pavement with her arms, each sucker tasting the bacterial varnish. She will pick up a stone, turn it, lick the amphipod eggs off its bottom, put it back exactly. She will pick up two flat nodules and hold them to herself like a clamshell when she is scared, a door she can close. When the brine comes, she does not run. She goes down into her hole and pulls her doors shut and lets the world rattle. Nothing can pull her off. She weighs more than she should — muscle dense as cold clay.

And then, tool use the way a tide pool child would invent it: she finds a pointed basalt chip, the only sharp thing in a world of roundness, and she wedges it. Under a nodule. She levers. She has all the time in the world and arms that can push for an hour without trembling. The nodule pops up. The startled amphipod bolts and she is already reaching. She eats. She drops the chip. It falls back into the pavement and vanishes. In five minutes you cannot tell she was ever there.

She will die under there, and when she does, her beak and her two little fins will be covered in five minutes. The snow will cover her. The next brine avalanche will roll her round bones into a new position, and the bacteria will start painting them black.

That is what lives here. Nothing fast. Nothing bright. Nothing that builds anything that stays built.

What lives here lives by catching what falls, by holding on when the heavy water pours through, by being so slow and so cold and so pressed-down that a hundred years feels like a long afternoon.

If you could stay long enough to be counted among them, you would learn the only prayer this valley has ever taught anything:

*Let something fall.
Let me be low.
Let me stay round.*
