Nothing lives here.

That's the first true impression, and it stays true even after you find out it's false.

Nothing *stays* alive here. Not in the way you mean when you say "lives" — with a home, a habit, a territory. This plain is too new and too hard and too loud for that. The water that comes up from below has no food in it, only cold and dissolved stone. There is no soil to rot, no leaf to eat, no soft thing to lay eggs in. So what lives here doesn't live here. It passes through, or it endures, or it hardly qualifies as alive at all.

Start low. Start with what you can't see.

Every pillar sweats, and under the sweat there is a thing that is almost not a thing. If you crouch for a long time in the dim chirring light until the clatter of ten thousand drops settles in your ears like a new kind of silence, and you put your face close to the glassy black where the bead is swelling, you will see that the bead isn't quite clear. It has a skin to it. An opalescence, an oil that isn't oil.

It's film. Bacteria, or something before bacteria. It doesn't grow as green or yellow because there is no light worth harvesting and no logic to color here. It grows as transparency. It lives in the minute exchange between basalt and condensate — eating the iron out of the fresh rock one atom at a time, making the water a fraction less nothing. It is everywhere and nowhere. It blackens the black a little. It is why the droplets bead the way they do. It is the only softness this place allows, and it is so thin you can't touch it without destroying it. It is not enough to make mud. It is enough to make a taste.

And where there is film, there is a grazer.

That pale thing you saw — you were right, there is about one per acre, maybe fewer now. They are not crabs. Crabs are armored. This thing is un-armored on purpose.

Think of a hand made of clear ice and then stretched. The body is a loose sac about as long as your thumb, cloudy white like skim milk, with nothing inside that looks like organs, only a faint brown knot off-center which is its gut. No eyes. No pigment at all, because pigment is expensive and eyes are useless where there are no shadows. It has eight, ten, twelve legs depending on when you catch it — long, hair-thin, jointed like pulled glass threads, each ending in a little soft pad that can taste the film. It moves like a daddy-long-legs moving through broken bottle necks, if the daddy-long-legs had all the time in the world.

Call it a weeper, or a seep-strider.

It doesn't walk across the ground. There is no ground. It spans. It plants one leg on one column-tip, another leg two columns over, and lets its body hang over the black mirror pool between. It lowers its mouth — a soft, unfolding tube — and kisses the stone where a droplet is about to fall. Drinks the drop and the film in it. Takes minutes to do this. The drop never hits stone, so for a second, in its little one-meter parish, the constant clacking stops. That is how you hear it. A pocket of quiet, one beat wide, in the endless percussion.

It feels vibration through those legs more than it hears. The whole plain is a drumhead to it. Your boots, booming through the columns. The low wind-hum when a hollow flute catches a draft — to it that must feel like a god speaking. It stops when you come close not because it sees you, but because the world goes suddenly too loud. Then it waits until your loudness becomes part of the rain again.

There are no males or females. If you turn one over — don't — you'll see it carries its young inside its own translucence, like bubbles in that milky sac. Five or six, each already leggy. When the parent finds a particularly rich sweating face, it will press and split, and leave one behind, already tasting.

They do not die on the plain. If you watch long enough you never find a corpse. They just become clearer and slower until they are film again. Nothing softens hard, but the soft can become hard by becoming nothing.

Above that, there is a second kind of life. The kind that built the little windbreak you found.

They are human.

Not residents. Pilgrims. Listeners. The people from wherever people live beyond the mist where the sea must be.

They come here because of the noise. Because this is the only place for town-sizes in any direction where the world never stops confessing.

They don't stay. You can't. If you stay a day your skin goes white and puckered without ever being rained on. Your boots are full of clear, tasteless water. Your fire won't take because every surface sweats. Your throat hurts from shouting over the clacking. After two days your ears ring even when you leave.

So they come in day-trips, or in vigils. Young, mostly. They wear the same dark oiled cloth that never dries, the same hoods. They come out onto the almost-land with nothing metal, nothing that will rust or clink and add to the noise. They find a clearing where the pillars made an amphitheater by accident and they sit behind three flat shards they've stacked, just enough to take the wind off their neck. And they listen.

They have a faith, if you can call it that: that if you listen to ten thousand random drops hitting stone for long enough, long enough that your brain stops trying to make it into rain, you will start to hear the pause where the plain itself inhales. The center that all the leaning teeth are leaning away from. The thing that exhaled and made the crust buckle into knives.

Some say they hear it. Most just get cold and stack another pointless little nest of points-inward, or a cairn of five stones balanced so precisely that only hands that have gone numb from wet cold could have done it — because this is the only way to leave a mark here. You can't carve wet glass basalt. You can't write. You can only put it briefly in a different order. *I stopped here. I heard it. Or I didn't.*

They leave before their own water — sweat, breath, tears — adds too much to the plain's weeping. They leave the weepers alone. There's an unspoken rule: you never step on a black mirror pool. Not out of kindness. Because when you look into one, two meters of doubled pillars falling into forever under your feet, you get the violent vertigo that you are standing on air above a city of teeth, and your body decides for you.

If you stay very long after the last of them has gone, and the light gets a fraction dimmer, you become the third kind.

The plain itself. It lives the way a fresh scab lives. Not because it wants to. Because it's still cooling, still contracting, still pushing bad water out of microscopic vesicles. *Tick. Tak. Tok.* That fizzing you hear inside the columns if you put your ear to them — that is its metabolism. The slow closing of glass. The stacks and nests left by the pilgrims are not on it, but briefly *in* it, like splinters it will work out over a winter when the pools freeze black and shove.

It sweats without end, it sings when the draft from the unseen sea moves through its hollow flutes, and it is so young it has not yet learned to be quiet.
