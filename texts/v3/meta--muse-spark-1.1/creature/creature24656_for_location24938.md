It is not empty. That is the first mistake.

In the dim that isn't dark, your eyes want walls. You want rough basalt, breakdown, still water with a skin on it. For a long time that is all you get. You have to stand still long enough for the place to decide you are part of the temperature.

Then the wall flickers.

Not all of it. Never all at once. A single point unsticks itself from the texture and straightens an antenna. Then another. Then ten more you hadn't been looking at move because the first one moved.

What lives here lives *spaced*. That's the arrangement. Nothing here swarms. Nothing piles, except where it has to.

The crickets own the air just above the rock. Small — the first joint of your thumb — and the white of old wax. Not albino, just unpainted. No eyes worth naming. They are on everything that is vertical. In any hundred feet, a thousand of them, but each one keeps a polite distance from the next, a handspan of empty black rock between. They have long hind legs folded like knives, and antennae twice as long as their bodies that never stop writing in the air. They don't jump unless you breathe on them. They saw. One thin leg against a ridged wing. Alone it's something you could ignore. Together, wall after wall, bend after bend for a mile, it becomes the air. A high, glassy, ceaseless trill that has no peak and no trough. It makes the back teeth ache.

Under that, the bats.

They are the ceiling where the ceiling is still there. Pallid. Bone-colored with ears so long they look crumpled, translucent at the edges. They do not bunch into a mass like other bats. They hang in a loose sheet, one per square foot, one per square foot, as far as the slot-light lets you see and farther than that. All facing the same way, into the long drift of the rift, like they are listening down its length. They breathe shallow and in sync — you can see it if you watch one and let your peripheral vision go soft, the whole veil of them lifting and settling together. They click to each other constantly. Not hunting clicks. Talking clicks. A dry, soft *tik-tik, tik* like fingernails on stone. That's part of the roar, too.

On the floor, the water-bearers.

Every pan, every knee-deep, glass-still basin that the ceiling has been dripping into since this place tore open, is full of salamanders. Blind and pink-white, with external gills that feather out like red ferns. They don't swim so much as they hang in the water, stacked. One on top of another, three, five, ten deep, fitted together like spoons in a drawer to share the little warmth there is. Only their gills move, waving gently. Every few minutes one rises — not fast, no panic — breaks that dusty skin on the water and takes a sip of the cool air with a sound you can hear in the otherwise stillness: *plip*. Then sinks again. The pools never ripple for long.

Between the pools, in the black silt that never quite dries, live the workers.

Two kinds, and they know each other.

The beetles first. Big as your fist, and black in a way that isn't color but absence of it, their backs polished by squeezing through cracks. They are everywhere if you crouch. Not many in one spot — always that spacing — but continuous. They move slow, deliberate, nosing silt. And they carry.

You'll be sitting on a fresh block that could still cut you and you'll see one pick up a flake of chert in its jaws — one of those bright, feather-edged shards that have only existed since the world dropped here, so sharp it is still clear at the edge. The beetle holds it like you would hold a good knife in your teeth. It finds a lichen-eating snail — small, conical, also pale — and wedges that flake under the lip of the shell and levers, patient and strong. The flake often snaps. The beetle drops it. Finds another. There are places by the pools where the ground is a little workshop: a pitted piece of basalt used as an anvil, a scatter of failed flakes, a little midden of empty shells. Not built. Just used, for generations, in exactly the same spot.

And the diggers.

You smell them before you see them, a faint musky smell like potato earth. Something like a rat, but wrong. No eyes, only a seam where eyes would have been, and forepaws turned out in great spades, mole-hands, pink and clawed. They don't make burrows that last — the silt is too wet — they make shallow scrapes, temporary beds. They are shell-eaters too, and stone collectors. Each one has a cache. Not a nest. A choice. From a hundred yards away, from the foot of a breakdown pile, it has carried back three or four stones that felt right — smooth basalt, hard quartzite cobbles that fell from somewhere up above when the ceiling was still deciding. It keeps them at the edge of its scrape. When it finds a snail the beetles couldn't open, it picks up its favorite stone in both hands, sets the snail on the anvil, and taps. *Tick. Tick. TICK.* Until it opens. If it loses a stone, it goes looking for it. You will see them snuffling, dragging, patting the silt.

That is as far as it has gone. No stacking stones. No carrying fire. No pictures. Tool use that woke up and then sat back down and has been quite content to sit there forever, stone on stone, because it works.

Plant-life lives here too but it doesn't act like it lives here. It acts like a guest that was invited and never left. Lichen, the color of wet newspaper, stretched absurdly thin over boulders so as not to presume. Ghost ferns, translucent, the size of a child's hand, only where the splash of a *plip* reaches them. Moss like a soaked sweater in a deep crack. And one juniper — just one — that hit the lottery and germinated under a slot, its trunk twisted white, its roots doing something impossible, crawling fifty feet across bare rock like veins looking for a pan.

None of them press in. They all leave room.

That is what lives here. Not a teeming. An occupation. A rift that broke open fresh and then, at the exact moment when there was enough damp for something to try, paused. And everything that managed to get in took one step in, spaced itself out to the exact distance where it wouldn't have to compete, picked up the nearest good stone, and stayed.

If you sit long enough the clicking and sawing and plipping stop being sound and become a pressure, like air. Loud enough you have to raise your voice. Even enough you forget to hear it.
