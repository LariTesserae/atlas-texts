They live here. At first you think they don't.

At first you think the kelp forest is empty, because you are looking for the wrong kind of living. You're looking for walking, for standing upright, for shapes that cut the water. Nothing here cuts. 

Then a frond moves against the drift. Then a bell clacks — a low, water-logged *tok* that isn't rock cooling — and you realize the shadow between two bladder-roots is a shoulder. Is a back. Is a person holding utterly, perfectly still.

The Summit Folk.

You don't see them until you learn to stop thrashing. They see you coming from a hundred meters away because you are noisy, bright, panicked with air. Your bubbles rip the quiet. They watch the way you would watch a wounded animal blundering through your house.

They hang.

That is the first true thing about them. Not stand, not swim, not sit. They hang in the tangle, one long arm hooked over a rope, knees tucked into a net, ankles looped around a kelp stipe. Stillness is their skill and their prayer and their breathing technique.

In the lowlands you would call them human, and you would not be wrong, but you would be imprecise, the way you would be imprecise calling a cave fish a fish. They came up as humans. Their grandparents came up, fleeing or curious or driven out, from below the new range. The mountain kept being born under them. They stayed.

Look at one closer — an old woman, because the old are the stillest and therefore the easiest to see.

Her chest is enormous. Not fat, not muscle. Barrel. The ribs have bowed out over a lifetime of slow water-breathing, flared like the hoops of a cask. When she was a baby her mother would sit in the warmest part of the air-house and press her thumbs into the soft cartilage, stretching it, morning and evening, singing through it, while the baby squalled and coughed and learned not to drown. All the mothers do it. It hurts. It has to.

Her skin is the color of tea left for days, stained by tannin from the black kelp. It never truly dries. It is slick, not with sweat but with a fine biofilm that keeps the constant wet from parching her. Liver spots look like lichen. Very old folk have green growing in the creases of their elbows, their scalps, actual filamentous algae that they cultivate and trim like hair. Her hair itself is cut very short — long hair is a snag, a drowning hazard — except for two long, ropy braids that she has woven with cord and small shell bells. The bells are her address.

Her eyes are large, and they have gone strange. The light here is always dusk, always diffuse, green-black. No sharp shadows. So their pupils stay wide, and the iris has thinned to a pale, almost luminous ring, amber or grey. They don't blink often. A membrane, like a second eyelid, slides sideways when particulates drift too close. She looks at you without focusing at first, then all at once, too close.

Her throat moves. That's the other thing. Open your mouth and look — if she will let you, and she might, because old people are generous — you will see the frilled, red-black pleats inside her neck where the water goes. Not gills, not really. Not enough for a fish. Borrowed lung. The lining of her throat and the roof of her mouth and the top third of her esophagus is ruffled into folds, into gills-the-body-remembered-how-to-make-when-it-had-to. It takes. It takes just enough oxygen from the hanging sea if she does not move, if she does not fear. You can hear it, if you put your ear near: a faint, wet whistling, like wind in reeds.

When she needs to move, she can't breathe. So she holds what she has and pulls.

And then she moves like the rest of them.

A child goes by you — you didn't see him arrive — and you finally understand the locomotion of the place. He is maybe seven, chest still small, still pink and new and not yet stretched. He cannot filter long. He darts. Three fast hand-over-hand pulls along the main rope, legs kicking not to propel but to keep from tangling, then he ducks — headfirst — into a bladder house swelling out from a kelp stem. The bladder is translucent, big as a lowland hut, full of almost-air. The kelp breathes it out, a slow exhalation of oxygen and rot. The inside smells of fermentation and warm skin. The child bursts through the membrane valve, which reseals with a wet kiss, and inside you hear him cough, huge whooping joyful coughs, then laugh, then breathe, then take another lungful and launch again. All the young live this way: sprint and hide, sprint and hide, like dolphins, like breath-holding games made absolute.

The middle-aged — the builders, the carvers, the fish-herders — have learned the middle rhythm. Pull. Pause. Hang. Breathe water. Pull again. They carry everything on cords tied to their bodies. Their hands never empty. In a loop on the wrist: a shell scraper, a basalt flake, a thorn needle, a bead knife for cutting bladder fruit. Fruit that looks like a drowned lung, speckled, and when pierced sighs sweet air. Their other arm is rope-burned smooth. Rope burn is a tattoo here, black kelp cord polished into the skin.

They herd, too. Not much lives here besides them, but what does live has become theirs.

The blind white fish — *yutu* they call them — are longer than a man, fat as pigs, sightless, and stupidly calm. They drift through the forest eating fallen fronds. The children ride them by hooking their knees under the gill plates and tapping directions on their cold flanks. The children sing to them, not with voice — voice is wasted here — but by knocking two stones together in a pattern that carries through water and flesh. The fish shiver and turn.

The shrimp-things, transparent as glass noodles, live only inside the living bladders, cleaning them. The people do not eat the shrimp. To eat your housekeeper is bad luck. They eat the beetles, though. The one beetle that learned to keep its wing-cases sealed and swim. It is big as your thumb, black, and when cracked and fermented in oil it burns with that low blue light they use for the birthing houses and the dying houses. Every home has a single blue lamp, kept alive.

They do not talk the way you do. Talking costs air. They have words, short ones, that they will say inside an air-pocket with much coughing and delight. But in the water, they touch. One tap on a rope means *look*. Two taps means *hold still*. A long drag means *rock coming*. They know whose tug is whose the way you know a voice. A whole argument can travel along three hundred meters of main-line in shivers. And at night — if you can call it night, when the evening never ends but deepens a little — they ring the bells. Each family has a tone, each house a different shell cut. You hang in your net and listen to the city locate itself to itself. Clack... clack-clack... *tok*. I am here. I am here. I am still here. Hold to me.

Death here is not drowning. Drowning is for newcomers. For lowlanders who keep the air in their chest until it burns. Death is when the throat finally tires, or when a new basalt shoulder comes up hot and too fast and there is no time to cut your house away, or when you go too still and become part of the tangle. Very old people choose it. They find a quiet fork where two old stipes meet, they wedge themselves in, they stop pulling themselves to air. They filter, and filter, and filter slower, until the water is just water again. The kelp grows over them quickly. Within a season there is a new knot in the wood where a person used to be.

They don't think this is sad. The mountain is still being born. The forest is still learning to be a forest. They are the brief soft things that live in the interval between water and air, while the world decides which it will be. They weave, and carve while the rock is soft, and hold their breath not as emergency but as home, and listen to the mountain tick and sigh beneath them.

If you stay quiet long enough, they will forget you are thrashing and let you hang beside them. One will reach out — skin on skin, slick and warm — and press your chest gently, reminding you: let it out. Let the water in slow. Don't be afraid.
