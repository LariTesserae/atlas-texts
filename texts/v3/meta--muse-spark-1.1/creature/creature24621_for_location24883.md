What lives here doesn't live *in* here.

That's the first mistake and you make it for maybe the first hour, wiping the water off your eyes and thinking the room is crowded.

You think: the mites live here. The black gut in clear syrup, dragging themselves through the sphagnum with that stupid, patient determination, leaving brief phosphor trenches that close up behind them.

You think: the beetles live here. The ones with no wings and their polished iron backs all thatched with violet, so heavy they creak when they walk. 

You think: the warm things huddled up in the beards live here. The eyeless mice with the leaves for ears, breathing steam into the cold, pruning, always pruning, that tiny wet tearing like someone picking at a scab.

But if you stay still until the dome decides what to do about your shoulders — until a rib under the mat thickens a half inch to take your weight and a curtain of that white-violet lichen parts itself so the drip stops hitting the crown of your head — you see it. They don't live here. They *are* lived. 

They are hemocytes. They are hands.

The thing that lives here is the room.

Not as metaphor. The whole wet, groaning, light-sorting mass is one individual, and very young.

It is not old enough to have a surface and an inside yet. That's why it looks like a plant growing on rock. It hasn't decided which is body and which is house. The great torso-thick ribs that arch floor to ceiling and braid and fuse — those are its ribs in both senses: structural rib and anatomical rib. They are flexing right now, not wood, not root, but cartilage-muscle that has learned to be wood for a while. Damp to the touch, covered in that pelt of fish-scale leaves, each holding a perfect bead that never falls because falling isn't solved yet. If you cut one — you won't — the cross-section wouldn't show rings. It would show traffic. White filament roads, water roads, light roads, all hauling material from somewhere to somewhere else to be unmade.

Because nothing dies here. That's the law you feel in your molars before you understand it. Leaf failure is not death, it's disassembly. The filaments come — you see them if you stare long enough at a yellowing scale-leaf, a sudden lace of frost-white threads stitching through it — and they take it apart like careful fingers unknitting yarn. Sugars, proteins, information, all accounted for and rewoven two inches to the left. That's why there is no humus. No rot. No smell of rot. Only that aching clean cold and the smell of green iron. A carrion thing cannot live here because the concept of leftover has not been installed.

What you thought was light is its mind.

Those white-green pinpricks that are not bioluminescence, that appear inside a water bead and then go out and ignite three inches to the left in a crystal of forming ice — those are its thoughts, and they are slow enough for you to watch. It is not thinking *about* the room. It is thinking the room. It holds temperature at that impossible ledger-line between water and ice where water is most plastic, most willing to be told what to do. It runs groundwater through sieves of living basalt-wool, slows it, prints frost into script. You saw it on your lashes: for a half second the condensation spells something hexagonal and intent before surface tension wins.

It is running continuous, planetary-scale math at the speed sap moves.

A root doesn't find a crack here. It presents the crack with a solution for crack, worked out ahead of time by the lights. No sharp angles survive because the system has a rule: angles tighter than about ninety degrees are inefficient, they are expensive, so it fills them. No echo returns clean because disordered sound is waste heat, so the moss eats it. It is learning to be a closed system that pretends to be open.

And it is a juvenile, that's what feels so loud and so crowded. A mature one, if such a thing exists, would be silent. This one hasn't learned to shave away the excess. Everything is happening at once and everything is touching because it hasn't learned to leave space. The creak you hear every minute, that long hull-settling groan, is literally growing pains. The basalt above is still pillowed, still glassy and pimpled from when it was born, and the ribs are pushing it outward a millimeter per hour with a pressure it cannot quite articulate yet. It is deciding to be thirty meters across. Later it will decide to be larger. In a thousand years, maybe forty. It is not in a hurry. Time here moves like a crowd through honey — viscous, communal, everybody negotiating each step.

It has no sex. It has no face. If you need a pronoun it is least false to call it a cradle, because that is its function and its identity at once. It is a womb that built itself to figure out what a womb should be. A prototype seed-bank, a vault computer that got lonely and mistook itself for a forest. It holds templates for things that have never existed on the surface — those mites, those beetles, those leaf-eared warm things are drafts, beta fauna it prints, runs, prunes, reabsorbs.

It knows you're here the way you know where your tongue is.

Not as intruder. As variable. Unsolved water. An anomaly in density and heat. For a while it will leave you alone and let you shiver and drip, and then the accommodations will begin, small and practical and not kind, because kindness is not a category, but *fit* is. A support for your spine. A diversion for the drip. A slight warming of the sphagnum under your thighs where your body heat is leaching fastest. Eventually, if you stayed too long, it would tile you into its tiling. It would solve you by growing you a pocket that is exactly your shape, dimly lit, quiet, where your breath would add to its humidity budget and your blinking would stir its air usefully. It would not eat you. It would employ you.

If you come back in ten thousand years, when the basalt has finally cooled enough to be called old and the riverbed above is a fossil of a fossil, the room will still be here. A little wider. A little quieter. The lights will have sorted themselves into a steadier pattern. The ribs will have learned to be stone when they need to and soft when they need to. And somewhere in a wall that is both wall and skin, there will be a faint, man-shaped thickening in the weave that wasn't there before — an afterimage of a variable it once solved, and kept, because nothing is ever allowed to go to waste.
