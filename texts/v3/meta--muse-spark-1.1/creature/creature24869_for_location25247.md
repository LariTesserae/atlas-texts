They live low.

That's the first thing about them. Not low because they crouch — low because they have never had any reason to stand tall. Not stooped — settled. Like water settles.

You see it before you see people. You see movement that is the same height as the crab-flow, as the toad-backs, as the roofs. Everything in the basin lives at ankle to knee to chest height. Nothing stands up into the steam column and stays there. The people here are broad and short-legged, even the children look compacted, as if the warm weight of the air has been pressing on the tops of their heads since birth. Not stunted. Compressed. Made dense to match the rock.

There are two kinds of inhabitants, and they are the same thickness of life.

First is the non-people. The ones who got here first.

They never left the wet. The crab-things, which aren't crabs but have decided to be crabs again, because new basalt wants crabs. Hand-wide, black-legged, dome-backed with a dirty yellow that is not color but bacterial film they farm on their own shells. They are the ground. They coat every horizontal surface, every wall that weeps, and they move as a single viscid sheet that clicks. When you stop moving, you can feel them under your feet testing your skin with their mouthparts, deciding if you are rock or food.

Above them, the air itself is inhabited. The gnats live in pillars, each pillar holding its shape for hours, a mile high and then collapsing and reforming somewhere else. They are born, breed, die in a day, and their bodies are a constant rain into the pans. That rain is what everything else eats. The white toads eat the gnats and the crabs. They are huge, the size of a human chest, loaf-like and the color of old fat, with legs tucked so tight you think they have none until they lurch. Their eyes are lidded against the steam. They sit in the intersections of lanes and in the doorways and on the flat roofs, hundreds, thousands of them, each throat inflating into a pale balloon in rhythm. They are not croaking — the sound never stops being a croak. The air shudders. 

In the deep organ-throats of the columns live the salamanders, long as your arm, blind, translucent pink-white with external gills that feather and close like hands. And the bat-things, which aren't bats, but leathery amphibians that never learned to dry. They hang in clots under every overhang, upside down, wet wings folded. They shriek just out of human hearing, and also far below it, so you feel them in your teeth.

None of this is background. It's the economy.

Then there are the people. And they are not separate from it.

They are wet people. You notice that before anything else — the skin of every person in the lane is wet, permanently, like the walls. Their hair, if they keep it, is plastered thin. Most don't. Most shave head and body clean, child and elder and mother and hunter alike, because hair holds the gnats and the fungus. Their scalps gleam in the dim. Their clothes are not clothes. A single cured gut-skin apron, or a drape of bat-leather to keep the crabs from testing you while you squat. Sometimes a vest stitched from crab backs that clacks when they move. Their feet are incredible. Broad, splayed, the toes far apart, the soles thickened into yellow pads cracked like the basalt itself, cut all over with fine white scars from the knife-edges of new stone. They never wear shoes. Shoes would fill with water. Shoes would slide off stone that is always slick with biofilm. They learn to walk with their ankles soft, their weight always directly over the foot, slow and certain. A child who runs here bleeds.

They are pale underneath the wet. Not white — the color of something that has never seen a true sun, a translucent, bluish, amphibian pallor. You can see the veins in their temples. Their eyes are large, dark-adapted, lidded often against the sting of mineral water and the flies. The rims are red, always. Their ears are small, tucked close, the canals swollen half-shut from constant water and noise. Many of the old are truly deaf — not from age, from decades inside the roar. They hear through bone.

Because of the sound, they never stopped touching.

The basin roar is total — toads plus gnats plus clicking plus the low hiss of a million seeps — you can't raise your voice loud enough to cross it for long. If you try you go hoarse by midday and everyone here is already hoarse. So they talk with their hands on each other. A mother presses two fingers to her child's throat to feel the buzz of words. Two men passing with baskets of live crabs lean forehead to forehead for a second to transact. Children speak a fast hand-flutter in the ankle-deep water in front of their faces, a sign-language that has grown too quick for outsiders. When they do vocalize, it's short, tonal grunts pitched to cut through specific bands of the roar — not sentences, markers: *here. danger-foot. lift. this-one-is-dead.* The real talk happens at night, or what passes for night when the diffuse light dims a fraction. They press together in the low basalt cells, many bodies to a room because the rock holds warmth, and in the dark and relative quiet they murmur throat to chest, chest to chest.

They eat what lives here and nothing else. There is nothing else. The old world at the rim of the basin has plants, has wood, has deer, but no one goes there for long — the air is too dry, they say. They dip fine nets woven from dried salamander sinew through the warm pans and haul up writhing black crab, gnat larvae the size of rice, white tadpoles big as fingers. They peel the crabs with copper pins kept like relics. They eat the toads — legs, belly, the bitter spawn jelly that tastes of iron. They scrape the tea-colored biofilm off rocks with flat shells and let it set into cakes that sour and hold. Everything tastes of mineral and ammonia and warm water. They drink the cold seep from the border where the old rock meets the new, carried in bladders made from toad throat-sacs.

They build with what dies here. Their houses really are part of the column field — they find a stand of basalt hexagons, knock the tops off with basalt mauls, stretch gut-skin across, weigh it with stones. Over generations they've learned to mortar crab carapace to crab carapace with a cement of ground shell, ash, shit, and the same bacterial mud they eat, until walls become hard and faintly stinking and still alive with crabs on the outside. A wall is never done. A wall is being repaired. That's what most of the people are doing at any given moment: crouched, ankle-deep, smearing, replacing, feeling for leaks, because the water never stops coming through. It's not labor, it's grooming, the way a toad keeps its skin wet.

There is no ownership of a house because houses are not discrete. The town is one low, labyrinthine mat of rooms that all share rock. You go from one family's sleeping cell to another's cooking pan by ducking through a hide door into yet another cell where an old woman is scraping skins. Privacy is a concept that would require dry air and silence to develop. Here privacy is going down into a deep column throat with a lid, to sit alone in the hot dark with a salamander, until your ears ring from the absence of roar.

Children are everywhere, and they are held constantly, not from affection alone — though there is that, soft and off-hand — but because if you put a small child down in a lane the flow will move around it and cover it. Crabs, water, people. So the little ones ride on hips, on shoulders, in slings of bat-skin, their bald wet heads bobbing just above the gnat cloud. They learn to be still very early. Stillness means the gnats don't find you as fast.

The dead do not leave the equilibrium. There is no ground to dig — the basalt is too new and too hard, and it weeps water that would bring the body back up. The dead are given to the pans. Weighted with a column-top stone, lowered into the deepest, warmest tea-colored pools where the bacteria is thickest. By morning the crabs have taken the eyes, by evening the shape is gone, and the numbers stay the same. The pan stays the same depth. The town stays the same size. The roar stays the same.

If you stand in a lane long enough, they stop seeing you as interruption and start seeing you as rock — something that has newly cooled and has not yet been covered. A woman will come and without asking scrape the film off your forearm with a shell to taste if it is good film. A child will touch your throat to ask if you have a voice under the water. An old man, blind from steam, will lay his broad cracked foot directly on top of yours to feel how you balance.

They do not feel sorry that they are here. They do not feel trapped. Trapped requires a memory of somewhere dry and loud in a different way. This is a steady-state people. They have been here long enough in this new place that they have become the soft part of the stone, the part that keeps it wet.
