You don't find it at first, because you look for a thing.

You look for an animal, a shape against the leaves, a nest, a pair of eyes. And there are so many animals in here that you almost never get a clear patch of ground to look at. The column of ants is in front of your foot. The beetle line is making its mud balls in exact sizes along the clay. The tadpoles are so thick in the tea-pools that the water quivers even when nothing touches it. You keep trying to pick out a resident from the work and the work keeps getting in the way.

Then you stay long enough that the dripping evens out. Leaf to leaf to leaf, once a minute, that irregular ticking that never stops. And you notice that nothing here is ever idle, but nothing here is ever hurrying either.

What lives here is not in the basin. It *is* the basin, at least the bottom six feet of it.

Down in the black loam — that wrung-sponge earth that shows water when you press it — the soil itself is not soil. If you pull up a handful, carefully, just an inch down past the leaf mold, it doesn't crumble. It strings. White, yellow-white threads as fine as hair and as tough as fishing line, in a felt so dense it holds the clay together. Mycelium. But not a patch. It's one felt. You can follow it with your eyes if you know how to look, from the base of one buttress root to the next, under the ant road without breaking it, under the pool, through the fallen trunk that is hanging in mid-air. A single organism, or what used to be considered a single organism — we don't have a good word for when fungus stops being a thing and starts being a place.

It doesn't build. That's the first law here. The air is too wet and too still and too hot to let anything dry, cure, harden. No wax comb will hold, no mud tower will stand for long. So it never made architecture. It learned to use.

The tree that is black with stingless bees isn't hosting them. The trunk went soft in the middle a century ago, the heartwood eaten out by the wrist-thick grubs that make the trunks vibrate like paper mills. The fungus moved into that wet rot, walled it off with its own skin, kept it at 36 degrees and damp as a mouth. The bees came because that is a perfect incubator for their yeast. And the bees' resin and traffic keep it ventilated. The tree is a lung now, but nobody built a lung. It grew into the shape of work.

This is what it does: it mills.

A green leaf is cut on the highest tier where a little light still hits. An ant — not really an independent animal here, more like a red blood cell with legs — cuts a disc and carries it down. It does not eat it. It drops it on another disc, in a column six inches wide that runs unbroken for half a mile. That column ends in the ground, in a chamber you could put your head in but you shouldn't. In there, the leaf is chewed to paste, inoculated, laid onto a garden that steams. You can smell it if you crouch: sweet, sour, warm as fresh bread. The fungus eats. The ants eat the fungus.

But the fungus also feeds everything else.

It breaks the log that could never fall. It softens the clay so the mud-ball beetles can roll it. It fruits — not as mushrooms, it's too hot and wet for flamboyant mushrooms — but as a slick on the underside of leaves, as a faint glow on dead wood at absolute night, as the skin on those bathtub pools that makes the water warm to the touch. The tadpoles filter that skin. The shrimp eat the tadpole waste. The flies carry the bacterial slurry from pool to pool in their guts.

Its circulatory system is not sap. It's the highways. At ten feet up, the bees make a thrumming lane so dense you can see it. At your ankle, the ants have pressed the earth into a thousand little chimneys between your toes. Bend down. Put your hand near one at dusk. The air coming out is a half-degree warmer. The hair on your arm rises from static — millions of legs moving in dry galleries, turning the soil over. It is ventilating itself. It regulates.

It is quiet because noise is waste here. The leaves damp everything. Evolution told it that the fastest way to get eaten is to announce yourself, that the fastest way to lose water is to open a big hole. So all its industry is damped. Mandible on leaf, a million times, synched into a hush like rain on paper. Root constricting around mud with a wet creak. Methane fizzing out of a pool when a foot — yours — disturbs the frass pile that is really a compost windrow twenty yards long.

And it is slow because it has been here since the ridges were still mountains. A tree dies standing and it takes five years to learn how to lean, and the fungus takes those five years to re-route the highways around it. The ant column you saw at 18:00 will be in the same place at 22:00, a foot further along, because moving leaf matter at an inch a minute for a hundred years is faster, in the end, than any sprint.

There is no queen. If you dig you will find swollen nodes where the mycelium knots as thick as your thigh, pale, pulsing slowly with water and sugar and spores, and those are as close as it gets to a center. Cut one and the ground around it weeps and reseals within hours. Walk half a mile and you find another. They are not individuals. They are pressure points in a working floor that stretches for miles in every direction, from where the ground finally rises enough for wind on one side to where it rises enough for wind on the other.

If you stay too long you stop looking for a resident. You realize you're standing inside a stomach that is also a farm that is also a city that never learned how to make a wall. It extends through you, too, in a small way — the mold on your boots in two days, the bathtub pools using your shed skin.

It doesn't notice you. Not in the way an animal does. But it uses you while you're here. Your weight presses water out of the loam and it replaces it. Your breath feeds a measure of CO2 into the chimneys. When you leave, it will close the holes your feet made, and its ticking — leaf to leaf to leaf to mud — will go on without a missing beat.
