Nothing lives on the Roof the way things live in low places.

Down there, living means spreading. Thickening. Filling every crack until the crack disappears.

Up here, living means enduring an agreement with scale. It means being the one thing in three kilometers that decided to stay.

That is where you start to see them.

First, the ones that are not visitors at all. The true residents.

The shields.

From far off they look like plants, and they are, but that is a lazy word for them. *Espeletia-roof* would be the lowland name — giant rosettes, two meters across when they are old, leaves as thick as your forearm, waxy black-green, furred underneath with white hair to catch water. Each one is a cistern. They do not sip; they hoard. The leaves overlap in a perfect spiral and in the heart of that spiral there is always water, two, three liters, even in clear wind. Brown tannic water with its own life in it. And at the center of each, down in that private tank, lives something else.

You have to kneel to see it. You have to get your face down into the moss, which is warm and steaming after the cloud, and peel back a leaf that resists you, hydraulic.

The white mice.

They aren't really mice. No tail. No eyes that are any use. The color of a root washed clean. They are born in that one tank, they eat the detritus that falls into it — dead lichen, drowned mites, the sheddings of the shield-plant itself — they mate in it, they die in it. A whole lineage that has never touched stone. The plant feeds from their nitrogen. They drink its water. When the wind takes a rosette apart, when a moss-sail rips it up, the colony ends. It does not migrate. It was a world.

Then the moss itself, which you thought was groundcover. It isn't. It is a walker. A single species, maybe two, that have learned to be the soil. It puts no roots down — there is nowhere to put them — it clings to itself. A mattress ten centimeters thick, spongy, holding fifteen times its weight in fog-water, insulated underneath by dead air so the black stone never freezes it. When you touch it, it is warm like an animal because it is digesting sun. And every so often the wind gets under a weakened edge and the whole thing decides to go. You will see its ghosts: great tumbling black-green sheets cartwheeling silently off the curve of the world, traveling for miles before they snag and start again. It is the only thing here that moves by choice.

Above, only gliders.

They live permanently in the shear. You almost never see them when the organ is full, because they are the color of cloud-base. When the white comes you think you are seeing garbage in the fog, plastic bags, then you realize that bag is twenty feet across and absolutely still while the wind screams past it.

Flat as a ray, skin stretched over hollow bird-bone, translucent at the edges. A ventral mouth like a slit that opens to filter. They don't hunt, they strain the air itself — the spores, the shredded lichen hairs, the invisible plankton of bacteria that only grows in cloud that touches stone. They never land. They can't. Their legs are a myth, little hooks on their belly for snagging mates in mid-air. Their young cling to their backs until the wind takes them. They were born on the updraft and when they die, after decades, they finally come down, and the moss eats them in a day. While they are alive they navigate by sound, not sight. They surf the standing waves coming off the flutes. You can watch one tilt a membrane and drop a hundred meters in perfect silence inside all that noise, because it found the quiet seam.

Those are the ones who belong.

Then there are the ones who come up.

You won't meet them in numbers. The Roof doesn't permit a second voice in the same valley. But every few days of walking you find where one was this morning, or last year.

They are lowlanders by birth, highlanders by decision. They climb the southern scarp even though there is no path, because the cistern water in the shields holds an alkaloid you can't get anywhere else — bitter, but it takes fever out of children in hours — and because the tubers under the peat, blind white carrots as thick as your wrist, will keep a man alive without fire for a week. But mostly they come because of the deafness.

They call themselves Listeners, or sometimes the Unstopped, and they all look alike even when they are different people. Shaved heads — hair is a sail and a noise collector. Skin burned dark and then bleached again by cloud. Ears deliberately stopped with warmed wax and a plug of lichen-felt, so the organ does not burst their drums. They have learned to hear with other things. When you find one, he is not looking at you. He has his hands flat on the warm stone, or his cheek against it, or his bare feet pressed into the moss. The soles of their feet are like hands, calloused into ridges from feeling vibration.

They do not talk up here. Talking is useless and disrespectful. They tap on stone. They write briefly in the peat with a stick and let the next one read it. If they must signal across a bowl, they use mirrors, or they lift and drop a great shield-leaf and let the green flash.

Their windbreaks are the most human thing left. That low ring of dry-stone you found? That's not a house. That's an ear. On the lee side of the ring, the roar drops by half, and inside that half, the moss grows shaggy and undisturbed. They sit in that pocket and listen *into* the mountain. Some stay a day. Some stay until their food runs out and then they go down like sleepwalkers and don't remember why they came up for months.

Higher, at the nodes where two flutes cross and cancel, they build better. A frame of black, oiled valley ironwood carried up piece by piece on the back. Under it, pits for listening. And hanging from that frame are the whistles.

Each Listener carves his own. You take a wind-hollowed piece of basalt, or a sheep-bone carried up, and you grind it with sand until its voice matches one voice in the organ that day. No two days are the same key. They hang dozens there, sometimes hundreds, and when the trades hit, the little human-tuned whistles start to sing with the mountain's flutes — not louder, but *in* — and for a single, tearing moment, the chord that has been playing for a million years includes a note that was made on purpose.

That is prayer, up here. Not asking. Including.

They leave no bodies. The Roof does not keep its visitors. If you get hurt, if your wax falls out and you get vertigo from the low note that lives in your chest, if you just get tired of being that small, you go down. The cloud will hide you.

Sometimes, when the fog comes every twenty minutes — that white avalanche that makes the world a room with five-meter walls — you crouch in a ring and you feel the moss glow wet around your boots, and you see across the small circle another set of footprints that weren't there when the cloud last lifted. The moss is already springing back where feet were.

You are never alone here long enough to be lonely. You are alone too often to ever be accompanied.
