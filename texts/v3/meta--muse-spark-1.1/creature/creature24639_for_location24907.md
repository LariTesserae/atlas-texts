What lives here doesn't live *on* it. It lives *as* it.

At first you don't see anything alive at all. That's the trick of density when the density is perfect. You see a floor. A patterning of tile and plate and concrete pour — mottled brown, dishwater white, oil-slick black, rust-red — stretching to a horizon that never gets hazy because the air itself is wet. And then you stand a little longer and you realize the mottling has a gradient that wasn't there a second ago. The brown has flowed an inch to the left.

Then the entire plain moves.

The base of it, the thing everything else eats whether they know it or not, is not an animal at all. It's the darkening on the tile. A biofilm, thick as paint, slick as soap, black-green and iron-red, that grows on every surface that stays wet — which is every surface. It has no leaves. No structure. It photosynthesizes without ever having learned to be green, drinking that sourceless white-yellow light and the heat and the trace minerals that weep out of the false rocks. It doesn't grow tall. There's no point. It just makes more of itself sideways, endlessly, and makes the floor slippery. If you scrape it, it smells like a hot battery. It is the pasture. It is the only plant this world ever needed.

Grazing it are the ones that make you think the floor is tiled in living plates.

**The rollers.** 

They are isopods, but the word stops meaning anything when you see them at scale. They are the same animal printed at three different sizes and they all do the same job. The smallest are thumbnail-sized and they move like lice, legions of them, polishing each ceramic tile with their soft undersides. The middle ones are the size of plates, of hubcaps, and they fit exactly into the expansion joints and scrape them clean with their faces. The large ones — the ones the size of suitcases, of footlockers, of small cars — move so slowly you think they are part of the architecture until you come back ten minutes later and they have advanced a foot. Same segmented back, same seven pairs of legs tucked discreetly underneath, same ability to roll into a perfect, seamless hemisphere when you get too close. When they roll up, their backs are indistinguishable from the concrete around them. That is on purpose.

They don't fight. When two meet head-on in a channel, they don't click or climb. One simply yields — folds its legs, lowers its head, and lets the other walk over its back. There is no sound. Their shells are kept wet by the film and their gills are so efficient they can breathe in a millimeter of water.

**The carriers.**

The paling crabs. White, almost translucent where they are not stained, with legs so long and thin they look like compass needles walking. Every one of them carries something, and that something is the floor. A flat chip of tile, a flake of concrete, a rivet-head still attached to a rust scale. They pick it up as they go, and cement grows over it. Bacteria weld it to their backs. After a month you can't tell where crab ends and catwalk begins. They are not camouflaged. They *are* camouflage that learned to walk. Their job is to move weight around without point A or point B. They take a piece of floor from here and put it there. The floor never gets thicker or thinner. But it is tended. Their undersides are soft and leave no scratches.

**The circuit.**

In the water that shouldn't be deep enough for anything to swim in, lives the water itself. Translucent eels, the thickness of a shoelace and as long as your arm, or as long as a hallway — again, same animal, different print scale. You cannot see them until they turn, because they are the same clear warm water as the film they live in. They don't swim so much as they flow, in loops, following the imperceptible slope from pump to drain to pump again. The troughs between the big tiles are exactly one eel deep. The perforated pipes that exhale mist are exactly one eel wide.

If you crouch, you can see them carry nothing but a faint electrical charge, a tingle. They are the wiring. When they pass under the rollers, the rollers shiver.

**The driers and the strainers.**

There are things that are slugs, but only because "slug" is the word for "wet thing that moves and leaves a trail." These are low and wide and their backs are textured like wet canvas, like burlap sacking soaked and left out. Dun-colored. They move at the speed of a shadow. Where they pass, the water film vanishes for eight, nine seconds — truly dry concrete, pale and perfect — before it wells back up from below and from the air. They drink the film. They filter the biofilm from it and excrete clear water behind them. They are the mops. Their dry track is the only way you ever see the true color of the floor.

Above them, not touching, in the heavy laundry air, are the fliers. Not birds. Nothing here ever learned to make a feather, because feathers would rot instantly. Instead: kites of leathery skin stretched over hollow bone, two, three meters across, so thin you can see the light through them. They have no heads, only a trailing comb, a baleen of fine hair that rakes the condensation as they drift. They never flap. The air is too thick, the distance too low — they tack on the humidity gradients from the weeping filter-housings, drifting six inches, a foot, two feet above the plain, endlessly. When the big piston sighs under the floor, they rise half an inch. When it inhales, they sink.

And on every low wall, every pipe, every valve wheel placed exactly at the right height for something that is not you — the huddled ones. At a distance they look like pigeons. Up close they have no beak, no feathers. Their bodies are covered in a dense, short, hydrophilic fur that wicks water up and into gutters along their backs. They sit shoulder to shoulder, haunch to haunch, in rows that run for kilometers without a break. No gaps. They face the same direction, always away from the faint electrical hum of the third rail. The rail is warm — perfectly, exactly the temperature of brooding fur. It would blister your hand. For them, it's a nest.

They coo. But not where you can hear it well. They coo through the concrete, through their feet, a low vibration that travels through the slab and tells the rollers to shift and the crabs to turn.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing needs to. When one of the rollers gets too old — you can tell because its plates go opaque — it stops in the middle of a tile, tucks its legs, and waits. Within minutes the driers converge on it and drink it clean from underneath. The smallest rollers come and chew the soft liner out of the shell. The biofilm blooms over it while it is still warm. By evening the shell is just another tile, slightly bowed, slightly paler than its neighbors, with a seam so sharp you could shave with it.

They are not tame. Tame implies they know you are something to be tame for. They know you as weather. If you stand still, the eels will flow over your boots. The rollers will detour around you at first, then, when they decide you are not going to move, they will climb over your feet, their legs finding purchase on your laces. The fur-pigeons will part for your hand and close again. The whole plain adjusts its density around you and keeps its level.

They do not build. They do not need to. The world was built already, exactly, once, and it is still young. Their job is to keep it young. To keep the edges sharp and the joints clean and the film exactly one millimeter thick everywhere and forever.

That is what lives here: a workforce that was grown for a factory floor the size of a world, that never needed foremen, that never stopped working, that never made a sound louder than water thinking about becoming water again.
