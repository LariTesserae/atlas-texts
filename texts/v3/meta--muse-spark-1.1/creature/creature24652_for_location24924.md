There is no one here who lives the way you live somewhere.

Nothing here arrives and leaves quickly. Nothing here can afford to be open.

The first citizens are the domes.

From far off you thought they were plants. Up close, you see they are villages. Or bodies. Or both - the distinction does not hold where water does not exist to make things soft and separate.

Call them bloated felt or glass-wool or forest. Each one is a colony that decided, a very long time ago, to become one thing. The outer layer is dead, or rather, it was made to die in place: thousands of overlapping, silvered hairs, each one hollow, each one sealed at the tip with wax, each one angled to throw that hard, cold sun back at the indigo sky. The living part is underneath. Inside, the hairs are still alive, and black-green, and wet - not with water.

If you kneel and part the thatch, you see them breathe. In late afternoon when the high haze comes - that dry, sweet snow of salts and organics sublimed off the cloud sea miles below - the whole colony inhales. It's not a metaphor. The entire hemisphere loosens. Hairs uncurl with a sound like a field of tiny fingers brushing. The dome goes from sleek to shaggy to hungry, and the air snags on it. It combs.

What it catches, it doesn't eat right away. It sweats it inward. Along each hair a bead forms, clear and heavy and slow-moving as cooling resin. The bead runs down, down, to the stem, to the trunk-braid, to the belly where the colony keeps its sea. 
Under the dome, cupped in that peat of its own past selves - leaf after leaf after leaf it has lived and shed and kept - is the reservoir. A lens of amber gel, hydrocarbon and sugar, cold and bright-smelling. That's the blood. That's the well. That's the child. A dome is 90% memory held as this wax. Sever a frond and it doesn't bleed. It closes, like a lip.

They are not fast. They do not need to be. A dome grows a hand's breadth in a year that has no real seasons. When it gets too big for its polygon seam, it does the only generous thing it can do: it dies in the middle. The oldest boughs in the center compost themselves all at once, creating a hollow, cool, green-lit cathedral, and from the rim of that hollow a dozen daughters start as tight silver knots. The parent becomes a ring. The oldest rings on the plateau are triple, quadruple - a set of broken circles fifty paces across, like a slow ripple frozen in felt.

They are aware in the way a forest is aware. Touch one side and the other side tightens, a little. They know the pressure waves are coming before you do; they all turn their shaggy faces into the wind at the same time. At night they glow, not to attract - there is nothing to attract - but because they cannot help it. Cold light is a waste product of their slow chemistry. So the plateau becomes a field of dim green lanterns, each alone.

That is what the rest of the ecology is for: carrying messages between the lanterns.

The second citizens are the ones you thought were empty air.

They come overhead without shadow, until one does. Sheets - veils - prayer flags the size of quilts. Translucent, with no front and no back, with a lace of dark green around the edge that is their stomach and their solar panel at once. They have no mouth. They have no inside, really, until they inflate.

They live by being almost nothing. In the morning they lie flat on the mineral pavement between polygons, looking like discarded cellophane, absorbing heat. The stone rings under them and ticks. When they are warm enough, the air trapped in the thousand little blisters on their underside expands and they lift - a meter, two meters, ten. Not flying. Falling upward very slowly. The thin indigo air can barely hold them and they can barely hold themselves, so they drift.

They graze light and they graze static. The dome hairs, when open, throw off a faint charge. The veils feel it and sag down over a dome like a bridal veil, and for a long moment the two trade. A puff of black spores from the dome sticks to the veil's margin. A dusting of fertilized, silvered hairs from the veil's belly sticks to the dome. Then the wind takes the veil and it goes, tumbling, to the next lantern a mile away. It is pollination as faith.

Sometimes a veil misjudges the descent and sinks too deep into the thatch. You will find them there if you look inside a dome - a great clear skin half-digested, becoming peat, becoming reservoir. The domes do not hunt, but they do not refuse.

Underneath all of this, in the dark seams between the polygons where the wind has sucked all fines away and left a trench of shadow, live the third citizens. You have to stop and let your eyes adjust to their scale.

The pavement looks lifeless until a grain of it walks sideways.

They are salt-mites. Each the size of a sesame seed, each the same bone-white as the ceramic ground, with six thick legs for bracing against wind. They click. That's their communication and their echolocation - a faint ticking you thought was the stone contracting. They farm. Inside the dome's dropped thatch, in the cool peat, they tend gardens of black mold that breaks the wax down into something they can drink. They carry crumbs of dome up onto the pavement to dry in the hard light, then carry them back down. They have highways, invisible to you until you are still for an hour: little polished grooves in the anhydrite.

And where there are herds, even herds of things that small, there is a shepherd that is not a shepherd.

It is so thin you see it only when it moves. Four stilts of near-invisible chitin, a body like a hyphen, a shadow with no thing casting it. It walks above the mites, never touching the ground except with the very tips of its legs, and when it finds a cluster it lowers itself and becomes, briefly, a pool of dark. When it lifts again, the mites are gone, sealed inside that dark. It leaves no tracks. It is the only thing here that hurries.

None of them struggle. That is the wrong word, with its wet, lowland panic. They conserve. They keep. A veil can stay folded on the ground for a season and a half, waiting for enough heat to rise once. A dome can close and wait three years if the high haze fails. A mite can lock its legs and become a grain again.

If a person were to live here, which no one does for long, they would have to learn the same rule: seal yourself. Breathe out less than you breathe in. The only ones who belong here are the ones who already know how to make their own soil, their own water that isn't water, their own dusk inside their own bodies, and carry it with them.

There are a hundred of those green lights tonight, from where you stand, each one alone and sufficient and quietly glowing at the rim of its own slow sea, and between them - if you hold your breath the way they do - you can see the veils drifting, and hear the pavement ticking, and feel how old and complete the arrangement is.
