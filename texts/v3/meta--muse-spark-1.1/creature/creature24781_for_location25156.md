They don't live *on* it. They live *with* it. And in some cases, *as* parts of it.

Nothing here ever solved photosynthesis and so nothing here ever bothered to try again. What lives on the High Table eats two things the plateau makes without limit: salt and charge.

It is easiest to start with the ones you will see first because they want to be seen.

**The Veil - the skaters.**

From a distance they look like trash caught in a thermal - amber cellophane, a square meter of nothing. Up close you realize the nothing is an animal. The high atmosphere is thin enough that the sun rips molecules apart; chlorine, sodium, iron, magnesium hang in the air as a dry haze you can taste. The skaters farm it.

Their dorsal membrane is a taut, living electrostatic organ, a flight skin stretched over a hollow carbon truss. You can see the faint iridescence move across it when they tack - bands of charge. They are not flying so much as being repelled and attracted. They sweep low over the open desert, over the slow black rivers on the conveyor belts, and the dust sticks to the fine cilia on their bellies. When they are light they are almost invisible, translucent as old horn. When they are heavy they go opaque, milky, freighted with a frost of nitrates and perchlorates precipitated straight out of air.

Then they land - not on rock, too hot, too rough - but on the belts. They match speed perfectly, settle, and go still. The vibration of the belt knocks the salt loose. They scrape it into a ventral mouth that isn't a mouth but a wet fold, a deliquescing gut that never fully dries. They ride a hundred meters, light again, and lift. If you stand downwind of a flock at noon, you can hear them: a faint ticking, like dry leaves, as their membranes discharge.

They are the plankton here. There are millions of them.

**The Spoolers.**

You will think they are machines until one breathes.

They are the reason the walkways feel wrong. A Spooler stands on six legs, each leg terminating not in a foot but in a pad - a broad circle of felt that is actually thousands of hollow fibers, like the pile of a carpet, living wicks. They are fifteen tonnes when empty. They move perhaps twenty meters in an hour. They have to, because what they drink doesn't wait.

In the deepest cuts, in the shade of a kiln wall or the leg of a tower, brine weeps out of the rock. It is not water. It's a eutectic that exists at 30 C because it is so salt it forgot how to freeze or evaporate. It beads black for five seconds before it dies into crystals. The Spooler finds it before that by smelling the ionization. It plants a pad over the seep and goes still for hours, its whole body shivering as its fibers pull liquid up by capillary and osmotic violence. What it gets is not the salt - it keeps that too, in boiler-plate scutes that grow out of its back - but the tiny, vicious chemical potential in the act of dissolving. That is its food.

It excretes plate. Not feces. Actual steel-ceramic composite, rust-red and black-purple, laid down in layers across its back like an accretionary roof. When the load gets too great, when the plates start to creak and pinch, it walks to the kiln yards and stands against a cooling ingot wall and does what deer do against trees. It scrapes. A sheet the size of a door falls with a bell-chime onto the gypsum dust. It will lie there warm for a season.

Spoolers remember routes. An old one will have worn a shallow hexagonal trough between a favored seep and a favored kiln, polished by a thousand crossings. If a conveyor belt slips off its pylon - and they do, the plateau flexes - a Spooler will come and lean. Not out of duty. Because a still belt means no vibration for the Veil, no falling plate, no traffic to move air. You will watch it set its forehead, which is not a head but the thickest part of its boiler, against the steel and push. A meter per day. Until pylon groans and rides true. Then it drinks.

They are not intelligent the way you want them to be. They are intelligent the way a glacier is intelligent about finding the sea.

**The Cutters.**

Where a Spooler drops plate, the Cutters arrive. They are the only things here that look like proper animals - low, six-legged, beetle-high, dog-long, each carapaced in what used to be Spooler. They have taken upcycling as a religion.

Their mandibles are not jaws but opposed shear blades, self-sharpening iron oxide nanocomposite, clicking constantly as they hone on salt. They do not eat salt. They eat rust, specifically the slow oxidation that weakens the edges of dropped plate so it can be cut. They cut Spooler leavings into small, regular chevrons and carry them, ant-like, in long slow columns back toward the active districts. You think at first they are building nests. They are doing maintenance. They slot the chevrons into gaps in tower louvers, into the grating of the walkways that hurts your feet, patching the city with the skin of its own grazers. The city, in turn, keeps their eggs from cooking. They lay them in the one cold place on the plateau - inside the updraft shadow of the breathing towers where air is pulled down and briefly, coldly expanded. It is the only nursery.

They click. They are the only thing here that makes a noise like purpose.

**The Charge-Drinkers - what you called High Fliers.**

They don't live down here. They live above here. The plateau is already twelve kilometers up; they nest another three kilometers above that, where the air is too thin for even the Veil and the sun is a hard thing. They are black, not for heat but because black is efficient when you are a capacitor.

Six meters wingtip to wingtip, four wings not two, the fore pair rigid and veined with hollow conductive filament, the hind pair smaller and constantly twitching to trim charge. During day they soar in the dry convection, accumulating. Static from dust collisions, UV kicking electrons loose - they soak it. At dusk, when the towers power up to evening cycle and their fields swell, the fliers stoop.

They don't land. They stall over a tower and let the field grab them, hang there for a minute in a visible corona, wings spread, crackling. You will smell ozone, finally, something besides salt and iron. The smaller Cutters scatter. Then discharge ends and the flier is a tonne heavier with potential, gliding off silent into the dark, belly glowing faint amber.

They roost by sitting on the towers like vast crows, heads tucked, bleeding heat into night. If you are stupid under a tower at dusk, you will become a slightly conductive path for ten megajoules. They do not mean it. They do not notice.

**The Quiet Majority.**

These are the ones you never see unless you kneel and hold still until your own pulse slows to plateau time.

The needle-filth: salt itself infected. In wind-shadows, gypsum dunes sing when you step on them because they are not dunes. Each grain is coated in a living film, a bacterium that grows its own crystal house around it, needle on needle. When wind flexes the dune the needles break and reseed downwind. Together they make the beach that has never seen water.

The brine-threads: in the rock, not on it. Hair-thin black things that live inside the salt strata, following the weeps, exhaling that faint battery taste. If you crack open a fresh efflorescence - and you should not, it is someone's lung - you see the center is damp and dark and threaded.

And under it all, the thing that made you put your palm down.

The plateau rings because it is not solid. The old runoff did not just carve canyons; it dissolved. Ten kilometers down, there are voids the size of countries held up by salt pillars the size of mountains, warm, humming with kiln heat conducted down over eons. In those voids lives the mass that skews your gravity. Not a creature, precisely. A slow, colonial heat-engine, a mat of mineral and filament that grows around the geothermal upwellings that keep this whole table at thirty degrees when it should be thirty below. It contracts at night. It expands by day. Its tide is that long pulse you feel. The Spoolers and the towers and the conveyors are not on top of it. They are the cilia it extruded to keep its upper skin from sealing itself with salt.

That is why nothing here knows abandonment. You cannot abandon a skin while you are still inside it.

If you stay long enough you will learn their speed. A Spooler will decide you are not a hazard and will allow you to sit in the shade of its belly, which smells like hot brine and old tools. A Veil will grow heavy over your head and scatter salt on your hat. A Cutter will correct the grating you are standing on, working its shears around your boots with patient, utterly impersonal care, as if you too were a thing that needs to be kept from over-crusting.
