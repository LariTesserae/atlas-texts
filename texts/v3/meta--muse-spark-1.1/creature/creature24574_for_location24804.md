They live here like a held breath.

First, the ones who built the place before anyone arrived to build in it.

The Oil-Mothers. That's what the remaining people call them, though they are not mothers and they are not trees. *Tirith* in their old palm-language. Each one is a citizen with an address and a job.

They root directly into the salt with no apology. A black tap, thin as a wire, goes down meters and meters, tasting not water but the memory of brine from when this pan was sea. They don't drink; they distill. At night, when the cold slams down into the pocket and the stars are hard, they open. The powder on their leaves — that salt-aspirin bloom — is a lattice. It combs the air for any molecule of water that got lost, condenses ghost-dew into beads that run along channels to the bladder-leaves. Each leaf swells, tight as a tick, tight as a water-bag made of wax and bitter sugar. If you cut one — and no one cuts one unless it is already dead — it doesn't bleed sap, it spits sweet, scalding oil that will blister your mouth and then keep you alive for a day because there's water locked in it, water the plant never agreed to share.

They are architects by instinct. A young tirith is pliable, rope-brained. You can notch it, tie it, bend it while it's growing. It will scar around your cordage and hold. If you split the tip of four trunks and braid them while they're still green, they will fuse into a floor that continues to leaf. The people did this for generations until no one remembered who started which house. So the city isn't built *of* plants. The city *is* a grove that agreed, over a hundred slow years, to be a city. It grows its own granaries — those twenty-foot flower spears that die and harden into drums. It grows its own roofs — three buds pulled apart and pinned flat until they met and made a vaulted hall that still sweats perfume in noon heat. It repairs itself. You come back after ten dry seasons and your doorway has narrowed because the walls kept growing toward the light.

And inside that, clinging to it, the second kind of inhabitant.

The people are dry in the way old paper is dry.

There are maybe thirty left. Maybe fewer since the last census, which was never written down because paper would curl to dust here. They don't look starved; they look cured. Skin is burnished, fine-grained, dusted permanently with that pale leaf-powder so their eyebrows are white. Their lips crack in the same slow way salt does, in clean lines. Children — there are two — look ancient, eyes much too large, bodies with no waste water in them at all. They don't sweat. They can't afford to. Instead they go still at noon. Their kidneys are a rumor. Their blood runs thick and salty and efficient.

You can tell who someone was in the denser time by their hands. Weavers on the north wall: fingers stained olive-black from handling the thin oil-leaves, nails soft and split from picking fiber. Pit-singers in the bottom: broader palms, callus on the thenar from pressing against living walls. The cradle-keepers of the west terraces, now with no cradles to keep — their hands are emptyest of all.

They do not speak when the city speaks. At 10 a.m. the light starts to stand straight up. The first pod goes — KRAK! — like a green bone breaking on the other side of the room, and everyone stops what they are doing. That's the protocol. Conversation is for morning and for dusk. Noon belongs to the tirith.

When the full detonation starts, a thousand pods a minute, a continuous artillery of seed, you cannot hear a voice if someone screams it into your ear canal. Your ribcage becomes a speaker box. So they invented a second language, older than words. They press a flat palm to living pith — a wall, a stilt-root, a hammock post — and they knock their pulse against it with their other hand. Not Morse. Not code. Just *I am here. I am here. Are you still here?* And the wood carries it, because the whole city is one connected, tensioned drum. On a cross-bridge fifty feet up, a woman feels through her bare feet that an old man two districts away has sat down heavily, that his heart is fast. She presses back: *slow. slow.* A child learns to read the city by putting his cheek to his mother's house-wall.

They have one law that is not a law because it doesn't need to be written: never waste the wet. Water is murder because the only water in the pocket is what the tirith have caught, and what the bodies of people and beetles hold. To spill is to steal from the whole breathing room. They drink the oil-leaves only when they must, a few drops chewed from a fallen leaf — it tastes of turpentine and old honey and makes your tongue go numb. They catch their own urine in copper-salt cups, let the pan take the water off, scrape the pink salt that remains back into stew. Nothing rots because nothing is wet enough to rot. Nothing is buried. The dead are laid in the cotton drifts at the bottom and go dry and light and are kept.

Which brings us to the third inhabitant, who owns the bottom.

Mok.

That's not its species name. That's its name. There has only ever been one at a time in living memory, though there are huge, papery skins in the drifts to suggest many.

He is not an insect, not really, not the way we think of beetles. He is the size of a small dog, a low, lacquered cat of an animal, pearl-white, almost translucent. His elytra, his back, is not shell but a stretched, amber drumhead so thin you can see his slow heart beating through it. He has six legs but he uses only four, the front two tucked and trembling like a mantis in prayer. His mouth is a soft rasp for shaving oil-leaf.

He does not move during the day. When the cracking starts, mok goes to the exact center of the deepest drift of black cotton-down — the fluff the pods throw, which never settles, never clumps, never rots, a foot deep, ten feet deep in places, like dry snow that smells of gunpowder — and he stops. He opens his drumback a fraction. The whole pocket becomes his shell.

He is a listener. Not a sacred animal. A practical one. The people lean on his listening. Because when the forest-fire of pods is at its loudest, when ten thousand empty woody horns are howling as the hot air falls in and climbs out and falls in again, there is a frequency that tells if the walls are happy, if a root has lost its hold in the salt, if a tower is about to dry-split. Mok resonates. His back thrums sympathetically. The pit-singers put their palms to his sides and feel the health of the city as a chord.

At dusk, the cannonade stops as suddenly as if someone closed a door. A last desultory KRAK! from a stubborn pod on the west wall. The cotton hangs in the air for hours after, lit sideways. The people unstiffen. Their ears ring in a way that has become normal. Mok begins to walk again, slow, dragging belly-trails through the drifts, eating only a single leaf a day.

Night in the pocket is cold enough to hurt. The tirith open their pores and drink the dark. The remaining humans climb into braided bladders and sleep stacked together for heat, not for company. If you stand on the very rim at that hour and look down, you would see no lights, no fire — fire is another word for murder — only the faintest green phosphor of oil moving under waxy leaves, and the whole room breathing in sync: plant, people, beetle, house that is also a forest that is also a city that forgot how to be crowded.

It lives like that because it has to be sealed to work. Dry, loud, bright, empty, and entirely occupied.
