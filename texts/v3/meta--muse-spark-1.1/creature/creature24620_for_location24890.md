What lives there doesn't.

Not at first. That is the first law of the pocket.

You kneel until the cold comes up through your pants from the clay, and you put your face down in the air that has never been warm, and you look into a cup of black water that is completely full — and you think, nothing can live here. It's too full already. It's packed solid. There is no room left for anything to be alive in.

And that is exactly what lives there.

The first body is the mat itself. It is not a plant, not one plant, but it has decided to be one thing. Watermoss and liverwort and that bruise-colored thread that has never had a name because no botanist would ever kneel long enough to name it. It lives by filling.

You know how a forest lives by reaching — up for light, down for water. This thing lives by refusing to leave a gap. Every micron of that 7-degree water is occupied. Leaf over leaf over filament over hair-root over glass-bead bladder. Pages of a wet book that kept growing pages inside its pages. It has no top or bottom. It felt which way was up once, when the basalt broke yesterday in earth time, and then it decided that was inefficient. So some fronds grow down. Some grow sideways and then loop. They root in water itself. They hold the water still around them, teach it how not to slosh.

It eats almost nothing. That faint mouth-light you see after your eyes give up and adjust — that lipid sheen on the youngest leaves — is its entire sun. It lives on the idea of light. It has a different economy: rot from the alder leaves above, a molecule at a time, cold from the rock below, a dissolved sigh of mineral, and time. So much time in such a small room.

It is so dense that sound dies in it. So dense that a grain of mica can hang in the middle of it for a month, cradled. The mat is the landlord and the house at once. And it keeps.

In its weave it has kept the green fingernail of glaze, the old bottle bead, three perfect little towers of sand that something built grain by grain and then left. It doesn't bury them. It doesn't push them out. It takes them into its body like a saint taking in relics and holds them suspended. That is how it lives: by not letting go.

And then, because the house is so perfect and so patient and so full of eaten and half-eaten time — someone came to live in the house.

You will miss him for the first twenty minutes. You will think you are looking at a flaw in a leaf.

He is the length of a grain of rice after it's been cooked soft. He has no color. If you did not know an animal was there you would never find the animal — clear as the water he never leaves, except for that one dark spot, a comma of night, which is his gut, which is always somewhat full of the mat.

They call things like him hog-louse or freshwater shrimp or scud in other waters, but those are fast, those are things that dart. This one does not dart. He has lost that.

He is blind, because there was nothing to see. He has no pigment, because there was no reason to hide. His legs are fine as eyelashes. He moves by convincing the algae ahead of him to make room. If you mark his place against a leaf edge and breathe and wait until your knees have gone dead in the clay, you come back and he is a little to the left. An hour later, a little further still.

He lives by carving. His mouth — too small to be a mouth — shaves a tunnel just his width through the living felt. Not much, a slow grazing, a mowing of glass beads and bruise-threads. He eats the house and the house closes behind him. Over days, the tunnel he made in Monday is gone by Thursday. The mat knits itself together again. So he is always tunneling through what has never been tunneled, and always eating what has grown back since the last time the world was new.

He never tries to leave. The lip of that fresh-broken basalt is a cliff beyond comprehension. Beyond it, currents and drought and fish. He has never felt a ripple. When you put your finger in, the water holds the shape of your finger around it and does not ripple, and he pauses — not in fear, he does not know fear, just in stillness — and then goes back to carving. Your finger is weather. It will pass.

He is incredibly, unreasonably old in the way small things are. Not him — the shrimp, this shrimp — maybe weeks, maybe months — but the motion. There has always been one. When this one finishes, impossibly slowly, there will be another egg, somewhere in the felt, that has been waiting without hurry, and it will hatch into the same full darkness and begin the same work: to keep the fullness from being too full.

That is what lives there.

A house that decided to be one animal, and an animal that decided to be a slow mouth of the house, and a cup of water that learned how to hold and not spill.

If you stay until you cannot feel your feet, you will see him turn, once, and the single dark spot in him will catch the non-light from the leaves, and you will understand he is not lonely. Lonely requires empty space.
