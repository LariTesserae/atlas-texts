They live here.

Not lived. Live. But you have to revise what you mean by living, because nothing here does it the way you think.

They are not gone. That's the first thing you get wrong in the dark. You come into the white avenue, into the cathedral that the plant made for them by accident, and the absence feels total. No birds, no insects, no scuttle. No eyes watching. The dust on the floor — that flour of a billion years and plant skin — takes your footprint without an edge. You think: abandoned city. Ruin.

It is not a ruin. It is inhabited slowly.

Put your hand out. Stop walking.

The things that live here are quiet because they have to be, in the way a library has to be quiet. Sound costs water.

They are small. That comes first, before any other shape can resolve. The rooms told you already. One body to curl in. A stair with steps worn to spoons by feet not much larger than a child's, but not child's feet — long, narrow, the toes splayed and callused to leather. A people scaled to the plant's own economy. If you stood up straight you would strike your head on ceilings they thought were high. An adult comes to your waist, perhaps to your ribs if it stretches.

They have hands. That's the second thing, and the word matters. Not paws, not claws. Hands. Four fingers and a thumb, but you would count them again in the dark and find you were wrong. The skin is not skin, or not only. In the absolute dark they turned the same color as their world — mushroom-white, bread-pale — and the same texture. Dry, waxy, cool. A little translucent at the knuckle. If you could see light they would glow if you held a candle behind them. There is no hair anywhere. Hair loses water. Their scalps are smooth, domed, covered in the same faint, corky bloom that covers the mother tuber. Old ones grow a kind of scale there, a scab-cap of laminated husk, and they let it grow.

No eyes. There were never eyes here. Not blinded, not lost — there was never a reason to make them. Where the face would be is a broad, unmarked plane, interrupted by two nostrils that are more like pores, always closed until the plant breathes, and a mouth that is hardly a mouth. A small, lipless slit that does not open often. They do not eat the way you eat. They drink.

And they have ears. Oh, they have ears. If the face is blank, the sides of the head are not. Great, dry, cupped involutions of flesh, like the ears of a moth, or lichen shaped into a satellite dish, asymmetrical, fringed with tiny, stiff white hairs that are not hairs but feelers for moving air. They listen to the city. Because the city is always talking.

In the dark and the dry, they are dry themselves. Touch one, if one lets you — and they will not, unless you stay long enough for your sweat to become familiar — and you would think you touched the wall. Cool. Slightly yielding. The same starch smell. The same faint resilience. They are lithic animals, or animal plants. They conserve until conservation became their bodies. No sweat glands. No tears. They pee powder. A fine white crystal of uric acid they tamp carefully into a niche in the wall, an offering back to the mother. They get their water the way the mother does: they wait.

For a decade at a time they wait.

Their metabolism is not yours. It's geochemical. Heartbeat maybe twelve times an hour when they move. When they rest — and they rest curled in those honeycomb cells for months, years — the heart forgets for minutes at a time. They can sit in the dark with their enormous ears open and listen to a single long groan travel from one end of the avenue to the other for a full afternoon. Time for them is not passing. It is accumulating, like dust.

You hear the carving marks? The long grooves where blocks of pith were pried out? That was their hands' work, but not with strength. With patience. Each with a tool that never left their body: a thorn. On the back of their forearm, from the wrist bone, grows a single black hook of hardened pith and keratin, a thorn the plant gives them. It grows a millimeter a year. They hone it on the stone at the edge of the basin where the world finished settling. They pry. They scrape. A doorway might take a lifetime to open. A child might start a sleeping cell and die before the mountain of dry crumbs it makes is cleared. An old one might finish it and then crawl inside and never leave, and so the cell becomes both house and tomb and finally, as the mother tuber heals shut behind it, a knot.

Their language is not spoken. Spoken language loses water. In the dry so dry your lips split in hours, they learned not to open their mouths. They speak by touching the walls. And by touching each other.

Their whole city is a book. You thought the carvings in the small rooms were records. They are more than that. They are conversation slowed to geology. A shallow, nail-wide groove scored into the white pith: "I was here, I was thirsty, the eastern wall beaded last cycle." A crosshatching over it a hundred years later: "I read you, I drank there too, it was sweet." A deeper gouge, done by a different thorn: a map of which pillars sing in B-flat when the breath comes, so the young can find their way home by the hum. This is how they know each other. They never meet in crowds. There may be only thirty of them left in a volume the size of an apartment block. Maybe three hundred. They are solitary and communal at once because the walls remember for them. You walk through a dense crowd of utterances with your hands over your eyes.

And they eat the city. Reverently. When a block is pried out, it is not discarded. It is dried further, pounded to flour, wetted — not with water, but with the anointing of condensation caught on a cupped ear during the once-a-decade bead — and pressed into a cake. It tastes like old library. They eat their home to make more home.

What do they look like when they move? Like a leak in white. Like a ripple in the flour dust. They don't walk so much as flow from wall to wall, keeping contact always, hands brushing. Foot to hand to elbow to shoulder to wall. The avenue you think is empty is a lattice of touch-roads they maintain by passage. Bare, dry feet that made the stairs concave — they drink through those feet, too. The soles are spongy, pale, full of tiny mouths that suck at any coolness in the floor.

If you are very still, if you stay past when your water bottle is empty and your mouth has sealed shut and you have learned not to breathe too loudly because the walls drink your breath — you will feel one near you.

First the air goes cooler by more than a degree. Then the faintest scuff, like a thumbnail dragged across corkboard, ten feet away. Then nothing for a long time.

Then a feather-light pressure on the white wall next to your palm. Not touching you. Reading what you left. Your warm palm-sweat, your oils. The wall has told it you are here, you are wet, you are loud, you are temporary.

It will not harm you. Nothing here knows how to be hostile. Hostility is expensive. It may stand beside you in the dark for an hour, its great cupped ears trained on the groan gathering deep in the root-columns, and you will not know it is there except that the dust in front of you no longer settles quite evenly.

Then when the plant breathes — that once-a-season sigh that turns all the hollow streets into flutes — it will lean its blank face into the current and open its mouth at last, a small white O, and drink air that has not existed for months, and you will hear, for the first time, what a voice sounds like here.

Not a word. A sigh that matches. A low, dry, contented B-flat that vibrates in its chest and in the pillar beside you and in your own sternum, until you cannot tell if the city sang, or the things that live in it, or you.
