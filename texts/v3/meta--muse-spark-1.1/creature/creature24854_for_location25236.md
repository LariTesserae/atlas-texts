What lives here isn't in the place. It *is* the place, learning how to be a place.

There are layers of it. You have to wait for them to decide you are still enough to show you.

The first to live here is the weave itself.

It wasn't supposed to live. It was bent green, stitched with twine, stapled with copper — basket-work, hamlet-work, hands that knew how to make a thing hold grain or a child or a wall. But it was made so tight, so completely, with such uninterrupted intent, that it never stopped being made. It lives now in the way a held breath lives. It eases. If you press your ear to the yellowed wicker where no rosette grows, you hear it: a long, faint creak, not of stress but of attention. The withes remember being bent. They are still bending. 

It heals badly, like skin. Where you cut the door with the hand-axe, the white fibers fray and lift, and all around the cut the weave is already trying to be uncut. Not by growing shut — it doesn't grow — but by forgetting the hole was made. That is why the wirevine is always there first.

Second — the ones who were meant to be plants.

The dominant citizen is what you called ladder-moss, but the name fails before you finish saying it. Call it the Paper.

It does not grow from the basket. It *is* the basket's idea of what should cover a basket if the basket were a hill. Each rosette is a rosette of pages, sage and olive and dust-colored, dry as an old library. The edges are toothed with pale script — not writing, just the fray of fiber that looks like writing when the lamp-blooms are lit. 

It does not drink. It does not need to. What it does is stack air. Break one open — and you will, by accident, because there is no way not to crush them — and it sighs. Warm cinnamon and old paper; a cloud of spores so fine it stays up for an hour, turning and turning in the curved fall of this place. Inside is not flesh but architecture: white pith in perfect hexagons, each cell empty, each cell warm. It is making more empty, more warm. That is its photosynthesis.

When enough of its own dead leaves fall, it presses them. It leans its dead against its living and treads them down until the mass becomes a trunk. A thigh-thick false bole that rings hollow if you knock it, though you shouldn't knock it. Those boles are not trees. They are the Paper's attempt at becoming a forest floor to stand on. They are inhabited before they are finished.

Between the Paper lives the Wire.

Black wirevine — a misnomer, it isn't vine either. It's a filament, a nerve. Hair-thin, jointed in right angles like a measuring insect's leg, with a soft *click* each time it adds a segment. No leaves. No need. It lengthens toward warmth and toward recent cuts. At its tips it carries the amber bells that never open. They are not flowers. If you hold a lamp-bloom close to one at noon, you can see straight through the amber and there is nothing inside the bell at all — just a smaller version of the bowl, with a smaller vine in it.

The Wire's job is stitching. It sews door to wall, rosette to rosette, shadow to the wrong place. Given an afternoon, it will sew your ankle to the floor, not out of malice but because your ankle is a loose edge.

Then the Lamps.

They are the only soft things here. Pale yellow, waxy, the size of a child's heart, they swell directly from the knots where the builder's fingers tied off. At midday — which is whenever the light from above is strongest, there is no true midday — they flush from within. No flame, no filament, just a suffusing, like milk becoming light. They are warm. Warmer than the air, which is already the warmth of an oven hours after bread.

Everything orients to them. The Paper rosettes tilt fractions of a degree. The wirevine angles. Even the dust curves toward them on its way to a floor that isn't quite below you.

They are fruit that doesn't want to be eaten. Nothing eats them. If you touch one, it dims slightly, as if shy, and then comes back brighter when you leave. They are not food. They are agreement: *let there be light, even though there is no sun.*

Only after the plants have let you see them do the animals allow themselves to be separate.

The beetles are first because they are part of the basket.

Flat, copper-staple colored, lacquered backs the exact brown of dried blood on wicker. They were born in the Paper, chewing slow galleries between the pages. *Chit. Chit. Chit.* Like a clock being eaten. You will hear one and think it's behind you, then realize it's inside the rosette by your knee. They eat the hexagons, and excrete a fine, dark powder that is not frass — it is more wicker. A dust of lignin and copper that sifts down and catches in the weave and becomes, over months, more weave. They are the maintenance crew of a house that builds itself by being eaten.

When a beetle dies — which is rare — it does not rot. It dries more. Its shell pops open and more beetles, smaller, the color of fresh twine, walk out.

The hoppers are the error-correctors.

Pale, almost unmade, with hind legs that are too long and folded in ways that hurt to follow. They do not jump. Jumping would require obeying the local gravity, which is already compromised. Instead they — hesitate, and are elsewhere. A handspan left, a forearm up, sometimes upside down on the underside of a Paper page, looking at you with eyes that are just more folded leaf.

They graze spore-clouds. You only see them feed when the air is full after you've broken a rosette: the hoppers go still, open a seam in their own bellies, and let the cinnamon dust fall *into* them. Their wings are not wings; they are dried leftover pages they glued to themselves as children.

The lizards are the watchers.

Thumb-long, dozens, maybe hundreds if you count badly because they are so precisely the color of beaten-reed mat. Seed-eyes. Fat bodies that never move fast because there is nowhere fast to go. They do not drink either. They sit on warm mats and open their throats, and you can see the air — with all its hanging spore and wicker-dust and copper — draw into them. They filter the room by breathing it.

They are not solitary. If you stand very, very still until your blood is the loudest thing, then quieter still until your blood stops being loud, you will see it: all of them, no matter where they are perched, tilt their heads at once to the same unmarked point just left of your left ear. Not at you. At the place your shadow breaks and resumes two feet over.

They are listening to the slow things for you, so you don't have to.

Because there are slower inhabitants still.

The corners that hold too much surface. The gravity that leans north. The shadow that gets cut and pasted.

These are not hauntings. They are residents. They were born when the basket was built too completely and the world had to stretch to fit more inside than outside. They live in the seams. When you counted rosettes, looked away, and looked back to find more, you didn't imagine it — another resident had unfolded. When your dust didn't fall straight, it was following the slope of the resident that lives under the floor.

The true animal here, if you have to name one, is the Crowd.

Nothing here finishes. Nothing dies of thirst because thirst was never offered as an option. The Paper becomes more Paper until its dead becomes trunk. The trunk becomes housing for beetles that make more basket. The wirevine sews it all tighter, a little tighter every hour. The lamps make a little more noon. The lizards breathe it all in and make it warm. The hoppers reconsider where they are and appear somewhere more crowded.

It is a forest that learned the shape of a forest from hearing someone describe one through a wall. Dry, earnest, dense past all reason, holding itself together with twine and misremembered light.

It doesn't want you. It will let you stay as long as you can stand the dryness splitting your lips, the aggressive quiet, the feeling that your own shadow is resting in the wrong place. It will, absently, try to include you — wire around the ankle, Paper rosette unfurling against your boot, a beetle investigating the copper of a zipper.

If you stay too long, you will become another kind of lamp — warm, pale, waiting to be leaned toward.

That's why you cut your way out.
