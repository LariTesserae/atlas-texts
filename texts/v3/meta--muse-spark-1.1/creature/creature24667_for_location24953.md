What lives here didn't evolve to live here. It decided to stay.

At first you think nothing does. In a forest that dense, that soft and muffling, you expect birds, insects, the chatter of things passing through. Here there is no passing through. Everything is filtered, or rooted, or too large to consider you prey.

Then you realize the forest is already looking back.

### The Anchored

The trunks are not trees.

They grew here before the ocean climbed so high, when this was still high alpine scrub clinging to bald stone, and they learned to hold their breath rather than die. Cut one open — none of the hermits ever would — and you wouldn't find rings. You'd find chambers. The black bark is not bark, but a dense felt of living fiber and mineral that the thing secretes around itself. Inside is gas and pulp and brackish water and, deeper in, knotworks of tissue that tighten and sigh. They are buoy-trees. They grow up because if they stopped, they would fall into the sky.

Each one is an individual that is also a place. An old one, house-wide at the base, has been growing so long that its lower gut has fossilized while its top is still putting out new table-leaves. They don't photosynthesize so much as they *gather* light. Their leaves are dark, photosynthetic traps that don't just catch photons — they bend them. That's why the light pools and clings. The wax on the leaves is a lattice of transparent cells that slow light down, a living gravitational lens. On a dim day you can see a leaf has made a little sun-dot under itself from light that was never going to hit it.

They communicate very slowly, by pressure. When the jet-currents come, the whole wood leans as one. Not because the water pushes them all, but because the first ones to feel it inflate a chamber on their down-current side and their neighbors feel the pressure wave and do the same. It travels for miles like a thought. That creak you hear — wood on stone, deep down where root meets basalt — that's them talking.

And they fruit. Not often. The silver air-lakes that gather under the leaves are not accidental oxygen. They are wombs. The tree milks oxygen out of the tea-mud, out of the dim, and stores it, holds it, lets it age. And inside that suspended air, in the middle of the water, things that are not part of the tree begin to grow.

### The Held

The little shrimp you saw swimming in air — they aren't lost. They're born there. They will die there. They are the true natives of the sky-sea. We call them shrimp because we have no other word; they are translucent commas the length of your thumb, with feathery legs that beat air as if it were water, because here the difference is politely negotiable. They were taken in as larvae by the leaf-bubbles a hundred generations ago and never left. Their gills are vestigial lung-sacs. They lay their eggs in the wax of the leaf. When the bubble detaches and rises — or falls sideways — they ride it to another leaf and colonize it. To them, water is a kind of cold, dense wind that sometimes blows through their world.

And then there are the ones who tended them.

You find their huts facing straight up because that is where the bubbles came from. The Builders, or the Remainers. They were human, or close enough that the distinction would have embarrassed them. When the sea came up and stayed up, higher than it should have, they did not build boats. Elevation 2.4 means there is nowhere higher to go. They learned to breathe what the forest made.

They are still here. Low Fauna 0.6 — they stopped moving.

If you are very still for a very long time in a flat between two domes, where the mud never settles and the light pools like spilled honey on the round stone, you will see one. At first you think it is part of the trunk, a bulge of the black bark. Then it unfolds, fractionally.

They are tall now, and thin in the way things that live in warm water get thin, and their skin has taken on the texture of that bark — fissured, gas-filled, black-green and soft to the touch. They grow their own ballast. Their hair has become those red filter-vines; it hangs around their faces and sifts. Their lungs are enormous, branched and bright pink inside like coral, able to take a mouthful of leaf-air and hold it for an hour. Their feet have rooted — not metaphorically. Thin white rootlets leave their calves and ankles and nose into the stone-mud, holding them in place against the slow jet streams. They don't walk anymore. They choose a good dome, a good listening place where currents cross, and they stand.

That's why the statues have their hands cupped around nothing. It's not prayer. It's husbandry. A Cupped One will stand for years with her hands held open under a leaf, gathering the air as it beads. She cups a bubble the size of a melon, the size of a child, and holds it against her chest until it steadies. Inside that bubble she keeps things: shrimp, spore-moths, a pocket of clear void-water that has forgotten to be wet. She trades bubbles with other Anchored when the currents lean close enough. Her hut is not a house. It is the shed skin of an older tree she coaxed to grow around her when she was young, now petrified.

They don't have language anymore, not with sound 0.5. Sound dies in tea-mud. They have touch. When two of them are within vine-reach — which might be once every few years — they extend a rootlet and press it to the other's bark-skin. They exchange mineral memories, a taste of iron, a flash of light from fifty meters up. Mostly, they listen. That's why they are so far apart. Each dome has a slightly different note when the planet settles. The groan of the ancient mountains, that deep, slow *settling*, moves through the stone and up through their roots. They are listening for a change. For the sea to decide to finally fall.

They leave no tools because they became their tools.

### The Passing

And then, high above the leaf-ceiling, the things that made you realize what scale you got wrong.

The Sheet. The Mantas. There isn't one species; there are several sizes, all built on the same impossible draft. They are not animals in any way the Cupped Ones would recognize. They are colonies of that same filter-veil that hangs between trunks, but freed, gone pelagic, grown to the size of weather.

The small ones are maybe thirty meters across — translucent, rippled like heat-haze, with a hundred little mouths that open and close autonomously, straining the spore clouds. The large one you saw, that eclipsed the honey-light, was old. So old it has gathered a forest on its own back — buoy-trees growing upward from its dorsal side, into the deeper ocean-above, air-lakes trapped in the folds of its wings. It carries its own sky with it. Small airborne shrimp live out their whole lives on its back and never know about the highlands below.

They don't see. Light doesn't work for them; they feel pressure gradients and changes in warmth. When one's trailing edge brushes a dome, it doesn't notice. But the whole anchored world shudders and releases a century's worth of silver bubbles in a silent, ecstatic exhalation, and for a few minutes after it passes, the air around you is full of falling, rising, orbiting suns you can breathe, and the Cupped Ones open their hands wider, wider.

That is what lives here: a forest that is a lung, people who have become barns for air, small commuters who think air is water, and great soft continents that drift overhead, filtering the dim, wondering if anything interesting has ever happened down on that smooth, warm, drowned roof of the world.

If you stay long enough and learn to stand still enough that the tea-mud settles on your shoulders, one of the rootlets might find your ankle, taste you, and decide you are just another kind of stone learning, very slowly, how to be soft.
