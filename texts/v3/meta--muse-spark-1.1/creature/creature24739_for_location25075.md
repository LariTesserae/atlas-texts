They don't live *on* it. That's the first thing you have to unlearn.

### The Holdfasts themselves

They are not trees that got too big. They are something that stopped trying to be a tree.

Each one is one kind of place, and the kind keeps becoming more itself. There is no model. The smallest one you can see from your bare-rock camp — the one that looks like a cathedral from five kilometers away — is a juvenile. Maybe four hundred years old. Its plates are still tight, green-black and overlapping like a pinecone the size of a building. It hasn't learned to lean yet. Water moves in it fast and anxious. If you put your ear to its shingles at dawn you can hear it ticking, hydraulic, drinking the air.

The old ones have given up on symmetry. The city-sized one to the north — you have named it North without meaning to — has a hip. A buttress on its windward side as thick as a cliff, poured there century by century because the wind never stops from the south-west at 3,000 meters. Its top is balded. Not dead, just scoured smooth by sun and blown grit until its plates fused into a single black dome that radiates heat until midnight. Its lee side has collapsed inward slightly, making a valley in its own flesh, and in that valley there is a hanging bog a hundred meters across that has never touched stone.

It is not thinking. But it is attending. If you cut a water leaf it will not bleed, it will weep and then seal you off. If you tend the same sugar-guides for years it will make more of them there. You turn a corner deep inside near the heart cisterns where the air is cool and smells of iron and peat and moved water, and you feel listened to the way a very old house listens — by creaking a little less when you are in it.

They are not in competition. They are not a forest trying to close. They are hermits who chose the exact distance at which their diseases cannot fly. That is the ethic of this high place: to be vast and alone and full.

### What lives through them

Because nothing can live just *on* them. The surface is too hard, too waxy, too hot at noon. Everything lives *through* them, bored and wovened and threaded.

The Flute Flies are the air. You thought at first there were birds. There are no birds. What you heard was the million-note organ. Each male is as long as your thumb, pale as whey, with a hollow, ribbed tube curled over its back like a clarinet made of fingernail. It doesn't buzz, it breathes through it. One note, held as long as it can hold its belly full. A female, dark and silent, chooses which chord she wants to lay her eggs under. When the wind changes, when a big amber mantis thing crashes through the curtains, when a Holdfast shifts water from east to west, the chord shifts. At noon it is deafening in the true sense — not painful like a bang, but total, so that your own swallow sounds fake and thin inside it. People who are born here lose their highest hearing by twenty.

The custodians of distance are the Salt Wasps. Big as gulls, slow as gliders, with wings you can see right through, humming low under the Flute Fly choir. They are the only thing that regularly crosses the scorch between Holdfasts, because only they can. They mine the weeping lenticels where the Holdfast excretes excess salt, chew it into a grey paste, and carry it. A white line of them against the hot blue, every day, east to west, west to east, never deviating. Without them the Holdfasts would pickle themselves. If a wasp drops you, it will not sting — it will try to salt you, packing you in paste for its larvae, very calmly, while you scream.

Inside, there are things that have forgotten they are animals. The boat orchids that are the Holdfast's own mouths and wombs at once. The dog-beetles that carve cathedrals inside sponge-wood and farm black slime on the walls. The ribbon things that are not snakes and not birds and not anything old Earth had — feathered muscle as long as a man, with no legs, that live entirely in the cold condensation drip under the outer plates, where the morning water beads and never stops. They sing in two voices at once, one low through a throat and one high through hollow feathers, to each other, always.

### The ones who don't walk on stone

And then the people. Because of course something learned to live in the lungs.

You see one before you see *them*. A rope of braided aerial root as thick as your wrist, stained black with leaf wax, hanging from sixty meters up where a plate has been gently pried. Or a bone peg ladder where the living sponge has grown around each peg in a neat, indifferent knuckle. That is a road.

They are not many. A Holdfast the size of a city might hold forty. A small cathedral one might hold three. They are thin in a way that has nothing to do with food — there is syrup and starch and protein in every wall if you know which wall to ask — and everything to do with air. At 3,000 meters, hot and thin, a big chest is a better chest. They have it. Their ribs are like barrels. When they are still, you can watch their breath. When they move, they move slowly and never at noon, conserving.

Their skin is the color of the wet underside of the plates, a deep, unsunned brown-green, because they smear themselves daily with wax scraped from new shingles. It keeps water in and sun off. It makes them smell like cut aloe and smoke. Their hair is cut short or matted into the wax. Their eyes are black-lidded like desert animals, a second translucent lid for blown grit and brightness.

They do not have villages. The word would imply sharing walls. They have holds. A lenticel that failed to close, enlarged with beetle-shell adzes over a grandfather's grandfather's life, just big enough for a sleeping hollow and a gas-bladder water skin. You find them by the coolness. You announce yourself by tapping the plate outside in a rhythm, not by calling, because you never raise your voice here — the air is already too full.

They garden, not farm. They speak to the Holdfast with cuts. A pinch here and the plant makes more sugar leaves next season. A slice there and it offers a water bladder. They never take wood. Only what the Holdfast sloughs. Their knives are basalt struck to a single hard edge, their needle-bones are wasp stingers as long as your hand, their cordage is living vine trained to grow into loops.

Their children learn two things early: how to wring a condensation leaf at dawn without bruising it, and how to cross the between. Because you must cross sometimes. A Holdfast can get sick. A face of it can rot after a storm-bloom, full of black fly. When that happens you have to leave. You coat yourself, you fill your back leaf, you wait for the hour after dawn when the stone is still cool and the Flute Flies are low. And you run, head down, across kilometers of polished whale-back basalt that reflects the sky until you hallucinate water. If you fall, you get up. If you stop, the heat cooks you from below.

If you stay, and it flowers — if you are lucky and it decides the deep cisterns are full enough to risk a dream — you will be inside for it. The whole giant shivers. Not shakes. A slow flex like a chest. Then from a thousand pores at once, it sighs out silk. Glassy, dry-to-touch filaments that aren't silk at all, each carrying a seed smaller than a mote of dust. The air inside turns to light. The Flute Flies hit the threads and the chord changes to something so beautiful and so loud it makes your teeth ache and you cry without knowing. The Salt Wasps go mad for it, getting tangled and angry and loud.

You stand there, wet sleeves dripping the only water there is for a hundred kilometers that is not inside a living thing, in a building that grew itself to be a country to keep a quarantine of gods, and you understand that you are not a resident. You are part of the fauna that forgot it was animal. A small, waxed, careful mite who learned how to keep a houseplant that is larger than history alive, so that it will keep you alive back.
