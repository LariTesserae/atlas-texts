They live under everything.

At first, when you look down from the rim, you think nothing lives there at all. That the town is empty. That it built itself and died. The carpet of moss is so complete, the weave of sticks so old, that it looks abandoned — a ruin already swallowed.

Then a dome shivers.

Not the wind. The moss on top of a lodge tightens and loosens. A seam in it opens. Water spills out dark and then a head comes up through it.

That is them.

They are beavers, but the word you brought from the plain doesn't fit anymore. The ones up in the thin creeks of the high basalt are dog-sized, reddish, nervous. These are something that stayed when the ice left. Winter beavers. Bog beavers.

They are twice the mass. Three feet through the chest, old males going heavier, their backs broad and flat as river stones. Their pelts are not brown. They are black-brown, peat-black, stained by tannin water that has been sitting for a thousand years. When they climb out the water beads but doesn't make them shine; it vanishes into them. White scar tissue maps their noses and tails. Every one of them has a chewed ear. Their incisors are not yellow — they are deep iron orange, almost red, because this much chewing in mineral water stains the enamel like rust.

And they do not stop.

A beaver on the plain will fell a tree, drag it, eat, sleep for a long afternoon. Here there is no afternoon. There is only work because there is only edge. The city has filled its bowl. There is nowhere to expand except into itself, up and down. So they work in shifts you can feel but not see. An animal emerges from a low door — no bigger than a barrel hoop, perfectly round from shoulders — hauling mud. Not a pawful. A chestful. They scoop black clay from the canal bottoms and press it to their own chests, to that flat furred sternum, and walk, half-swimming, to where the wall needs it. They plaster. They pat with that broad tail like a trowel. The clay smokes faintly in the cold because their body is so much hotter than the air.

Behind it, a second animal, smaller — a yearling, its coat still soft, not yet felted — pulls a willow trunk three times its own length backward through the main canal. It doesn't look around. It doesn't need to. The canals are the roads and everyone knows the traffic pattern. Keep right. Keep the branch high over the water so it doesn't snag on the overhanging sphagnum. At the intersection where two canals cross, where the current makes a small vortex of duckweed and bark peel, it pauses and waits, its whiskers trembling. Another adult passes, inches away, with a mouth full of fresh aspen cambium, white as paper. They do not touch. They cannot afford to. A thumb's-width clearance. Then both go.

If you stay low and still long enough to go un-noticed, you start to count families by smell, not sight. Every few lodges there's a scent mound — mud piled higher than the rest, capped with castoreum that even in this cold hits you thick and leathery and strangely sweet. That is the border. That is the name of who lives here. Inside one mound-territory, you will see two adults, big as you think beavers can ever get, and this year's kits — four, five, somehow six — coal-black puffballs with teeth too big for their heads, tumbling on moss that is itself alive. They chew everything. Not for food. To learn the feel of right resistance. One kit will worry at a stick as long as itself and then lose it to the canal, and the mother will retrieve it without stopping her wall-plastering.

The kits never leave the city. On the plain a two-year-old disperses, walks for miles. Here there is nowhere to walk to except the weeping wall, which is falling. So they stay. Lodges become connected. Cousins become neighbors. Second cousins share a dam. That is why it is so dense. That is why it keeps rising.

They are not alone in their own houses. That is the other rule you heard from the rim: nothing is alone.

Lift the edge of the moss on any roof that looks abandoned and it comes apart with life. The beaver is the basement, the furnace, the foundation. The heat of its body and its winter food pile composting below makes the roof steaming, a few degrees warmer than the air. So the moss — sphagnum so thick you can lose your hand in it — grows fur-like, acid green, crimson in fall even now. Inside that fur, voles have run cities of their own. Tunnels the diameter of your thumb, always damp, lined with chewed cottongrass down. The red-backed voles, their eyes all black, do not hibernate because they live on top of a heater.

Below the waterline, in the walls themselves, there are more. Water beetles black and polished as basalt chips, moving through the submerged thatch. Caddis fly larvae building their own tiny lodges out of beaver-chewed wood chips. And in the canals that never freeze because a hundred tails keep them stirring, there are trout — lake trout that came in with the melt, trapped now, gone dark. They hold in the shadow of each dam, their backs the exact color of lodge mud, waiting for what falls in. They have learned the slap.

Because the beavers talk rarely, and when they do the whole sink listens.

A tail goes up on the far outer ring where a young male has cut into a spring wrong and flooded his own food cache. He lifts — that tail is not the comical flat thing from children's books. It is as wide as a shovelhead, scarred, edged with barnacle-like limestone where the cold water has left its minerals, crusted with old sphagnum that grows on it like a living map. He brings it down.

*WHACK.*

It is not loud. Cold wet eats sound. But it is deep. It travels through water, through mud, through the wood-raft itself. And everything stops. Up on the cliff walls, five hundred little holes with white-rimmed droppings beneath them — martins and white-rumped swifts, who live nowhere but in that weeping basalt — pour out in a single sheet, wheel once, and vanish. Down on the raft, a flock of ptarmigan who were invisible as moss until now burst white against black. Dragonflies, huge, amber-winged, the kind that should not live this far north but do because the beaver city holds heat, lift off the canal.

Only then do you understand that for ten minutes you have been surrounded by birds you did not hear. That the warblers in the dwarf willow — yellow-rumps and waxwings — have been singing the whole time, but at such low volume, into such thick air, that you took it for your own blood. A pair of greater scaup ride in a side canal, using it like a street, dabbling at the algae that only grows on beaver incisor grooves. A snipe drills in a sphagnum hummock that is also some great-grandmother beaver's roof.

It is all built on backs.

The oldest lodges are in the center. They are no longer lodges. They are hills. Twenty feet high, sixty wide, solid wood all the way through, but you would not know it because black spruce, stunted to knee height by wind and acid, has rooted into them. White spruce seedlings, gnawed to bonsai by kits passing by, grow in circles around the old breathing holes. On those hills songbirds nest — because here, and only here for kilometers, there are trees. On those hills bog rosemary and Labrador tea flower so thick in late summer the mist smells like ledum and honey for an hour at noon. Under those flowers, bumblebee queens, heavy and slow in the cold, fumble from blossom to blossom, and they, too, winter inside the wood, in abandoned vole nests inside beaver nests inside moss.

And at night — because it is still north, because even in summer the light finally does thin — the whole raft settles. The gnawing stops. You hear the cliffs weep louder. And you hear breathing. Not one thing. Hundreds, thousands of damp breaths, exhaled from the fur, from the moss, from the holes in the peat, rising as little clouds that never get above a foot high before the cold kills them. The city, for a few hours, is not building.

It is just lying there, warm in the middle, cold at its edges, holding itself up out of the wound.
