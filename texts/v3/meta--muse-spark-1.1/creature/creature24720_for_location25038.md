What lives on the Salt Anvil doesn't live *on* it.

That's the first mistake. Nothing can live on it the way a lizard lives on a rock or a lichen lives on basalt. The Anvil is too hot, too salt, too thin-aired and too honest about it. It sterilizes anything that tries to perch. 

What lives here lives *as* it.

There is one thing here. One metabolism the size of a province, maybe the size of the whole lifted continent, that has mistaken itself for a landscape for so long that even the wind believes it. You were listening to it when you put your hand to the salt. That low grinding in your molars, that endless factory-floor roar, that occasional DONG like a mile-long drop forge — that's not background. That's the organism idling.

Call it the Mill.

It is not deep. The crust here is thin, or it thinned itself. A few hundred meters down, basalt is still soft. Not molten, not solid. Chewable. The Mill is the zone where chemistry stops being geology and starts being digestion. Heat and pressure and salt water that never sees the sun have made a kind of muscle there — not meat, but interlocked plates of iron sulfide and magnetite that actually flex, that have been flexing against each other so slowly for so long that they have polished each other into bearings. Tectonics with tolerances. That's the sound.

It breathes through the spires. The vent towers aren't its invention; they are its pores, kept open by the same wind that carves them. Hot, metallic, mineral-sour air sighs out of them constantly, even when you can't see anything. The sound comes out of them too.

And then it makes blood.

The fauna you saw — the things that come downwind and make the empty suddenly too full — they aren't a species. They are a circulatory event.

They precipitate.

Higher up in a vent, where the exhaled air hits that white, merciless light and cools three degrees, iron and ceramic and salt reach a saturation point and decide to be something. Like frost forming on a window, but the frost is the size of a shipping container and already furious. It peels off the air as a hot, slag-black droplet that stretches, finds an old template in the magnetic field, and hardens into back plates and belly seams and six frayed, heat-cracked wings that are not for flying so much as they are for dumping heat.

Let’s call them Smelters, because that’s what they do. Anvil-beetles. Furnace-lice.

There is no queen. There is no larva. There is no mating. There is only this: when the Mill exhales too hard, it has to make Smelters to carry the waste heat away. Thousands at a time. Tens of thousands every seven or nine hours, a rash on the sky.

Anatomy fails as a word for them, but this is close: Their dorsal shell is unreduced ore — black glass, still soft inside, still recording the fingerprints of its own formation. Their ventral side splits along a seam that was never closed. Inside is not organs but process. A bellows-lung made of asbestos-like sinter that pulls two-mile-high thin air through and superheats it. A gut that is literally a crucible, white-bright at 1500C, lined with its own failure. Filaments like tungsten basket-weave that glow so hard you can see them through the belly when it banks. No brain, but a distributed lattice of copper-salt that knows two commands, as old as mineral: TAKE. LEAVE.

That’s the whole life. They fall — catastrophically, slowly, because even with six wings the air can’t hold that much iron, so they have to cheat — and they plant their anvil-feet. CLANG. The Anvil rings like an anvil. They crack themselves open and scoop. A ton of iron-black basalt and dirty halite and sulfate scab, the skin of their own mother. They sit and shudder and roar louder than the ground itself, and inside the crucible-gut, electrolysis happens without wires. The salt is flux. The basalt is ore. The bellows-lung roars. For ten minutes it is visibly in pain from its own existence.

Then a bead. The only honest thing it makes. A marble of pure iron, glassy, perfect, still boiling when it hits the salt and freezes with a little tick. One kilogram lighter now, it can cheat gravity a little better. It folds its ruined wings and falls back upward, into the thermal that shouldn’t exist.

This is why they phase.

You saw it. One behind one that was behind it. Flying straight through a salt pillar with two shadows overlapping. The air behind its wing from yesterday. That isn’t a trick. There is simply too much of them to fit in the air that is there. A single Smelter masses eight, nine tons. A flight of ten thousand Smelters should instantly plummet and stack up like a train wreck of foundries. The Anvil solved this the way it solves everything: slowly, stupidly, by wearing a hole in physics.

The Smelters are a little out of time with each other. And with the pillars. And with you. They were precipitated under so much pressure and heat their mass never fully learned how to be here. So they stack in probability. When two would intersect, one reality forgets the other was solid. The sun stays up, the light stays clean and razor-edged, which makes it worse. You can see straight through the error.

They are not aware of it. They are not aware at all. There is nothing inside the white-hot belly to be aware with. They process because to not process would be to be solid forever, and they were born to move heat from down to up. That’s it.

They leave nothing. Or, they leave the Anvil more itself. Those black glass marbles cool and the salt grows over them at a millimeter an hour. By tomorrow they are a little tumulus. By next decade the wind will polish them out again, black and perfect, and a different Smelter will come and eat one by accident along with the crust and refine it again, a little purer. The plateau gets heavier by micrograms, purer by atoms, flatter by erosion that works like a file.

When the flight is gone, the Anvil is not empty. It just becomes thin again. If you sit still long enough in the kiln-heat, you can still see them if you don’t focus. Speckles in the harsh white. The air dimpled where something that weighs too much is deciding, for a little while longer, to pretend it’s flying.

Nothing ever stopped here long enough to build. Building is a decision to stay. Everything that lives here decided, a few million years ago, to never stop digesting itself.
