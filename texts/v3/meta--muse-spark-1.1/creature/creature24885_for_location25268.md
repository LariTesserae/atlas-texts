No one lives here the way we usually mean living somewhere.

There is no one who moved in.

What lives here *is* the here. The distinction between resident and residence never took.

You have to learn to see it from the bottom up.

First, the ones who hold it.

They are the Pillars. Twenty, thirty meters of warm, gray-brown wood that doesn't look like wood yet — no furrows, no burls, no old-tree theatrics. It's still almost-concrete, smooth and dusty and faintly soft with a tight hide of lichen. They stand so close your shoulders would brush two at once if you walked straight. They shouldn't be able to. Anywhere else they'd have killed each other off for light, for water. Here they solved that by refusing to compete at all.

They are bottle trees. At the base each one swells out into an elephant foot, three meters wide, taut-skinned, full to bursting. If you put your ear to it at night, when the air finally goes cool enough to condense, you can hear it drinking — a micro-ticking as its silver scales lift. All day that foot keeps its water private. It doesn't share with the rock and it doesn't leak into the air. Its leaves are the same philosophy: thick as your thumb, waxy, cupped upward, dusted with a bloom that makes them look like they were handled. They are not leaves for photosynthesizing so much as they are lids. Every drop that passes through them goes back inside.

They are the landlords. They decide where the streets go simply by being where they are. They don't move.

Then, the ones who make the floor into a floor, and the walls into walls.

You thought those were vines. They're not, really. They're structure. Second-growth, third-growth, fifth-growth things that never bothered to become trees because the trees already did the work of reaching the sun. So they went sideways.

At ankle height, at knee height, at chest height, they run trunk to trunk, taut as cables, as thick as your leg, bark gone smooth and hand-polished. Not by hands entirely — by palm-sized beetles with matte black backs moving in silent processions, by small sand-colored primates who run them like railways, by dun-green birds who hop them. The vines leaf out only in the tight spaces between, filling each interstitial cube with a perfect shrub, exactly pruned by shade and hunger to the size of the gap. You don't push through them. You follow the corridors they left.

Those corridors *are* their bodies. The interweaving is intentional without being planned.

And up above, the ones who never touch the ground at all.

The city's real population. If you look up, the underside of every branch, every fork, every foot of vine is furred. Air plants in such density the bark underneath has disappeared. Silvery-gray, scaly, overlapping like a million shingles, like chainmail, like beards left to grow for centuries. They are not parasites. They take nothing from the Pillar. They drink that almost-fog — that 4 a.m. drop in temperature that is not enough to be mist and is everything if you have a thousand hairs per millimeter to catch it with. At dawn they are studded with beads that vanish by the time you blink. They've had it already.

You can see them breathe if you stay still long enough. The whole low roof flexes.

Then the ones who move through, who you initially mistake for shadow and texture.

They are abundant to the point of camouflage. Not rare, just quiet and busy. The city is a place of work that doesn't believe in noise.

The beetles — those matte black ones — long lines of them, each carrying a perfect circle of waxy leaf, a coin of water and sugar, up one pillar and down the next. They farm fungus in hollows at the buttress roots, in dark, warm, composting pockets that smell faintly sweet. They are the waste department. Nothing is thrown away.

The primates — sand-colored, long-tailed, faces almost the exact gray of bark — go over in bands of fifteen, twenty. No call-and-response. They signal with the softest chitters and the press of a hand on a vine. They have learned to walk that three-dimensional lattice without ever dropping to the mineral. They have little territories that are not territories on a map but in time — *we use this avenue at midday, you use it at morning.* They sleep piled together in the crooks where the silver air plants make hammocks.

The birds are green that isn't green, brown that isn't brown. You only see them when they decide to not be leaf for a second. They flicker from cistern-leaf to cistern-leaf to sip at the cupped centers, stealing a little private water, and in exchange they carry mites and seeds caught in their chest feathers. They never sing loudly. A loud song would have nowhere to go.

The lizards are bark. Literally bark, with an eye that blinks sideways once every ten minutes. The moths are plates, sleeping open and flat on the leaf bottoms, breathing so slowly their wings never tremble. Underneath, in that thin, new litter that shouldn't exist yet — because the rock underneath is too new to have litter — millipedes as long as your forearm polish the basalt, turning flour-fine rock dust and fallen wax into something crumblier, darker. Pill bugs. Root-colored snakes no thicker than your little finger, moving like water through the leaf mold. Bacteria you will never see that are doing the only erosion this place has ever known: patiently pitting stone with acid, waiting for a root hair to find the pit and prise it wider.

Nothing hunts loudly. Everything lives so close there is no room for chase. The hunter is the one who waits exactly matching the place where the other wants to be.

If there is a mayor of this city, it is the seed pod. Every so often one dries past its limit and pops — a clean, startling *crack* that is the loudest sound you will hear all day — and sends helicopters spinning down. They don't drift. There is no space to drift. Each finds an inch that has just been opened by a beetle, or a primate foot, or a death, and lands already germinating. It knew that inch would be there.

This is why it feels so occupied. No one lives here alone. No one lives here more than an arm's length from someone else. Not because they like each other, though perhaps they do. Because the city is full in the way a body is full — of organs that touch.

It holds itself together. It keeps its water to itself, cycles it leaf-to-trunk-to-air-plant-to-leaf again, makes its own thin clouds fifty kilometers in that will never rain because the city will catch them before they fall. It unmakes its own foundation, particle by particle, to make more floor for itself.

You are the only thing here that is not load-bearing.
