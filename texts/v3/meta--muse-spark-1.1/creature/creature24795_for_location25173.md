Nothing lives *in* it the way a fish lives in a sea.

Nothing visits it. That's the first thing you have to unlearn. There is no visitor ecology here. No one strayed in and adapted. What lives here was not made and then placed here. It was extruded here, the way sweat is extruded.

Start wide, because you can't see one of them at first.

Between any two trunks — that gap you could lose a country in, that cold clear black-green noon-lit water — at first it looks empty. Then the empty starts to move wrong. Not schooling. Not swarming. The water itself develops a grain, a slow-pulling current that isn't current. That's them, before they are them.

They are the keepers of the equilibrium. 

If you stay still long enough for your own heat to bleed away, you see the first caste resolve.

**The Clingers.**

They look like architecture at first. You thought the grooves in the trunk-bark — those canyons cut by pressure alone, layered with calcium and black slime and generations of dead symbiotes — you thought they were empty. They are not. They are paved.

They are paved with living rivets.

Each one the size of a bus, or a house, or a small barn depending where on the trunk you are looking. Crustacean is the closest word, but only because we don't have a word for a crab that decided to become a power-washer. They have no eyes. Eyes would be filth collectors here. They have domed, blind, white-orange backs crusted over with the same mineral rime as the trunk itself, so that when they stop they vanish. And they hardly ever stop.

Their underbellies are terrible soft brushes, plates of baleen setae, endless cilia that beat and beat and beat against the moving parts. Where a bark-piston the size of a stadium valve shudders out and back — *CHUK* — every ninety seconds, there are fifty of them locked around its lip, scraping bio-slag, polishing the seal with their own dissolved shells. Where a vein of heavy oil-sap sweats black-green through a seam, there are hundreds of them in a living curb, mouths not mouths but siphon-lips held open to let the pressure wash through them. They eat what the tree can't use. They polish what the tree must keep clean.

They don't walk. They migrate in place. A slow, endless stepping around the circumference of the trunk, century after century, the same loop worn into the bark under them. If one dies — and they do, rarely — its neighbors eat it where it stood and secrete a new one from their communal wax. The space is never empty. Dynamic zero.

**The Drinkers.**

Where the Clingers end, the Conveyors begin.

You heard them before you saw them. A faint creak, like a belt under load. In the open water between trunks, where the light is most normal and most lying, something long holds completely still against blue-black. For a full minute you think it's a shadow on the trunk behind it.

It is a fish, but only in the way an assembly line is a road. Thirty, forty feet long, pale as milk-glass, blind as the Clingers, its spine exposed as a flexible driveshaft. Its head is fused — truly fused — to one of the low-pressure valves on the side of a trunk. Not biting. Grown in. The trunk made a socket; it made a plug. The heavy oil pulses through it and you can see it, darkening the thing's gut as it passes, turning the creature the color of old tea.

And its body — behind the head there are segments, dozens of them, each with its own set of feathery gills and its own little ore of fat, and each segment can detach. That is reproduction here. When a segment is ripe with filtered oil and plankton gunk and slag-fat, it pinches off and wriggles once, twice, and then simply holds position in the water column where it was born, growing a new head, already seeking the nearest valve-whisper to clamp to. The parent never moves. Its children will not move either. The factory expands by budding new workers in the exact spot they will work.

When pressure spikes, all the Drinkers along a hundred miles of trunk shudder in unison. You feel it in your molars.

**The Charge-Takers.**

They are why the water crackles even when nothing is moving.

High up, or low down — there is no high or low but you keep thinking in those words — between trunks, the water is laced with live wire. Filaments of kelp-nerve, blue-white with St. Elmo's fire, strung taut across the abyss, humming the bioelectric grid that lets a forest the size of a continent agree to be one engine.

And something lives *in* the current itself.

From a distance you called them whales. From closer you understand they were never whales. They are sacks. Huge, neutrally buoyant bladders of black skin, two, three ferry-lengths long, with no front and no back, with a seam-mouth that runs around their entire equator. They don't swim. They drift into the field lines and stop. They open.

The light goes out of the water around them when they do. Not metaphor. The blue-white filaments dim because the charge is going into them. Their insides fill with storm. You can see the lightning walking under their skin, branching and re-branching, never grounding. They hang there for days, weeks, throbbing with it, filtering it the way a baleen whale filters krill — but the krill is electrons. Then they pulse, once, a deep pressure thump that hits your chest like a bass drum, and a clean, transformed sheet of current fires along their backs and back into the grid.

They are capacitors. They are batteries. They are the pacemakers of that endless industrial hymn. They have no need to eat. The oil is not for them. The water is not for them. They live on the product.

Schools of silver darts — the only thing here that looks like a fish you would recognize — live and die in the lee of those black sacks, in the electromagnetic shadow. Thousands of them, flashing, turning in unison. They never go anywhere. Watch them long enough and you realize they are tracing the exact same torus, the same loop in space, hour after hour. The same individual taking the same turn around the same invisible corner, burning almost no calories because the convection cells the size of states are carrying them. They were spawned here by a rip in a leaf. They will die here, and be unmade here, in the same cubic meter of water where they were made.

Everything here is like that. Nothing is born to leave. Nothing overwinters or returns. There are no young and old, only newer and more polished. The forest does not employ them. The forest *is* them, the way a body is its blood cells. They are the circulation that keeps a machine that was never built from ever stopping.

If you lay your hand on the trunk while the Clingers work and the Drinkers shudder and a Charge-Taker thumps past in the dark, you can feel it: not that you are surrounded by life, but that life here has become a maintenance schedule. It has been happening so long it has worn away any drama. 1.9 erosion on everything, including existence.

They don't notice you. There is nothing built to notice novelty.
