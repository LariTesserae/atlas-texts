What lives here does not live *in* it. It lives *as* it.

At first you think nothing does. The floor looks empty — black glass and cooling basalt — until your eyes unlearn what empty means.

Then the floor resolves.

### The Tiles

They are the floor, now that you see them. Or the floor is wearing them.

Each one is about as long as your forearm, low to the ground to the point of being ground. No head that you can properly call a head, only a forward margin — a lip of soft, heat-sensitive keratin that ever so slightly overhangs the rock. It opens and closes on a timescale you cannot catch in the moment; if you stare for five minutes you will swear it hasn't moved, if you blink and look again an hour later it has chemmed a different fraction of a degree.

The rest is back. A single shield, domed, matte, the color of cooled slag. Black until you get within inches, and then you see it isn't black at all but a very deep amber-brown, translucent at the rim where the edge thins to almost nothing. That's where the light comes from. Not bioluminescence the way a fish does it — nothing so wasteful — but a slow chemical glow trapped in the wax layers under the shell, a byproduct of whatever incredibly patient oxidation they live by. A dimming ember, one per animal, so faint you need averted vision, but there are so many of them.

There are so many of them.

They tile. Not in a perfect hex grid, because the ground underneath cracked without geometry, but they obey the same rule: touch but do not overlap. Edge to edge, lip to back, back to lip, leaving no basalt exposed except where the plates have heaved too sharply for even them to settle. Continent-wide, horizonless, a living varnish. If you could lift one — you can't, not without a hammer — you would see six legs underneath, but legs is the wrong word. Stilts, folded six times under like a spider crane made of dry tendon and chitin. They are tucked so tight and so still that the muscle, if it is muscle, has gone to a kind of taut cord. They do not walk. Walking would cost.

What do they eat? The air. Or what the air is carrying for them. The rock above is still cooling after its birth, still sighing out trace sulfurs and iron carbonyls and heat, and the Tiles drink that. The heat itself is food; there's a gradient between their cooler bellies and the warmer shield and the warmer air above, a few degrees from floor to ceiling that has stayed stable since this place formed. They live on the difference, and because the difference never changes, they never have to change.

Their metabolism has found the floor of what is possible. One inhalation — that soft, iron-scented sigh you hear once if you hold your breath long enough to hear anything at all — one inhalation might take three months. The amber light pulses not with a heartbeat but with a molt that takes a decade. The *tak... tak* you hear is not communication. It's growth. A leg settling one molecule deeper into basalt. A shield adding one more microscopically thin layer of wax and iron oxide from the inside out. They are not dormant. Dormancy is a pause before resuming. This is not a pause. This is the speed at which life runs when it has achieved equilibrium. 

They do not reproduce so much as they blemish. Very rarely a Tile's back will show a hairline fault that doesn't heal. The fault widens by a grain of sand a year. Then two Tiles are there, touching edge to edge, where there was one. No frenzy. No scattering. The world just has one more tile.

They generate most of the warmth in this place. Billions — no, uncounted trillions — of small chemical furnaces, each burning so slowly it will never go out, holding the air at blood-heat because that is the temperature at which their wax stays pliable. They made their own climate and then agreed to keep it.

If you stand still long enough, they will forget you are not one of them. Your heat will be counted in the average.

### The Ring-Makers

But something else learned to count.

You only see the rings after the Tiles have taught your eyes to look for deviation. A low circle of basalt shards, each shard set points-up, hand-placed. Not to keep anything out — there is no wind, no predator that moves — but to mark an intention. To say: *this much space is mine for a while*. To say: *I was here and I was separate*.

The builders are almost impossible to distinguish from the rings when they are inside them. That is deliberate.

They are smaller than you expected. When you finally crouch — careful of the knife-edges — and look into a ring, the thing inside is folded to half your size. Its skin is not skin but a similar varnish, grey-pink and dry, cracked at the joints the way old paint cracks. No hair, no sweat, no eyes worth the name, only a pair of shallow, heat-pitted spots set deep. It breathes once an hour. You can time it if you have the patience, which down here, you will learn.

It wears the Tiles. That is the first tool and the last art. A cloak of a dozen shields that have been pried up — a labor that must have taken seasons — polished on the outside until they shine like wet obsidian, scraped meticulously clean on the inside, sewn edge-to-edge with the dried sinew of its own kind. When it pulls that cloak over its hunched back and tucks its stilt-like limbs under it, its thermal signature breaks up. To the carpet, it is just a slightly uneven patch of Tiles. Warmer in one spot, but not enough to matter.

Inside the rings there are always tools. Never more than four. Never less than two. A flake struck from basalt so its edge is one molecule thick, for prying. A heavier hand-axe for cracking a shield's rim. A bone-awl — not bone, but the stiffened leg of a Tile — for punching holes in the shields to sew them. And often, a bowl: a single great shield, inverted, its wax interior scraped out and eaten. That's how they eat. Not the Tile itself — the Tile is almost all shield and wax and mineral — but the reservoir of concentrated hydrocarbons it stores under its back, a thimbleful of dense, amber fat that takes fifty years to accumulate. Enough for a meal. One meal.

So they live as quarrymen live, not hunters. They choose a Tile at the edge of their awareness, and they wait next to it. They wait until it has grown fat enough. Then they work, quickly by their standards — an hour's flurry of chipping — and then they are still again for years, digesting, while the Tiles slowly close the gap they left.

They do not gather. There is no need to be close when the world is the same everywhere and food grows wherever you sit. The spacing of the rings is the spacing of patience. Too close and you'd be waiting over the same Tile.

And the occupied rings — yes. Many are occupied. You cannot tell by looking because looking is fast and they are slow. But if you stay near one, you will eventually see it: the almost invisible rise and fall. The cloak that is not a cloak. The hands that are not hands anymore but have gone to hard, chipped spatulas from a lifetime of striking rock against rock. They sit with knees to chest, facing no direction, because direction doesn't matter in a place without light. They have learned the final lesson this place teaches: if you want to continue, stop.

Some of them have been still so long that Tiles have begun to grow over the outside of their rings, tiling over their basalt windbreaks, tiling over the hem of their cloaks. Soon there will be no ring visible at all. Just a faint irregularity in the carpet that holds a little more heat than it should.

That is what lives here. A carpet that became a world, and a few small, dry, eye-less people who figured out how to become part of the carpet without ever being asked to.

You haven't seen one move yet. If you wait, you might. 

Or you might realize you have already been waiting, here in your own ring, and the ticking you heard was your own shield settling.
