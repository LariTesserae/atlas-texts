Nothing lives *on* it. That's the first thing you have to unlearn.

Even the things that look like they live on top of it don't.

Take the rust-colored monkeys you saw. At first you think, okay, canopy primate, lowland animal that got lifted with the hot air and never left. But sit and watch them and you realize they never actually touch tree. There's no tree, not as a separate thing. The trunk is a core inside a sleeve of moss that is itself soil that is itself inside a jacket of epiphytes and inside a net of climbing aroids — the whole column is five feet thick with its own weather. The monkeys move in the sleeve. They part the hanging clubmoss curtains with hands as dark and wet as the moss itself. They don't leap. It is too soft and too close for leaping. They wade, they pour themselves from one soaked mass to the next. Their fur never dries. It is green at the tips with algae. When they stop, orchids grow against their backs and you lose them.

Everything that lives here lives *in*.

The most obvious citizens aren't animals at all. They're the mat. Two feet, three feet deep in places, more animal than plant. You pull it up — you shouldn't, but you will — and it's not roots in peat. It's all roots, all the way down, living and dead braided so tight you can't tell where peat ends and root begins. It's white with fungal cord, shot through with beetle larvae the size of your thumb, fat and blind and pumping water through themselves constantly. The mat breathes. That's the pressure-hum you took for blood. When the heat from below builds and the water in the sponge approaches saturation, the mat exhales. Millions of tiny chuffs from valve-holes the ants keep open. At noon it sighs in unison. That is its work: to keep from drowning and to keep from cooking.

And the ants are its maintenance crew. You saw the highways — not built, just worn. They don't carry leaf confetti to a nest. There is no nest, no mound, no architecture, that would violate the second rule. They carry it *through*. A leaf is cut in the upper story, softened by the perpetual wet, carried down a living highway that leaks water with every footstep, chewed, inoculated with the mat fungus a few inches down, and then left. They are not storing food. They are feeding the floor. They are an intestine that walks around outside the body. Their lines are so constant that the moss grows to the edge of them and no further, trimmed by acid from their feet.

In the open pans, where the sun finally gets through clean and turns the brown water to steaming glass, the real abundance shows.

The pan itself is a single organism if you count badly. On the surface: duckweed and azolla making a tight green skin. Just under: tadpoles, but not tadpoles like you know. They never become frogs. There are frogs here too, the slate ones that you only see blink, but these things — in the permanent hot pans — stayed larval. They are black, flat, as big as dinner plates, mouths underneath like catfish, vacuuming biofilm. They boil and churn when the light hits them, thousands, keeping the pan from choking. And through them swims the pan-eel. Not an eel. A caecilian, limbless amphibian, head like a shovel, the color of the iron mud. On cool mornings when the steam is thick, you see their backs break surface to take a gulp of that heavy air. They are as long as you are tall. They live off the plate-tadpoles and the detritus and they are warm to the touch, because they have been lying coiled around the warm upwellings where the basin leaks heat.

Every hot pool has its smith.

You thought those orange shelves were mineral deposits. They are, but they are also being laid down on purpose. The water that clicks and hisses out of the root-mat is blood-warm and full of dissolved iron and sulfur. Where it meets air, bacteria bloom — great slicks of rust-orange and virulent yellow-green that you can see move at the edge if you stare. They oxidize the metal. As they die they brick themselves into shelf. The shelves grow an inch a day. And on each shelf stands a bird.

You haven't seen it stand still long enough to register as a bird. Gunmetal black, beak like forceps, completely featherless on its legs. It doesn't ratchet. Its two-note *tink-tink* is the sound of its beak snipping a fresh bacterial tile and flipping it to check the underside for larvae. It is a farmer. It tends the iron garden, culls it, eats some. Its droppings are pure magnetite. When a flock takes off at once from a mud roll, all that mineral in their guts makes your compass swing.

Nothing here hunts the way the lowlands hunt, with chase and crash. Sound is too expensive. Everything absorbs percussion. So everything became quiet and close and chemical. The black bees — coal bees — don't buzz. Their wings are fringed and move in near silence. They don't pollinate by brushing past. They land inside the barrel-pitchers, which are not traps but fermentation vats. The pitcher produces a weak ethanol and a stink. The bee gets drunk, wallows, coats itself. Then, sedated, it crawls out and flies heavily to the next vat. Pollen is slurry.

The big hunters are waiting, not stalking.

In the low gully where the wall weeps in a steady sheet without a source, you will finally see one if the cloud thins. A mat-caiman. But not a caiman. An amphibian that never left its giant salamander blueprint, head broad and flat, eyes like wet pebbles on top. It wedges itself into the throat where water has made a throat, its back completely covered by living liverwort that has rooted into its armor of sub-dermal bone. It's fifteen feet, maybe more, and cold on top and hot underneath because it lies half in the heated seep. It doesn't move for days. The water brings food to it: pan-eels that misjudge, monkeys that slip during a pour. When it does move, it's so fast and silent and wet you only know it has happened because the water is now where it wasn't and the curtain of weeping ferns is still swinging.

They all share one adaptation. They don't keep heat in, they dump it. The monkeys have naked, veined faces and palms. The salamanders have those vast liverwort saddles that transpire like radiators. Even the trees — those short, swollen trees — have leaves the size of doors that are cold when you touch them, because they are air conditioning. They pump cloud-water up and exhale it. The forest sweats for everyone.

If you stay till evening, when the rim clouds spill over and the basin begins its nightly condensation cycle and the steam columns finally lie down, you'll feel them settle. The mat sighs. The pan-plates resume their vacuuming. The coal bees go still inside their vats. The caiman elevates its nostrils above the liverwort. The monkeys huddle, not for warmth but to share the load of drying.

None of them built this place. None of them need to. It builds itself through them. The water that failed to get over the eastern scarp this morning is now in their bodies and will be cloud again before midnight. They are not residents. They are the temporary shape that water and heat and old, patient rock take when they have to work in order to keep from stopping.

You, standing there, not transpiring, not filtering iron, not feeding the mat, are the only thing in the bowl that isn't working. That's why it is so quiet when you hold your breath.
