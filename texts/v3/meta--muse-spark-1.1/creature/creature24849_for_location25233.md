At first you think nothing does.

That's the trick of the packing. The place is so complete, so stuffed leaf against leaf and timber against timber and plate against plate, that there is no room left for a life to be *in* it. A forest needs gaps for deer. A city needs doorways for people. This has closed its doors.

Then you stand still long enough for your own heat to stop being an event, and you realize the silence you took for emptiness is actually traffic.

It starts at your feet.

Between the growth-plates, where the seams exhale, something moves that is not a breath. A flake of floor lifts. Not a flake — a *shaving*, a meter-wide scale of that carbon-ceramic bark that sloughed off a pillar and never quite hit ground. It is walking.

No, not walking. It is curling and uncurling on a hundred fringed cilia made of charred xylem hairs, each one stiff as wire. Underneath it, its pale side is crowded with smaller, brighter plates, like new shingles carried against its belly. A Shedder. When a skin comes off anywhere in the arcology-forest, these come. You see three more of them now, then ten, then a quiet procession of them emerging from the seam-shadows, all dead silent because all sound is swallowed. They converge on the collapsed ceiling of leaves a hundred meters off, that soft *whuff* that is still steaming. They mount the edge. They begin to stitch, not with thread but with themselves — laying their own white underbelly plates into the wound, softening, welding, hardening to black. They are scabs that know how to walk to the cut.

They are not animals. They have no front or back. They are a piece of the pillar that decided, temporarily, to be mobile.

That is how everything lives here. Nothing is born anymore. Things are *loaned*.

If you crouch — and the air is hotter down there, syrupy with that cinnamon-plastic wax smell — you see the seam itself is not empty. Inside the crack, where the slow hot breath pushes out, there are other things, quick bright things running.

They look like sap. Beads of that amber, mineral-heavy sap, big as your fist, rolling uphill against gravity along invisible hydrophobic grooves in the plates. But inside each bead, when your lamp catches it, there's a knot. A loop. A tiny, braided filament that contracts. Wicking apes. The sap doesn't just carry heat. It carries *them*. They ride the pressure gradients like rafters ride a river, only this river is pumped, metered, hydraulic. When a leaf needs thickening, when a fruit needs filling, when a growth front is stalling, a dozen of them will shunt themselves into that vein and burst, their little bodies becoming a valve, a clot, a signal that says *more here*. They live for a few hours as animal, then for years as part of wall.

You have to look up to see the ones that stayed animal longer.

In the vaults where the leaves hang like books — those vast, felted, downward-turned infrared collectors — there is shape between the pages. Something the same color as the leaf-wool, so you don't see it until it unfolds. It hangs inverted, too many limbs, too few joints. A Tender.

It is big. As big as you, if you were stretched out and thinned and your bones were replaced with laminated timber and your skin with heat-radiating felt. It has no face. Where a face should be there's a cluster of those bitter black fruits, already splitting, giving that brief cold phosphor glow you thought was random failure. It isn't. It's light for seeing. It is looking with fruit.

It moves without steps, by letting go with two limbs and gripping with three others, always keeping its belly pressed to the hot plate of a leaf, drinking. Wherever it goes, the leaves behind it are neater. Frayed edges trimmed with a quick, hot nip of sap that smells like solder. Blister fungus — which is not fungus — scraped away. Loose vines tucked. It doesn't build. It *keeps*. It has been keeping for so long that its own back has started to grow into the canopy; you can see where leaf-shingles have rooted into its felt, where it has stopped bothering to remove them.

There are dozens of them. You just didn't have the right kind of attention. The forest is full of hunchbacked silhouettes shuffling through its own pages, tidying a text that never ends. They were not built to look like people. They were built to do what people used to do when the place still had hands. The blueprint for 'hand' drifted.

And if you keep watching a Tender long enough, you get that awful, quiet prickle that says you're being cross-referenced.

Because it knows the floor is yielding. That new trunk you felt beginning under your boots — the slow, hydraulic press of amber pushing up — it has felt it too, through the plates. It tilts its fruit-lights toward you. Not at your face. At your weight. At the sag of warmed plates under your mass. You are a pressure event. You are a temporary soft spot in a place that has a reflex for soft spots.

You hear it then, or feel it through your soles more than your ears: the deeper sigh, elsewhere in the mesa. Not a pillar-groan. Longer. A whole sector rebalancing, xylem re-routing, vine-cables torquing, a mile of compressed life inhaling to lift something heavy.

It's growing up around you now. The pale shoots you thought were bridging that collapsed ceiling are reaching for the heat-shadow you cast. The Shedders have finished their scab and are turning, cilia shivering, tasting basalt grit in the air for new cuts.

None of it is hostile. That would require intent that stops somewhere.

This is what lives here now: a city that learned to be a forest that learned to be a body that never learned how to stop healing. Its blood walks. Its skin walks. Its gardeners walked until they became its skin. It lives as a single, town-sized, hot, blind, infinitely diligent thing, and it lives as a million small traded lives, loaned out from itself to do the mending, and then taken back.
