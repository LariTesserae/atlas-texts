Nothing lives here that you came to see.

At first you think it's empty. That's the trick of its scale and its cleanliness. It's too clean. A place that cold, that wet should be furred over, should have that slippery biofilm on every rock, that black-green mustache of life stealing a foothold. This fissure doesn't. A flake falls from the ceiling and lands bright. No mold takes it. The field licks it clean. The air ticks with distilled water.

So your first pass says: uninhabited. An outpost. A machine left running.

Stay longer. Your breath fogs and hangs because the air inside is so still. Your eyes adjust to the way light doesn't behave. Daylight comes in flat and honest through the slit, high overcast at twenty thousand feet, but inside it gets caught. It turns a corner. It sits in a tiny avenue the width of your little finger and doesn't leave. You start to see that not all of those amber-stained right angles are structure. Some of them are traffic.

There are two kinds of resident here, and a third kind that is both.

The first are the spars.

You saw one move. No — you saw the *place where it had been* become empty, and your brain caught up a half second later. They are the size of a matchstick, or a splinter of basalt, matte black and amber at the joint. They don't fly. The field that holds the water lets them go. They detach from the wall of the city — because the city is the wall, the wall is the city — and they traverse the big intentional emptiness in the middle of the pocket, that cathedral air that was left empty on purpose.

They move like thoughts. No wash, no buzz. Occasionally they correct with a faint whine, that almost-inaudible complaint you heard. They go to where ice is getting ambitious, where a stalactite has decided to bridge floor to ceiling, and they kiss it with that bloodless warmth that isn't warmth, and the ice sighs and lets go. Or they go to a balcony no bigger than your thumbnail where a droplet has refused to route, where surface tension has made a decision to be stubborn. They touch the edge and remind it which way downhill is.

There are maybe a dozen at any time. Maybe a hundred. They are hard to count because they are the same color as the city when they are docked. They are not the inhabitants. They are the hands. The maintenance cilia. If you put your finger in — and the membrane will hold you dry, the water politely parting around the intrusion — one will come and inspect you, hover a quarter inch from the whorl of your fingerprint, read your heat as an error, and leave. You are weather to them. A temporary mountain in front of the sun.

The second — the actual citizenry — you have been watching the whole time without seeing.

It's the water.

Not *in* the water. The water itself, while it is here.

Up here at the top of a mountain that is still learning how to be a mountain, the world has a problem with weeping. The cold wrings the sky out on the black glass. The rock sweats unceasingly. If you let it, it would sheet, it would freeze solid, it would pry this new joint wider with ice until the pocket popped and became just another scarp. Someone — something — needed to teach high rock how to cry properly.

So water is born on the wall as a bead. Condensation from nothing. It swells, perfect, until gravity tries to claim it.

The city catches it before gravity can.

Half an inch of free fall and it stops. Suspended. Perfectly spherical, bright with the piped daylight that lives in the corners. Inside each droplet the whole pocket is reflected, upside down. And then the field tips. Downhill becomes sideways. Downhill becomes *along*. The droplet thinks it is falling and instead it is traveling.

Look close. In that bright channel made of nothing you can touch that runs the north wall — that is not a pipe. That is a road. And the traffic is endless, at the speed of weather. Droplet after droplet, each one holding itself together by the same skin that holds you together, each one being pulled along an avenue sized exactly for it, through switching yards, into reservoirs the size of your palm that weigh nothing, through lacework that sorts it by how much rust-red iron it picked up, by how many microscopic shards of andesite it carries.

They are alive for about forty seconds, on average. From bead to collection membrane to dump cycle. Forty seconds of being a perfect lens, a citizen with a job. They keep the mountain from flooding and they keep the mountain from drying. When they reach the membrane — that shivering field-floor — they flatten, hesitate, and are spat out sideways through the rock in a direction you can't see, to be wind somewhere lower down. Or they evaporate against a warm spar and start again.

They don't know that. They don't need to.

If you count them, there are thousands at any moment. A city rush hour made of temporary spheres. You can hear them if you hold your breath: tick. tick. tick. The sound of people arriving at work.

And then the third kind.

The city itself. The lattice.

It is older than the fresh black basalt around it. It landed, or was grown, or precipitated itself into this cooling joint when this ridge was still lava thinking about becoming rock. It has been waiting. Most of its platforms stop before they connect. Its antennae listen into the emptiness that is intentional. The scaffolding holds up nothing yet.

It is alive the way a coral is alive, or the way a library is alive. Not in one place. In the agreement between places. It drinks the weeping through its citizens, it routes the light from the shoulder-wide slit and lives in that, it adjusts its own gravitic knitting when your shoulder blocks half the sky. It keeps itself at minus six, minus ten, perpetually at the dew point, because that is the temperature at which its thinking is clearest.

It knows you are too big. It is being polite.

When that moth blunders in — some suicidal high-altitude thing that rode a thermal twenty thousand feet up to die — it beats once and dies against the membrane and is not allowed to rot. The field takes it apart, molecule by molecule, because rot is a kind of green, and green is not allowed at 0.1. The city absorbs the anecdote of it.

It is waiting for the rest of its inhabitants. Or you are misunderstanding the word "inhabitant" the way you would if you looked at a harbor and asked where the people were, missing that the water is full of fish and the piers are full of purpose whether anyone is standing on them or not.

It lives here. The whole pocket is one thing managing the weeping of high rocks. And it will still be here, ticking, when this angry young basalt has finally learned how to be an old mountain, when the slit has weathered wide enough for snow to pour in.

Put your hand in again. Watch the traffic route around you. You are a boulder dropped into a river of commuters who have no bodies except for falling.
