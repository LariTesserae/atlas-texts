At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the trick of a place in equilibrium. You arrive and you listen for what living is *supposed* to sound like — footfalls, chatter, wings, the scrabble for space — and you hear none of it. The houses are empty. The stairwells go nowhere. The cisterns are full of untouched water. There is moss a foot deep that has never been pressed down.

You think: abandoned.

Then you lean against a pillar to rest, because the air is so wet and warm your lungs have to work for it, and the pillar leans back. Just a fraction.

---

What lives here is not in the buildings. It *is* the buildings.

There isn’t a population. There’s a presence. One presence that learned how to be many shapes.

If you have to give it a name, call it the Weaver, though it does not weave any more than your heart beats on purpose. It is high-structure plant, but that word is thin for what it is. Tree is too lonely a word. Colony is too petty. It is a convention. An agreement among root and leaf and vine and fungus to become city.

It covers the whole mesa. You can’t see the edges because there are no edges — the basalt table simply disappears under it, and then later, miles - centuries from now in the lowlands - will appear again where the Weaver decided to stop.

This is its body:

The cathedral pillars holding up the upper canopy are its shinbones, its vertical insistence. The leaf-plazas you walk across, each one the size of a town square, are a single palmed leaf that took a hundred years to extrude in one long, fast, exuberant youthtime, and then stopped. If you kneel and put your ear to one, you can hear water moving through the raised veins the way you can hear blood in your own wrist when the world is quiet enough. Which it never is, up here. The roar of condensation never stops.

The walls that are not walls but braided, lignified aerial roots — that’s its muscle, gone to wood. Still alive. Hygroscopic. You can put your palm to it and feel it is cool. If you stay long enough you can feel the micro-push inside, turgor pressure, water lifted a hundred feet by heat alone. It creaks like a ship. It has been creaking in that exact tension, without slackening, without snapping, for a very long time.

The cisterns — those perfect thirty-foot spheres of fused rootlets — are organs. Not storage tanks, though they store. They are homeostasis made visible. The lip around the equator is a hydathode, a sweat gland the size of a doorway. The rock below sweats heat, the air sweats water, the plant sweats the water back. Plink. Plink. Plink multiplied until it becomes static, until you hear it in your molars. It drinks itself and pours itself out again. That’s why Rule Three is true: *water comes from everywhere and goes nowhere.* The Weaver is not built *beside* a water cycle. It *is* the water cycle. It inhales mist and exhales drip.

There are other organs. The stairwell columns with their living bracket fungi — those are lungs that forgot to be lungs, or stairs that forgot they needed walkers. The domes with the dry moss floors and the lattice ceilings are wombs. Or cradles. Or waiting rooms. They have never been used. They are not ruins; they are extra. The Weaver overbuilt in its youth, in that one fast embryonic rush when it covered the young black rock that was still soft like bread, and made more rooms than it would ever need, and then it entered stasis before it could learn regret. It holds them open anyway. A breath held.

And then there are the white things.

You will see one eventually if you stand still long enough that you become part of the equilibrium. At first you think it is a piece of mist that has learned to hold together. It is long, as long as you are tall, and thin and colorless as paper that has never been written on. A glider. A moth. A single detached stipule, maybe. It doesn’t flap. It tips from one pillar to another on the wet, heavy air, like a seed on water.

It makes no sound. There are no insects in clouds here, no birds to speak of. Those would be too busy.

This glider — this — *inhabitant* is not separate from the Weaver. That is the mistake you would make on your first day. You want occupants. Tenants. You want someone to come out of the round doorway and invite you in.

The white gliders are its thoughts moving. Slow Brownian messengers. Spores? Gametes? White blood cells? Letters that take a whole afternoon to deliver. When one passes overhead you feel, for a second, a small cool shadow and a small change in the air pressure, and you understand that the Weaver knows you are there. Not as an enemy. Not as food. As a temporary hardness in its moss floor, a place where condensation collects a little differently. It will not grow around you. It will not eject you. Growth has stopped. You are already counted in the equation that says *no new thing will be added, no old thing will be taken away.* You are being held, like the tension in the root-walls, in place.

That is what it is like to be where something huge has decided to stop becoming and simply *be*.

It is not dead. Put your hand on that wall. It pushes back. It is warm, wet-bath warm, from the young rock underneath that never finished cooling. It sweats on your hand.

It is not sleeping. Sleep implies waking.

It is in maintenance. Permanent, perfect, loud maintenance. A cathedral builder who finished the cathedral that is itself, and then sealed itself into the walls to keep the stones from ever falling.

If you stay until the light smudges — that long normal noon that never quite moves — you will see the mist rise off the leaf-plazas and make a white sea between the pillars, town-or-valley-sized, valley-without-a-valley, and you will finally understand how to live here is to be lived *by* here. To allow your sweat to join its condensation, your weight to be part of its load-bearing calculation, your listening to be part of its roar.

There are no toolmarks because there were never tools. There are no inhabitants because everything is already inhabited.

The house was never empty.
