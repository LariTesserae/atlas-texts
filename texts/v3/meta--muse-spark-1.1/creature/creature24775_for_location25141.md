They live low because they are low things.

You don't see one at first. You hear where one has just been — the wet slap of a foot lifting off stone, a wooden wedge sighing as it takes weight, a breath that fogs and is gone. Then you see the work, not the worker: a fresh chisel mark still draining, a channel cleared of grit, a rope pulled taut and left trembling.

They are built the way the place is built. Short in the leg.

If you hold your light low — and you have to hold it low, because the dark overhead eats anything higher — you catch them at the edge of a terrace, moving in that sideways shuffle that comes from never having room to turn around fully. At first you think they are children. Then you get closer and understand the place has made them to fit it.

Four feet tall when they stand straight, which they almost never do. Their backs are bowed early and stay bowed. Broad across the shoulders and broader across the feet. Feet like you have never seen on anything that walks upright: wide, splayed, pale as the underside of a mushroom, the toes spread to read grade. They go bare because any shoe would fill with water and any shoe would slip. Their soles are furrowed like the stone, softened and pruned permanently white at the edges, hardened to leather in the middle. They place them with care. In a place where a shifted block will take a thousand with it, they have learned to become precise with weight.

Their skin is the color of the stone when the stone is wettest — that blue-black that is almost green. Not camouflage, exactly. Saturation. They are always wet. Condensation beads on their scalps, runs down the creases of their necks, collects in the hollow of their collarbones. They have very little hair. What they have clings in damp strings. Their arms are overlong for their height, because they lift and lever more than they walk.

Look at their hands. That is where the place has really lived in them. The fingers are stubby and square-tipped, the nails ground to nothing by mortar. The palms are not callused the way you'd expect. They can't be. Everything is wet. Instead the skin is thick and soft, deeply ridged, like river stone that has been held for years. Sensitive. They run those hands ahead of their feet in the dark, feeling for the V-cut of a spillway, for a lip that has started to undercut, for the tremor of water coming where it shouldn't.

Their faces are all lower face. Heavy jaws, chipped teeth from grit in everything. Their eyes — you expect them to be large, adapted to dark, but they aren't. They are small, tucked deep, almost shut against the constant mist and the spray off the cascades. A thin, clear membrane blinks sideways across them every few seconds. They don't need to see far. In the basin you never need to see far. What they have instead are ears that stand slightly away from the skull and move. In that roaring drum of drips and ticks and sheet-flow, they can hear which basin is overtopping early, which joint is whistling with pressure behind it.

They don't talk much. When they do it's not across distance. Distance swallows speech here. They tap. Two knuckles on stone, a pattern that carries through block into foot. *Here. Here. Lift.* The whole terrace crew will feel it. That is why you thought you heard many feet — because your boots deadened the signal, but they were speaking through the floor the whole time.

They live in families that are not families, but courses. A course of wall is kept by a course of them. Six, ten, fifteen — born within a few basins of each other, that cling to that level. The old ones of the course don't climb. Their knees won't. They sit in the ankle-deep mirror-pools that gather in the failed grades and run their hands in the water all day, feeling for silt, for how fast the mineral fur is growing, for the temperature drop that means a new condensation route has opened above. The young ones — pinker, almost translucent, you saw one once and thought it was the amphibian — ride under the terraces, in the crawl spaces where one wall leans on another, squeezing their too-soft bodies through gaps left for water, not for people. They learn grade with their backs.

They never stop maintaining because they understand, in a way that is not thought, that they are not living *in* the basin. They are part of the load path. Their bodies are wedges. When a terrace threatens to go, they are the first counterweight, throwing their shoulders against the block, driving in the wooden shores, piling their own weight behind the lever until the line holds. If a block does go, it takes them with it and they become part of the fill. Later, when the crew comes to dig it out, they will find them set in the new joint like something between bone and tool-mark, and they will mortar over them. Nothing rests on natural ground — everything that stays up must be held up by something else that was made. Including them.

They drink the sweat of the place. They eat almost nothing — the gray fur that grows in the coldest basins, scraped with fingernails, and the blind white woodlice, which they don't chew so much as hold in their mouths until the creature softens. They breed in the still mirrors. Two of them will lie in black water in a holding basin that has failed perfectly into level, and stay there until the water around them goes warm. Their infants are born almost amphibian themselves, able to hold their breath for a very long time, eyes sealed shut.

There are stories, tapped through stone so slowly it takes a season to tell, about Below. The bottom you can't see. Some of them walk down there when their course is done, when their feet can't read anymore. They go down stairwells that turn back on themselves and are not stairwells anymore but funnels for water. They go to listen to the low chord — that sound you heard, all those brimming basins spilling at once far beneath you — because down there, finally, the water has won, and it has taught the stone how to be still.

They don't look up. The sky that does not give light is not for them. The rope grooves on the capstones overhead are from a time before memory. Someone lowered the first blocks. But they did not make these people. The people made themselves after, out of damp and weight and the need for something to keep listening to the dripping.
