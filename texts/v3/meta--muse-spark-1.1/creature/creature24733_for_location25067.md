The people who lived there are gone. That's the first thing that lives there.

Not gone like ruin-gone. Gone like a room you left in the middle of a conversation. The moss still has the shape of them.

They were not builders. They were negotiators. Sixty at the most, in a cathedral that could have held ten times that, because the Building would not allow it. You can see it in how they slept.

One pod per person. One empty pod between every occupied one. A law the tree taught with softness: if you put two sleeping mats in one chamber, by morning the floor had sagged, the walls had sweated, the ceiling had lowered itself an inch to look at you. The tree wanted emptiness inside its fullness, and they listened.

So they became a town of polite distances.

They were quiet people, not by temperament but by engineering. They spoke low, in the chest, because anything louder got lost anyway. When the cicadas sang, you could not speak at all. You put your hand on a column and felt the speech of someone three pods over through the bark, bone-conduction. Their language had a lot of touching in it. Palm to warm bark to say *I'm here.* Two taps to say *move away a little, please.*

They wore soft things and made soft things. Everything was boiled vine and gourd and leaf-cloth that never fully dried. No metal, because metal hurt it. No fire inside, except in little clay thimbles that they tended like sick animals, cupped in their hands and smothered quickly. Food was what the Building extruded for them - waxy nutmeat in pods the size of a child's head, sweet water that they learned to sip only when the tree offered it from its columns, not when they cut for it. The water from a cut was too sweet. It made you dream of being bigger.

They were good at waiting. At being in a room that was too big for one person and not trying to fill it. Children were raised with a strange etiquette: you did not hold your baby all the time. You put them down one column over, where you could see them through the leaf-film wall, so the floor wouldn't sink. They learned early not to cluster. A mother would sing to her child across an empty pod. The loneliness of it made them gentle.

That is why they left. Not because the Building was cruel. Because it is loud in a way that a mammal cannot be from.

What lives there now is the reason.

The cicadas *are* the town. You think of them as insects in a tree, but that's backwards. They are the tree's voice box, its throat. The nymphs are not on the bark, they are *in* the bark, sealed in like seeds, their eyeless faces flush with the wood, identical to the lichen until they flex. When the light and heat and pressure tell them to, tens of thousands of them at once tear open at the back and scream. It isn't mating call. It's enforcement. 

120 decibels of solid, metallic sawing that turns the whole vault into a drum. The moss shivers. Your jaw aches from the inside out. The Building uses them to keep its rooms empty. One hour of that and any monkey, any human, any thing with soft ears and blood close to the skin stands up and walks out ducking under the root-door and doesn't come back that day. The people who lived here learned to leave before it started — they felt it in the columns warming — and sit outside in the bowl in the normal sun with their hands over ears made of wax and leaf-wool, waiting for their home to finish shouting.

What holds the chorus in check are the second citizens.

The geckos. Hundreds of them, each one as long as your forearm to your elbow, old rose pink and nearly clear, with blue eyes like blistered glass. There is one per column. Exactly one. They never fight. They don't need to. The tree gives each a column and a share of cicadas and they take their work seriously. When the singing starts you see them open their mouths impossibly wide and go *tok* and *tok*, swallowing sound itself, the molted singers, the new singers, their feet making that soft sticky applause on the warm bark. They are the janitors, the deacons. When the singing stops they lick their eyes and go still again, spread-eagled on the living pillars, breathing.

Above them, never landing, never leaving, are the third.

Swifts that were born inside and have never seen sky. They should not exist. Little black needles, faster than thought, they stitch the air of the vault through the screaming, eating the emerald wasps that farm aphids on the leaf-walls, eating the black beetles that roll balls of flower. They nest in the hairy armpits where a branch becomes a room, one nest per twenty meters, never closer. One swift pauses mid-air in front of you, its heart a buzz you feel more than hear, and then it is elsewhere.

Under all of them lives the oldest resident, which is the Building itself.

It lives in the margins. In water that drips sideways and up. In light that slides the wrong way across a column, that jumps from your left hand to your right without crossing you. In the pod at the back that is bigger inside than the wall is thick, where if you stand still you can hear yesterday's cicadas singing underneath today's silence, a little behind, out of sync, like an echo from tomorrow. The tree remembers when this basin was a shallow sea. That's why the air at the deepest knot smells of salt. It is still filtering that sea. It still thinks it is bigger than it is, so it keeps growing rooms for people who will know how to stand far apart from each other.

That is what lives there now that no one lives there.

A hollow city that is full to bursting and enforces one empty pod between every full one. A town that grew itself a people to take care of it for a little while, and then outgrew them with its own noise. 

If you stay long enough for the next chorus to inhale — the bark under your hand tightening, the geckos all opening their eyes at once — you'll feel it decide you are too close to yourself, and politely begin to roar you out.
