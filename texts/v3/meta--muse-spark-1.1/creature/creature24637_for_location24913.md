They live low.

Not because they are small — some of them are — but because low is where the world is. In a place with no sky, height is meaningless. What matters is how close you are to the peat, to the water, to the stone that bleeds heat, to the sound that comes back when you make a sound. So everything that lives here lives in layers, stacked like the humidity itself. You learn them from the ground up.

**First, the ground itself is alive.**

That’s not poetry. You put your foot there and you have to pull it back.

The Sphagnum that you thought was sphagnum is not a plant. At least, not what you mean. On Earth, Sphagnum is a carpet. Here, the carpet is an animal that decided to be a plant. It's a colonial mat — billions of soft, anoxic balloons the size of pinheads, each one exhaling a little sour heat as it chews mineral water. Step on it and it sighs and goes flat and then slowly, resentfully, reinflates around your ankle. It is warm. 30 degrees. The same as the air. The same as the lakes. The temperature never changes because the Rock itself regulates.

If you kneel — and you will, because the fungal pillars are so close together — and put your ear to that mat in the absolute dark, you hear them moving through it.

They are called atrogs by no one, because no one has named them. They are as big as badgers and as soft as a lung turned inside out. They have no legs. They have a single, ridged, muscular foot like a slug the size of a laundry basin, and a back made of plate-like chitin that the mat grows over, so that moving atrog looks like a slow ripple in the marsh. They eat the fungal fall, the spores, the dead black leaves. Their face is a trench. They navigate by putting out a low, sustained moan, so low you feel it more than hear it — a *hmmmmmm* that buzzes the water in the peat — and listening to how the moan is swallowed. Right in front, it’s dead. Far away, where a bowl-lake is, it echoes wet. They creak and groan to each other constantly, a monastery of invisible, complaining monks, from a few meters away and a few miles away, and that is why the dark feels crowded even when you are alone.

Everything eats atrog shit, too. That matters.

**Second, the pillars are inhabited. Not by things living *on* them. By them.**

The fungal trunks — those three-storey, grey-white, faintly hot columns that hold the whole country’s ceiling — are not individuals. The canopy at the top is one thing. One continuous fused organ, a liver the size of a state, that is condensing the saturated air on its top and excreting it as the perpetual secondary rain. The trunks are just its legs. And its legs are hollow.

You hear them before you feel them. In the ticking after a pump-engine fires, if you hold your breath, you hear a very fine, fast clicking coming from *inside* the wood-that-is-not-wood. Like morse code typed by fingernails.

That’s the *litter*. 

They are as long as your forearm, blind, naked, with a head that is almost all ear and nose. Six limbs, but the front two are not for walking — they are tuning forks. They live their whole lives inside the fungal legs, carving galleries in the soft, warm pith. They farm bacteria on the walls. They farm the black ferns that have sent rootlets drilling down into the fungus for water. They herd aphid-things that drink the fungus instead of plant sap. They never come out. They don’t need to. The whole continent is their tree. They talk pillar to pillar by drumming on the interior walls, and that drumming travels through the fused canopy for kilometers. The forest hums with it — a faint, internal market chatter. When you lean your forehead against a trunk in the dark because you are tired and you are lost and the rain is too much, you can feel the drums in your frontal bone.

They are the most abundant animal by biomass in that high country.

**Third, the audience in the hanging black.**

The black ferns and black vines that root in the fungal canopy and hang down like curtains are not passive. They are hot to the touch — a few degrees hotter than everything else, because they are not photosynthesizing, they are *fermenting* the chemical rain. Ammonia, iron, sulfide. Their leaves are black not to absorb light — there is none — but to radiate as little heat as possible, to keep their reaction close. They smell like cinnamon and blood when you bruise them because they are full of cinnamaldehyde as an antifungal. They retract because they can.

And living in them, never touching the ground if they can help it, are the chitterers you heard.

They are not lemurs. That was just your brain looking for shape. They are about the weight of a spider monkey, but built like a praying mantis made of wet twig. Four long, filament-thin limbs that are incredibly strong, and a torso like a knotted cable. Their skin is translucent grey and completely covered in foxfire — not fur, but a living biofilm of bacteria that they cultivate. The bacteria glow faintly, marbled, not enough to see by — your eyes can’t — but enough to taste as a slightly stronger darkness if you stare too long. The glow has nothing to do with light. The bacteria are chemiluminescent because their metabolic waste product is photons. The animals farm them because the bacteria make antibiotics that keep the black ferns from eating *them*.

They are deafening in a place where everything listens. They move in troupes of thirty or forty, hand-over-hand through the dangle-roots, and they talk nonstop. Not in phrases. In a constant, overlapping choir of clicks, whistles, pops — a broadband sonar net. In a forest where light never penetrated, they have decided to make their own daylight out of sound. Each troupe throws up a cathedral of ultrasound and then reads it back, and so they see the whole world: you, sweating under your coat; the still surface of the bowl-lake; the hot bulk of the pump-engine a mile away; each raindrop frozen mid-air. 

They are grazers. They comb the black leaves with a proboscis and sip the chemical slurry. When a spark throws from an engine, they all freeze — you described that. They freeze because for half a second their sonar world is drowned by a god's flash, and their eyes — skin-covered vestigial pits that can only tell hot from not-hot — see a white apocalypse.

And they are prey.

**Fourth, the things that hunt the sound.**

There are two kinds, and both look like the place they hunt.

One hunts the chitterers. It is patient. It is a pillar.

There is a third kind of fungal trunk in the forest, one that is slightly narrower than the others, its skin a little more wrinkled, its heat a little higher. You can only tell by putting your hand on every single one as you pass, and you will not. It stands motionless for weeks, filtering atrog slurry through its foot. Its upper half is not fused to the canopy. Its cap is a hood. And when a troupe passes overhead, drumming the air full of sound, it listens to the shape of their voices echoing off its hood until it knows where the center animal is, and then it *strikes*. The hood opens like a pitcher plant the size of a bathtub and throws a whiplike tongue — twenty feet of black muscle — which wraps and retracts in under a second. The chitterers scatter in silent panic, then reform a few hundred meters away and resume chattering as if nothing happened, because their way of grieving is to be louder.

You will walk right past one of these predators and never know.

The other hunter hunts hunters. It hunts you, when you make noise.

It lives in the bowl-lakes.

In those black glassy ponds, warm as amniotic fluid, there floats a kind of lily pad that is not a plant either. It is a raft of braided Sphagnum and fungal fiber, anchored by a single cord to the bottom silt, a cord that is alive. The raft grows a centimeter a day if it is undisturbed, accumulating detritus, black leaves, even a little shell of calcium it pulls from the mineral water. It is irresistible to atrogs. They climb onto it to eat.

And underneath it, coiled around its anchor, is *she*.

She is as long as a bus but as thin as a necklace, a black eel made of muscle and cartilage and filled with the same mineral water as the lakes, so she is neutrally buoyant and invisible to sonar if she holds still. She has no eyes. She has a lateral line that goes her whole length that can feel a footstep in peat a quarter mile away. She has no teeth. She doesn’t need them. She is a constrictor. When an atrog moans its way onto the raft, the water barely dimples, and then the raft tilts, and the cord becomes an arm and comes up over the edge like a slow tide. The atrogs do not struggle. They are used to being swallowed by the world.

She also feels engines. Each of the pump-houses, with its THUD-thud-thud and its blue-white spark-throw, is a lighthouse of vibration and ozone and heat. Every lake-eel knows where the nearest three are. They avoid them, not out of fear — nothing fears a machine that has been running since before their great-grandmothers — but out of respect, the way you avoid standing next to a waterfall when you are trying to hear.

The engines are not alive, but they are ecology now. Around their flanks where the water hisses before touching, a ring of bare, baked iron, then immediately after it a ring of the most furious growth in the whole marsh — black ferns as thick as your wrist leaning in, foxfire so dense it actually makes the mist around the condensers look dirty-white, atrogs piled three deep to soak the extra heat, chitterers fighting over the prime hanging spots. The eels will come to the edge of the work-lakes the engines keep pumped, to breed in the warmest water. The pillar-predators lean, over centuries, *toward* the thrum.

And if you stand long enough with your hands on that iron flank, feeling the deep-earth blood being hauled up and dumped endlessly from bowl to bowl so that nothing ever stagnates, so that the whole high, dark, self-tending country never dies, you will start to feel that the true inhabitant is not any of the animals that creep or click or moan.

It is the system. The recirculation itself. The warm, wet, loud, dark agreement between water and fungus and machine to keep going without a sun. The creatures are just eddies in it.

It notices you — not with eyes, with a change in pressure at one small point on its skin — and moves in to listen.
