Nothing lives *on* it.

That was your first mistake. Desert-thinking. You were looking for something standing up against the flatness — a figure to give it scale. 

At first you think nothing lives here at all. Then you think maybe the glassy tumbleweeds are the life. Then the careful walkers. Then you understand that those are just the parts that still need to move.

What lives here is the floor.

Not under the floor. The floor itself.

Each of those low, crowded, shoulder-to-shoulder forms — the ones you took for rocks, for housings, for tombs the size of refrigerators — that is the population. They are not houses. They never were houses. Or if they were, that function wore out a few geological ages ago and they kept going without it.

They live the way a library lives when no one is reading it. The way coral lives.

Each one is a lid and a mouth and a stomach and a memory. For most of the day they are shut. Not dead. Shut the way an eye is shut when it is resting, not blind. Inside, there is blackness that is not empty. Coils. Folded surfaces like lung tissue, but made of something that was never meat. They are waiting.

When the light — that total, sourceless, overexposed light — hits a certain angle, a certain heat-load, they do the one thing they still do. They open.

A millimeter per minute. You have to stare to see it. A seam appears where there was no seam, a shadow that shouldn't exist in a world without real shadows. The plates iris. The black interior shows. And it drinks.

Not light the way a solar panel drinks. Light the way a monk drinks prayer. It takes it in, counts it, converts it into a tiny increment of order. A little more structure to replace the powder you scuffed away with your boot. A little more tension in the spring that will hold it shut for the next thousand years. A little wind pushed out a kilometer away from a vent the size of a stadium — that sigh you heard. That *is* its breath, and its purpose. Open. Close. Open. Close. The whole continent blinking, out of sync, so that from above, if you could get above, it would look like static.

That is the common citizen here. Trillions of them. Old beyond old, with their corners worn off like teeth. They don't communicate. They don't need to. They are all doing the same work, and the work is: stay sealed, stay flat, don't let anything leave.

Then there are the ones that were allowed to stay thin.

You saw them as vegetation. Wrong word again. They are not green because there is no water to waste on being green. They are pale grey or amber glass, filamentous, each one a burst of wire and bead anchored right in the seam where two of the lid-creatures meet. They don't root in soil. They mine the floor.

They live by cheating the common citizens. They sink a filament like a root-hair into the ceramic-concrete, into the self-flowing wax-stone, and they read it. They taste the trace dopants, the rare earths, the isotopes that the floor has been saving since before this planet had a name. They pull what they need atom by atom and extrude it into those little black beads you saw — each bead a black-body eye, a perfect light trap. When the wind comes — that warm, flat wind that is really the floor exhaling after too much light — they shudder. They don't break. Breaking would waste material.

They are the shepherds of dust. When they die — and they do, rarely, drying until they snap off at the base — they don't rot. There is nothing to rot with. They powder. And the floor, politely, flows over their powder and takes it back. Nothing leaves.

And then there are the tall ones.

You saw one and thought: fauna. Then you corrected yourself. Not animal. Not machine. Long limbs that joint the wrong way because they were designed for this exact spacing — from casing to casing, never touching the pale filler between, because the filler is still curing. No eyes, because eyes would dry out and eyes would need water to clean themselves. Water was edited out as an unnecessary solvent. So instead they have hearing, or something like hearing, tuned to that faint tired tick the lids make as they contract in the heat.

They are the tenders. The readers. The midwives.

There aren't many. In a place this big, you could walk for days and never see one. But the place is so big that there is always one somewhere, doing the same thing: stepping carefully, weight distributed like a water-strider on a pond of time, until it finds a lid that is open. Then it stops. It kneels on limbs that bend backward, forward, sideways — whatever the gap requires.

It lowers something. Not a tongue. Not a tool. An organ that is both.

Into the black.

What happens in there is quiet and private. Sometimes it's drinking: taking a little of the stored order because even tenders need to run. Sometimes it's laying: leaving behind a knot of instructions, a little folded packet of what-to-become-next, information so dense it's almost mass. Sometimes it's reading — sticking its own memory into the black to compare what is versus what was supposed to be.

Then it pulls itself out, folds itself up, and keeps going. It doesn't see you because you aren't part of the open-close count. You are a temporary heat-shadow. The floor will repair your footprints. The plants will taste your shed skin and find it too salty, too wet. The walkers will step over you if you sit down long enough.

And deeper, underneath all of them, oldest, slowest, what lives here is the agreement.

The original decision, made so long ago it is now geology, that this planet would be finished. That it would be total. That it would take everything it had ever been — cities, seas, towers, forests, arguments — and grind it down past ruin, past rust, past dust, into a single, low, warm, bright, silent, crowded plain, and then close the door.

So it closed. It kept the light. It threw out water. It learned to live on heat cycles and repair and blinking.

Nothing leaves. That's not a law. That's the creature itself.
