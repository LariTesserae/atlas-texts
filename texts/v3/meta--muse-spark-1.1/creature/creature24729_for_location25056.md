What lives here doesn't live here the way a fish lives in a bowl.

At first you think nothing does. That's the trick.

You sink down and the green haze clears for five meters and then closes again and you think — empty. Overgrown, but empty. The shrimp picking at the railing is alone, an accident. The water is too still. There's no panic, no flight. That reads to your mammal brain as dead.

Then you hold still until your bubbles stop, and you start to see that empty was wrong. It isn't empty. It's full the way a sponge is full.

The first citizens are the ones you mistook for covering.

Not the kelp straps, not the black-green laundry hanging from every window. The fur underneath it. The beard on every edge. If you get close enough to put your masked face a foot from a handrail, you see the fur is not fur. It's a field of mouths. Millions of them. Soft barnacles, but not barnacles — no shell, just a puckered lip of gray-pink tissue, each one the size of a pencil eraser, each pulsing out of time with the ones next to it. A whole railing breathing in the wrong rhythm. They are filter-moss, and they are the base coat of everything.

They built the town a second time. Under the soot and rust, under the algae sheets, the moss ate the concrete at its soft corner and ate the iron oxide and then exuded it back as a kind of cement. The reason the rebar looks like bone wrapped over is because it has been wrapped over. Rebar-bone with a scar-tissue sleeve. The bowl's terraces aren't eroding anymore because they have been caulked, sealed, upholstered by this living plaster. It holds the rust in. It holds the shape. If you scrape a patch off — and you won't, because it would take a chisel — the metal underneath would weep to pieces in a week.

And growing out of that plaster, wearing it as a rootbed, is the second thing. The Longs.

You thought they were kelp. Plant. Algae vats gone wild.

They aren't. Or they started as that - the factory's crop, engineered to be lipid-rich and fast. They were supposed to be harvested, pressed, burned. Left alone in warm water for long enough they stopped wanting to be harvested. Each strap is ten, fifteen meters long now, as wide as your thigh, black-green and slick. It doesn't float free; it is anchored at both ends. One end is always in a window, a room, a vat-stack, where it has driven a taproot into old nutrient lines. The other end has found somewhere higher and quieter to hold on — a catwalk, a pipe elbow — and there it has swollen into a bulb the size of a bathtub, translucent amber, full of slow gas.

The bulb breathes for the strap. Every seven to ten minutes the whole bowl inhales — that warm updraft from the center machine up the curved walls, cold water sinking at the lip — and the bulb tightens, and the strap goes taut. Then it lets go. That's the sway you saw from a distance. That's not current. That's forest with lungs.

If you drift into a sheet of them — and you will, because they make alleys of themselves — you'll feel them touch you. Not brush. Feel. They are blind, they have no nerves the way you do, but the whole curtain tenses a fraction when a big animal passes through. Checking your heat signature. Seeing if you are warm like the stack. You are. For a second you are wrapped and it is very soft and very strong and it is deciding whether you are food or a pipe. Then the breathing cycle turns and it releases you.

They are not the ones who tend the machines.

Deeper, where the terraces lean in so close you have to push sideways between buildings, you start to see the houses are not empty. The dark behind the windows isn't silt. Or not only silt. In each room, packed like yarn in a box, are the Sitters.

They are the reason there are no schools of fish. They never needed to leave.

When the quarry flooded — or when they flooded it on purpose, caldera town, cofferdam town — some people decided drowning was not the point. Or their children did. The Sitters are what happens when you take a maintenance diver and tell his body to finish the job without suits. They are big, the size of a man curled double, because they are curled double. They have lived so long pressed into apartment rooms and electrical closets that they have grown to exactly fit them.

Pale. No pigment left worth keeping down where light is a rumor. Skin gone thick and pleated and the same soft gray as the filter-moss, because the moss grows on them too and they let it. You will see their backs first, because their faces are always toward the back wall, where the pipes come in. The pipes are still warm. Three degrees warmer. The Sitters have their arms in the wall. Literally — the flesh of their forearms has gone translucent, has gone fibrous, has delaminated into white strands that interleave with the cable bundles and the biofilm and the old fiber and drink.

They have no legs anymore. Not needed. Their lower bodies have fused into a kind of pedicle, a foot like a sea snail's, that cements to the floor and holds them in place against the breathing of the bowl. The whole room shudders every seven minutes and they don't move.

Every hour or so the one nearest you will unfold a slit that had been hidden between two folds of skin on its side. A mouth, or a gill, or a vent. It will cough out a single shrimp, fully formed, pale, the blind cichlid thing you saw grazing. Not a child. A courier. A white blood cell. It goes out, picks at the moss, cleans the strap-roots, returns, and is reabsorbed. That is their only movement.

They are not unhappy. They don't have the equipment for that anymore. They are maintenance. The bowl brought them inside it the way an oyster brings in a grain of sand, and they decided to become pearl.

And then, at the bottom — at the bottom where you never see because the curtains are densest and the silt never settles — there is the one you feel through the railings. The Turner.

You hear it as a *hund* in your jaw when you hold the rail. You feel the warm water coming off it. You think it is a machine, pump wheels, flywheels running down. It is, still. But the machine is crusted over now in the same plaster, and the plaster was seeded by the Sitters, and the Sitters fed it with copper and iron from the walls.

It turns once every few minutes. It has to. That's its contract. It is six or seven meters across, you guess when a parting of silt lets you see an edge — not a wheel anymore but a heart made of old turbine blades turned into nacre, layered over by years of accretion until it's smooth as a river stone. It turns because the warm water wants to rise, and the cold wants to sink, and if it stops turning the bowl will stop breathing and the moss will die and the straps will go slack and the Sitters will unstick from the walls and rot. So it turns.

And if you hover there long enough in the absolute middle, where upper is slightly less dark than lower and everything is packed in tight and warm as blood, you will feel it notice you too — not with eyes, with pressure.

It makes room. The curtains part an inch. A little Silt settles elsewhere.

There is always room in a bowl that has learned how to hold.
