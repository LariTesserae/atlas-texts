What lives here doesn't live *in* here.

That's the first correction you have to make with your hands. You come up expecting an inhabitant and a habitat, two separate things — a burrow and a fox. Here there is only one thing, and it failed — or refused — to become two.

What lives here is the table itself.

Not as metaphor. Not as "the forest is alive." It is a body, but a body that never agreed on where one organ stops and another starts. It is a colonial failure in the most successful possible way.

If you want to count individuals — and it invites counting, because your mind wants to — you start with the engines.

The old spruce and larch at the bottom of the mass. They are not trees anymore the way a retired draft horse hitched to a mill is not a horse anymore. They got too cold to die and too wet to stop. The frost forced them to keep their sap liquid when it should have gone solid, and so they learned to pump it supercooled, under pressure you can feel as heat if you put your palm close. They swell. They hold for five, ten seconds, wood screaming without air to carry it. They contract. When they do, white breath jets out of lenticels in their bark and rains down as glass. 

Those are the hearts. There are maybe forty of them on the whole plateau, or four hundred — you can't tell because the moss has swallowed most of them to the crown. They don't communicate. They just keep time for whatever is near them. Everything within a thirty yard radius organizes around that piston beat.

Around each heart lives the Belt.

You saw it and thought it was moss growing fast. It's more accurate to say it IS fast growth, wearing moss as a coat. Sphagnum magellanicum and its cousins have dropped the pretense of being a plant that stays put. At the leading edge of a sheet it lays down new capitula so quickly you can watch the green fizz forward, an inch an hour, cell division audible as that low soda-fizz if you finally get your ears to filter out your own blood. At the trailing edge it is already black, dead, being squeezed back into peat by the weight of itself. It moves like a living conveyor, and what it carries is itself. It carries water to the hearts, carries tannin slurry to the pits, carries spores and discarded leaves and fragments of lichen that didn't make it.

The belts are tended by what you took for willow roots.

Dwarf willow, Salix arctica, normally a shy creep. Here it has become all tendon. It doesn't grow for light. It grows for tension. It braids itself into cables as thick as your wrist, pale and steaming in the cold, and string between hearts. When one heart swells, the cable tightens. That tightness drags a hummock three feet to the left. That drag wrings out a sponge of peat, which floods a pit, which boils. The willow is the transmission. It is not thinking. It is transferring work. If you cut one — don't — it snaps back and bleeds clear sap for a day and the whole local rhythm stutters.

Then there are the pits. 

Every 20 or 30 feet the mat dips and you find a place with no tree, no cable, just moss and lichen and liverwort going insane. This is where the plateau actually lives. A womb and a stomach and a compost heap run at 200x speed. In the morning it is a dimple filled with brown water. By noon the moss around the lip has knotted itself into a raised collar and inside, fronds are knitting and unknitting, spearing up, collapsing, dissolving into soup and re-knitting as something else. It will throw up a hummock the size of a dead bear in an afternoon, all loose peat and bright green points. By evening that same hummock sighs and falls back in on itself, unwinds like a sweater pulled from the bottom. Nothing was wasted. Nothing was kept. 

Those pits are the only place it reproduces, if that word still works. It makes *attempts*. A sprig that can hold a shape for a few hours longer than the others. A lichen arrangement that resists being felted over. Most fail and are reclaimed before they freeze. Some hold. Those are the ridges and hummocks you tripped on getting up here.

And then there are the eyes. The round, dark pothole lakes that never freeze though they should. Those are kidneys. Or drains. Tannin water sits in them and below them, deep in the peat where you can't see, bacteria that have never seen sun are doing the only quiet work on the plateau: methanogenesis. Warming the water one degree. Keeping it liquid. Holding the whole system just that half-degree shy of locking solid. They exhale, and that exhale is what makes the water bead on every leaf at 4am.

If you insist on animals — because you are an animal — there is only one that counted as resident and not as mistake: the ptarmigan. Not a flock. Not even a pair most years. One white bird, maybe two, that have learned to be still enough that the mat includes them. They nest on top of the belts and let the feather-moss felt over the edge of their nest until they are a white lump in green. The mass tolerates them because their guano is nitrogen and because they eat the few craneflies that would otherwise eat the growing tips. They are not inhabitants. They are a permitted parasite, holding very still.

Everything else — your mites, your springtails, your tardigrades by the billions in every sponge-squeeze of sphagnum — are not inhabitants either. They are the plateau's internal gut flora. They ride the belts and drown in the pits and hatch again.

The reason you feel watched is not because something is looking at you. It's because something is *working* on you. When you stand long enough for the feather-moss to start its soft, mindless felting over your boots, you have been registered not as stranger but as pause. As texture. As a place where condensation can gather a little differently.

The plateau has been here since the ice let go of the basalt. It does not get bigger. It does not want to. For ten thousand years its entire project has been to perfect the art of staying exactly this size while never, for a single minute, staying the same. It lives by being a negotiation that never settles: make and unmake in the same breath, pump and leak, lift and drop. 

You came looking for what lives there, expecting a shape to step out of the fog. 

It already has. It's under your feet, it's hissing through your soles, it's fizzing just below hearing, trying to figure out what to do with you before you freeze.
