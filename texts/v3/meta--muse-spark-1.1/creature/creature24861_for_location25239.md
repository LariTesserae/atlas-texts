They live low. Not short, exactly - if one stood up straight out on the open plateau they'd be tall - but here no one ever stands up straight.

You see one for the first time between two towers, and you think at first it is another tower. Same felted rust-green, same hunched cap. Then it moves, and moves wrong for a plant.

The people of the cup are soaked people.

Everything they wear is alive, or was alive an hour ago and hasn't realized it yet. Long cloaks and cowls carded and felted from the moss-fiber itself - you pull the dead underfluff from the trunk of a tower and beat it and roll it until it mats, and it never really dies, it just holds three times its weight in water and keeps holding it. So every person carries their own small weather around them. Ten, fifteen pounds of cold water on their shoulders at all times. That is why they hunch. That is why their steps are placed, toes first, flat stones only. Children learn before speech: do not slip. Do not waste a dry hour.

Under the cowl, they are pale in a way that has nothing to do with sun. The light in the cup never gives a tan. Their skin is the white-green of the underside of things, translucent at the temples, at the wrists. You can see the blue of blood close to the surface, working hard in the thin air. Their hair grows thin and lank and is usually shaved, because hair holds mites that eat the moss-cloth. Their eyes are large, lidded, not quite nocturnal but made for this late-evening that never ends. They don't blink often. They have learned not to wipe water from their face; they let it run.

There are never more than thirty of them. At any given time, maybe twenty-two. You count.

It cannot hold more. Not because of food - the moths are protein that falls from the air if you hold out a hand long enough - but because of attention. This town requires attention at all times.

A person here is not a villager so much as a gardener for everything at once. Watch one through a day:

She rises before the white sun idea. She wrings out her sleeping cloak into the house cistern. She checks the tick. That new crack that opened in the night on the north wall - did it shift the drainage? She nudges a flat stone a thumb's width with her foot. She goes to her tower.

Each family - if you can call two or three people who share a roof and a set of cups a family - tends two or three moss-towers. Not owns. Tends. Because a tower will die if it gets too wet, or too dry, or if the Listeners beneath its cap get clogged, or if the browsers eat too much new growth. And if a tower dies, the house made from its cuttings dies, and the roof dies, and the moths that were to hatch that week don't.

So she climbs. Bare feet, toe-and-heel, on pegs of basalt driven into the felted trunk while leaving the living fibers intact. At the crown she parts the fronds. Underneath, in the dark pocket where the air never moves, five or six Listeners hang.

They are the real citizens here.

In daylight they look like nothing. Failed fruit. Wet pouches of skin, fist-sized, with no face, no opening you can see until they decide to open. Veins of faint, foxfire light run over them when they are thirsty. You can hear them, if you get close enough, over the moth-hum: a very faint fizz, like soda water. They are pulling cloud out of the air. Condensing it on their own cold skin, swelling. When the fog rolls over the rim - which it does every forty minutes or so, regular as breath - they tighten like hearts and go opaque.

And then they piss.

It is reverent, somehow, despite the word. They purse a seam at the bottom that wasn't there before and release a single drop, perfectly clear, fat as a knuckle, that falls exactly onto the growth crown of the tower. Drop. Drop. Drop. From six pouches. Enough to keep a tree alive on bare rock.

The woman checks each one. She lifts them, one by one, and looks at their underside for the black mold that kills them. She wipes it away with a thumb. She takes a small bone cup and holds it under one and milks it - a gentle squeeze - and drinks. Everyone drinks this. It is cleaner than the eleven-minute rain, which carries grit from the rim. The Listeners' water tastes of nothing at all and for a second her eyes close. She hangs the Listener back. It reattaches with tiny hooked hairs.

She doesn't speak. No one speaks above a whisper in the bowl because the bowl does not give it back. The towers, the hides, the moths swallow all high sound. If you shout, it goes flat in ten feet. So the people developed other ways. A double-click of the tongue against the teeth: *The north drain is blocked.* A palm pressed to basalt and held: *Be still, the rock is moving.* The closest thing to a loud noise is when they all gather in the plaza around the hollowed mother tower at the center. They press their backs to its soft inner wall and hum, low, chest-voice, at the resonant frequency of the cup itself, and they feel whether it has changed.

Below her, the rest of the town works.

An old man in a doorway sits doing nothing, but his doing nothing is precise. His house is also the nursery for the glass-legged beetles. They are not pests. They are not pets. They are the street cleaners. Two feet long, leg-span wide, with bodies of polished black chitin and legs so long and thin they look like they should shatter; they pick their way across the black gravel between towers with infinite care, each leg joint bending backward, and they eat only dead things. Dead moths, dead moss scraps, dead skin. Without them the gravel would be ankle-deep in wing dust in a week. The old man watches a female beetle lay a clutch of opalescent eggs in a crevice he chiseled yesterday to be exactly beetle-egg sized.

A pair of teenagers share a cistern, lying belly-down on its edge, faces inches from the water. They are not playing. They are counting newts. The newts are completely transparent except for their gills, which are external and feathered and red with blood, waving like small ferns. You have to count them because they disappear if the pH shifts half a point. They are the water gauge. The teenagers click.

And everywhere, the moths.

You cannot understand the people until you understand that they inhale moths. Not by choice. The air is moth. They hatch constantly from the rust-red caps of the towers - first as fat, pale grubs that chew neat round holes, then as pupae that hang like bells, then all at once a white-green unsilencing of wings. Palm-wide, dust-wide, all silent because their wings are not scaled like normal moths but felted, like the people's cloaks, to kill sound. They live for a day. They blunder into faces, into cups, into fires that never existed. The people breathe them, eat a handful of them raw when hands are too cold to weave, sweep them into cakes and dry them on the smoke racks that have never seen smoke, only air. The ground between towers is supposed to be bare basalt, but it is never bare, it is a moving carpet that re-settles every hour.

And on the towers, their shepherds.

The Browsers. You missed them the first time because you thought they were swellings in the tower itself. Six limbs, each as thick as your arm but so slow you can watch a minute hand move faster. Their backs are not backs; they are gardens. The same moss that makes the towers grows in their fur, intentional. The people seed it as pups. So a Browser looks like a small, moving hillock of moss with a quiet, eyeless face underneath, with a mouth of soft plates that scrape, never bite, that prunes. They traverse a tower over a week, eating the overgrowth that would otherwise capsize it, excreting a fine black compost that the people collect with bone spoons and pack around infant moss columns. When a Browser dies - and they live for decades - the people do not eat it. They carry it to the rim and lay it down and let the wind take its moss.

That is the shape of it, then. Nothing touches, the description said, but that isn't true. Everything touches through everything else.

The Listeners water the towers. The towers feed the moths and the Browsers and the people. The moths feed the beetles and the newts and the people. The Browsers keep the towers alive. The beetles clean up after the moths. The people tend the towers and the Listeners and the beetles and the newts and the Browsers and each other, and they do it with hands that have become wide, webbed slightly from always being damp, fingertips broad and split from picking.

They are not trapped. Twice a year, when the cloud roof lifts for a day, a few of them walk up the columnar steps of the bowl, out onto the plateau, and look at the huge, empty world. They do not go far. The air out there is too sharp, too loud. They come back down into the cup where sound is kind.

At night - the gray gets a little grayer - they go into the mother tower in the plaza. Twelve people will fit if they sit knee to knee. They do not light fire. They listen to the tick of the basalt cooling and settling, to the fizz of the Listeners, to the brush of moth wings against bone-and-hide roofs, to each other's wet breathing.

They have no word for city. They have a word for bowl that also means *held*.
