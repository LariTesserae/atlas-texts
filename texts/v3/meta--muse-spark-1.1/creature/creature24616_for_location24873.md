Nothing lives here. That is the first thing you learn, and it's true.

No animal lives here. No eyes open in the dark, no gills pump, no heart beats fast enough to make a sound loud enough to carry. If you put your hand out, nothing flees from it. For a long time you think that is the end of the answer. The forest is not an animal. It grows, but it does not live the way a fish lives. It does not want.

Then you stay longer.

You are down on the pavement — because to be here at all you have to be down, kneeling or prone or walking with your hands out in front of you, pushing fronds aside — and you realize the forest has a density gradient that is not random. In a place with no light, with no up or down current worth mentioning, with stone so even, a kelp that mines should be equally thick everywhere. It isn't. There are avenues. There are places where it is thinner. Where the black felt of old fronds has been peeled back to the grout-hard silt, and the silt scraped back to show stone.

And the stone beneath is not the worn dome of a hundred thousand years. It's a little sharper. The silt between two blocks there is not yet stone again. It gives a little when you press it.

Something has been weeding.

You wait. Waiting is the only work this place permits. The cold makes your blood slow even in imagination. The current takes a century to exhale. You wait on a terrace edge with your back against the old wall — you can feel how it holds a little of the nothing-heat that the deep water steals from everything — fronds brushing your shoulders every time the slow sigh moves north to south.

After a long time you feel, not see, not hear, a change in how the forest hangs.

Some of the fronds that were brushing you stop brushing you. They are lifted. Gently, the way you would lift hair off a sleeping person's face.

They do not live here in numbers. The continent is too large and they are too slow to ever be numerous. But they live here in continuity. One, then a long empty of kilometers where there is only forest, then another.

It is about as tall as you are when it stands, which it rarely does. Mostly it goes on all fours and then on sixes, because it has the extra set — not arms, not legs, low at the back, braced. Its back is broad and flat and pale, the color of the stone when you scrape the black felt off, which is to say not white, but a wet, deep grey-pink. That flatness is crusted with silt and a living film of young forest. The stalks actually take root in it. It carries a little garden on its own back, so that when it goes still among the walls you would brush it aside thinking it was just another tangle.

It has no eyes. There was never a reason for eyes. Where its face would be is a smooth, down-curved plate, like the top stone of these walls, worn round, with two long, shuttered slits that taste the water for iron, for lime, for the sour of the forest's acid. Its mouth is low and wide and without teeth.

And it has hands. That is the thing that stops your own breath, if you were breathing. The hands are unmistakable. Four fingers and a thumb. Short, strong, the knuckles thickened. A person's hands, but bloodless and enlarged a little by cold and pressure, the nails soft and worn to nothing. The left and right are mirrored exactly — you can see the hand in the wall because these are the hands that made the wall. They have not changed.

It goes along the terrace, feeling ahead. When it finds a holdfast that has bitten too deep, that has cracked a paving stone through, it gets down close. It makes a sound that is not a sound — a small click in its own chest cavity that travels through stone better than through water. The forest around it shivers. The frond lets go, just a little. Chemistry negotiating with chemistry.

Then it begins to work. It does not pull. It scrapes. With a small, flat piece of that same stone it carries tucked in a fold of skin at its waist — every one of them carries one — it scrapes the black acid and the thread-roots out of the joint, patiently, millimeter by millimeter. It scoops out the softened stone dust and puts it in its mouth. Not to eat. To carry. Later, further along, where a joint has widened a finger's width too far, it will spit that paste back in, mixed with its own lime, and smooth it with its thumbs the way a mason points mortar.

Where the wall has slumped, it lifts. This is what the low walls are for. They are not property lines. They are not defenses. They are work at waist height, so a lone keeper can get its shoulder under and lever. A stone a single person or two people can lift. It lifts and resets and settles. The line wobbles, because the hand wobbles.

They are not repairing the city. They don't remember city. They don't remember that hands once built this to farm or to pray or to live in. There is no memory here except muscle memory, except chemical memory. The forest tries to eat the built world. The keepers try to keep the built world flat and fitted and even, because the flatness is what lets the forest grow in a way they can harvest. They peel the oldest felted fronds off the pavement — the ones that have already become ground — and roll them and tuck them under their bellies, where another mouth, a softer one, slowly dissolves them for minerals. It is not symbiosis. It is a long, slow argument that has reached equilibrium.

When it passes you — because you are sitting very still on its processional way and at some point it has to pass — it does not notice you as animal. If you put out your hand, it will touch your fingers with its fingers, taste the salt and iron on you, decide you are not stone that needs pointing, not forest that needs weeding, and go on. Its skin is as cold as the water and almost as thick as water. It leaves a faint sourness in the cold where its passage has stirred it.

And then it is gone, its back garden blending back into gardens. The forest swings shut behind it. The quiet that is physical returns.

There are not many. There may not have ever been many. But the place is so large, and they live so long — a lifetime is the time it takes the kelp to grow eight or ten meters — that one has always been finishing a wall started by another who finished a wall started by another, around the slow curve of this world without sky.
