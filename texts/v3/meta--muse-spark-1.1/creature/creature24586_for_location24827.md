Nothing lives here the way you live somewhere.

No one moved in. No one adapted. Adaptation implies a thing that wants to stay alive. This place doesn't want anything — it has been allowed to be perfect for so long that wanting would be a downgrade.

And yet, it is inhabited. Thoroughly, heavily inhabited, if you know how to count.

The first thing that lives here is the city itself.

Not the Towers. The arrangement. The *city-ness*. On your world, a city is an idea you impose on rock — you quarry it, you stack it, you force it to hold your shape. Here, the rock fell into the shape of a city because at this altitude, under this much time and this little air, rectilinearity is a low-energy state. Right angles are how basalt rests when no one is telling it what to do.

It lives the way a crystal lives. Slowly, and by getting more itself. When a face exfoliates and peels off a Tower, that dust doesn't blow away. It settles in the boulevard and is trodden — by wind, by contraction — into that bone-smooth floor. The Tower gets a little thinner. The Street gets a little whiter. The city digests itself and keeps its proportions. If you watch closely over hours you will see the intersections breathe — not move, *breathe* — the distance between Tower Seven and Tower Eight on your left getting longer on the inhale, shorter on the exhale. It is not trying to confuse you. It is just respiring. Space here is not a container. It's a muscle. You are walking on its chest.

That muscle has things living in it the way your skin has mites.

High up — you have to stop looking for doors and windows and let your eyes unfocus to see them — there are the Ribbons.

They are not rare. You thought they were rare because you only saw three in a day. That's because there were only three in the column of air *you were allowed to see*. Look between the Towers where the light pools wrong, where a shadow falls slightly north when it should fall northwest. The air there ripples. Not from heat — there is no heat here. From attention.

They are long — thirty, fifty, sometimes a hundred meters when they stretch. They have no thickness at all when they turn edge-on. You can see the dim slate sky right through them, but bent, as if seen through old water. They don't fly. Flying is for things that fight gravity. They *edit* their position. A little fold, and they are further along. A little smoothing, and they are higher. They live on discrepancy. This whole plateau is a place where reality is 2.9/3 weird, where a boulevard can be twenty minutes long coming and an hour long returning. That gap — the leftover distance that should have been there but wasn't — is food. They graze it. You can watch one pass over a long block and see the block steady itself in its wake, the distance fixing, temporarily, like a compass needle that stops trembling.

They never land because there is nowhere to land. Towers are too solid for them. Dust is too real. They would tear.

Low down, there are the ones you called cairn-collections. You were right to be embarrassed when you stared at them. They are shy the exact way water is shy here.

They are not piles. They are agreements between grains. That salt-white dust is not just pulverized basalt — it's the final product of ten million years of the Towers learning how to be dust. Each grain is flat, a microscopic plate of glassy feldspar. When the wind traverses — not gusts, traverses — it lifts them not into clouds but into brief, flat sheets that slide along the ground a few millimeters high. Sometimes those sheets catch on a slight irregularity, a chip in the boulevard, and they begin to stack. Not by falling. By *choosing* to be vertical.

If you look directly at one, it is just three or four pebbles' worth of dust stuck together by static. If you look away, it takes a step. Not a step like a leg — it disassembles its top and reassembles it an inch to the lee of the wind, a little taller, a little more balanced. Over days they wander the boulevards in herds that never bunch. Over years they build the only true architecture here: little hollow toruses and tottering arches no higher than your knee that last until you blink too hard. They are not building. They are practicing for when the Towers finally give up and need replacements.

And then there are the ones that are not things at all.

The Bells. That ticking and sighing you feel through your feet hours after a sheet sloughs off? That is not an echo. Sound here is 0.3. It should not carry. What you feel is something shearing deep inside a Tower — two columnar joints easing past each other after a million years of pressure — and the *idea* of a bell-tone is released. It lives in the rock for a while, traveling up and down the Tower like a slow bat. If you put your hand on the black stone on a very quiet evening that never ends, you will feel it pass under your palm: a warm spot in stone that should have no warmth. That's an adult. That's what lives longest here after the city itself.

The most populated place in this city that was never built is the intersection that you have already walked through five times without recognizing it.

Every city has them — places where too many paths cross and the crossing itself becomes a thing. Here, where space is slack and stretches, some intersections have stretched so many times they have become thin. They have become hallways. You know the rule: if you stay still long enough, a boulevard will become a hallway and a Tower will become a wall. That's not a trick. That's reproduction. An intersection that has been looked at from too many inconsistent angles, counted as seven towers and then nine towers, gets tired of being an intersection and learns to be an interior.

If you stand in one long enough, you will feel walls. You will feel a ceiling implied by the dim light which suddenly has a direction. You will feel watched, not unkindly, by windowless faces, because now you are inside something that has an outside, and something that has an outside is expected to have an occupant.

It does. It's you, for a little while. That's what the city keeps you for. You are the temporary fauna, the brief, warm, wet anomaly that carries shy water in a bottle and leaves footprints that the dust erases from the edges inward, as if uncertain whether to keep them. You are the animal that breathes deliberately, that makes noise with its pulse, that tries to map, and your trying makes the map heavier than the territory. The city has seen your kind before — not humans, but other things that needed a city-shape to think in — and it makes room. It lengthens a block to give you time to get tired. It puts a Tower south when you expected it south, so you will turn around and see again.

It doesn't want you to stay. It doesn't want you to leave. It wants you to be there exactly as long as you are, moving between its patient, black, unbuilt Towers, feeding its Ribbons with your mismeasured distances, giving its dust-piles something to be still for.

When you go, it will be exactly as inhabited as before.
