Nothing lives here the way a forest lives. Nothing insists.

At first you think the room is empty except for water trying to be something else. That is the first inhabitant, and the oldest.

It lives in the stone.

Not *on* the stone. The rock here hasn't sealed yet. It's still full of connected bubbles, a black sponge squeezed almost shut when it cooled. The mountain's deep heat is still in it, low and patient, and it pushes water that has never seen sky up through those pores one bead at a time. The water is the true body in the room. It is not a pool, it is a skin. A thousand swollen eyes open on every nub and close somewhere else, handing themselves from tip to tip, never falling far enough to make a sound. It breathes against you. It keeps the air at blood-heat because it cannot afford to get cold. Cold would mean snow, and snow would mean outside, and this place has never been outside. It has been in perfect trade with itself for so long that it has become an animal: inhaling through basalt, exhaling as mist, circulating through a thousand tiny mouths. If you hold still long enough, you feel it condense on your eyelashes and run down into your own mouth. You become part of the loop for a minute.

And where there is a loop, there are mouths that learn to live on the waste of it.

The second ones live in the overflow.

Where each hand-pecked basin was chipped out of a blister and then worn smooth by other hands, the warm water sits a fraction too full. Always. It never spills, it *sweats* over the rim, a sheet thinner than paper, and in that constant thinness something white has rooted.

They are not moss. Moss wants light with a desperation this place has never been taught. These are rootlets that gave up on leaves entirely. Each strand is as fine as a hair from your head and as clear as if it were made of the water itself, only a little whiter where it has sucked dissolved silica and calcite from the seep. They hang in little beards from cracks, from the lips of the basins, from the underside of the nub-stalactites. They don't grow up or down, they grow toward warmth. Toward the wetter bead. If you bring your wet hand close, you can feel them shift in air too still to move anything else — a slow lean, over minutes. They are all one thing. If you could trace them back into the porous glass behind them, you'd find they connect — a single mycelial net, or something older than fungus and plant both, that has spread through the entire pocket, living off the mineral sigh and the heat and nothing else. It does not rot. It does not spread aggressively. In a place where nothing is allowed to change, it learned to live by almost not living. Pale fur that trembles only when a drop takes too long to fall.

And then — in the basins themselves — what they came to drink.

You have to give it time. You have to lower one hand into a basin — the water is so warm your nerves don't at first know it is water, only that the air has gotten thicker — and you have to be still until your own pulse stops feeling like an intrusion. Three minutes. Five. The blood in your ears quiets.

Then it moves against your palm.

The size of the last joint of your thumb. Flat as a wet leaf left in a pocket. Blind. Not eyeless as if it lost eyes, but eyeless as if eyes were never an idea here. Its back is the exact translucent amber of the basalt water, so you see its gut as a darker thread inside it, full of white. It has legs — too many — not spiny or quick like a centipede's, but fringed, soft, slow, each one paddling with a deliberation that makes no ripple. Isopod, syncarid, something that belongs a mile down in cave water, but stranded up here when the mountain pushed this lung to the surface and then left it here, warm. It doesn't flee. Fleeing is a response to light and to predators, and there have never been either.

There are never many. One per basin, sometimes two. They graze. They walk their endless circuit around the smooth pecked rim, scraping with a mouth you can't see the veil of bacteria and mineral that the water leaves, and the white rootlets that dip in. When they meet your finger, they step over it. They have no urgency. Their metabolism is tuned to the tick… tick… tick of drops that take a minute to fall. They can live a long time on a single bead.

When one dies — rarely — it doesn't float. It settles to the bottom of its basin and the pale fur finds it in an hour and unwraps it thread by thread, so quietly you would not see it happen. The obsidian flake left beside the basin still has a blood scale on its edge from the last time someone cut themselves here, and that, too, is gone by morning, taken by the water and the threads.

That is why people kept coming. Not the water itself — though in late August when the snowfields had gone to gray ribs and the streams were stones, warm water that never failed and never froze was a kind of miracle. But the feeling that something here would let you put your hand in and be stepped over, not bitten. That something would live off your leaving.

No one lives here now in the way humans mean living. The mat of alpine grass still holds the press of fingers. The basins still hold the shape of cupped hands. But the residents — the water that breathes, the white net that leans, the flat slow ones who tend each basin like monks — they have resumed their agreement: to stay embryonic. To crowd together, each beginning almost touching the next, and to agree, collectively, never to begin.
