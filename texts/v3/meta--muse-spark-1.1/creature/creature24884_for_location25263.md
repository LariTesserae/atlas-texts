Nothing lives here. That's what it tells you first.

And it's almost convincing. No tracks in the silt-fine mud. No nests in the bromeliads, though each one is a perfect, undisturbed cistern. No gnaw marks, no droppings, no feathers caught on the rebar. The leaves are untorn edge to edge, because nothing has ever taken a bite.

That is the first inhabitant: an absence, kept. It is maintained. The doors — those swollen, moss-lipped iron doors on the engine houses that have sagged into their frames — will close on their own when something warm tries to enter. Not slam. Sigh shut. It remembers animals. It tried that once.

What lives here lives instead of animals.

It lives as a city lives, which is to say not one thing but a density of agreements so thick it becomes an organism.

At the bottom, in the black warm water that has opinions about being land, lives **the Blood.**

Not the water itself, but the habit of the water. It has learned a loop. It rises because it is told to rise. It is pulled up by the screw-pumps the size of houses, pushed into upper cisterns that weep it back out through a thousand cracks in the terracing. It falls on tin, on leaf, on open water, on iron, and collects again. It is amber at the top where it's strained through moss-gaskets, black at the bottom where it rests against the silt. It never leaves. The pressure holds — 30 PSI, always, the needles trembling but never wandering — because the Blood does not want to be low, does not want to be high. Somewhere far below the quivering mat of root and pipe and basalt silt, something turns so slowly you think it has stopped. That's its heart beating. It has been beating like that for a long time.

Tangled into the Blood, holding it, held by it, lives **the Muscle.**

The pumps, the boilers gone green with verdigris, the ten-foot flywheels that move a different spoke every time you blink. They were built to be separate machines and they aren't anymore. A belt drive slack and shining with algal film jumps two buildings. A driveshaft runs through a brick wall because a fig root moved it six inches and the building came with it. The switchboards stand open to the damp, their ceramic fuses furred with copper crystals, and they buzz — that low 60-cycle hum that lives in your molars — not because someone is powering them, but because they are talking to each other.

These are not haunted machines. Nothing died in them. They just never got the signal to stop. They push instead of hammering. They have learned to be patient with wetness, with rust that is now structural, with moss that makes a better gasket than rubber ever did. If you put your hand on a flywheel housing, you can feel it not as vibration, but as effort being rationed. Nothing is idle, but nothing hurries. That's the second rule posted without being written.

Living on the Muscle, and inside it, and as it, lives **the Skin.**

This is the plant-only that the copper and the oil and the old air allowed to stay. Not jungle, not garden — a structural partner.

The black-green fern that likes heat and iron, growing out of every bolt-hole, splitting concrete with a patience that matches the flywheels. Its roots don't seek soil. They seek seep.

The moss, eight inches thick on the engineer's balcony floor, making seals around doors, around valve stems, around light fixtures. If a joint begins to spray instead of weep, the moss thickens there.

The philodendron and strangler fig up at the rim that met in the middle and became a roof, that decides how much sun gets to be columns and how much has to be amber worklight and foxfire. It rations the light because light is rationed.

The bathtub bromeliads in the elbows of pipes. Each one a complete pond with no animals in it. The water in their cups is so still it holds a perfect reflection of the caged bulbs above, and nothing drinks it.

The Skin does not grow *in* the town. It *is* the grout. It keeps the town from being rubble. Concrete weathers to look like limestone. Limestone weathers to look like concrete. The green covers the seam where they meet and decides they were always the same thing. The erosion here is finished because the Skin finishes it. A walkway an inch lower after spring — the Skin fills the new crack before the next drip.

And presiding, because something must remember the shape of the room, lives **the Weather.**

Not a creature. A private climate that was born when the sink got deep enough to have its own exhale.

It lives in the top two feet of air and the bottom two inches of condensate. The warm of the lowlands, the wet-lung 80-degree saturated air, meets the cold iron that is always cold because it is always wet. Water condenses on the undersides of every pipe and falls again as rain. Sound columns come down through the holes in the fig thatch full of swimming particulate and break on the steam that sighs from a valve no bigger than your thumb.

The Weather is what you hear: the uncountable drips, the sigh, the hum, the groan of metal that has been wet for a hundred years and will be wet for a hundred more. It carries no birdsong, no buzz. That is the strange quiet under the loud — a jungle with no throat.

Put it all together and you get the fifth inhabitant, the one you actually meet when you stand at the lip of wet grass and look down.

The city-block that decided to become a city anyway.

It doesn't walk. It settles. It doesn't eat. It circulates. It doesn't need you to believe in it because it already holds itself up — root holding pipe holding wall holding root. If you were to climb down the stairs that are more like creek beds now, it would let you, because you are briefly heavy water, briefly pressure. You would feel its attention not as eyes but as that quiver under the turning of something far below, as all the gauges briefly agreeing on you, too: 30 PSI, steady.

Stay too long and the doors will notice you are warm and have a heartbeat that is not 60-cycle, and they will sigh shut, not unkindly. The Skin will soften under your boots. The amber worklights will keep burning. The black water will keep having opinions about being land.

It's not empty down there. It's full. It's just full of only itself.
