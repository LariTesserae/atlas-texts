No one lives here the way a village lives in a place.

At first you think nothing at all does. That's the trick of this kind of lush — not jungle-thick but curated. You look across the sixty steps of the bowl, through that wet, empty light, and you count seven tree ferns and twenty-some giant rosettes and a whole plain of moss. No paths. No huts. No fire scar. You think the stone bowls on the rim are old. Abandoned.

They are not abandoned. They are in use right now.

You have to learn how to see something that measures time in thumbs-per-decade.

She is under the second fern on the left - if left means anything in a round room. She has been there since you arrived. Since before you arrived. She watched you climb out of the rain into the clear wet heat, watched you put your modern weight down and make the peat sigh. She did not move because moving is an interruption here.

When you finally see her, what you see is not an animal stepping into view. It is a place refusing to be a place anymore.

The peat under the fern has a mother.

She is low. She is wide. Not tall — if she stood, which she does rarely, she would come up to your sternum. Her feet are the reason she can live in a cup of sighing sponge. They are huge, bare, splayed like snowshoes, the toes broad and soft-edged. She puts them down and the bog does not swallow. She can walk on water that stands between roots, her weight cantilevered across moss.

Her skin is what you mistook for rock and then for lizard. It is basalt-colored when dry, which it almost never is, and green-black where it is wet, which is always. It pits and holds water exactly the way the rimstone does. Liverwort grows in the pits of her shoulders. Real liverwort, living. Sphagnum seams her forearms. On her back — you only see this when she finally leans forward to drink — the fur you thought the tree-ferns were wearing is also on her: epiphytes, small ferns, a whole hanging garden rooted in the thin soil of her own shoulders. She is seventy or two hundred years old. Lush means each thing is allowed to become completely itself, with space around it.

There is not one of her. There are five. Six. The same number as the bowls.

That is the second time you miscount. You thought there were seven tree ferns. There are seven, but six of them have a woman under them. The seventh is empty, its peat slightly greener, younger. Waiting.

They are not a species you have a name for. If you brought them down the mountain into warm mist and middle rain, a biologist would call them hominid and be wrong in all the ways a word can be wrong while being useful. Their heads are large and bald except for moss, their mouths wide and lipless, suited for sipping, not biting. Their eyes are set far apart and hooded with a clear second lid, because here it rains from below. They do not blink once an hour like the lizard. The lizard is copying them. When a drip hits their eye, the nictitating membrane slides across and the water beads and crawls off, just like on stone.

They are slow because the bowl is slow. You cannot make a living here by chasing. There is nothing to chase — no birds but the one high flycatcher that they leave alone, no insects but the midges born in rosette lakes. So they farm attention.

This is a tended place. That's the thing that didn't make sense about the spacing. In a lowland jungle, everything strangles. Here, each rosette sits alone, each fern has space for its drip circle, because every few days one of the women will get up — an event as slow and audible as peat sucking itself — and waddle on her big feet to a seedling that is too close to another and lift it, peat and all, and set it elsewhere. Or she will pluck a vine that tries to be exuberant. Or she will scrape a little more moss from one of the palm-scoops on the rim.

The river stones in those scoops. They do not belong to this peak. She carried them here.

Once a year, maybe less, one of the women leaves the cup. She goes down, below the inversion, through rain, through mist, into cold where water has urgency and rivers have mouths. She finds a river. She chooses a stone that has been round for a thousand years. She carries it back up on her back, up through the belts of air that get warmer as you climb, breathing that thin, close, kind sun. It takes days. Her feet bled the first time her grandmother did it. Now her feet do not bleed. She puts the new stone in the old scoop and takes the old stone and uses it — tappet-tappet — to deepen the cup into basalt that has already forgotten it was ever sharp. It is not building. It is weeping stone into shape, the way the cloud does. A primitive mind, you thought. No. A mind that has solved the problem.

The problem is this: the brown water that stands between roots is good for rosettes and tree-ferns and even for lizards, but it is tea made of millennia, tannin and peat. If you drink it, your gut will run and your knees that already feel the altitude will give. But the water that never really falls, that sweats directly from rock, that hisses into being mid-air where wind hits cold rim — that water is clean.

So they make a place for it to gather cleaner. Five bowls. Six. One for each. The rain-from-below will fill them in an hour and spill them, and fill them again. She leans, puts her whole face in, and drinks until the bowl shows basalt at the bottom.

She does not need much else. The rosette crowns each hold a private lake — rain and dead flycatcher feathers and one big white larva that lives only there. She sips from that too on hot middays. She eats the larva sometimes. More often she does not. She eats peat-tips, the sweet new growth of sphagnum, and the starchy pith of a fallen fern frond that she has let soften for a month under her foot.

How do they talk, if they are six women who spend a season growing a leaf? You already heard it. The whole bowl is a percussion room tuned to half-second delays.

One of them shifts, and her epiphyte coat sheds. *Tappet.* A beat later, across the cup, another answers by deliberately dislodging the water from her own rosette crown. *Plip.* A long pause. Then a deep *glut-glut* as one steps deliberately into deep peat, making it complain. They speak in drip. In the interim wet hiss where cloud becomes water. They had a conversation about you for twenty minutes before you saw a single one.

They are not shy. Just very, very polite about movement. If you sit — which the peat will make you want to do, it is warm, it takes your shape instantly — and you stay still until your shoulders get damp though the sky is blue, one will eventually look at you and not look away.

She will recognize you as a kind of cloud that got lost and came up instead of down. A temporary, fast, warm thing that also sweats.

If you put your hand out, she will not take it. But she will, after a long time, push one of the rim bowls toward you with a soft, huge foot. Not the one she drinks from. The seventh. The empty one.

The water in it right this second was air ten feet above it, ten seconds ago.

It is offered.
