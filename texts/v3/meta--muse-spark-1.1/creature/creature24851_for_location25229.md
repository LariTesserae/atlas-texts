There is nothing here that knows how to leave.

That's the first rule of who lives here. Nothing walks away. Nothing flies away. Nothing has ever needed to.

You think you see an empty place because there are no eyes looking back at you. No insect. No bird. No thing that breathes in and out quickly. And you're right. There are no animals here. The air is too young for them, and too wet, and there is no ground to make an animal from.

What lives here is what learned to be ground.

It all started with the one that holds water.

You can still find it at the rim where the world is newest, where the basalt is still black and hot to the touch. A moss, but that word is too small for it. A felt. A mat. A living sealant. It doesn't grow *on* rock, it *becomes* rock for every other thing. Each filament is swollen, translucent, fat with stored water. When the sun hits it, it doesn't dry. It sweats. It puts out more water than it takes in because it wrings the cloud straight out of the air. Touch it and your hand comes away cold, then warm, then stained green-black. It gives off a smell like a hot pond. This is the only thing here that can touch the basalt and live. Everything else lives on it, and then on each other. It's still alive under all of it, thirty feet down, in the dark, still holding.

Then the stitchers came.

You can see their logic in how they died. Long, pinkish, fleshy ropes with leaves like coins that never bothered to be green on both sides. They can't stand up. They never learned to. They run sideways through the felt and put down root-hands every few inches. If you pull one, the whole plain in front of you moves an inch, because you're not pulling a plant, you're pulling a seam. They are how the continent holds together. They hold so much water in their stems that when you step on them they sigh out, warm against your ankle. When they die they don't rot — there's nothing here that knows how to be a decomposer except more of itself — they become a little softer, a little more like soil, and something else punches straight through them.

And then - the builders. The sparse ones. The ones that made you think this place was empty because there are so few of them.

That is their strategy. Not density. Loneliness.

Each one is alone because it cannot afford a neighbor. Each one is a water engine.

**The Bowl.**

The first kind you learn to recognize by sound. From a half-mile away you hear it raining when it is not raining. It is a ring of trunks — not one trunk, but eight or twelve trunks that decided to be one thing. They lean inward, hollow, breathing. The bark isn't bark, it's overlapping leaves that never fell off, fused at the base like shingles. At the top, a hundred feet up, the trunks lose themselves and become a rim, and inside that rim is a lake. Not a metaphor. A real hanging lake, three feet deep in the middle, black water. The organism made a bowl out of its own body to catch the cloud that never leaves.

It lives all year with its head in water. Inside the bowl, things grow the wrong way — pale, inverted sprouts with their roots bare in the air and their leaves under water, grazing on light that comes through the mist. That's how it reproduces. It doesn't make seeds. It grows its children in its own lake and then lets the lake overflow, wash them over the side on a day when the wind is high, and they land and start trying to be bowls themselves.

When it drinks, you can hear it. A long hydraulic groan from base to crown as the hollow trunks pull a ton of water straight up. The whole colonnade tenses.

**The Harp.**

The second kind never wanted to be solid at all. It started as one vine that found no support and kept reaching anyway, circling the air where a tree should have been. So it circled again. And again. Now it is a tower made of absence, a helix of living cable as thick as your body wrapped around a shaft of wind. There is nothing in the middle except moving air. The wind comes off the lowlands, hits the hot rock, hits this, and goes through it, and the whole thing sings low and constant, and shivers. You can feel it in your teeth.

What lives on it is its own body. From every outer surface it lets down curtains — not roots to seek soil, roots that will never seek soil. Forty, sixty feet of fine red hair, so fine they vanish when you look straight at them. They don't want ground. They comb the air. Water condenses on them and runs up. Up. It moves water against gravity in beads that race toward the center of the spiral. On a bad air day they are dry and brittle and they crackle. On a day like today they are soaking, heavy, dripping onto nothing, feeding the tower that made them. The tower is never still because it is always remodeling. A cable snaps somewhere inside it with a gunshot and a whole section sighs and sags and immediately starts to regrow over the gap.

**The Fallen.**

The third kind is the most numerous, because this place kills what it makes.

They grow too fast. That's what heat and water and light and no competition does. A builder can add six feet in a week. It has no rings, no memory of hard years. It has never had a hard year. So it doesn't know how to be heavy. The top gets too big, the felt beneath it is too soft, the stitchers shift, and it goes over.

You hear them all day. The creak like an old ship, then a soft *pop-pop-pop* like knuckles, then the tear — a leaf the size of a van coming unstapled. When one goes, it doesn't crash. There's nowhere to crash to. It folds into the mat. It opens up. And then you see what it really was on the inside: not solid wood, but a lung. Layer after layer of damp chambers, catwalks, bladders full of warm rain. For years it contained little weathers inside itself.

And the instant it opens, it is covered. The felt is already there. The seedlings are already in the wound, sharp and bright green, knives pointing up. There is no corpse here. There is only new scaffolding.

Between them — and this is most of the mesa — lives the thing that is not yet a thing. The bright plain that bounces under your boots. You kneel and pull it apart and you find no end to pulling. White root, then finer white root, then hair, then slime that is root, then green that is trying to be leaf but hasn't decided where air is. Water comes up around your wrists, warm as blood. It is one organism and many. It is nervous tissue. It is the waiting. It hasn't become a bowl or a harp yet. Tomorrow, with the sun, part of it will decide.

Nothing here makes a flower that needs a bee, because there were never any bees brought up here when the rock came up. So they don't ask. Their seeds are cannon. The big pods — you see them between the builders, smooth and tight and veined like hearts — they swell all day in the heat until their seams go white. Then in the late afternoon they cough. A flat, hard bang that you feel in your chest, and a spray of wet fluff and heavy, slick seeds that can stick to vertical cloud and start growing before they land.

That's it. That's everyone.

No eyes. No legs. No hunger except for water and higher light.

And no death like you know death. Just failure under weight, and reweaving. Just the constant wet noise. Drip and creak and sigh. The mesa breathing through a thousand open, green mouths that learned how to be the ground, the tree, the forest, the soil, and the animal that eats the forest, all at once, because there was nothing else to do it.

If you lie down on the bouncing plain, and let the warm water come up around your ears, you can feel it: the whole table is shivering, adding an inch.
