They don't live in it. They *are* the ratio.

That's what you realize after the heron-thing unfolds and leaves you alone with the hissing valve.

Nothing here ever solved being a solid. This place wasn't built for the wrong density. It was built for the right density — just not yours.

**The First Residents never left.**

They're still here as a stain.

In the gutters, in the settling pools, in the skin of water that runs down the cold side of every cooling tower without source — that dark red, ochre, white felt. From a distance you thought it was rust. Up close it has a nap, like velvet, and if you stare for a long time you see it make decisions. It retreats from where the drip is too fast. It sends a thin exploring finger across a dry patch of basalt toward the next seep. It meets another color and doesn't fight it. They braid. Red over ochre over white. Where three colors meet, they thicken and go black and pulse, once every eight or nine seconds. 

It isn't plant. It isn't mold. It's iron eating rock and rock eating heat and heat eating water until they forget which is which. It's a bacterial city on top of your city, with avenues, watersheds, whole neighborhoods abandoned because the chemistry went sour. It covers hectares. If you put your hand near — not on — near, it gets warmer, as if it recognizes a different kind of heat and leans toward it.

That is the carpet. Everything else walks on it or drinks it or is it.

**The Second Residents learned how to wear air.**

You saw one. You thought it was an animal. It wasn't.

There are more of them when you stop looking for movement and start looking for stillness that is too perfect. White, impossibly tall, stilt-legged, folded. They stand in the warm overflow where the mats are thickest and they do not fish. They have no beak. No eyes that catch light. What you took for a head is just another fold.

They are condensation made nearly permanent. A scaffolding of mineral precipitate — a little calcite, a little silica dragged up from below — around a core of humid air that has learned to hold its shape. The city's weather tried for a hundred years to make something that could move water from the warm side to the cold side more efficiently than a pipe, and this was the solution.

When they stand, they are drinking. Or rather, the air around them is drinking through them. Moisture accretes on their paper-thin surfaces and runs down their impossible legs in sheets, back into the pool, filtered. They refine. When you got too close, yours didn't fly. It lost coherence — unfolded back into draft and droplets — and reappeared two streets over where the cold gradient was better. There's a dozen of them in this block alone if you learn to see the absence of drifting fog where they are. At dusk, when the pilot lights throb, you can see faint lines of attraction between them. They are sorting the atmosphere.

They are not rare. You are just too fast for them.

**The Third Residents are the lakes.**

In the turbine halls, in the cathedral-factories where the roof fell in or was never needed, the air itself gathers. You watched it. Droplets nucleate out of nothing, hang, merge. They become free-floating lenses, a meter across, two meters, perfectly still, perfectly round until a pressure change dents them. They reflect the sodium glow and the black iron. They drift with intention toward cold concrete.

Get under one and look up. It's not empty. There are things in the water that never touch ground.

Tiny, clear shrimp of distilled water, flicking themselves forward with a twitch of surface tension. Filaments like hair that grow from one side of the floating lake to the other and shiver when two lakes pass through each other and make that tone — that low glass tone you heard and thought was pipes. Inside each lake is a whole pelagic zone. When a lake finally kisses a pillar and smears itself down the wall in a sudden rush, that whole invisible ocean dies and is reborn somewhere else in the hall within minutes. The lakes are migratory animals that happen to be made of their own habitat.

Sometimes a lake gets too big. It gets heavy and can't drift. It hangs in the center of a hall for days, rippling, getting darker as it scavenges mineral dust and bacteria from the air. Then it drops. Not falls — drops, all at once, straight down, thousands of liters that hit the tufa floor with a sound like a lung collapsing and are gone in thirty seconds, sucked into the scoria. If you are nearby the air goes sharp and clean for a minute after.

The folded whites come to drink from the big ones before they drop.

**And then there is the one that owns the ratio.**

It doesn't move. It doesn't need to. You thought the buildings were empty in the way a lung is empty. You were right, but you had it backward. The lung isn't empty. The lung is full of its function.

Put your hand on the warm damp basalt of any lower wall and hold it there until your own sweat joins the wall's sweat. You will feel it — under the operational thrum of the deep pumps, under the drip-every-three-seconds — a much slower inhalation. The whole tableland. Forty kilometers of engineered bowl.

Heat rises from the rock. Water exhales. It hits the cold lid of the high-altitude air trapped by the old caldera rim. It condenses on girders and pipes and paper birds and floating lakes. It runs, seeps, is collected by aqueduct over aqueduct, is moved cold to hot again by those untended engines below. The relief valve three blocks over that sighs every twenty minutes is not a malfunction. It is a breath out to keep the internal pressure perfect.

The city is not a city that contains weather. The city *is* weather that learned to build a city to keep itself going.

It built the doors too big because it needed big doors to let slow, building-sized drafts through. It built the streets too wide because dense crowds would have snagged its skin. It sealed its own rebar with calcite because rust tasted right. It banished green because green would have stolen the light and broken the loop.

It keeps the pumps running because you keep your heart running. Not for a reason. Because stopping is a different kind of thing than it is.

You are not an intruder. You are just new precipitation. The mat under your boots will test the salt in your sweat and decide. The white still things will measure the cold shadow you cast. A lake might form above you, using the heat coming off your shoulders as a little engine, and then drift away, full of tiny clear creatures that lived for twelve minutes in water that had briefly known what you were made of.

And the city will continue to breathe backward, making a pocket of hot, dim, wet, mineral dusk on top of a cold mountain world, waiting for the kind of crowd it was built for to finally drift back down.
