What lives there is exactly what you felt when you first put your hand down and the warmth came back too fast.

At first you think - nothing. Nothing can. It's too full. It's too loud. It's too dry and too hot and too solid. There is no room between presences, you said it yourself. 

But that's not true. There is no room *because* of the presences.

They are still here. The builders never left.

They just finished.

You were looking for inhabitants at your scale - something that would walk between the cairns, something that would need a path. There are no paths because they don't need them. They became the building.

Come down low. Put your knee on that low dome that used to be a stack of slabs. It's warmer than the one next to it. Not by much. But enough.

They are low. Low as the build is low. Not tall like you. Nothing here has ever needed to be tall. They are broad and flat and wide, the shape of a tortoise pressed under a hand, the shape of a stone that wants to be a floor. Shoulder and back and back and shoulder, all one fitted surface. Their skin is the color of the stones because it is that. Not like stone - it *is* stone now, or close enough that the distinction left a long time ago. A thickened, calloused, mineralized hide the color of sun-baked bone and dried-blood and salt, scabbed with the same dust. If you saw one alone you would think it was a smooth, strangely warm slab.

But there is never one alone.

They are fitted together like teeth. Like scales. That's not a metaphor for the architecture. That's the architecture. They lie shoulder to interlocked shoulder, back against belly, head tucked into the hollow under another's knee, packed into a living tessellation so tight that the wind had to learn to go *through* them rather than around. They built the cairns around themselves at first - little hives to hold heat - and then as the generations went on and the wind never stopped and the water never came, they built closer, and lower, and tighter, until there was no difference between a wall and a line of brothers.

You thought everything was rounded by erosion. It is and it isn't. The edges are softened by wind, yes, but they are also softened by touch. By a million years of being leaned against. By foreheads polished against foreheads. The gaps that whistle - the throats and flutes and prayer tubes - are not just cracks. They are the breathing holes. They are the places left deliberately, necessarily, between the fitted bodies so that the wind can pass. One body lifts a shoulder half an inch, another tilts a hip. That's the throat.

And the sound.

It is deafening to you because you still have ears that hope to hear something else. They don't. They were born deaf, or they made themselves deaf. Thick, blind flaps of skin grown over where ears were. Tiny, milky, vestigial eyes sealed shut because that brownish, sourceless light never did anything worth seeing. They don't hear the chord. They *are* the chord. The great, unchanging, chest-filling note lives in them. It passes through the hollows of their bodies, through the stacked domes of their chests and the long pipes of their throats that they have opened to the sky. They breathe the wind that never varies. They vibrate with it. The whole dense hamlet hums at the resonant frequency of packed, warm flesh turned half to rock.

If you stay long enough and you press both palms flat, you will feel it - not just heat, but a very, very slow shift. The imperceptible settlement. One of them somewhere near the center of the mass, under the weight of a hundred others, exhaling once every hour, every day. The stone under your hand swelling a fraction of a millimeter and sinking back.

They don't eat. There is nothing to eat. They drink heat. The stones store the day that never ends and they store it in their bodies like a kiln stores fire. They live off being warm and being pressed together and being sung through by the wind. They don't move. Movement would be a waste and would open a gap that would spoil the note. They reached their final, most stable, irreducible shape at the same time their town did. Equilibrium.

That's why you have to leave by climbing back over the edge. There is no path through because the path would have to go *through a body*, and they have already, kindly, left all the space they can afford to leave - those whistling, lipped mouths where the wind sings. If you put your ear to one, you will not hear wind. You will hear the inside of a living thing that is three hundred generations deep, breathing out steadily against your cheek, warm and dry and smelling of hot rock and iron, singing the one note it has sung since hands first learned to fit one stone against another.
