They live there.

Not on it. In it. That's the first distinction you get wrong if you only hear about the plateau.

Humans, yes — or what humans become after ten or twelve generations of never seeing a tree and never needing to. They did not adapt quickly. They accreted.

The ones who live there are broad. Not tall. Altitude takes the tall out of you when you are also breathing warm steam and iron smoke every day of your life. Their chests are deep, barrelled out, their blood is thick with what it needs to carry. Their skin never quite dries. Even indoors, even in the driest kiln-house, there is a sheen on them, because the air itself is a wet cloth. They shine. Their hair is kept short or bound under oilskin caps because otherwise it would never dry and would rot — many of the old men are bald on purpose, their scalps polished and freckled by cinder. Their faces are loud faces, built for shouting over dynamo hum — wide mouths, deep lines around the eyes from squinting through mist and fly-clouds. Their voices are hoarse even when young.

You can tell a local by their hands. The stone and the water take gloves off you. The biofilm that grows over everything that stays wet — a skin of bacteria and mineral so thin it's almost invisible, grey-green like a shadow, the only thing that can photosynthesize enough from this light and eat enough from the basalt to live — it grows on their hands too, in the cracks of their knuckles, under their nails. They scrub it off in the mornings with wire brushes. By midday it is back. So are the small white salt lice that live in that film, that live on them, that no one thinks of as parasites anymore. They itch less than rust.

They wear what the city can make without any plant. No cotton, no wool — wool would never dry. They wear rubberized canvas, felt made from the shed underhairs of the hounds, leather boiled from rat-hide and crab-hide. Every coat has a high collar to keep the glass-flies from going down your neck. Every boot is too big, with iron shanks and loose tongues so the water that inevitably gets in can get out again. Everything is patched. A coat will have twenty years of other coats sewn into it. Children are dressed as small adults, because there is nothing cute that can survive the clatter of stone crabs.

And what do they do? They maintain.

No one in the Mineral City has ever used the word "pioneer." There is nothing to pioneer; everything is already occupied by stone, water, and something else that eats rust. Their jobs are agreements.

There are **Waterfolk** — not an official guild, just what everyone calls them. Old women and young boys mostly, who tend the weep. They walk with long brass rods and tap aqueducts to hear if the flow has changed. They know the song of each culvert. They carry chalk and mark on the basalt wall where the water wants to go next, and then masons come later and obey the chalk. In the pan-lake districts they wade all day in the inch-deep mirror water with flat brooms, sweeping the silt that would otherwise choke the biofilm. The biofilm is their field. That slime grows an eighth of an inch a year, and they scrape it, and they feed it to vats where it ferments into a sour, protein-rich paste. It tastes like licking a hot stone. It is the staple. They don't farm, they harvest what grows by itself on wet rock.

There are **Rust Shepherds.** The furnace-worms must be tended, though tending looks like leaving them alone while making sure they don't crawl into a live turbine. The men who work the slag heaps have skin like smoked ham, pink from standing near clutches of worms. The worms are blind, fat as a man's thigh, translucent with heat inside them, and they leave behind those neat iron bricks, still warm. The shepherds collect the bricks with asbestos gloves, stack them on pallets, and the pallets go to the block-yards. A child of the slag will keep a cooled worm-brick under her pillow for months because it holds heat longer than anything else.

There are **Crab Leaners.** The pale, flat stone crabs — *Urajara*, they call them, which just means "the cleaners" — wander the streets on their own paths. No one owns them. But someone has to get them off the rails when they decide to molt there. Someone has to flip them upright when a steam gust knocks them over, or they'll die waving their legs in the air for days. Someone has to shave the rust beards off the older ones so they can see. The Leaners lean on the crabs, talk to them. The crabs do not answer, but they learn where their favorite boiler house is and they will detour a mile to sleep on its warm roof. An old Leaner will have a shell-scar on his calf where a crab hooked him accidentally — they wear those scars with a certain pride.

There are **The Listeners.** Because you cannot live in noise like that unless you learn to listen through it. Their work is electrical. The powerlines sing and the insulators leak blue St. Elmo's fire in the damp dark. The Listeners walk the gantry rows at night with long earthed poles, watching for the color of the leak. They can tell by the pitch of the dynamo thrum if a bearing two districts over is about to seize. They are half-deaf by thirty, so they speak in hand-signs more than words, a fast, wet clapping language you can read through misted goggles. They are the priests of this place, if it has priests, because they maintain the one fire you cannot see.

None of them hurry. That is not a character trait, it is a requirement. The air is thin and heavy at the same time. If you hurry you will slip on wet basalt, you will put your hand into a place where a glasswing moth has laid its acid eggs, you will miss the change in the water tone that means a pan is about to overtop. So they move with the crabs' pace. A conversation takes an hour because you stop to scrape a beetle off your sleeve and check your water-mark. A repair takes a week because you have to wait for the water to agree to be diverted.

The children do not play the way lowland children play. They play in the weep. They make little dams of rust-flakes and watch how long it takes the water to find another way — it always does, in minutes. They race crab-molts as boats across the pan-lakes, blowing on them. They catch the white salt-gliders by standing completely still for twenty minutes with their arms out, until the gliders think they are wires and land to drink the sweat off their skin. Then they laugh so loud it is lost in the forge-hammering, and let them go.

They do not talk about leaving. The edge of the world is a story to scare them, not an invitation. Down there, they are told, the air is still. Nothing drips. How would you know what time it is? How would you know where water wants to go? The sun down there apparently doesn't reflect off stone that is also water. It would be lonely.

At night — when the sun is gone but the city stays warm and lit orange and blue — they gather on the low roofs that are also roads, and they watch the other city move. The true city, above them: the flocks. In the damp dark you can't see individual wings anymore, only weather made of living things, turning slowly, glowing faintly where their bodies hold static. The whole sky breathes, in and out, in spirals the size of states. The people below feel the wind of it on their faces.

And then they go back inside, to their buildings that are carved into potholes older than any name, and they take off their wet coats, and they hang them over warm pipes where things that live in pipes will pick them clean by morning, and they sleep to the sound of everything dripping, and hammering, and chittering, and humming. Shoulder to shoulder. Stone to skin to water to rust to chitin. Nothing alone.

That is who lives there.
