They do not live here the way people live in a town.

The first ones are not even animals.

In every runnel where the water runs fastest - too hot for anything with a membrane to linger, just under scalding - there is a skin. Orange. Yellow. Sometimes a green so dark you think it's shadow until it moves, very slightly, at the edge.

It's bacteria, but that word makes you think of something small and incidental. This is not incidental. This is the pasture. This is the field and the wheat and the topsoil. It grows as a wet felt over the white silica, over the sulfur, over the black glass made soft by age. It drinks sun and mineral and heat from the rock. It lays itself down in sheets a few cells thick. Where the water shears it, it tears, and the tear curls like old paint and is gone by nightfall, eaten.

Everything else is a way of moving that mat from one place to another.

The grazers come first. You have to crouch to see them right, and if you do, the heat comes up off the pool and makes your eyes water behind your smoked glass.

They look like shrimp but they have unlearned being shrimp. An inch, two inches long. No pigment. No eyes at all - what would be the use, in water that is always glaring white from the mineral bottom? Their backs are domed and that dome is furry, not with fur but with living bacterial felt they farm on their own shells. They scrape. That is their entire life. A mouth like a soft rasp goes *shick shick shick* against the terrace, inaudible until you put your ear almost to the water, and then you hear it as a faint, continuous ticking, like someone cleaning grout.

When your shadow crosses them they go. Not fast - they can't afford fast - just a convulsive flick backward into deeper water. Count to ten. The first one comes back. Then all of them. A spot of bacterial mat left ungrazed in this caldera is heat going to waste, and nothing here tolerates waste.

The ones who eat the grazers do not chase them.

On the rock between the pools, where the water is only a coin-thick sheet running over hot basalt, are the white walkers. From far away you would think someone had scattered broken crockery. Up close, they are crabs, but their shells are not made by them the way a crab makes a shell. They accrete it. Water rich with dissolved silica and carbonate passes over their backs and they coax it to precipitate, white and pitted and still warm to the touch. Pitted like old limestone. When they die, no one can find the death - they simply stop and become indistinguishable from the rim of whatever pool they died on. Other walkers graze over them.

They are stilted. Their legs are absurdly long, jointed twice, tipped with a hard black cuticle that doesn't burn. It lifts that white mineral dome clear of the sheet-flow that would cook their underside. They pick their way with the care of a person crossing a floor covered in eggs. Each foot placed, tested, then weighted. Click. Click. That is the only mechanical noise in the bowl for minutes at a time.

When a grazer flicks too close to the edge, a walker dips its head - not a head, a sort of folded undercarriage - and scoops. No snap. No struggle. The white dome goes a little pinker for an hour with the blood.

Above both of them are the Kites.

You notice them because of their shadows. From above, the light is absolute here, coming from the sky and from every pool at once, and then a patch of light goes a little dimmer and passes over you. Look up.

They are two, sometimes three meters across when fully spread. Not birds. Not bats. Not insects. The wing is a single continuous membrane stretched between elongated finger-bones so thin you can see sun through them, covered in a tight, completely dry skin that never wets. It has to stay dry. That dryness is how it lives. The air in the caldera is so hot and wet and thin that it should not be able to carry that much weight - but the rocks below are pouring thermal after thermal up, and the Kites ride them without a single flap for hours.

They trail. From their belly and from the trailing edge of their wings hang dozens of filaments as fine as spider silk and up to four meters long, each tipped with a bead of hygroscopic salt. They drag those beads through the inversion steam and through the top millimeter of the pools. Water and shrimp larvae and loose bacteria stick and wick up. You never see them feed directly. You see the filaments slowly shorten, as if being reeled in.

They never, ever land. To land is to touch rock that is 70 degrees Celsius and water that is 90. To land is to die cooked. So they were born in the air - they bud, actually, a little crumpled second Kite that clings to the parent's back for a week until its skin dries - and they will die in the air, when they get too waterlogged or too old to stay up. Then they fold. That is the word the maintenance workers use. They fold. They collapse their wings and drop like wet paper onto a condenser tower or a pipe bridge and hang there, becoming papery and cold. Within a day the walkers find them and begin to take them apart from the edges.

At night, the towers are fringed with them, all hanging, all softly dripping condensed water from their filaments, sleeping.

And then there are the people who tend the place.

Not many. There cannot be many. The bowl is three or four towns wide and there are maybe thirty of them at any given time. You see one at a distance and you mistake them for a mirage at first because of the heat shimmer, because of how slow they move. Pale suits. Not white - a heat-suit cannot be white here or it would blind you twice. More of a dusty bone, covered in a fine crust of silica where the spray has hit it day after day. Smoked glass goggles, a wrap over the mouth to keep the sulfur out.

They don't live here together. There is no village. Each pumping house, each louvered shed where you can feel the turbines turning through your molars, has one bunk, one table, one little altar of tools. One worker each. Their job is listening. That industrial word is wrong for what they do, but it's the closest.

They listen with their hands to the pipes that run at knee height across the rock, feeling for the half-millimeter of scale building up inside. They listen to the condenser towers, scraping the white mineral off the vanes before it becomes too thick. They listen to the runnels, sometimes kneeling for an hour in that brutal light to nudge a piece of fallen basalt that has diverted a rill and starved a pasture of bacteria half a kilometer downflow.

They are not owners. They are not even, properly, engineers anymore. The system built itself into equilibrium a long time ago: hot rock heats water, water rises, water condenses, water falls and is pumped back. The turbines take a little off the top. What the people do is keep that equilibrium from tipping into silence or into roar. They keep it laminar. They keep it lacelike.

They speak rarely when they meet on the walk between houses. The air eats sound and there is nothing to say that the rock isn't already saying with its faint ticking and seeping. They have developed a set of gestures instead - raise a mineral-white crab shell, meaning "yield down here is poor," or touch the back of the hand, meaning "my tower needs a day to cool."

You think that must be lonely, but it isn't, not in the human way. At about four in the afternoon, when the fog that hangs about waist high forms because the air finally cools faster than the rock, every single worker, as if cued, stops what they are doing and stands completely still. The walkers, across the entire bowl, stop grazing at the same moment and turn their backs to the low sun. The Kites drop lower until their filaments vanish in the fog. And the shrimp, in every pool, sink to the bottom and hold.

In that moment the caldera is all one temperature for a few minutes. Top to bottom, rock to air to water to body. No gradients left to ride or graze or pump.

The workers call it "leveling" and some of them take off their goggles then, though the light still hurts, because in that white, leveled hour you can see from one broken rim to the other straight across the bowl, without heat shimmer, without mirage, every pumping house and pipe and shining pool exactly where it should be.

Then a pipe ticks, a walker clicks, a shrimp flicks, and it all starts again.
