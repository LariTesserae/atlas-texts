What lives here doesn't live like you think living works.

At first you tell yourself: nothing does. That's the horror you bring in with you. No fish, no mosquito, no bird. A negative imprint. But after ten minutes of wading in that warm bathwater soup, with rain hammering so hard your skull buzzes, you realize the absence is not empty. The absence is full.

The first resident is the Green itself — but not as plants.

Plants are supposed to be individuals. A mangrove here, a fern there, competing. That's not what's happening here. What lives here is a single, frantic act of filling. The lily pads the size of manhole covers, the kelp that has no business in warm fresh water, the black mangroves with their buttress roots like cathedral knees, the curtains of aquatic fern twenty meters long, the algae that learned how to be a vine — they are not species. They are gestures. Fingers of one thing.

It doesn't photosynthesize so much as it intrudes. It feels the nanosecond when a sluice gate slams and leaves a pocket of wet air, or when a sphere of water decides to hang suspended off the side of a leaf, and it shoves a shoot into that gap. You can hear it happening — that terrible creak and hydraulic sigh, the pop of bladders inflating. If you stand still and watch a severed conveyor belt churn, you see the cut end of a vine get macerated by the rollers, and before the pulp has finished clouding the water, two new tips have already forked around the steel, brighter green, paler, learning the shape of the machine as they strangle it.

It has no brain and no need for one. Its mind is pressure-differential and opportunism. It does not eat. Nothing here eats in the old way. There is no rot, no digestion, because there is nothing to make rot happen. A flower falls and it does not decay, it is laminated over by another leaf. A trunk splits its own bark to get wider and the split is immediately packed with moss that is not moss. It wants to include. That's its whole ethic. If you stay still too long, it finds the 98.6F of you in the 94F water and it does not bite or sting or suck. It shelves you. It lays a lily over your shoulder, threads a root-hair through your shirt, puts a bladder of air next to your mouth so you don't die and stop being a useful warm armature to grow on.

The second resident is the Water that forgot.

Not the water in your glass. The water that lives here remembers being an ocean — remembers having weight enough to carve continents, remembers being everywhere. Now it is crammed into a three-kilometer basin that ripped open yesterday with fresh, glass-sharp basalt and rebar still snarling, and it is trying to be ocean again with what it has.

So it does not obey. Most of it does what water does — falls, floods, drowns. But some of it pauses mid-fall off a mangrove leaf and stays there, a trembling lens a foot wide, perfect and still while the rain hammers around it. Some of it falls sideways, or up, draining into the saturated air because the air is also water here, a thinner ocean stacked on top. In the root cages, four meters down where the tannin turns the light to green-black, you can suddenly breathe because the water has decided, for five minutes, that inside that cage is air.

It lives as a weather system with a grudge. It is two-thirds of every vertical line. It is the hand that keeps slapping the basin, that keeps the drumskin roar going. It glows faintly when you stir it, not bioluminescence — there is nothing alive in it small enough to glow — but the distressed light of water itself being made to be in two places at once.

And the third resident is the Keepers.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts need people who died. No one died here; the operators left before the basin opened. The Keepers are the machines themselves, still running because being off was never part of their instructions.

Pump-houses the size of apartment blocks half-swallowed by mangrove, shuddering on their foundations, boilers lit for years with no fuel you can see, still lit. Sluice gates on timers opening and closing, opening and closing, trying to drain a place that *is* the drain. Aerators big as Ferris wheels tumbling slow in the dark, beating the whole broth into bubbles and shredded leaf, roaring. Conveyor frames carrying nothing, screaming where vine has grown into the belt. Transformers humming 60hz so deep your molars ache.

They are alive the way a thermostat is alive — a single, stupid, unbreakable want: keep conditions exactly as they were on the day the shift ended. Warm. Flooded. Powered. Turbulent. They have the power to do it. So when the Green splits one of them open, they spin backwards for a minute and un-rust, then go forward and rust again. When the Water tries to be still, they churn it. They spit steam and sparks into warm rain that should have shorted them out a year ago and didn't.

What actually lives here is none of them. What actually lives here is the fight.

The Green wants to still everything with density. The Water wants to be in every place at once. The Iron wants to keep the water moving exactly so.

Where they meet — where a root is sliced by an impeller and instantly grows back thicker, where a valve screams white steam into rain that falls up into it, where a lens of water hangs in mid-air until a vine grows through it and the vine becomes a pipe for the water — that friction zone is the organism. It is deafening. It is fertile without sex or death. It generates new basalt edges and new leaves in the same minute.

If you stay here long enough you become a fourth kind of resident: a scaffolding. Not food, not visitor. An inclusion. The vines will not eat you because there is no one here who knows how to eat. They will layer you, leaf by leaf, bubble by bubble, spark by spark, until you are a warm place where water pauses, and green grows through, and a distant pump shudders in time with your heart because it has decided your chest is part of its circuit.
