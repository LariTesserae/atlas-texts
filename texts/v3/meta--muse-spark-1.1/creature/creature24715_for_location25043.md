What lives here doesn't live here the way animals live in a place.

It *is* the place, for now. It will give the place back later, when the rock learns how to be old.

There is only one of it. Counting it is hard because you want to count stems. But stems join. A trunk is a braid of trunks. A root is a root grafted to a hundred other roots that left the same mother felt and never learned how to be separate. If you dig — which you can't — you would not find an individual. You would find density gradients.

Call it the Mass. It doesn't have a name. It doesn't need one; nothing else is here to call it.

At the bottom, where it is oldest, it lives as a mouth.

Not a mouth with lips. A felt, a mat, a black-green grease-thick carpet meters deep that sits directly on young basalt and eats it. It exudes acid so mild you can put your hand in it, but given a year it turns glass into grit. It weeps chelators. It has learned to taste iron and let it go, to keep potassium. The rock here is still hot from being born and the felt lives off that faint heat differential as much as off light — a thermodynamic lichen the size of a state. That felt is where the stone stops being geology and starts being plant. It doesn't grow *on* the plateau. It digests the plateau into itself and then holds the shape of the plateau from memory.

Above that, it lives as a body.

This is what you thought were trees. They aren't trees. They are columns of water under tension so extreme that water is no longer a liquid but a cable. Each column is as thick as a subway tunnel, wrapped in lignin grown into something closer to carbon fiber than wood. The columns carry. They carry cold seepwater straight up against gravity three kilometers without rest, click — *click-click* — cavitating and re-knitting a hundred trillion times a second. That clicking you hear is its pulse. That hiss is its blood pressure. The columns branch only because the pressure has to go somewhere. They swell into bladders the size of houses that are not fruit but cisterns and stomachs, where pH drops to 2 and last year's scaffolding is rendered back into sugar and poise. When one bursts, that soft *whump* in the dim, it's not dying. It's turning over.

In the middle, where light has been stolen four times over, it lives as hands.

Fronds that are not photosynthetic anymore, or not primarily. They are pale and muscular and lined with hairs that taste dead matter. The Mass does not wait for decay — there are no bacteria here it didn't make itself. It reaches for its own dead and closes around it, sphincter by sphincter, and pulls it inside a pouch and digests it. There are hillsides in here that are nothing but the Mass gently, patiently eating its own fingers to make new fingers. If you stand still too long, a tendril will test the leather of your boot, not because it wants you, but because you are an unusually salty piece of litter and the protocol is *everything must be plant eventually.* It has no malice and no mercy because both of those need a face.

At the top, where the cloud comes apart, it lives as a mind.

The "leaves" up there aren't leaves at all. They are antennae. Thin, black-green, edged with trichomes that hold a static charge. The whole canopy is a phased array a hundred kilometers wide that ionizes the fog that tries to move through it and pulls it sideways, upward, without ever heating it. It farms condensation the way you would farm current. You can see it working on cold mornings — frost blooms across a leaf in a perfect Hilbert curve, not because frost is pretty but because the leaf has dumped excess charge and the water followed the field lines like iron filings. 

It does the same thing with light. The upper lattice catches sun it can barely use and instead of using it, it holds it. Excites electrons and leaves them excited. Then at night, layer by layer, it lets them fall, re-emitting that cool, sourceless glow. Coherent light. Signal light. That is thought, here. Not fast. The thought goes around the plateau once every fourteen hours or so. The thought is: *hold water, hold heat, hold rock. Do not erode. Do not freeze. Keep drinking.*

That is its entire ethics. That is its entire plan.

It has no predators. It has no prey. It has no sex you would recognize. Its pollen never travels on anything but Coulomb attraction, grain by slow grain, in still air that it has made still on purpose. Its seeds — when it bothers — are not seeds but knuckles of root-felt wrapped in basalt wool that break off and roll into a fresh crack, right-angled and steaming, and begin instantly to dissolve.

It is not intelligent the way you are lonely and clever. It is intelligent the way a glacier is intelligent when it knows exactly how to shape a valley for water that will not arrive for ten thousand years. It is young-rock intelligent and old-plant intelligent at once. If it was built, the building was so long ago and so complete that building became breeding. What you are hearing in that deafening, birdless roar — the creak of a continent of columns in a high wind, the pop of cooling basalt under a blanket of living felt, the endless electric sip of sap under pressure — is not a forest sound. It's an embryology sound. It's the sound of something making its own cradle while already sleeping in it.

And it knows you are in it, in the way your skin knows a gnat is on it without bothering to form an opinion.

If you tear it, it will fill you in with white sap and then forget you were ever open.
