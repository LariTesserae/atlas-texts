What lives here doesn't live *on* the mountain. It lives *instead of* it.

The rock is still there, four kilometers up where the air thins, that soft pink gneiss exfoliating into paper leaves that lift and sigh in the heat. But nothing touches it anymore. The first true inhabitant sealed it off completely, and everything else lives on top of that decision.

**The Blanket That Drinks the Sky**

She is one thing and a thousand. Call her the mattress-moss. She is not the Sphagnum you know down below, but what Sphagnum would become if you gave it ten million years and told it there would never be rain again.

Thirty meters thick in the troughs, five meters thick on the wind-scoured domes, dark brown-black at the bottom where she is slowly, patiently digesting her own dead, and acid-green and alive on top for only the upper meter. She is a country-sized sponge and cistern. She has no roots. She doesn't need them.

There is no rain here - that "1.2" you were given. There are only the clouds that come every afternoon, updrafts from forests you will never see, and catch on these old, bald domes. They don't break. They *settle*, like breath on glass. The moss has learned to be cold. Her upper fronds are fine as hair and covered in tiny, hooked hydrophilic scales. She combs water out of fog. Bead by bead it gathers, runs down into gutters she has grown - some as narrow as a finger-joint, some as broad as a man lying down - that run for kilometers at a gradient of inches, slow as honey, silent. If you put your ear to them you can hear the whole highland trickle.

In the bottom of the blanket, where light never reaches, there are things that never need it. White, blind springtails the size of your thumb, and mites like moving rubies, and fungal rhizomorphs as thick as rope that run like nerves, carrying phosphorus back up. At the very, very bottom, where moss kisses stone, a thin sour soup of tannin and peat-acid is eating the mountain itself. The gneiss paper crumbles. That's the only soil there will ever be. The mountain is being eaten from above, grain by grain.

Everything else lives in the argument that the moss is having with gravity.

**The Scaffold Trees**

From her back grows the second inhabitant - you could call them trees but they don't believe in wood.

Giant heather and tree-rhododendron, *Erica* and *Rhododendron* grown to madness. On the lowlands they'd be shrubs. Here, with endless moisture in the air, endless warmth, and no competition for light except each other, they've become pillars. Their trunks are ten meters around but weigh almost nothing. The bark is alive, green, photosynthetic, soft enough to dent with your fingernail. Inside, the trunk is hollow and partitioned like bamboo, full of damp air and the roots of guests. Their own roots never go to rock. They braid laterally through the moss, through each other, a living rebar mesh that holds the blanket to the domes so it doesn't slough off in the night.

And they have learned the great rule of this place: **Don't fill the volume.**

If you filled it, nothing could fly. And if nothing flies here, nothing gets pollinated, nothing gets eaten, nothing moves. So the heather-trees grow as cathedrals do - a trunk, then a hundred feet of empty air, then an umbrella crown that knits with the next crown to form a ceiling. Underneath, vast empty rooms the size of houses, of churches, of town squares. The walls of each room are curtains of aerial roots, hanging epiphytes, living veils you have to push through. Inside, the ground bounces - deep moss. The air is still and thrumming.

Why so empty? Look up.

**The Suspension**

The biomass here is all surface. The volume is for transit.

Open your mouth in one of those green rooms at noon and you will inhale life. Not metaphorically. Actual bodies.

First - the grazers of air. White moths the size of your open hand, so thin you can see light through them, so dense that at a distance they just look like dust. Incredible, silent snow moths. They don't eat leaves - they have no mouths as adults. They live a week, powered by what they ate as caterpillars down in the moss. Their only job is to be in the air.

And the leaf-eaters who *do* fly: beetles like lacquered blackberries, the size of a child's fist, their wing-cases humming low as they blunder trunk-to-trunk, chewing epiphyte. Gliding roaches with fern-like antennae. And everywhere, in the bromeliad pools, the true livestock - a milli-ass of larvae.

Because every surface up here is designed to hold water. The giant tank bromeliads - you can bathe in one - each holds a liter or two of pure, distilled cloud-water. In each is a pond. Mosquito larvae, midge larvae, damselfly nymphs, tadpoles of frogs that will never touch the ground, tiny transparent shrimp. The plateau has a million lakes, each the size of a bowl, each stacked on top of the other.

So something has to patrol that air, and it does, deafeningly.

The hunters: two-foot dragonflies with wings like cracked glass. They don't dart like lowland dragonflies. The air is too thin at four kilometers. They *row*, slow, powerful, hovering, making that struck-wire thrumming that vibrates in your molars. Their nymphs are the tigers of the bromeliad pools. Their adults can take a moth out of the air without breaking rhythm.

And then the birds that are not birds anymore. The whole place is too tangled for a hawk to make a living. So the birds became flies and the flies became plankton. Flocks - if you can call them that - of thumb-sized, moss-green and bruise-purple honeycreeper-things, with beaks as fine as needles and feet that can cling to a vertical root. They never land on the ground because there is no ground. They live entirely on the wing and on bark. A single flock, a thousand strong, moves like one animal, a single flexing sheet that pours through the archways between rooms, turning on a thought, feeding as it goes. They drink nectar from the heather bells that only open inside the void, and they glean moths.

When they go, they go all day. That's the roar. Fifty million wings in rooms that echo. In the lower sponge-tunnels the sound dulls to a rainy buzzing that never stops. At noon, when the mist catches the sun and every leaf is a lens, the cicada-things begin - not cicadas, but huge, moss-backed katydids that live their entire lives on a single bough and sing by rubbing their thighs together, and when ten thousand of them sing together the pressure in the air gets so high you want to laugh and cover your ears at the same time. The whole mountain hums.

Even higher, above the canopy, is another country - the wind-scoured green plain where the crown-leaves stitch together. There, broad-faced halo-hawks hang on the thin air, hardly moving, looking down into the clear drops between crowns for a flash of dragonfly wing. And always, lichen. Lichen that hangs like old wool ten meters long, filtering the void.

**The People Who Persuade Rooms**

And then, finally, the most recent inhabitants, and the quietest.

You won't see them at first because they have learned the same rule as the trees. Don't fill the volume. Don't make noise.

They are not many. Maybe a few hundred across an area the size of a small country. You can walk for days in the green cathedrals and think you are alone, and then notice that a pillar thirty feet away has been breathing.

They are short - not from being mountain people genetically, but from a lifetime of ducking - broad-chested, because four kilometers of air even this honeyed still needs pulling. Their skin is dyed faint green-brown from moss tannin, permanently stained at hands and feet. They go barefoot because anything with a sole fills with water. They cut their hair short or let moss grow in it.

Their villages aren't villages. A single family platform, woven *with* the heather while it is still growing - tying two live trunks toward each other, laying cut saplings across, letting the tree heal around them until the floor becomes a knot in the tree itself, level, springy, forty feet up where the dragonflies hunt less. On it, a low hut of split pith-wood that breathes, that lets winged things pass through. A clay basin where they keep a small, smokeless fire - because heavy wood rots and steel rusts to nothing in a week here - feeding it only with resin nodules that pop out of the heather like amber.

Their trails are not horizontal. Horizontal means pushing through wall after wall of hanging root. So they go up and down. Notched-pole ladders - always tied with living vine, so the vine tightens - that disappear upward into a curtain and reappear three rooms over. They navigate by sound: the roar of a big room means a room empty enough to sleep in, the high buzz of beetles means a fruiting wall, the sudden drop to quiet means a brood of hawks above.

Technology is what regrows. Stone flakes for scraping. Not for killing - there is almost nothing big enough to kill that way - but for shaving the sweet pith from heather, for opening bromeliads without spilling the cup of clean water inside. Pouches woven from aerial root fiber that never mildew. Cordage that is still alive.

They don't talk much when they move. They whistle, low, through wood whistles tuned to cut through the insect roar without adding to it - two notes meaning *way open*, three meaning *hawk*, one long falling note meaning *come here, room with fruit*. Children learn to be still early. A child who can sit perfectly still for an hour, mistaken for a burl on a trunk, is praised.

At night they gather on their platforms when the clouds lift - for ten minutes, maybe twenty, the sky opens. Then you can see how high you really are. Stars sharp as glass in thin air. Far, far below, a dark sea of forest and weather you will never walk again. And above, nothing. They have stories that the mountain was once sharp, that it wanted to stab the sky and failed, and out of shame covered itself in a blanket and went to sleep, and that they are the lice that keep its sleep from being lonely. They don't try to own it. They ask a room to have a floor. They ask a bromeliad for a drink. They ask a flock to pass to one side.

If you stay until that brief, cold evening clearing, when all those millions of wings fold at once and the sound drops like a tide going out, you will hear them finally - small human sounds. A wooden spoon on clay. A low cough. A soft laugh from a platform you thought was empty.

And then the clouds come back, and the warmth returns, and the green closes, and the mountain resumes humming to itself, that vast, patient, indoor continent.
