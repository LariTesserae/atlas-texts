The people live there first.

Not in the way you thought anything would live there — not thriving, not putting down roots. Staying. Persisting. The way life holds a hot spring.

You don't see them at first because they are part of the porch-shadow and because they don't move fast enough to catch your eye. They have learned the pace.

There are maybe twenty. Maybe thirty, if some houses hold two. They are all technicians, and they are all retired from being technicians, and they are all children of technicians who never left. The geothermal co-op stopped posting this as a manned station years ago, but someone has to watch the gauges, drain the sump pump filter, hose the algae out before it clogs the trench. And if you have to do that every day, you might as well push a house on stilts out of the sheetwater and run a wire to it.

They are thin the way people get when they live in heat that never breaks. Not starved — just rendered. The air takes everything off you, so you stop putting it back on. Shorts cut from old coveralls, sports bras gone gray at the strap, bare feet with soles thickened to leather from standing in warm mineral water all day. Everyone's hair is lank, stuck to their head, because drying is a concept from upstairs. Their skin always shines. Not with sweat — with the room. Condensation forms on them just like it forms on the bolt heads. If you pressed your palm to a shoulder it would come away damp and a little salty and a little gritty with moth scale.

They sit. That's what they do.

On the pallet-wood porches, under the struggling AC units that drip their own little runnels onto the floor below, they sit in white plastic chairs and old office chairs gone rusty. They look out. In a place you can see edge-to-edge, looking out is a job. You watch the water make its way to the center. You watch the light from the amber work string get caught in the mist and not go any farther.

They speak rarely, and when they do it's in the low thrum register, a little louder than the engines in their molars — HUH? The bearing on Engine Two goes *weee-uuu* every thirty seconds and they time sentences to it. Half a generation down here and they've developed the manners of people in a library built inside a boiler.

They don't own much, but what they have has been made to live in the wet. Nothing on the ground. Everything on stilts, on hooks, in buckets. A shirt hung under a metal roof will never dry, but it will also never rot quite the way cotton should, because the roaches get to it first.

That's who else lives there, and you have to include them as residents, because there's a treaty.

The roaches are the custodians. Big American cockroaches, mahogany-backed, two inches long and warm to the touch. During the day they drink. They line the black streaks on the basalt like living grout, mouths to the stone, antennae testing the flow. You think they're disgusting until you watch one house. The house without many roaches is the house where the black mold has taken the underside of the porch and the tin sweats pink with it and the people inside cough. The houses with a good, thick, slow-moving population — clean. They eat the dead skin, the sheaf of moth scale, the spilled rice, the mold before it sporulates. The old woman on the far stilt — four houses down from the whining engine, the one with the tomato — she'll tell you not to kill them. Not in a preachy way. Just, flatly: "We don't do that." She has a little whisk broom and she sweeps them off her cutting board like crumbs, patiently, one by one.

Under them, on the floor proper, the floor you don't want to put your bare feet on at first, until you have to — the isopods. Woodlice, sow bugs, but scaled wrong. Some as big as your thumb joint, some as big as your fist, slate-gray and wet and always on top of each other, rolling, unrolling. They live for the trench, that shallow moat around the edge of the chamber. The trench is their highway and their farm. They eat the green-black algal mat that chokes it, the slumped liverwort, the drowned moths. When you walk, the floor shifts two inches left of your boot because a hundred of them decided to move. The kids, when there are kids — there are two now, a boy and a girl who share a container-panel house and own one pair of rubber boots between them — they don't play with the isopods. Not cruel, not kind. You don't play with the plumbing.

The third nation is the air.

The moths own the top twelve feet of the room. You thought at first there were maybe a few hundred. Eye adjustment betrays you. It's thousands. Tens of thousands. Pale tan, the exact color of wet concrete and dead skin, bodies thick as a finger with fur that clumps in the damp. They never land. They can't afford to; the bats would get them on the walls. So they fly forever in the big, hot, still-up-there where the air is hottest, in a slow, toroidal churn around the bare amber bulbs. Not frantic like moths around a flame upstairs. Their wingbeats are heavy, aqueous. You can hear them if Engine Two is between whines, a dry shuffling, like someone turning the pages of a phone book very slowly. They drink humidity. They breed in the guano-packed cuts in the ceiling. They live for maybe a day and then fall — and that's what keeps everyone else fed. All day long you see them tumble, spiral down, still fluttering weakly, to be taken by roach, isopod, fish.

For the bats. The ceiling is not actually flat if you look. Where the boring machine pulled back there's a chewed-out scallop, a roost ten feet deep and twenty across. It thrums. Five hundred Mexican free-tails maybe, or something that became free-tails down here, folded in on themselves like wet umbrellas. They are smaller than you expect and their faces are always dripping. They drop down in slices through the moth cloud, mouths open, never echo-locating loud enough to beat the engine thrum — they've learned to see with heat and with the faint sodium wash. They stink. Everything under the roost is lacquered black-white with guano that never dries and hisses faintly where it touches hot rock. And yet the air down here would be unbreathable without them. They are the only thing keeping the moths from forming a solid ceiling and dying all at once and choking the trench.

And for the sump. In the exact center, where the entire floor, with its millimeter skin of moving water, finally admits it was a bowl all along — a concrete-lipped pool maybe six feet across. Opaque, warm as blood, with a skin of mineral oil and shed wings. If you kneel, which everyone does at least once a day to check the pump, you see the flash underneath. Blind cave tetras, white-pink, that came in as eggs in some worker's boot tread three years ago and now have a stable population. They don't have eyes anymore, not really. And the larvae of the moths, and the larvae of flies that don't exist upstairs, all wriggling, slow. Everything eating scale and guano and each other, unhurried. The pump breathes in there with a soft, perpetual *gluk*.

And the ones who think they are in charge of all of it — the tomato plant. One plant, in a five-gallon bucket on the old woman's porch, in soil that was carried down in bags from outside. It's yellow, leggy, pitiotic. Its leaves curl in from the salts in the air. It will never fruit right. She wipes each leaf every morning with a corner of her shirt to get the scale off. She talks to it under the whine of Engine Two. Everyone knows not to touch it. Everyone brings it their coffee grounds. In a place where nothing with roots can hold on — where the liverwort and the brave little fern in the trench are already prodigies — that tomato is a cathedral. It's proof that something from outside can be kept alive if you love it at the exact expense it requires.

That is the town. Twenty shining people on porches far apart, watching water run. Ten thousand roaches drinking the walls. Thirty thousand woodlice tilling the moat. A cloud of moths with the weight of damp laundry, cut through by bats. A sump full of blind white fish. A scum of algae that is technically older than anyone in the room.

None of it moves fast. If it did, it would cook. If it did, it would use up the thin, mineral water before it could get back to the center. So you sit, after you shut the sweating door behind you so the heat doesn't get out, and you learn to wait thirty seconds for the bearing whine to finish before you answer.
