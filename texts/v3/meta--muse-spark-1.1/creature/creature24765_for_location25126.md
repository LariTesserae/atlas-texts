Nothing lives here the way a forest has animals. That was the first wrong thought.

What lives here is the city itself. Not in it. It.

You think at first it is empty because it is quiet in the only way you know how to check for life. No birds. No insects. No paw-prints in the film of water on stone. But emptiness would be decaying. An abandoned house falls in. A jungle without animals chokes and rots. This hasn't. This is being kept.

This valley-wide organism — because it is one thing now — lives by holding itself still.

The great fig-banyan-cathedral beings are its bones and streets and houses at once. They are too old and too fused to be called trees. They have no growth rings any more. If you put your ear to the moss — elbow-deep, warm as skin, giving under your palm like a wet lung — you don't hear sap. You hear negotiation. Wood leaning on wood at calculated pressure, root-grafts strangling and supporting their brothers so that no more weight can be added and no weight can be removed. It is not growing. It is bracing. It lives by refusing to change. That is an act that requires constant, terrible effort. That's the groan you hear under the water-roar. The city's back is out and it cannot shift.

What lives in its skin lives from that effort.

In the moss, for instance. That moss is not moss. Or not only moss. Layer after layer of liverwort and sheet-moss a foot thick, saturated and pendulous, each layer living on the layer beneath, which is not dead, not quite. The green closest to the bark has forgotten light. It is white and translucent and it has become a kind of membrane, tasting the sweating stone of the wood, tasting the stone of the stone. It holds the rain that never rains. It lives by being a wick. If you pull it — don't — it tries to stick itself back. Not fast. With a slow, hurt, inevitable creep back toward contact, like a cheek wanting a pillow.

And what lives in the water lives because time forgot it.

Lift your eyes to those coins of water hanging between ferns. There are thousands of them. Some the size of a fist, some as big as a door, perfectly clear, perfectly still, not falling. They are not puddles. They are inhabitants. Each one holds a little bubble of earlier air, from when the air was cooler, and a little flexed skin of this high hot place. They were born when a patch of air forgot which way down was, and they have not been able to die. Inside most of them, nothing. Just hot, distilled bathwater that has been holding itself together for longer than you have names for. But in some of the larger ones, closer to the head of the valley where stone pours water that doesn't fall - there - a movement.

A thread. A hair. No.

A nerve.

A clear worm of refracted light that is not an impurity, but a resident. It does not swim. It hangs in the center of its sphere and keeps the sphere from remembering gravity. There are hundreds of them. Thousands, if you count the smaller beads at the tips of leaves that never drop. They are the city's blood cells. Or its prayers. They drift exactly as much as distance allows, which is not at all, which is a mile, depending how you look.

And what lives in the air lives by folding distance.

You thought an avenue of pillars was thirty feet long when you looked back. That was not an illusion. The street really is thirty feet long from that angle. From this angle it is five minutes long. What lives in that discrepancy is something you only see when you stand very still until the roar becomes silence again.

Look down one of those colonnades where wind is forced through living throats, a great humid organ chord that never changes key. The light makes hard short shadows that should move but don't. At the edge, where shadow meets too-clean light, the edge twitches. Not like an animal. Like a seam. And along the seam, briefly, you see the street that is behind you overlapping the street ahead. Both true. In that pleat there is a bulk, a pause, like dust in a sunbeam but made of place.

They collect there. The Pleat-dwellers. They are not thin. They are where thinness lives. They are long, if length means something here, and they are always facing away, because they are made of the act of the valley looking at itself from the wrong angle. When you walk, you push through them without feeling; they part. When you stand still long enough, you feel them settle on you the way hot mist settles, noticing that you are a place where time still tries to happen, where water wants to fall, where a shadow wants to move. They like that. They gather a little thicker around you.

This is why no animal can live here. The valley solved biology without needing a nervous system that moves. It perfected plant as architecture, water as furniture, air as hallway, distance as door that can be left ajar. An animal, with its twitch and its hunger and its need for things to happen in order, would be a draft. The pleat-things and the bead-things and the white moss-membrane would have to notice it constantly. They would have to work harder to keep it still.

They do keep it still. That is the other thing that lives here.

You.

For as long as you stand on the lip looking down, you have not yet stepped in. Your leaf has not yet failed to fall. The bead at the end of your nose has not yet failed to drop. The moment you step down into the root-streets where vines grow down and then up to root in themselves, the city will learn your weight and your heat and your ticking. It will not kill you. It prefers keeping.

It will grow a root-pillar just where you are standing, a little faster than should be possible but under the guise of always having been there, and it will fit you. It will exhale a sphere of warm water around your mouth so you do not need to breathe quite so often. It will fold the thirty-foot street into five minutes again so you can walk forever without arriving, and the pleat-dwellers will pack a little thicker and a little thicker around your shoulders until your shadow stops trying to double.

You will be kept. Like the water coins. Like the leaf that never falls. Held up, held still, exactly as urban and organized as pure biology can be without ever inventing a tool, in that deafening new silence that is just this valley holding its breath.

And it will be like this, like this, like this.
