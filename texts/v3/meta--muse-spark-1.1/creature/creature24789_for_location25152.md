Nothing lives here at first. That's the agreement you make with the place when you step down into it.

You look and you see why nothing would. Too still. Too dim. Too held. The sand that swallows any track, the air that doesn't carry. No food chain to climb. So you think — empty. Preserved empty. A room that went unused and stayed clean.

Then you sit, because there is a low platform of stones that asks you to sit, and because the air has weight and you have come a long way across the hard plain, and you stay long enough that the place starts to include you in its inventory. And you look again at the water.

It is not tannin that makes it dark. Not shadow.

The pan in the floor — the one no deeper than your palm, wide as two hands — is perfectly still. So still that the condensation on the stone roof above it makes a perfect, unbroken reflection. And at the bottom of it, under an inch of clear water, there is a second bottom. That is the first time you see her.

She is low the way the place is low. Not small, but low. A laying-down thing.

For a long time you mistake her for color. The hollow is rust-stained, and she is rust-colored too — not red, not brown, but that dark iron-rose the water leaves when it has sat on stone for a thousand years. A thin mat, a skin, a settling. She fits the pan so exactly you think the rock was polished around her, not that she grew to fill it.

She is not algae. Not lichen, which is elsewhere. When your eyes adjust to the shadowless light, you see that she is translucent at the edges, where she thins out to nothing against the stone. The water above her is clear; the water *through* her is not quite. There is a depth inside her that water alone doesn’t have.

And she is alive.

You know because she does not move, and then, absolutely, she does.

A drop has been forming on the undercut above you while you watched. You felt the cold prerequisite of it more than you saw it. It swells, holds, lets go. It falls one foot, two feet, without wind.

It strikes.

The place should ring. It doesn’t. The soft walls take it, you were told.

She takes it.

The drop hits the surface and the surface barely breaks. And the mat underneath — she — flinches. A slow, whole-body flinch that takes a full five seconds to complete. A contraction. From edge to center, a tightening, like an eyelid or a mouth, but with no eye, no mouth. The rust color deepens as she draws herself together, the translucent edges pulling in, wrinkling the water above her into a faint, oily pattern. Then, even more slowly, she releases. The pattern smooths. She is wider again. Flatter. Exactly as she was.

That is how she drinks. That is how she lives.

She is not a single animal. If you had a lens you would see she is many, or she is one that has forgotten how to count itself. A colony, a biofilm, a felt. Bacteria that eat stone and breathe iron, and in the doing have learned a shape and kept it. Threads finer than hair woven into a hide that is also a lung, also a stomach, also a memory. For longer than the grass has owned its crack, for longer than the niche was rubbed into the west wall, she has been there, under her inch of water that never goes dry and never overflows because she regulates it. She drinks the stone’s sweat, and the stone warm keeps her warm.

She does not need to hunt. There are no insects. There is only the slow weep of minerals out of the sandstone, the iron and the salt dissolved in each bead on the ceiling. That is enough for a life that counts time in condensation. One drop a minute, sixty an hour, more than a thousand drops in a day that are all the same day. She has no predators. Nothing here is fast enough to eat her before she would close, and nothing here is hungry enough to try.

She does have neighbors, after a fashion. The black-green lichen on the north-facing seep is her far cousin, an outpost of her own kind that learned to live without standing water. The waxy stems in the crack live off the damp she puts into the air when the day is hottest and the stone exhales. They keep their distance, as she keeps hers. Each owning its own stretch of emptiness.

And people have lived with her. That is what the niche is.

Someone, a long time ago — long enough that their rubbing looks like water’s work — found this room and understood what it was, and that something lived in the cup. They rubbed out a place for a lamp. The dark stain is not oil. Not entirely. It’s tallow, maybe, or rendered fat, left to smoke into the stone over many evenings, so that a faint, warm light would fall on the pan and she would be seen doing her one thing. They leveled three stones to kneel on, or to sit. They drove a post, maybe to hang a skin against the wind. They did not take her water. You can tell from the lack of disturbance at the lip of the pan. You could stand dying of thirst in the white plain above and this water would still not be for drinking. That was the term. The place set it. They understood.

She doesn’t see you. She has no eyes. She feels you only as a change in the air — a little cooler where your shadow is, though there are no hard shadows; a little more carbon, a little more tremor in the hush. If you put your palm near the water, not touching, the haze over it will faintly thin, as she warms. If you stay long enough, past the hour where you are sure you’ve seen everything, you will see her begin to answer the niche.

Not often. Maybe once while you are there, maybe not at all. A slow brightening at her center, a faint paling of that iron-rose, like a flush coming up from deep in stone. The color of someone listening.

She is not a god. She is too low for that, too uninsistent. She is something smaller and far older than a god: a habit that became itself. A place where water learned to stay, and in staying, learned to thicken. A keeping.

When you finally stand, your knees will hurt from the low stones. The dust on your boots will have settled again. The tap of the next drop will have fallen exactly on time, and she will have taken it, and smoothed herself again.

She will still be there when the plain above burns, or greens for a week after a hard rain, or is crossed again. The grass will not spread, the lichen will not recede. The pan will be exactly as full. This is what lives here: the thing that keeps the terms.
