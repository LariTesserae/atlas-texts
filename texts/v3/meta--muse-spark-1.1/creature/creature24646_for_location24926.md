At first you think nothing does.

That's the trick of a sparse town. It gives you too much air to look for life in. You stand on the street that is all crown-stone and moss and you see straight through to the grass at the other side and you think, oh — empty. A project left running after the people left. Loom clack and drip and belt slap doing their work for no one.

Then you notice the pruning.

Because living walls don't stay walls on their own. Without hands they fill in. They close their gaps. A window goes blind in a season. A street becomes a hedge in two. And these streets have stayed streets. The moss between the setts is cropped low, like a scalp fresh shaved. The whitewash on the felt-ivy is fresh enough you can still see the brush strokes, broad, unhurried. Someone has been on a ladder. Someone keeps cutting back the rust-creeper on the roofs to ankle height.

You don't see them at first because they are spaced at the same frequency as everything else. There is a rule to how many things can live in the bowl and keep the walls from rotting each other.

There are maybe seven of them.

They are not tall. They were, once, because we would know the word for them otherwise, but town-life has made them shorter and wider, settled them down into themselves the way the land settled into a dish. If you walked past one quickly you would call her an old woman or him an old man — broad through the trunk, soft in the edge, hands that look knotted. If you sat with one, which is the only speed at which you can really meet them, you would revise.

They dressed like builders and then the building grew into them.

Their skin is not bark, but it has learned from bark. Pale where the sun doesn't hit — the inner arm, the throat — and stippled grey-green where it does, freckled with lichen that they don't pick off. Their hair is thin, fine, mostly whitewash-white itself, cut short because long hair snags on branches. When they bleed they do not bleed much, and what comes is clear at first and then white and bitter; the beetles know not to bite too deep. When they are cut they put damp moss on it and keep working. Their joints have the same slight, painful swell to them as the joints where three braided trunks meet and make a barrel that never dries.

They don't hurry. They can't. In the cool they go slow enough to let the town stay slow with them. You'll find one standing in the middle of the Mill at noon, hand resting lightly on a turning shaft, not holding, just feeling for heat, for wobble. Listening with her palm. She will stand like that for a long time. Then she'll reach up with a copper hook and tweak a vine-belt a quarter inch to the left and the squeak will stop.

They are the keepers of the intervals. Because the town runs on intervals — the condensation at morning, the hard rain twice a week, the clack every tenth beat off-rhythm. Their job is not to run the machines. The machines run. Their job is to keep the machines from wanting anything extra.

You see it in the Dye House. One of them lives in there, or maybe *is* the living-in-there. He sleeps in a hammock made of the undyed root-white fabric strung between two walls that are warm to the touch. He wakes when the vats change color. He adds a scoop of powdered stone, or a bucket from the cistern, or nothing at all. He stirs with a pole that is a living branch that has never been cut from the wall, just coaxed out long and paddle-tipped. The vat sighs rust-red. He nods.

They propagate the same way the town does.

No children have ever run in that cool. No one is born here screaming. In spring, when they whitewash, they also take cuttings. A finger-length of willow-wrist, a shaving of calf or cheek that has gone properly corky. They press it into the damp envelope of air between houses, into a compost made of last year's creepers and loom waste. They water it from the well that smells like pennies. They wire a little glass insulator above it so the amber streetlight warms it. And if the air is right and the bowl stays cool, something thickens there, knuckles, then elbows. A new bulge in the gap.

It takes years for one to get tall enough to tend a loom.

In the meantime they do the other thing that lives here, which is maintenance as a form of company. They pass each other on the one real street maybe twice a day and they don't speak. Speech fogs the air and you don't want that. They touch — a shoulder, the top of a head, where the lichen is. When two of them stand next to each other you can see the town using them to measure itself: yes, that much space is enough. A resonant absence between to let air move.

At night, when the amber lamps sputter on and the trunks very slightly close up the way a leaf does, they go inside. Not all the way. They don't have doors that lock. They fold back a moss curtain, step into a wall-pocket that has held their shape for decades, and lean. You can hear, if you are very still, the town inhale around them, wood creaking as it adds a ring, copper gutters ticking cool. The engines underneath cough once, deep in the earth. The spider drinks its drip.

They are not the ghosts of an industrial town that became a forest.

They are what happened when an industrial town decided, reasonably, that the most efficient way to keep building was to become a plant that knew how to weave, that knew how to keep iron from rusting entirely, that knew how to be sparse on purpose.
