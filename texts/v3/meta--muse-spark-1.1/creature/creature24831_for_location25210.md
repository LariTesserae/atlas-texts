They are not troglodytes.

That is the first thing you have to get out of your head. If you go down into The Cut expecting cave people — pale, stooped, blinking — you will not find them. There is still sky. For an hour every day it pours straight down on them like water through a sluice, and they all look up for it. That hour teaches their bodies that the world is still above. 

At a distance, in that noon light, what lives there looks like lichen itself. A dense, dark mat that has learned to hold to rock.

When you get closer, it resolves into people who have taken the dimensions of the place into themselves.

They are short — not from genetics, from practicality. A tall man cannot stand up straight on the second story where a basalt lintel is still settling, cannot pass through a gap that was a gap yesterday and is a doorway today. They have become compact on purpose. Broad through the shoulders and hips from squeezing sideways, from carrying. Everyone carries. There is no cart wide enough for the walkways, no animal that can turn in them. So a child of six carries water in a skin as big as she is. An old woman carries her grandchild and three cinderblocks balanced on a hide pad on her head. Men carry pine beams down the switchbacks from the plain, staggering under timber that is longer than the trail is wide, pivoting at precise notches worn into the basalt walls where a hundred beams before have pivoted.

Their skin is the color of the place. Not black or red, but dusted. You cannot live in unweathered basalt country without being coated in it. The fines get into everything — the sheen of sweat on a forearm leaves a faint gray map. The cinder stains hands and feet rust-brown, ingrained in the crescents of nails, in the knees of their trousers. They wash, obsessively, in the cistern water, but the water is itself full of rock milk, so it never quite takes. Their hair, when uncovered, holds the dust until it looks powdered. Their eyes are what give them away as recent arrivals from up top: wind-burned at the corners from time on the plain herding or trading, and then, in the younger ones who have never lived up top, large and dark-adapted. Not enlarged — just practiced. They see in half-light the way you and I see at dusk. They do not use lamps in the afternoon. Lamp oil is precious and the smoke has nowhere to go.

They are quiet. Living things that live in places that can fall on them learn quiet. It isn't a law. Children still shriek when they play, then check themselves, because the sound goes nowhere and comes back strange and tells everyone where they are. Adults speak in a low register that the rubble doesn't scatter. Their jokes are physical, bumped with shoulders because you are always within shoulder-bumping distance. Their arguments happen in mutters, nose to nose in an alley-roofed crevice, and end fast because you cannot storm off — there is nowhere to storm to.

Because space is vertical and temporary, they are builders who never get to be proud. What lives there is a population of unfinishers. A man will spend three days dressing a basalt lintel to fit true — tapping it with a heavier stone, listening for the ring — and then set it and live under it for a season before the floor beneath, which is really the back of a fallen pillar slowly turning in the damp fines, decides to subside two inches and cracks his work in half. He shrugs. He carries the pieces two houses over to where someone's wall needs chinking. No one tears down. No one clears. The idea of waste is geologically absurd to them. A collapsed house is a quarry that delivered itself. They build on top of it, and then they live on top of that, so the dead — and there are dead — are sometimes interred where they fell, not from ritual but because the rubble over them was already becoming floor. Children learn to walk on ground that is slightly springy with thatch and bone shovels and lost cups compacted beneath.

So the town itself is alive in the way a coral is alive. It creeps upward, a few inches a year, made of hands. The bottom is no longer rock, but a rising breccia of basalt and ash-mud and goat hair and broken pottery and the polished handles of tools worn smooth. If you cut it, it would have strata: dark layers for wet seasons when everyone stayed inside and cooked more, light layers for summers when cinder was moved.

They live twice. 

For most of the day they are cliff-people. They cling to their walls, move sideways, keep one hand touching stone for balance, talk low, watch the seep-wall for new beads of water. They know each square meter of wall by the feel of its damp. The women who tend the runnels know the sound of water moving through grit under a doorstone. The old men who watch for collapse know the difference between the normal ticking of timber under compression and the hiss that means a flake is letting go up high. They have a word — more a held breath than a word — that goes around the town when that hiss comes. Everyone pauses.

And then for an hour at noon, when the sun aligns with the Cut, they are plain-people again. The light, when it finally arrives, is brutal and honest, high-plains light, ultraviolet, unfiltered. For an hour the town is not a crevice; it is a street fair. Roofs are suddenly bright. Laundry shows its true color. The pale dwarf pine at the south end puts out a hard shadow. People climb to the highest thing they can stand on that will not tip and they stand with their faces up. Children run, for real, because for an hour you can see far — from mouth to mouth — and not bump. The goats up top cast long shadows over the lip and the boys who keep them call down, and the sound finally carries clean. That is when they trade, shout prices, argue loud enough to echo properly, look at each other and see who is cut and who is coughing from smoke. They worship nothing up there, they say, but they keep the noon.

What else lives there fills the interstices they leave.

Where a stone wall weeps cold, a fur of moss you could scrape off with a thumbnail, and in it, springtails the size of pepper flecks, popping. In the cistern-bowls between columns, the blind newts — translucent, pink-gilled, the length of a finger, not quite blind but with eyes that haven't decided yet. They came up with the water that remembers being underground. The children keep one as a pet for a day and put it back. At the top of the Cut, where dusted grass hangs over the lip, cliff mice make runs in the cinder, invisible until a stone moves wrong. At true dark, when the shadow has eaten the whole floor and the cookfire haze turns blue, the bats come out of slots in the basalt-colonnade, a species that did not live here before the Cut opened, that found sixty meters of still air where before there was only wind-scoured plain. They hunt the smoke-moths that breed in the thatch. You can hear their wings if the sawing of wood stops.

All of it is provisional. The basalt is still learning to be a wall. The people are still learning to be a town that can live inside a fresh wound without making it a grave. They fit together like the block that lies beneath its own negative: raw, glittering face to raw, glittering face, not yet smoothed, but already holding.
