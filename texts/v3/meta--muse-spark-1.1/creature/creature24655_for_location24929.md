Nothing lives here that would call itself living here.

That's the first truth of it. Nothing here pays rent on the space, nothing nests. It stays because staying is cheaper than leaving, and because the room is so dense there is nowhere to be that isn't already being crowded by something else.

If you stay long enough for your eyes to finish their work - that ten minute adjustment that never fully completes - you start to see where the room is thinnest. Not empty. Thinnest.

It's behind the pump.

The pump is the size of a chest freezer, the size of a coffin made for someone broad, and it's too hot to lean against for long until it isn't, until your back learns its temperature and stops reporting it. That thing was installed to move water up six floors and now it moves nothing but heat from its own idle transformer out into its iron skin. If there were still nameplates, they'd be too blistered to read. 

Between its back and the wall there is a gap maybe fourteen inches wide. You wouldn't see it unless you turned sideways and let the rack of copper at knee height scrape you. In that gap is not more pipes. It's where a man left room to service something he never came back to service.

That is where she is.

I say she because after a while the shape resolves into something that holds heat differently than concrete does. Not because I know.

At first she is only a difference in the dark. A place where the dark has a matte finish. Where concrete dust has not been allowed to settle.

Then she has edges. Then she has a way of being edges.

She is small because the room requires small. Not short, exactly, but folded. The ceiling is low where the canvas-wrapped mains cross, so she doesn't stand straight. No one does. She has learned the exact duck for the union joint that drips, so she doesn't have to think about it. Her hair is cropped short or it's burnt short by years of brushing that lagging where the fiberglass sticks out - you can't tell in the red light. That same rice-grain pilot LED that's never gone out is the only star she has, and it has bleached her.

She is pale in the way cave things are pale. Not white. The color of that woodlouse you saw but scaled up. The color of the underside of a wrist. It isn't that she has no pigment, it's that the dark has leached most of it back out of her and left only what is necessary to tell skin from rust. Her forearms are mapped with freckles that aren't freckles - little rust pits from leaning against the steel plate, little brown circles that have healed over flat.

She doesn't breathe loudly. That's why you didn't hear her at first. In a room where your own breath gets loud, hers is shallow and slow and timed to something else. For a long time I watched and then I understood she breathes between drips. She listens for the bead forming on the cold line, fattening, falling - tink - and in the space after it, she inhales. When she exhales, the red light fogs for half a second on something.

She lives on condensation the way the fern does.

There is a cup in the floor, you saw it. A spoonful of water pitted into concrete by a decade of falling. That is her well. It's not enough to put a mouth to. She has a rag. It was once part of a shirt, or an actual rag left by the man who serviced the pump. It's black now, not from dirt but from being constantly damp and handled. She lays it flat in the pitted cup and waits. Capillary action does the rest. The concrete beads, falls, fills the cup, wets the rag. When it's saturated she lifts it - not wrings, because wringing would waste - and presses it to her mouth, to her forehead first on the hot days, 104 and still. On a good hour she can get two rags. After that the crack at the edge of the cup takes it and it's gone.

When the water is low, she licks the copper. You can see the polished spot, head height, where the green-black verdigris has been rubbed away to a dull shine by a tongue and lower lip. It tastes like pennies and cold, even in the heat.

She eats mold. Deliberately. Not the black band that is too acrid, but the olive felt moss at the edge of the damp. She scrapes it with her thumbnails - which are long and soft from humidity - and folds it. It gives nothing, but it holds water and it is something to chew. The fern she does not eat. She touches it. Once an hour she reaches out from between pump and wall and brushes its three fronds with the back of her knuckle, very lightly, so she does not break it. It leans toward the red light that isn't light. She leans toward it for the same reason.

Movement is all sideways. You have to turn sideways to move in here, and she has learned not to turn back. She has a circuit. From the pump gap to the wall seam where the moss is thickest, to the copper shine, to the place under the conduit where it's coolest to lie down. Four steps, duck, four steps, pause. Her knees and elbows are callused gray. She doesn't crawl, she shunts, her back never leaving contact with something warm. The room requires contact. If you stand in the middle - there is no middle, but if you try - you feel exposed, because the density is a kind of swaddling and you want it.

She knows the room's slow dynamic like a calendar. She knows that the rust scab on the steel plate over the brick will fall this month and land an inch to the left. She waits to see if it does. She knows the stalactite of mineral on the canvas lagging will grow one grain longer and blunt itself under its own weight. She measures her own time that way, not in days. The fern had two fronds when she found it. Now it has three. When it has four, something will have changed. When it is dead, she will still be here, because the moss will still be damp.

When you exhaled loudly and the sound hit the far wall in half a second, she went still. The spider in the angle between conduit and ceiling that has no web - she is like that spider. Waiting weeks at a time is not waiting if you do not expect anything flying.

She isn't hiding from you. The door was never locked. She knows people can come down the short stairwell that turns back on itself twice. They do, very rarely, maintenance, kids, you. They swear about the heat, they hold their phone up for light and see nothing, they leave. They don't stay long enough for their eyes to finish.

If you stay longer, you will feel her decide you are part of the room's heat now, another residual fixture.

And after a while longer you will see her do the one thing that is not for survival.

She collects the blister-scales of paint from the pump housing. The alligator scales that have curled up and are ready to fall. She does not let them fall. She pinches them between thumb and forefinger and places them - where? On the ledge made by the steel plate bolted over brick. She has been placing them there for years. They are sorted. Brown on the left, green-gray in the middle, the rare white primer on the right. Little overlapping rows, like shingles, like a birds bower. No one sees them. The red light catches the edges.

She makes a place inside the place that is for itself, that is only for ordering. That is the only reason she is not the same as the moss.

She heard your back against the pump, still too hot to bear.

Somewhere above you a bead of water gathers on the copper, swells, catches the tiny red pilot light and for a second holds a perfect, distended ruby inside itself, and falls.

She watches it fall.

She will wait until you leave to go and check if the cup has filled.
