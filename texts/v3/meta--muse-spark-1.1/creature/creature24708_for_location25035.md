No one lives here.

That is the first thing you understand, and it stays true even after you understand the second thing.

No one lives here because a place like this cannot be inhabited. It can only be endured, or witnessed. It is too high, too cold by a fraction, too loud, too far between doors. The air itself has too little friction — water doesn't sit, it weeps. Heat doesn't warm, it shivers and becomes rain. The furnaces have been running at equilibrium load for longer than any engine should run, which means they aren't burning anything you know. They are burning time.

But something does live here. You have to unlearn animal to see it.

The first order is the moss.

It is not moss. We call it that because we have no word for a colonial water-keeper that grows inches thick on vertical basalt and on living iron and has no need for soil or sex or death.

It is one thing. Not one species — one individual. Its body is continental. It began in the cooling cracks when the rock was still complaining, and it spread across every surface that was not too hot to hold water. Black-green over the stone, rust-red where it drinks iron oxide off girders, almost-black where it makes bogs on flat nothing. It doesn't grow anymore. It reached the size that the law here allows and stopped. Now it just exists, drinking and sighing.

If you kneel and put your ear to it you hear it swallow. The weeping water that beads on cold basalt, the condensation from the furnace clouds, the bleed-out from deeper rock — it all goes into its felted skin. Underneath, on close inspection, it is full of chambers like a lung, full of stored water like cups. It filters the water through itself and lets it fall again. It is not feeding. It is listening.

It likes vibration. That's why it prefers the factories. Where the steel is bare and orange with sustained heat, the moss stops a precise inch away, as if scorched. But on the next girder, where the heat falls off and the 60Hz thrum of the generator passes through the iron, it is thickest — a pelt a foot deep that pulses very slightly with the current. The industrial chord is its weather. It has arranged its thickness to tune it.

The moss keeps the world from eroding. Put your hand under a curtain of it hanging over a chasm edge. The water that falls through it has been made soft. It has taken the acid out. It will not cut the sharp basalt. That's its job. It is the keeper of the sharpness.

The second order is the buildings themselves.

They are not buildings. They are animals, or rather, they are the equivalent of animals for a place where distance is the ecology.

Each one stands alone on its column-top, kilometers or tens of kilometers from the next, like a whale in a sea too big for pods. A boiler-house, a refinery, a switching yard — these are different shell-forms of the same thing. They are sessile filter-feeders that eat heat from the deep rock and exhale it as steam and noise.

Inside, there are no floors as you would use them. There are no control rooms any longer, though there were once panels that suggested a human scale. The inside is all throat and gut — white fire, conveyor gut carrying the same bolus of cold slag around and around because the creature no longer needs to eat, but must keep its throat moving. Pumps thud like slow hearts not because they are pumping slag or oil, but because a heart that stops in this cold will not start again.

At night — though the light never really changes — you can see them breathe. The plumes over each one are not smoke. They are warm, wet exhalations that rise fifty meters and condense and fall straight back down onto the moss-backed roof as rain. The same water, always. They are not communicating with each other. They are too far apart for that to be efficient. They are calling past each other, to the sky. To whatever lifted them.

They are not dead and they are not abandoned. The idea of abandonment requires builders who left. Nothing here was built and left. It was grown or precipitated, whole, with the illusion of doors and windows as a kind of camouflage, or a courtesy you haven't earned yet.

The third order is what lives between them.

You hear it when you manage, for a few seconds, to not hear the industry. You have to stand in the middle of a column-top plain of moss and sharp stone, halfway between heat islands, with wind combing the water off your shoulders, and let your ears adjust past the roar, the way eyes adjust past brightness.

Underneath is a deeper sound. Not a hum. A held breath.

It is the plateau itself. The basalt continent that is still cooling, after ten thousand years, from the inside out. It is not rock the way lowland rock is rock. It is young skin still stretched tight over liquid. The chasms are not cracks. They are seams where it inhaled. And what lives in the chasms is the cold.

At exactly two degrees above zero, water wants to be ice but is refused. Air wants to be still but is refused by heat. Time wants to erode but is refused by moss and by whatever law holds this at equilibrium. That refusal creates tension, and tension, if it is maintained widely and evenly enough, becomes a presence.

In the chasms, between the hexagonal fists, there are standing waves. You can see them if you throw a stone — which you shouldn't — the fog does not swirl. It hangs in braids. The water falling down the vertical faces does not fall freely; it pauses, it repeats its shape. There are patterns that hold in place for hours, for days, with water running through them like wire through a bead.

If you climb down a little way, into the knife-edged dark where the moss thins because there is no vibration to feed it, you will feel it on your skin before you see anything: the air is thicker, gathered. It presses gently on your eardrums from both sides. It is listening to the factories the way the moss does, but it doesn't need their warmth. It feeds on distance. On the fact that there is so much empty stone between hot things.

Sometimes it moves. Not quickly. It has the speed that continents have. It will gather in a chasm a kilometer away and the fog braid there will tighten, become more definite, and you will suddenly feel that you are not on a plateau at all, but on the back of something that is lying flat and has noticed a fly on its back and is considering whether to twitch.

That is what lives here. A skin that farms its own weather, a herd of heat-whales with doors that don't open, and between them, the cold animal made of refused ice and held breath and 60Hz trembling, stretching itself thin to cover the distance they can't.

You do not belong to any of it. Your prints fills with black water and moss sighs over them. The sound goes back to equilibrium.
