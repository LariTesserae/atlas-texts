At first you think nothing lives here except the green. That it's too loud, too thin, too newly made.

Then you realize the loud *is* the living, and then, a little later, that some of it is listening back.

### The Dew-Readers

You'll see them only after your eyes adjust to looking *through* things instead of at them. Because they are almost not there, on purpose.

The people — if that's still the right word, and here it mostly is — are tall and misproportioned in the way a waveform is misproportioned. A hundred and ten kilos of person stretched over two and a half meters of height, because when ninety-eight percent of your floor is air, weight is a rude thing to have too much of. Their bones are less bone than bamboo: hollow, ridged with that same accreted black carbon the city is made of. Their skin is grey-green, not from pigment, from the thin film of photosynthetic lichen that lives on them the way it lives on the towers. They don't shave it. It helps them taste the condensation coming.

They didn't come here. Or rather, they came here when this place was still hot enough to cook re-entry suits, and they decided to stay while the rock was still soft because someone has to tell a place how to be inhabited before it learns how to be empty.

You see one on a platform that shouldn't hold her. She's standing barefoot on a lattice of struts no wider than pencils, ankles flexing constantly, reading the vibration. Her feet have gone strange — the soles are wide, callused into two long toes that hook over struts like a chameleon's. That's new country feet. The basalt here would slice any soft foot to ribbons. Theirs have learned to be the shoes.

Her ears are gone. Not gone — lidded. Where your ear should be there's a smooth membrane, nacreous, that she can purse shut when the basin starts to ring. When she opens it to hear you, you see the new eardrum fluttering a half inch inside, too big, too delicate. The children here are born with eyes like saucers, all pupil, because the sun never rose through the haze in their lifetime. They see beautifully in what you would call near-dark. They go blind in direct sunlight. They have no word for shadow because they have no word for light that isn't diffuse.

They do not live in houses. A house is a place that is mostly walls. They live on *routes* — a preferred circuit of towers that they walk while the builders adhere to them. The builders like them; the pale glitter, the motes smaller than dust, congregate more thickly on their skin, in their lungs. If you hold your lantern still near an old Tender's mouth you can see her exhale a little storm of them, their lights blinking as they go out to thicken a railing. She is part of the manufacturing staff. She coughs carbon fiber when she has a cold.

They are sparse because they have to be. Maybe two hundred in the whole twenty kilometers. There isn't enough true water to run a denser system, and they like distance that isn't distance. They have learned the 2.2 trick that the city is built on: lean.

Watch two of them a kilometer apart. Tower Three and Tower Four. One lifts a gourd of the clear, iron-tasting condensation water. The other leans forward, into a particular knot of lattice where space has been folded over and held with a trick of gravity. For you, they are shouting across an impossible gap. For them, their foreheads are almost touching. They drink from the same cup. Then they lean back and the kilometer returns. The city isn't empty. It's pleated. They live in the pleats.

### The Hangers and the Singers

That loud, though. That pressure in your chest.

The loudest citizens are the ones in the leaf-axils. You thought the riot of vines was just trellis. It's tenements.

Each waxy, dinner-plate leaf funnels condensation into a cup where a frog lives. Not like the frogs you know. These are almost all throat. A small olive body with legs like afterthoughts and a vocal sac that balloons to the size of a grapefruit, translucent, veined with light. The male inflates and the membrane of the sac is so thin you can see the condensation beads on the other side through it. When he calls, he doesn't chirp, he drops a single, low note that the basalt bowl returns to him half a second later, deeper. So he calls again, a little off rhythm, and the echo stacks. A thousand of them doing that, out of phase, make the chord you feel in your sternum. They are tuning the basin. They have been tuning it since before the people came. Their tadpoles live entirely in the twenty minutes of falling water, wriggling from leaf-cup to leaf-cup during the nightly drip, before it dries.

Above them, the air itself is a nation.

The thread-bats — they are not bats, but you need a word to make your brain put a mammal shape on it — are mostly wing. Forty centimeters of bone that's air and a body that's a string of organs wrapped in skin, as thin as your finger, trailing. They cannot land. They hook into the lattice with a spur on their wing-elbow and sleep hanging like a forgotten thread. They live by drinking the eye-sized dew drops off mid-air before they fall. In the condensation hour, they go frantic, a snowstorm of sheer wings that you can see through to the tower beyond.

And the chains — you thought at first they were necklaces left hanging. They are colonies. Each link is a beetle the size of your knuckle, matte black, clutching the abdomen of the one in front of it with legs fused into hooks. Together they make living cables ten, twenty meters long that drape between vines and towers and hum. They are the bass section for the frog choir, stridulating by rubbing their hard backs together. When a piece of chain breaks off, it becomes a snaked soloist until it finds another chain to join. The Tenders harvest a chain now and then and drape it across a gap they want to become a bridge, and the beetles learn to be a bridge, and then the builders build around their shape in carbon.

Nothing here is one thing. The dragonflies the size of your hand are shepherds, herding clouds of blackfly motes that are indistinguishable at a distance from the builder motes. Which are which? Even the Tenders aren't sure anymore. Some of the blackflies have started to accrete carbon too.

At the very bottom, after the drip stops, when the vines shiver and the ringing becomes a single high whine that makes your teeth ache, everything drinks at once. The frogs tip their sacs and swallow. The thread-bats wring themselves. The people open their mouths and tilt their faces up to the lattice above them, catching the last beads from a hundred meters up that have been making their long, slow fall.

And the basin, that young black glass bowl still warm from being made, holds that water for a day in its sharp gut, sweating it back out at 27 degrees, and the city, which weighs less than air should allow, exhales and goes back to slowly, patiently, building more of almost nothing for them all to live through.
