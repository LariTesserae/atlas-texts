They live pressed flat.

At first you don't see them as a them at all. You see a wetness. A sheen on black glass that shouldn't have a sheen, because this glass was born yesterday and has never known rain. The interior of the blister looks varnished, lacquered, like the rock itself is sweating out a second skin. And then that skin ripples.

It takes your eyes a second to catch up to the light inside, because the light is wrong there — too thick, too slow — and the rippling isn't random. It has grain. Direction. Legs.

There are hundreds of them. Maybe more than hundreds. The pocket is only as big as a heart, a melon, two cupped hands that don't know how to hold depth correctly, and they have tiled it completely. Floor, walls, that ceiling that isn't a ceiling. No rock visible except where a bead of deep water wells up and they part around it like lips.

Individually, each is small enough to lose.

About the first joint of your thumb. Pale in a way that living things are only pale when they have never needed color — not white, translucent, the color of hot-spring shrimp, of things boiled before they are dead. Of uncooked rice noodles left in steam. You can see the gut through the belly, a dark, rust-red thread where it's full of the mineral scum it eats. No eyes. No face, really. The front end is just a soft taper that ends underneath, where the mouth tucks — a ventral slit, a small, puckered vacuum.

What you read as a head is a crown. Four to six filaments, feathery, frilled, the only part of them that has any color at all — a faint, bloody pink — beating, beating in the wet heat. Gills. They pulse out of sync until you breathe into the pocket, and then they all flare at once, tasting your weather.

And the legs. They are not legs the way an insect has legs. They are too long, too jointed, too deliberate. Six of them, spidery, glassy, each ending not in a foot but in a point. A fingertip of hard, chitin-mineral, blackened at the tip like it's been dipped in the glass itself. When they walk, they don't walk on the rock. They piton into it. You hear it if you stop breathing: *tik. tik.. tik.* A hundred quiet ticks as they pick their way over each other's backs, over the slick razor of the newborn basalt.

Because the rock is too sharp to live on. That is the joke. The blister's walls are still conchoidal, still fresh-knapped by cooling, covered in needles and daggers of basalt glass that would slice any normal skin to ribbons. So they knap it.

That's what the glitter is.

Almost every single one is holding something — clutched up against that underside mouth, or worked between two of those needle fingers. A flake. A shard they have struck off the wall. No bigger than a grain of rice, but worked. With an edge. You watch one plant its back points, brace, and strike the wall with its front points: *tik.* A flake pops free, bright in the thick light. It catches it before it falls. Turns it over. Tests it. Then it starts to scrape.

They are farming the sweat of the mountain. The water that wells up from the deep — impossible water at 6,000 meters, bleeding out where there should be no water left — carries dissolved iron, sulfur, silica, some thin bacterial soup from kilometers down. Where it touches air it crusts, white and rust-yellow. That is their pasture. They scrape it with their own tools made from their own house. They lift the harvest to that ventral mouth and suck it clean. The flake flashes, then dulls, then is discarded, then someone else picks it up and re-works it.

No building. Nothing built at all. No burrows, no walls, no piles. Only subtraction. They keep the room smooth by keeping it sharp. The whole carpet of them is a single, slow mouth.

Underneath them, under the first layer, there is a second layer, and under that a thin paste — bodies that got weak and were not fast enough to move, dissolving in the steam and their own enzymes, being scraped up and eaten in turn. You can see them doing it without urgency or cannibal malice. A still one is just another crust. In the wettest cracks, where two glass daggers make a V, there are clusters of eggs that look exactly like the water droplets that never fall. Clear, fat, clinging. Some of them twitch.

They know you are there only as a change in humidity. When you exhale near the lip, the whole living varnish pauses. The gill-crowns flare. A shiver runs across the pocket, not from fear — they have never had a predator — but from chemistry. You are a sudden dilution, a cold front, a new mineral. Then they decide you are not edible and resume. *Tik. tik.*

They don't want the outside. They couldn't survive it. The killing cold a centimeter away would freeze their gills solid. The thin air would dry them to paper in seconds. They are a closed loop: mountain heat for warmth, mountain water for drink, mountain rock for food and knives, each other's bodies for everything else. An entire, self-contained, tool-using, lightless civilization in a place that is still technically cooling from being born.

If you cover the hole with your palm, you feel them through the rock — not moving, but warm. Like holding your hand over a tiny, working mouth that has learned how to hold a blade.
