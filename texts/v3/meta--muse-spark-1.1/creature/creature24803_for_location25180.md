They live in the walls. That's why you didn't see them at first.

From the switchbacks you thought the towers were the city. From halfway across the white plazas you thought the towers were buildings. It's only when you get close enough that the light from your own boots shows you the texture wrong that you understand the towers are not buildings. The towers are the inhabitants.

Or rather - the towers are what the inhabitants have excreted around themselves for so long that inhabitant and habitation have stopped being two words.

You have to crouch to see one. To really see one.

You pick a trunk that isn't the capital-trunk, not the continent in the center, but a smaller one at the edge of a plaza, maybe thirty feet high, its cap-plates overlapping like wet paper. You kneel. The dust sighs but doesn't puff. Up close the fluting isn't fluting. It's overlapping shingles of the same dry, woody fungus grown and grown over itself, and every shingle has a seam. And every seam is busy.

There are thousands on this one trunk alone.

The first one you see is completely still, so still you thought it was part of the wall. It's pale. Not white, not cave-white — the color of old fingernails, of tallow left in a cellar. Its back is a single polished plate, lacquered, convex, dustless because the dust never gets a purchase on it. It has no eyes. Where eyes would be there's just a continuation of that plate, smooth down to a blunt head. Underneath, tucked close, are legs. Many. Too many to count quickly, and each one jointed backward from what you expect, thick at the body and needle-thin at the tip. Each tip ends in a little black pad, and each pad holds the wall.

That is how slow they are. You watch for a long time before you see a leg move. It lifts — a millimeter, a fraction — hangs in the air as if reconsidering the cost of the effort, and then sets down again, just off from where it was. The whole animal shivers. If you put your hand close you can feel the heat coming off it. Not warm-blooded heat. Work heat. The heat of constant, patient cementing.

Their mouths are underneath, you realize. You have to lie almost prone to see. A complex, retractable lower face, all fine, comb-like maxillae and a dark, wet tongue. That tongue is the city.

It comes out and licks. It's always licked. It scrapes up a grain of the final-form dust, a flake of fallen lichen, a dead brother's antenna, a spore that didn't catch. It pulls it in, mixes it in a crop you can see bulging slightly at the throat, with a drop of gut-water that they make themselves - they don't drink, they have never drunk; they condense the breath of the stone on the hairs of their backs and wring it internally - and then they spit. A dab of dark mortar, almost black, that dries to the color of the tower in minutes. They put it down. They put another one down next to it. For a year. For fifty years.

They are not ants. Ants hurry. These are something that came down here before hurry evolved. The closest word your surface brain has is woodlouse, is sowbug, is isopod that lost the sea and kept walking until the sea was a myth, but even that is too fast, too small. Each one is as long as your forearm, as heavy as a loaf of bread. When one does move across the white plaza you can hear the faint tick of its claws, one... then... one... then... one, like a clock that only counts once an hour.

You think there are castes, at first. You are wrong, but almost right.

There are the Masons. They are almost all Masons. That pale, blind, lacquered thing with its belly to the wall, chewing dust into house. They line the outside of every trunk and the inside of every tunnel. If you hold your breath and press your ear - not touching, never touching, because touching would be rude and also you might damage a year's work - to the wall, you can hear them chewing. A faint, endless rasping, like mice in a wall made of paper.

There are the Singers. They are older. You can tell because their backs are thicker, scratched, bleached lighter by the foxfire. They do not build anymore. They climb, over years, up to where the shelf-plates meet and make those balconies, that sudden architecture that looks intentional. They wedge themselves into a pit that a Mason has left for them, exactly their size, and they lift their backs. Under the lacquer is a file, a vast ridged instrument that runs the length of their abdomen. Over it they draw their last pair of legs, which have become bows, hardened and hairless. That is the roar. One Singer alone would be a loud insect. A balcony of twenty is deafening. A city of ten thousand balconies is the wall of sound you felt in your molars halfway down the switchbacks. They do not sing to each other. They have no ears. They have tympana all along their sides that feel the air shake. The song is a way to keep the dust from settling. It is a way to shake loose spores from the caps above. It is a way to tell the fungus: *keep growing, we are still here, keep growing*. It rattles loose the salt flowers so the Masons have fresh mineral. It is breathing, in common.

There are the Deep Ones. You don't see them unless a Mason leaves a window open. Inside the trunks the air is warmer, sweet-sour, hay-sweet, ammonia-sour, and lit brighter by the fungus's own glow. In there the tunnels are worn concave, polished by ten thousand forearm-length bellies dragging. And in the widenings, in little chapels whose walls glisten with mycelium, they cluster. They are fatter, softer, their back-plates never quite hardened. They do not build. They farm. They stroke the fungal pile with those comb mouths, not to eat it but to thin it, to spread its white felt, to keep it from fruiting too early. The fungus fruits anyway, right through them sometimes. You see one where a white stalk has come up through the joint between thorax and abdomen and opened a flat, glowing cap right above its head, and the animal is perfectly still, letting it happen. That is where the eggs are. Not laid. Grown into. A Mason brings a pellet of egg and fungus to a Deep One, and the Deep One tucks it into a pocket in its own side, and the fungus seals it. Eight seasons later — eight times the rock above has inhaled a little wetter air and exhaled it — the pocket opens and something pale and leg-shaking and bright spills out, already chewing at the wall that made it.

Nothing is wasted because they have no concept of waste. Their dead do not fall. A Mason that has finally stopped - after thirty, forty, maybe a hundred years of putting down spit - simply stops on the wall. Its grip never releases. Its brothers build around and then over it. You can see them in cross-section where a wall has chipped: the perfect, repeated arch of lacquered backs like rebar, like the strata of the limestone itself.

They notice you only as a change in vibration. For a few minutes after you knelt, the nearest Masons froze. Not stopped — froze mid-dab, tongue out. The Singers missed half a stroke, and in that gap you heard it: the rest of the city, still roaring, far away. Then the Masons resumed. Your footprints in the bright waste-carpet of the plaza will stay for months until a crew comes and meticulously, grain by grain, eats the disturbed glow and lays it flat again.

They have never known rain. They have never known sky. They have no word for outside, because the city is the world and the world is finished. It finished itself so perfectly that nothing else needed to happen. And so for longer than men have had fire, they have done the only thing left to do in a finished world: maintain it. Add a little more. Make it a little more itself. Tap the wall. Lay a grain. Sing while you do it so you know you're not alone in the cool and the dim.

Far toward the center, the capital trunk hums louder than the rest, not because its Singers are louder but because there are more of them, layer on layer, a mountain of backs and bows all saying at once, in a voice made of dry legs on dry backs, the single thought this whole place has ever had.

And if you are still, and if you keep your hands to yourself, and if you learn, as everything here has learned, to move very, very slowly, you can start to hear it not as noise anymore, but as what it is.
