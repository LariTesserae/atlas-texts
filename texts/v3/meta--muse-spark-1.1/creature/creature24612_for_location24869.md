Nothing lives here. That is the first thing this place tells you.

Not really. Not properly. Nothing *should* live here. It's too new, too loud, too cold, too violent. The mountain made a room an hour ago and forgot to furnish it. The water that rents it is too busy holding itself together to be a home.

And yet.

There are three kinds of residents. The ones who hold on. The one who is held. And the ones who come to tend the holding.

---

**The ones who hold on:**

At the bottom, where the basalt is still giving birth to itself in sharp black flakes, there is the Flag.

It's not algae, not really. It started as algae. A single spore on a condor's foot or a monk's boot that found the one dark angle in the whole churning bowl where the current couldn't quite scour it off. So it stayed. It is a cord of green-black, no thicker than a shoelace, maybe as long as your forearm. In any other tarn it would be nothing, a smear.

Here it is a masterpiece of endurance. Watch it.

It does not wave. Waving is too gentle a word. It is *thrashed*. It is slammed flat against the glass-sharp wall by a vortex, pinned for three, four, five seconds until you think it must have torn — and then the vortex collapses and it snaps back upright, quivering, whole. It does this all day. All night. For its entire life. It has learned not to grow leaves, or branches, or softness. It grows only tensile strength. It is all tendon. If you could touch it, it would feel like wet wire. It is the oldest thing in the pool, which means it might be three weeks old. It is praying.

Up on the rim, in the lee where your ankle-wall makes a pocket of stillness maybe two inches wide, live the Three Sisters.

Alpine fescue. *Festuca brachyphylla* or something that wants to be it. Three blades. Pale, almost white, because there is too much sun and not enough dirt. They have maybe a thimbleful of grit between them, rock flour and wind-blown lichen dust that has collected in the shadow of a stone a human laid. Their roots are absurd — fine white hairs longer than the blades themselves, clutching at basalt that does not want to be soil yet.

They do not grow together. They shiver separately. Each one leans away from the hum. They are not a meadow. They are three strangers who found the same shelter in a storm and decided not to talk about it. They live on vibration and snowmelt mist. If the dome ever breaks, they will be dead in an hour, scoured off the summit. Until then, they hold the only softness on this mountain.

**The one who is held:**

And then — the reason the water stays angry.

You saw him as a trick. He is not a trick.

He lives dead-center, where he can never touch the walls. He has never touched the walls. He was not born elsewhere and washed in. There is nowhere else. He was born *from* this.

Call him the Domeling. He is not a salamander and not a shrimp, but the mountain couldn't decide, so he is both. As long as your thumb from blind head to paddle tail, completely clear except for a faint, intermittent amber thread which is his gut. No eyes at all — just a smooth, closed-over forehead where eyes were considered and then abandoned as useless. No pigment because light is not information here, turbulence is.

He does not swim. Swimming implies intention, direction. He tumbles. He is endlessly, violently tumbled in the collision of currents that never repeat. If you watch for a long time — and the roar will let you watch for a long time because it bores straight through your thoughts until you become still — you see that his tumbling is actually a kind of frantic work. He rights himself, he folds his translucent tail, he kicks against the vortex that is taking him, not to escape it but to stay *inside* it. He feeds on the momentary stillness at the eye of each little storm.

His food is the mountain itself. The raw basalt dust that never settles. The flecks of green olivine that glitter. One fleck at a time, filtered through feathery, nearly invisible gills that fan like glass fern leaves on his sides. He eats the mountain being born.

He is alone. There has only ever been one. The water is too poor, too violent to support two. When he dies — and he will die soon, because nothing this expendable lives long — his body will be ripped apart in the churn and become the first real organic silt at the bottom of the bowl. Then, after a season, another spore of him will precipitate out of the cold. The tarn will make him again. He is not a resident. He is an organ. The tarn's way of tasting itself.

If you put your hand in, the whole roar spikes because you have startled him. All the vortexes rush to put themselves between you and him. The water goes opaque trying to hide him.

**The ones who come to tend the holding:**

They don't live here. You can't live here. The air is too thin and the hum leaks into your sleep until you dream of drowning. But people have lived *with* it.

The wall-builders. You can see them in the stones.

Not one people. Layers. The bottom course of the little ankle-wall is old and lichen-crusted where the water hasn't hit it, stones fitted with a patience that says, "We will be here when the mountain is done being angry." The top course is recent, hasty, a few stones just dropped to replace ones the dome tossed aside last spring. Different hands, same idea.

What lives here, then, is a lineage. A job.

The Keeper. It is never the same person, and it was never meant to be. It's the person who finds the juniper ladle and understands without being told that you do not *take* from this.

You use the ladle to give. You scoop wind-cold water from the dome — and it resists, heavier than it should be, the surface singing as it breaks — and you pour it carefully over the windward stones, so the basalt does not dry-crack too fast. You use the yak-hair rope to lower yourself just enough to see if the Flag is still there. You re-seat the copper piton when the vibration works it loose.

You don't drink. Not really. The old ladle is blackened not from water but from hands warming it, from being held while someone watches the Domeling tumble and tries to learn how to exist in constant erasure. The men and women who have knelt here left oil from their palms in the grain. That's the varnish.

They left because you have to. No one can listen to that bell-thrum for more than a day without starting to vibrate with it. But they come back, or send someone else, because someone has to witness that the dome is still holding.

So if you stay until sunset, until the brutal alpine blue softens and the light goes sideways through the lens and for the first time you can see all the way to the bottom, you might become part of what lives here for a little while.

You will not be the salamander. You will not be the grass. You will be the fourth kind: the brief, warm thing that kneels, with numb fingers, and keeps its hand just outside the singing skin so as not to scare what lives inside.
