What lives here isn't a population. That's the first thing you have to unlearn.

At first you think: dragonflies, shrimp, lilies, a big thing under a leaf. Tenants in a marsh. A list you could make if you had a net and jars. That isn't wrong, but it's like describing a body as "mostly water and a lot of red things moving around."

Give it an hour. Let your weight settle into that cool, weeping rimstone. Let your breathing slow down until it fits into the interval between breaths of the thing under the water. Then the list comes apart and you start to see who is really in residence.

#### The Rowers

They are the most obvious, because they are in the air you were going to use.

Call them glassy dragonflies because you have language for dragonfly. They are not that. Their bodies are not glass, they are clear the way clear water is clear — you can see straight through to the other side but there is something in there that bends light wrong. No gut, no sac, no stripe. Just a faint, brighter filament running head to tail like a filament in a bulb. Their four wings don't beat. They scull.

The air in the bowl is too thick. You feel it on your skin as a kind of pressure, like being a little too deep in a pool. The arches are making it that way. For you, it makes each breath slightly too easy, slightly too much. For them, it makes the air an ocean. They row themselves through it with long, oar-like sweeps that never hurry, never buzz. That's why the bowl is quiet.

There are thousands of them. You didn't see them at first because they are holding position. All of them, nose into the same invisible current, fanned in strata from ankle-height up to maybe ten meters, where they get so sparse the white-gold light erases them. Compass needles pointing at the center of the bowl.

They are drinking. The slow rain that forms five meters up out of nothing — each droplet falling like honey — each droplet is their entire purpose. They tilt, open a mouth you didn't see they had, and take it whole. The water was never going to hit the marsh. It was always going to be intercepted. They are the irrigation system the arches hired. Or grew.

They live a long time. If one dies you never see it fall. Another simply rows into its station.

#### The Turners

Under those huge, armored lily pads, the water is full of holes. Thousands of clean, round holes punched in the pale clay, each the width of your thumb. If you kneel — warm water to your knees now, silt hanging around your calves and never touching you — you can watch them work.

White shrimp. No, not shrimp. Something older that solved the same problem. Blind, colorless, each about as long as your first knuckle. They never stop. Each one owns one hole. It goes down into it head-first, disappears, backs out with a tiny pellet of clay held in its forelimbs, carries it three body-lengths, sets it down with absurd care, smooths it, goes back. Another comes out of the next hole. The water never clouds because they never make a mistake.

They are turning the bowl over. An inch per century, maybe. Constantly rebuilding the clay bottom so it stays pitted, so it stays open, so the water that breathes up from below has somewhere to come through. They eat nothing. Or they eat the water itself, the faint mineral tang in it. Their backs are covered in a fur of bacteria that glows very, very faintly when a cloud — though there are no clouds — passes over the sun that isn't a sun.

And the holes are not just holes. Put your hand flat on the bottom and you feel it: a slow, clockwise rotation of the entire warm body of water, one full turn every few hours. The Turners are making that current. All of them, fanning in their holes, beating like cilia. The bowl is stirring itself.

#### The Keeper

Under the biggest lily — the one in the exact center, cartwheel-wide, its white trumpet sealed tight and smelling of cold cucumber when the Rowers brush it — there is the back.

It breaks the surface like a wet stone. It has been there so long that algae has given up trying to colonize it. It is dark grey, or dark green, or the black of deep clay, depending on how the light is bouncing. No head. No tail. Just a dome, as broad as a low table, with two shallow nostrils that never seem to open and close.

It breathes once an hour. You have to time it. The water around it dimples, then flattens. A sheet of warmth pushes out across the marsh and every Rower tilts half a degree. The lily pads bob, once.

That's the one who was here before the continent gave up. Before the hills became swells. It does not move. It doesn't need to. It is the low spot's reason for being low. The land sagged around its weight. The water table rose to keep its back wet. The whole quiet, bright, slow-falling system is just a way to keep one creature comfortable while it thinks a thought that takes a million years to finish.

Sometimes one of the Rowers will settle on it. The Keeper doesn't notice.

#### The Polishers

You noticed the beetles last because they move at geologic speed.

Fist-sized, iridescent black, each on a tussock or on the weeping rimstone or — most importantly — on the invisible millimeter that lives around the base of the white arches. That perfect gap where clay and water never touch. Where dust refuses to fall.

There is a film there. A faint, living slick of green-black algae that only grows in edited gravity. If it were allowed to grow, it would bridge the gap. It would ground the arches. The bowl would forget how to make slow rain.

The beetles eat it. One step per minute. Mandibles like fine black glass, scraping the very air. They have been doing it since the arches were set down. Their shells are polished by time and by the light that has no source. When they do move you only know because you looked away and looked back.

They are not beetles, really. Inside that armor is the same white, seamless, warm material as the arches. They are the arches' hands.

#### What lives here, then.

Not four species and a monument.

One thing, with four ways of touching the world. A lung — the Rowers, straining water out of thick air. A gut — the Turners, endlessly sieving and stirring. A mind — the Keeper, ancient, flat, dreaming the bowl into further flatness. And a set of careful fingers — the Polishers, keeping the machines that keep the dream from ending clean.

And the arches. The arches live here too. They are not ruins and not tools. They are the agreement the whole thing made with itself a long time after it started: *stay*. They glow a little brighter than everything else because they are remembering, constantly, for everything else in the bowl, what the bowl is supposed to be.

If you stay until your feet prune and your palms have gone numb from the cool, forever-wet rimstone, you will be included briefly. A Rower will hold station just off your nose, clear enough that you can see the filament inside it pulse in time with your heart, and then slower than your heart, until your heart tries to catch up. The water will clear another impossible degree. The light will get even brighter, bouncing up from the white clay through the water through your legs.

It lives here by keeping itself alive, quietly, without ever needing to say so.

That's why there's no birdsong. Nothing here needs to call out.
