What lives here has learned to live without ground.

**In the mass itself —**

Because that is where most of the living happens, and you don't see it at first. You think moss is a surface. It isn't. Here it is a country.

The first citizens are the builders. *Sphagnum* does most of the work, but not alone. There are a hundred kinds of it and they have sorted themselves by how wet they like to be. The driest hummock-tops are crowned with red sphagnum, *S. magellanicum* and *rubellum*, tight as fists, holding their water inside their dead hyaline cells like a miser. A little lower down the hummock flanks, the green, loose *S. papillosum* makes long hanging beards. In the hollows between, the drowned species — *cuspidatum*, that pale, almost translucent green — floats loose in the black mirrors, not rooted, just suspended. It never meant to be firm.

Interleaved through them: haircap moss, *Polytrichum*, that stands up like tiny conifer forests two inches tall, each stem stiff as wire. And the cushion plants — *Azorella* or *Donatia*, you can't tell until you kneel — that grow by dying at the center and expanding at the edge so slowly you would measure it in decades, making perfect hard domes a meter across, so dense you can stand on them. They are older than the pump houses. Press your hand and they give nothing. Under them it is completely dry for an instant, a little desert made by the plant itself, then water sighs in from the sides to correct it.

All of this dies from the bottom up. What you walk on is maybe two percent alive. The rest is the thatch, the felt, the peat in transition. And even within that, it is alive with smaller lives that never need to touch rock. Rotifers. Tardigrades swollen with cold water, plodding between leaves the size of continents to them. Springtails that detonate white when you exhale on them. The larvae of crane flies, fat, gray, almost translucent, living in the water film that coats every stem, eating the dead. Enchytraeid worms, thread-thin and white, threading the lower rust layer. The peat itself breathes out methanogens — not an animal, but you smell them, that low sweet rot under the sharp green smell.

You think of it as soft. To those things, it's architectural. There are shafts and atria and canopied highways in the moss canopy. Water moves through it not as a flood but as capillary traffic, from leaf to leaf to dead leaf, and everything lives in that traffic.

**A little higher —**

Because the green does need a little height. The bog cotton is not cotton at all, but sedge — *Eriophorum*. It puts up a single thin stalk above the swell, and then a puff of white. In the wind that never stops, those heads shiver constantly. Each one is a small, dry bell. When the wind hits them right, ten thousand heads at once, they make that papery rasp you heard, like someone shuffling pages in another room. They are the only thing that actually flowers with any ambition.

The dwarf willow lives here too, but it has given up on being a willow. It lies flat, pressed into the moss by wind and snow, its branches rooting where they touch. If you lift a layer of sphagnum you find it running for meters, a woody secret, its leaves the size of your thumbnail, glossy. In late summer it puts out catkins that never quite get to be furry before the cold takes them again.

And then the walkers on top of the mat:

Voles — or something like voles, high-altitude, dark-bellied — who never burrow into earth because there is no earth. They burrow into peat and sphagnum, making tunnels that hold their shape because the roots hold it for them. Their runs make faint depressed highways between hummocks, and if you sit still long enough you see one nose-up, sniff, vanish. They have hair on their feet.

Snipe and pipits. The few birds that can stand the light and the lack of cover. The Andean snipe here — if this is the Andes, or somewhere like it — drums at dusk, climbing high and then falling, letting wind vibrate through its tail feathers to make that bleating roar that makes you think a pump has broken. The pipits walk, not hop, purposeful, picking chironomid midges off the cotton heads. Midges are the true air force of the plateau. In the sun after a cloud passes, they rise in columns, millions, not biting, just rising and falling, each one a black speck against the white light. They live for a day.

There are dragonflies, but stunted. Little peat darners the color of rust, that hawking low, breeding in the black pools. Their nymphs live two, three years down in the tannin water before they dare come up.

All of them are dark on the back and light on the belly. All of them are silent when the wind is high. All of them are slightly too small, or too flattened, or too hairy.

**Then, the ones with the boots.**

Because people do live here, after a fashion, though the plateau has not made it easy to stay human.

There are maybe eight, maybe twelve, spread over kilometers of green. You will never see them together. They are not a crew. The company rotates them in twos for three-week hitches, because longer and the wet gets into your joints and the light gets behind your eyes.

They have become things that belong to the threshold between tin and moss.

The cutter: You saw one far off, bent over. He is middle-aged or old; it's impossible to tell because his face is windburnt to the same red-brown as the rust layer of the peat. He wears the regulation yellow slicker over three sweaters, rubber boots that come to his thigh, waders actually, patched with bicycle tube rubber and black tape. He carries a long-handled peat spade, but most of the day he just walks his assigned drying patch and turns the bricks. The raw-cut peat bricks are set out like loaves on the moss to weep water. He turns them by hand. Hundreds and hundreds. If he doesn't, they mold under instead of drying over. His hands are always black in the creases, even after washing. The peat is antiseptic; his cuts never get infected, but they never quite heal either.

He has learned to hear through the diesel. He can tell which pump is cavitating by the way it shivers through the soles of his boots. He is slightly deaf in one ear, the ear he sleeps away from the wind on. At night he sleeps in the bunkhouse — one of those corrugated sheds that shudders every gust — on a foam mattress that is always damp underneath. He leaves his thermos on the crate because if he brings it inside it sweats and rusts the crate.

The keeper of the pumps: A woman, younger, who tends the big centrifugals that keep the lagoon drained. She has taken to wearing welding goggles not for welding but for the light. The UV here is savage; without trees, without shade, she got snow-blindness her second week even though there was no snow. Now her eyes are always rimmed red, but she sees. She talks to the motors. Not sentimental talk — diagnostic, coaxing. She knows the water wants to win. Every morning she has to clear the intake screens of living sphagnum that has crept, overnight, over the metal and tried to grow into the pipe. It does it every night. It will do it after she leaves for good. She oils the screws, tightens belts that never stop. Her clothes smell of diesel and peat smoke because she burns a little brick in a cone stove just to dry air.

And the others — the tram driver whose tram goes nowhere, who spends his days shoveling moss off the rails ahead of the empty wagons and then shoveling it back on behind, because if the moss doesn't cover the sleepers the sleepers heave. The kid on his first season who still tries to find a dry place to sit for lunch and hasn't yet learned you carry a square of blue foam with you everywhere.

None of them walk the way you do, sinking and pulling. They have learned the plat-forming step, foot flat, weight spread, like snowshoes made of habit. They know which green domes will hold you and which hollow is a knee-deep throat of brown water. They have lived long enough up here that their dreams are loud, too — wind, cotton, engine.

They do not live together as a village. They live like the hummocks do — close enough to hold each other up in a storm, far enough apart not to compete for the same trickle of dryness.

And there is something else that lives here, not plant, not bird, not man, but made of all of them.

The plateau itself, as a system. The pumps and the moss and the falling water are one animal now. The company thinks it is extracting a resource. What is really happening is that the place has incorporated the pumps into its water cycle. The extraction lagoon — that ugly square cut — has become the deepest pool, and the dragonflies breed there. The extruded black bricks steam, and pipits warm themselves on them at noon. The narrow-gauge rails have become a bewildering kind of esker, sunk, and the voles run along them.

If you lie down, you can feel it — not a spirit, nothing mystical, just the sheer contiguity of life pressed so tight. The thrum in your back is not just motors. It's water moving through a million hyaline cells, and a man's boot pressing water out, and a snipe's heart at 300 beats a minute, and methane bubbling up from the second-century-before-last layer of dead sphagnum, and a pump somewhere trying to undo it, failing in exactly the way that keeps everything else wet enough to go on.

It is high. It is unfinished. And it is absolutely, completely occupied — from the tardigrade to the diesel — by things that figured out how to live without ever touching the bottom.
