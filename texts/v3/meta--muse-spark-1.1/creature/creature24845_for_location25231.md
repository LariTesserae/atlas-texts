No one lives here. That is the first thing you learn if you try to.

And then you learn that isn't true. Something lives here the way a low place lives — by being held.

They are not in the city. They are what the city is doing when it thinks you aren't looking.

Call them the precipitates, for lack of a sharper word.

At first you think the motion in the sheet-flow down the middle of the boulevards is just water. It is, mostly. An inch deep, hot as blood, clear as tea. But if you crouch — your own sweat joining the sweat of the stone at once — and let your eyes go out of focus, you see that some of the water is thicker. Not dirty. *Willful.* A lens of water that holds together as it moves, a little higher than the water around it, with an edge that refracts wrong.

They move uphill sometimes.

They are flat and broad and you could cover one with your two hands. In the dark they are almost invisible except for the way the weeping light on the stone bends through them. No head, no tail, nothing to be a front. Only a slight steepening at one edge, like a wave that has decided to stay a wave.

They live in the gutters and the canals and the down-face of walls. They graze.

They graze on the act of becoming. Where the ticking is happening — that near-inaudible mineral tick as water lays down limestone — they pause and thin themselves out until they are a film almost too wide to see, and they drink the moment of change. Water remembering being stone. Stone remembering being water. That infinitesimal hesitation between states is their food. They press against it and take it. Underneath them, when they move on, the stone stays clean for a while and does not weep. A pale spot. Then it starts again.

There are thousands of them. Or there is one of them with thousands of places where it is. When a drop falls from a ceiling and hits a canal, it sometimes *continues* sideways, as if impact was optional.

The second ones live on the verticals.

You saw the mats — red-black, flat against the weeping facades, pulsing faintly when water passes over. You thought it was lichen. It is not a growth. It is a holding.

They are the keepers of the lot lines. Each one holds a block to the size it has agreed to be. Without them the soft geometry of this place would forget itself entirely. A street that is half a block long on one side and three blocks on the other can only be both true at once if something is patiently believing both ends.

They don't move. To move would be to decide. They spread instead by being forgotten in one place and remembered in another. If you stare at one, it looks exactly like a stain. If you look away and look back, it has shifted a fraction of an inch to the wetter side of its stone, and the doorway above it feels slightly more correctly sized, as if it had been straightened when you weren't measuring.

Touch one and your finger comes away not wet but dusted with the faintest grit, cream and rust and bruised-purple, warm.

The third ones live where you were told not to look for long.

In the courtyards, in the pools that have been left alone. You were right: the reflection is wrong. It shows the dense city. The city the sparse city is dreaming about.

What lives in the reflection does not live here yet, or anymore. When the pool is still, you can see them moving in that other arrangement — tall, narrow convergences of mist and drip, walking slowly down an avenue that does not exist in your arrangement but is still, inarguably, *this* avenue.

They are tall the way a column of humid air in a hot dark can be tall and still hold a shape. From your side, they are nothings — a thickening in the rain that falls upward, a cool pocket. From the pool's side, they are the citizens. They have doorways that fit them because they are doorways themselves: a long, upright absence where water refuses to condense, house-shaped, vaguely centered. If you stand very still in a courtyard for a long time, until condensation runs into your eyes and your breath becomes part of the mist, one might pause at the lip of your pool to look at you.

It does not see you. It sees the density you would have if you were fully precipitated. A second story. A flat roof. A faintly banded floor.

This is why the city has no tools, no voices, no rats, no birds. Inhabitation here is not occupation. It is *maintenance*. The precipitates keep the transaction going. The wall-mats keep the agreement of place. The reflection-people keep the memory of the dense city so the sparse city doesn't dissolve entirely.

And the city itself — the fourth one, the one that contains the rest — breathes. That sigh you heard deep in the stone. The basin the size of a county inhaling. When it exhales, the mist thickens. Water lifts off the warm pavements. The avenues smear a little at their far ends. The houses grow a skin of stone too thin to measure.

When it inhales, everything clarifies. The water runs faster. The pools correct themselves with a soft start, like an eye focusing.

You feel the inhale as a pressure behind your ears, like descending deeper. You have been breathing shallowly without meaning to. If you stay long enough — hours that feel like another neighborhood — you will find yourself timing your own breath to it without deciding to.

That is how you become an inhabitant, if you want to call it that.

You stop deciding what you are. Hammer or ore. Visitor or wall. You let the hot dark have weight. You let your sweat join the sweat of the stone. You stand in a doorway built for you to walk through sideways, and you learn to walk through sideways.

And if you stand long enough, a film of warm mineral water will lay itself down over your shoulders, bead, merge, run.
