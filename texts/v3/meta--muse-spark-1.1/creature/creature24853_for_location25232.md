Whatever lives there lives wet. Whatever lives there lives loud. Whatever lives there has made a truce with both.

Nothing was designed to live there. The pit is too new. It was opened faster than a habitat can be colonized, cut last year, last decade, yesterday in geologic time. So what lives there is what arrived first because it could not wait to be invited. Pioneer things. Opportunist things. Things that love a wound.

At first, from the rim, you think nothing lives there at all — only machines. Then you come down the switchback and understand the machines are part of it, and the men who tend the machines are part of it, and so is everything else that has come for the water.

The men.

They don't live there, not really. They commute from the flatlands up top. But after a few months they look like they were grown in the pit.

They live in rubber. Knee-boots that never come off cleanly, bibs that shine with clay, gloves that are always damp inside and out. Their skin is soft in the wrong places — pruned at the knuckle, on the soles — and hard in the others, where vibration has made callus. They all shout. Even in the lunch shack on stilts above the mud, even with the door closed, even up on the rim road going home, they shout. Their hearing has been planed down by that trapped amphitheater throb. A few wear plugs, most don't bother anymore; the plugs make it impossible to hear the change in a pump that means it's cavitating, or the whine before the cutter binds.

They move slowly. Not lazily. Patiently. The work has taught them the tempo of water. You cannot hurry a sump. You cannot hurry a conveyor that takes twenty minutes to make a circuit. You learn to stand with your weight even while your boots fill and your shirt sticks. You learn to read the thin sheets of water on the limestone — this one iron, this one clean aquifer, this one sweat from the air itself — the way a farmer reads sky. There are pump-men who never leave the bottom level. They live beside their stations, which are each slightly different, each with its own quirks and altars of zip-ties and rags and spare impellers. They are as territorial and attentive as lighthouse keepers. There are electricians who have a permanent flinch because every cable is thick as a leg and lies in water. There are drivers who have memorized every turn so deeply they could do it asleep and sometimes seem to.

At the end of a shift they climb out and the air gets cooler by two degrees and their knees feel it, and they can suddenly hear their own breathing again. Some of them dream of the drizzle that isn't rain.

The birds.

The upper third belongs to the swallows and swifts. Thousands of them. The pit gave them what a cliff would, but better, because it is new and soft and horizontal. They have drilled the fresh limestone and clay faces full of perfect little bore-holes, a city of front doors on every bench. They live on the air column that the pit holds, that humid gauze. All day they cut through it, screaming. You can't hear them over the generators, but they are screaming anyway. They've learned to scream louder here than swallows anywhere else, they have to.

Lower, the heron lives. Just one. Maybe two. A gray adult that found the pump trenches and decided they were a river. He stands in that industrial water — water the exact color of concrete slurry, water that smells of diesel and hot metal — completely still for hours, until a pale fish from the aquifer, a blind thing washed out of the deep rock, mistakes the trench for a place to hide. He eats what the pit bleeds.

At night the swallows vanish and the walls give that space to bats. They pour out of the cracks that the water is patiently opening, one drop at a time, and they work the mosquito pillars that rise at dusk off the still pools. The swifts hunt mosquitos by day, the bats by night — a relay.

The crawlers.

Everything at the bottom that is not rubber or steel is rat. Sleek, almost black, big as house cats because they've never needed to be small. They live in the cable runs, in the hollow under the sorting building, behind the tank farm where it's warmest. The noise that deafens men doesn't bother them; or rather, they never had the kind of hearing that needed protecting. They navigate by whisker and nose and memory of wet. You see their highways — polished mud tracks along the same routes day after day, next to the men's boot prints. They drink the seep that is cleanest and they are not afraid of the loaders. At night when the sodium lights buzz orange over the soup, you can see ten, twenty pairs of eyes at the edge of the light, waiting for the lunch shack scraps.

Where the rats walk, the insects are already thick. Mosquitoes breed everywhere water stands more than a day, which is everywhere. They rise in columns so dense they throw a shadow on the water. Over them, the dragonflies that the warm air can hold up even though the air barely moves. Copper-backed, bottle-blue, four-winged things that hover in front of a man's face, look at him, then flick sideways to hawk. The bottom level is lashed with their empty exoskeletons clinging to rebar.

In the puddles you put your boot in, something moves. Always. Water beetles, black and frantic, carving wakes. Crayfish so pale they're translucent, pulled up from the water table, still carrying that deep earth cold in their bodies for a few hours before the pit's heat gets them. They try to burrow back down through the marl and fail. Little fish — gambusia, maybe, or something that was never supposed to be here but came with a flood — that flicker in the pump trenches and are gone with the flow toward the sump. Bloodworms in the mud, red as rust.

The film.

This is what truly lives here. What will be here after the pumps are turned off and the pit finally wins and becomes a lake.

It coats everything that stays still for more than a week. The green-black algae on the constant seep, thick as felt, streaming in the trickle. The mats of moss on the north benches where the white sun never hits directly — small, brave, protonemal, recently learned how to be moss. And under that, thinner, older: biofilm. Bacteria and slime. On the limestone faces that sweat, on the inside of the concrete pump houses, on the cables themselves. A faint, iridescent sheen that smells of iron and rot and fresh rain all at once. If you scrape it with a fingernail it comes away reluctant and it regrows by morning. It is the only thing that actually likes the constant warm drizzle off the cooler stone at depth. It drinks condensation.

It doesn't mind the noise. It doesn't know there is noise. It has learned the one rule the pit teaches: keep wet.

So that's the food web, if you want to call it that. Water bleeds from new rock. Air condenses on new rock. Algae and film eat light and iron. Mosquitoes and midges eat film and detritus. Beetles and gambusia and crayfish eat larvae. Dragonflies and swallows and bats and heron and rats eat that. Men eat packed lunches on stilts above the mud and hose the clay off their machines, and the rats eat what they drop, and the pumps eat diesel and electricity and spit water in a deliberate trench toward the sump, and the whole thing thrums in that stone bowl like one huge, wet, idling engine that happens to have swallows nesting in its valve covers.

If you stand long enough for your ears to stop ringing — really stand, boots filling, shirt plastered, until the whine of the big cutter passes and everyone looks down — you hear under everything that faintest sound, the water teaching the rock how to erode. And you realize the pit isn't really inhabited. It is being inhabited. It is embryonic, just like the geology. It hasn't decided yet what will live here when it is finished becoming something else.
