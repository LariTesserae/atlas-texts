Nothing lives here like an animal lives somewhere.

That was the first thing you got wrong. You walked in upright, looking for eyes.

If you keep kneeling — and you have to, because the air is so heavy and wet it has weight on your shoulders — you see that the Floor is not empty. It's covered.

**The Felt comes first.**

You thought it was a stain. On the lee side of every ripple, every bolt-head, every pitted lip in the concrete, there is a pelt, black-green and a millimeter thick. When your lamp hits it right, it has a pile, like velvet brushed the wrong way.

It's not mold. It is the base of everything. It drinks the weep directly out of the air. It eats the concrete slowly, exhaling the lime as a white rind at its edges. It eats the iron water. It eats the hum. If you put your cheek near it — and after a few hours you will, because there is nowhere dry to rest — you can feel it is warm. A degree warmer than the slab.

It is one thing, probably. A single organism the size of the plain, interrupted by expansion joints. It never died because there was never a dry season to kill it. It just keeps growing outward at the speed rust grows, which is why you didn't notice it was alive.

Where the Felt is thick enough, it makes hills.

**The Masons live in the Felt.**

You only see them when you stop looking for motion. They are the color of wet concrete. They are flat. Not flat in the way a bug is flat - flat in the way a trowel is flat.

Each one is a plate, maybe the size of your palm, maybe the size of your spread hand, edged with a fringe of fine black hairs that are not hairs but mineral cilia. They have no front. They bulldoze the Felt, very slowly, and leave behind a trail that is not slime but new surface: a gloss of reconstituted silt and lime, microscopically smooth, already beading water.

They are making the Floor. They have always been making the Floor.

If you watch one for ten minutes it barely moves. If you watch the shine it leaves, you understand it has been working that same square meter since before your grandparents were born. There are millions of them. Billions. The entire plain glitters with their fresh trails when your light hits it low. The Floor's softness, its rounded corners, its polished gantry rails — that's not just wear. That's maintenance. That's them licking it smooth.

They have no predators. They only stop moving when a drop of water pins them and they have to wait for it to pass.

**The Herd lives in the steam.**

Where the stacks exhale their perpetual hot rain, the industrial reeds grow thick. And where the reeds grow, there are things that stand among them that are not reeds.

At first you think it's more gantry. A little crane, a little lift-truck, left parked. Then you realize it has too many joints, and its legs are sunk into the Felt, rooting.

They are the old custodians. Service automata, man-wide and low, designed to weld, to sweep, to tamp. Their masters left or eroded. Their program degraded, then looped, then grew around the loop like a tree grows around a fence.

Now they graze.

They unfold a fan of rusted sheet metal and hold it up in the warm rain. Water hits it, runs down channels of green corrosion into a gut. The Felt that has colonized their backs fruits where the water runs. The Mason plates migrate over them, polishing them. And so they stand, motionless for days, drinking steam.

When one gets too mineral-heavy to move, it lowers itself. Its legs crack. It settles. It becomes a hummock. The reeds grow thicker around it. After a century, its chassis is a small, dry hill in the wet, and the other grazers come and rub against it, taking its rust onto themselves. They ring like bells, very faintly, under the roar, when they do.

You can hear their bells if you put your hands over your ears.

**The Drinkers live in the Drains.**

You never see them, but the Drains aren't just water. If you sit over one of those three-meter-deep cuts of silent black flow and you kill your lamp, you see blue.

The old high-tension lines sag down here, bare, spitting St. Elmo's fire in time with the wet. Their light throws down into the water. And in that light, the water is full of rope.

Long, black ropes of Felt that have peeled off the upper slabs and re-knotted themselves. They hang in the current, trailing, filtering. Every seven seconds when the pumps throb and the bulge of water comes through, they swell. They sieve the powder the Masons made, the concrete dust, the iron flake, the spores, and they compress it. You can see their peristalsis. They are making nodules. Loose clots of new aggregate that click along the bottom of the Drain like teeth.

Far downstream, a thousand kilometers downstream where the gradient ends, those nodules will beach on a bar, pile up, petrify, and become another plate. Another Floor on top of the Floor.

They live on the only rule this place ever learned: take what is worn away and lay it down again.

None of them watch you. None of them care that you have boots full of water. You are too quick and too loud and too dry in the middle, breathing hot mammalian air. To them you are a small, short-lived weather event — a warm, salty rain that passed over, made a shadow, and went away.

If you lie down long enough, which you will want to do, your back to the weeping dark, your ear to the slab so you can feel the Drain bass in your molars, the Felt will get onto the cuffs of your sleeves. A Mason will find you, taste the calcium in you, and patiently, without hurry, begin to smooth your edge too.
