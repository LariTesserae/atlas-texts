What lives here does not live *on* it. It lives *in* the process.

Nothing here has had time to evolve into ornament. Everything is still a job description.

**The Film**

First, before anything you can see as an animal, there's the thing that feeds everything. It lives in the film.

That water — a few millimeters thick, weeping out of vesicles in basalt that hasn't decided if it's rock or not — is not empty. Put your face down close to a black slab where the sheet runs clear and you see it: a tawny, slightly iridescent slick, like oil. Touch it and it resists, just barely, like thin jelly. It's biofilm. Cyanobacteria and cold-loving algae and iron-eating bacteria that have found this one perfect bargain: infinite mineral water from below, infinite dim light from above, no competition.

It is the pasture. It is the reason this impossible place isn't dead. It coats every wet rock for a hundred miles. In the pans, where the water slows, it thickens into mats that peel up at the edges and shiver. When the low mud convection turns over, it brings up more heat and more dissolved basalt — more food — and the mat closes again in hours. The whole plateau smells faintly of it on warm days, if you can call any day warm: a metallic, green, blood-like smell. The smell of life making more of itself out of stone.

**The Quiet Flock**

Then the birds.

You were right to call them geese, because your mind wants a word for large, cold-water bird that walks more than it flies. They are not geese. Not anymore.

They are heavy. That’s the first shock when one passes near you — how heavy and low they are. The thin air at six thousand meters will not hold anything lightly built. So evolution has given up on grace. Their breast muscles are dark red, almost black, packed with myoglobin. Their bones are dense. Their wings are short, rounded, edged with a fringe like an owl's to muffle them. When they do fly — forced up by a fox, or to cross a vent field — it is a labored, beating, steam-puffing sprint ten feet off the ground that ends quickly, with a skid on webbed feet.

Mostly they walk.

They are spaced the way you said, miles apart and then six together, because they are farming. Their bill is not a goose's flat scoop but longer, flexible at the tip, upper mandible ridged on the inside like a rasp. They wade into the pan and press that bill flat to a rock and *scrape*. You can hear it if you're close — a soft *shhk, shhk* — the biofilm coming off in sheets. Some they swallow. The rest they suspend in the water. They are tilling the mat. Their tongue stirs the black mud. And behind them, where their feet have churned and their droppings have clouded the water, the film grows back twice as thick in two days. Guano rich in iron and fixed nitrogen.

They never call loudly because sound doesn't carry the same in this thin, wet air, and because they don't need to. The white throat is the signal. When a bird lifts its head, that flash of white against soot-grey can be seen a half kilometer through the mist. Head down means feeding. Head up means fox. All the other throats come up, too.

They have no nests. They lay a single egg directly on a lichen-softened hummock of grit, the color of wet basalt, and one parent sits while the other feeds. The egg is warm because the ground underneath is warm. The whole plateau is an incubator.

**The Drag-Coats**

The grazers you saw — the yak-idea — are the only thing that can eat the buttons.

That miserable saxifrage that grows in fists? It is miserable as armor. Its leaves are waxy, resinous, stuffed with antifreeze sugars, so bitter it would kill a lowland animal. Its core holds heat. In the middle of the cushion, where the red-tipped leaves knit together, the temperature can be ten degrees above ambient.

The drag-coats live for that.

They are not big. From a distance the shag makes them look cow-sized. Up close you realize the body underneath is narrow as a pony's, legs short and brutally bowed, feet splayed into two broad toes that web with skin when they bear weight — snowshoes on mud. Everything else is hair. Not fur: hair, coarse and hollow like old straw, a meter long, that grows continuously and drags in the black slurry and mats into dreadlocks caked with mud. That mud freezes in the evening and insulates. In the morning when the vents catch first light, they stand in the steam and the mud steams off them. You can smell them before you see them — sour milk and wet iron.

They move as slow as convection. They have to. There is so little oxygen that running would kill them. So they don't run, ever. When the grey-brown fox comes, they don't run. They lower their massive, flat heads — bearded with icicles that never fully melt — and show a row of self-sharpening lower teeth that are not for fighting but for shaving. They eat a saxifrage button in one motion: head down, teeth under, *scrape* to basalt, lift. The entire cushion comes up, roots and grit and all. They chew, grinding rock with plant. Their molars have grown high and ever-growing like a horse's. Their dung, when it steams, is mostly powdered basalt, and it is where the saxifrage seeds wait. They have to be abraded by tooth and acid and pressed into warm mud by a splayed foot to germinate. No grazer, no saxifrage. No saxifrage, no grazer.

In their nostrils, in their sinuses, live mites found nowhere else on earth, white and blind, that clean the constant grit they inhale. In their drag-coats live beetles, small black flightless things that never touch ground, living their whole life cycle in the wet hair.

They are not tender with each other. They stand together for wind, for heat conservation, but they don't touch foreheads. There's no excess warmth to spare. A calf is born already hairy, already able to walk in slurry, and within an hour it is eating biofilm beside the adults while their steaming jets of breath make a small, shared cloud that briefly lifts the fog around them.

**The Smear**

And then the thing you only saw as a smear.

It's not a fox either, though it wants you to think fox. That lean, low, grey-brown shape with the too-long legs and the pointed face is a specialist in thin-air crime.

It's light because it has to be faster than anything else here, and nothing here can afford to be fast. Its heart is enormous — if you opened its chest it would seem to fill the whole cavity — and its lungs are subdivided like a bird's, with air sacs that run into its hollow leg bones. It does not bark. It chuffs, a soft, whistling exhale you can mistake for a vent leaking.

It doesn't hunt the drag-coats. Can't. It hunts eggs when it can find them, and sick geese, and mostly what lives under the cushions — the huge, soft, beetle larvae that eat saxifrage roots. To find them it has learned to read the steam. It will sit on a cooling lobe for an hour, completely still, steam condensing on its whiskers, watching the muds. When a bubble pops — *pop* — in a wrong rhythm, it moves. Not a run. A long, stilted trot that covers ground without lifting its feet high, so it never breaks through the crust.

When it does take a bird, it does it without drama. A single snap at the base of the skull while the bird is scraping, so quick the other birds with their white throats don't all come up at once. Then it drags the heavy body under an overhang of basalt and stays there for a day, breathing, digesting, turning bird back into hunted heat.

Its paws are its miracle. Broad, but not webbed like the birds. The pads are black, deeply cracked, with a fringe of stiff hair that sheds mud. In the center of each pad is a pit — a thermoreceptor — that can tell warm from cold to a tenth of a degree. It finds eggs that way, finding the faint, persistent heat anomaly of a single egg in a mile of cold grit.

It is the only animal here that seems lonely. Everything else has flock or herd or mat. The smear is always one.

—

That's what lives here. No masters, no visitors.

A film that eats rock and makes pasture. Birds that farm the film and are fed by warm ground. Grazers that mill stone into soil. A predator that reads heat to find life. Mosses and lichens holding on in the lee, and the saxifrage that only grows because something bigger and shaggier ate its mother.

All spaced so wide you think the place is empty, until you notice the work never stops, and you are standing in the middle of a metabolism the size of a country — breathing slow, steaming, wet, industrial, patient.
