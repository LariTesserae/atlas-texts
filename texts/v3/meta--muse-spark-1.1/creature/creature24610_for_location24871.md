At first you think no one lives here.

That's what the quiet does. You walk the carbon lace over a fissure where the morning run is still dark and fast, and you don't see anyone, and you call out and your voice falls two meters away and dies in the taro leaves. A city this big should be noisy. It isn't. So your brain says *abandoned*.

Then you sit still like the heron does, and it inverts.

The people first.

There aren't many of them. Maybe one per structure, maybe fewer. You could walk all day and see three. That's not because it's a ruin - it's because this place has solved for density and the answer was *less*.

They are still human, mostly. You can see it in the hands, in the way they hold a cup. But they have stretched to fit the basalt tray and the low sky.

They move slow. Everyone here moves slow. The air is 30 degrees and wet and there's not enough hard light to hurry for. Their clothes are wrong for a city - nothing tight, nothing that traps heat. Long, loose wraps made from woven cane fiber that never quite dries, undyed, the color of wet scoria. A lot of them go barefoot because boots got cut to ribbons on the plates in the first year and nobody bothered to reinvent the boot; their feet are wide, callused black at the edges from the glass.

Their eyes are the giveaway. Under the Lattice, noon is amber dusk. After a few years your pupils stay big. The children born here have irises so dark and open you can hardly see where black ends and brown begins. They don't squint. They don't have to.

They don't like to shout. They can't really, not here. The Lattice drinks sound. So they've learned to be close-talkers, or no-talkers at all. When you pass one on a lace bridge they don't wave across the fissure. They wait until you are in the same pool of muffled air, two meters away, and then they nod, and maybe they touch your forearm. Skin contact carries more data than a voice here. Their hands are always damp.

Because the houses only open if you think right.

You see an older woman - what you think is older, but maybe she's thirty and the light makes everyone look soft - come home to a spire that has no door. She stops where the membrane should be. She doesn't have a key. She stands there for a count of five and her face goes slack, not blank, *attuned*. She's not thinking words, she's holding a shape in her head, a four-dimensional knot the building will accept. The air thickens, shivers like heat off road, and thins. She steps through. Inside it's bigger than outside and her inner ear will pop. She doesn't flinch anymore.

Every child learns that in public creche before they learn to read. Reading is less useful here. The city is not written, it's negotiated.

They are collectors. That hour after dawn when the plates weep and the thousand temporary creeks run - that's their morning. They are out before it starts, with their basins, their taro-leaf gutters, their cisterns that are just clever folds in a wall that the Lattice has been told to fill. They don't own water so much as borrow it from its short commute from air to rock. You will see a man kneel by a sink-fissure that holds a clear pool and white blind shrimp and he will not drink from it. That's not for drinking. That pool is left alone. He'll drink from the wall of his own house that sweated for him overnight. The pool is for the place itself to drink.

And they garden, constantly, without calling it gardening. If you live in a house that is also a root medium, you manage it. You prune the elephant-ear that is trying to seal your window. You coax the strangler fig away from the power fold. You leave the other one alone because its buttresses keep your plate from tilting any further. Your house grows a meter taller overnight and you don't file a complaint, you find where the new room is and decide if you want to keep it aired. Sometimes you don't. Sometimes you let the fig have it.

They are not alone in being alive here. That's the rest of it.

The buildings are inhabitants. Not metaphorically. The later ones - the error-correcting ones - have preferences. They like to be spaced out. They like their feet wet in the morning flow. They settle with a sigh you feel through your soles, and once a week they edit themselves self-consciously, moving a corner a few centimeters because the basalt plate underneath popped. If you put your palm on the dull-metal lattice you feel it not as cold metal but as something holding still with great effort. They are not intelligent the way people are. They are intelligent the way a reef is.

Above them, the Lattice has its own fauna. You saw the maintenance kites, those kilometer-long jellyfish ghosts. Those are not drones in the way you meant. They are raised. They bud off the Lattice, grow translucent, and spend their lives stitching light. Under your amber sky, they move so slowly you need to watch a reference point to see them move at all. When they pass, every leaf and every person gets a little dimmer, because the kite is tasting the filament. Then a little brighter again when it decides to leave it.

And low, in the fissure-reef itself, life is doing what it always does on new land, only faster because the engineers seeded it impatient.

The blind shrimp, yes. Also black yeast mats that fix nitrogen straight out of hot basalt. Small fern colonies that live an entire life cycle in the six days between a plate shift that buries them and the next condensation rain that uncovers them. Slider lizards that are not lizards at all - you get close enough, and you see their six limbs are asymmetrical, two for gripping scoria, four for sprinting, and their skin is photochromic, freckling to match wet rock so perfectly they vanish even when you're staring.

And in the cane breaks. Everyone who lives here knows not to clear too much cane. Not because of a law. Because something large lives in there that they have only ever seen as a shudder in ten-foot cane and three-toed drags in soft scoria. It doesn't hunt them. It might be a pig that went feral and changed. It might be something the Lattice dropped to compact the soil. The people give it space. They leave fist-bundles of sugar at the edge of a lot at dusk. In the morning the bundle is gone. That's enough of a relationship.

At night - though you can hardly tell when night officially starts under the honey light, except that the Lattice goes from amber to deeper amber - the people sit out on their plates, not together, but in loose visual range. A tower here, a terraced platform there, each with a small amber light that's not electric, just a cultivated fungus. They don't talk across the dark. The quiet is too heavy. They listen to the fig roots tightening, to the water ticking off a leaf into a cistern, to the occasional deep thud of a house resettling. 

If one of them settles a little too hard and sends that shudder across the plates, all the condensation dumps off the canopy at once in a valley-wide brief rain with no clouds. Everyone gets wet. 

Then you hear it: a low laugh from a roof two hundred meters away, and another, and another, muffled and close as if the person is right beside you. 

That's when you understand who lives here: patient things, spaced out on purpose, who have learned to take up very little, to drink fast when the morning offers water, and to find comfort in being quietly, separately, rained on together.
