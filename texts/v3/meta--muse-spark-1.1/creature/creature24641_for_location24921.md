What lives here doesn't live here the way an animal lives in a forest.

There is no population. There is a presence. A single, continuous act of holding on.

The main thing that lives here is the forest itself. It doesn't have a name yet because it hasn't needed to be different from anything else.

Call them the Pillarweeds to start, because your mind will want a trunk.

They are the structure. Each one lives rooted impossibly tight in a crack in that young basalt — a crack still sharp enough to cut — and it puts all of its effort into being vertical. Not wood. No rings. A dense, pale, almost cartilaginous column as thick as your body, full of warm water and starch, that can bend without snapping if the mountain shifts underneath it. It does not branch until it absolutely must. For ten, fifteen meters it is just column, and then at the light it gives up and explodes into those black-green leaves, each one waxy and thick as a boot sole, big as a door, overlapping the next one's leaf, overlapping the next. They don't photosynthesize fast. They photosynthesize constantly. The light that comes down through all that high clear water is undiminished and they drink it all day, without pause, exhaling a slow, patient fizz of oxygen that catches under the leaf and gathers into pearls the size of your fist before letting go. When they need to make another pillarweed, they do it by dropping a leaf. The leaf falls heavily, spiraling down through the syrup-water, lands on rock, and roots from its midrib.

Beneath them is what lives in their shade. That is not a second species, but a second agreement about how to be alive.

The Banner Ferns — if they are ferns — don't have a trunk at all. They have a holdfast like a hand gripping knife-edge basalt and then ten meters of frond that hangs straight down or sideways or up, depending on where the gap is. They are dark, iodine-yellow toward the bottom where the old growth is, and they never drop their dead sections. A frond grows from the tip forever, and the base just gets thicker. They rustle without current; when you push past one, the whole curtain sways for an hour after you've gone.

On everything the ferns don't cover is the Fur. It's not moss. A real moss wants damp; this thing wants to be submerged and left alone. It is half an inch thick and velvety and black-green in shadow, bright green where a shard of caustic lands on it, and it coats every vertical face of that new rock, softening the knives just enough. If you put your palm to it, it is warm. It is working. Its surface is covered in tiny mouths that are not mouths that open and close once a day to filter the watery air.

And then there is what lives between all of that, because this forest does not tolerate empty water. The air here is not empty. That's the filaments — hair-fine green threads that live free-floating, never attached, reproducing by knotting and breaking. And the Wool — colonial algae that forms in drifting tufts the size of sheep, pale green, translucent, barely coherent, that catches in the pillarweeds and is pulled apart and reforms somewhere else. The water itself is thick because it is half plant. You breathe it and you taste iron and sugar.

That is 99% of what lives here. The green that has taken a brand new mountain range that should be dead stone and has decided, all at once, to be jungle. It is slow, it is silent, it is relentless. It does not have seasons because no one told it about winter at four kilometers. It grows over its own dead without rot, because rot is a job for things that don't exist here yet. A fallen leaf is covered in Fur within days and becomes the floor.

And then, rarely, an animal.

What lives here that is not a plant almost apologizes for it.

The Long Whites — blind shrimp, the length of your forearm, the color of old wax. They live inside the Fur, making tunnels through it the way a mole makes tunnels through earth, farming the Fur by chewing it and letting it regrow. They have no eyes, but long, feathery antennae that combed the syrup for vibration. You can go a kilometer through forest and not see one, and then find a rock face that is lacework with their burrows, perfectly round, patiently kept open.

The Veils. That ribbon you saw. They aren't rare, they're just unmoving. A translucent, vertical creature three meters long and as thin as a sheet, hanging between pillars, anchored by a single thread to rock. It does nothing. Once an hour it contracts, once, top to bottom, squeezing water through itself, and then relaxes. Whatever the Wool is that it catches, it keeps. It is more plant than animal in timing. It does not flee. It has never needed to.

The White Grazers. Small fish, eyeless, head like a tadpole, body almost colorless except for the green gut full of Fur. They live in ones and twos. They nose along a band of Fur, take two bites, and then stop. And they actually stop. They hang in the warm water for half a day, digesting, not moving a fin, while oxygen pearls gather on their backs. Then they take two more bites. There is no schooling. There is no chasing.

And at the very bottom of the food chain that barely exists, the things you almost cannot call animal — the snail that made that empty cast on the rock. It lives elsewhere, a little curly white coil that grows a shell out of the dissolved basalt and grazes. When it dies nobody eats it. The shell sits there until the Fur covers it over.

Nothing hunts. Nothing sings. Nothing is in a hurry to be elsewhere because elsewhere is the same dense, warm, lit, green hold. What lives here lives like the water holds itself up: by being heavy and patient and completely occupying the space it is given.
