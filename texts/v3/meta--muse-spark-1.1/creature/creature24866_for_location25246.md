No one lives here. That's the first thing it teaches you.

Not in the way a desert has nothing living in it. The way a cathedral has no one living in it. It is occupied — crowded, even — but not by residents. By practitioners.

### The Towers

You thought they were plants. That's close enough at first.

They are not rooted. They pretend to be. That hair-thin black stalk you saw coming out of the salt is not a trunk — it's a lightning rod, a ground wire. It doesn't carry water up because there is no water. It carries potential down. The whole organism is a kite that decided to stay.

Each one is one. Not a colony. A single, continuous, electrostatic scaffold that extrudes itself. Its core is a tight knot of glassy filament no thicker than your wrist, held rigid by charge. From that, it throws those sails.

The sails aren't leaves. They're lungs that never inhale. Each is a sheet of aerogel lattice a molecule thick, crimped with cilia so fine you can't see them until the light hits wrong and the whole tower shivers with rainbow. The cilia comb. That's all they ever do. At 5 degrees Celsius, with 0.2% humidity, the jet over the crater lip still carries things — a flake of ammonia from a volcano two ranges over, a nitrate crystal that rode up from the lowlands a week ago, a breath of copper off the basalt itself. The sails snag them out of the air on static. They don't photosynthesize; they electroplate themselves larger.

That's why they're lush. Lushness here isn't wet. It's surface area. Obscene, desperate surface area. If you unfolded all the sails on one tower you'd pave the bowl. They need that much because the air is so thin in food. So they furl and unfurl and furl again, never in season, always in panic — growing a new sail in six minutes, throwing the old one away as glitter. That glitter you saw hanging? That's the dead. Not falling because falling would require them to be heavier than the charge that holds them. For a few hours they hang in the air between towers, shrinking, until the cilia of some other tower catches them and reabsorbs them.

They don't talk to each other. There isn't time. A tower lives fast. In the hour you stand there, it will die twice. The disintegration you saw — all those sails letting go at once, that awful, silent undressing down to wire — that's not death. That's a molt. When the static load gets too great, when the tower has combed too much ice out of wind that never had ice, it has to short itself. It lets go. For thirty seconds it is naked and you can see how thin it really is. Then the charge builds again and it riots back, furious, louder.

There are thirty-eight of them. I counted. Thirty-eight hermits who can't stop building their own cathedral bigger.

### The Scint

And then the air becomes too many.

The second occupants aren't occupying the bowl at all. They're occupying the battery the first occupants made. Without the towers combing charge out of the wind and dumping it down their stalks into the iron-grey salt, the air would be neutral and dead. With them, the bowl is a capacitor the size of a church: negative floor, positive canopy, ten thousand volts waiting for a reason.

The scint are the reason.

You saw them as clear bells. That's what your eyes do with something almost too fast and too transparent to see. Look longer. Each one is a bubble of cross-linked silica, big as the joint of your thumb, hollow, with two long vanes of mylar-stiff membrane stretched behind it like a scull's oars. There's no muscle in there. No gut. No mouth. Inside the bell is a wisp of ionized air that never stops spinning.

They eat voltage.

They are born when two collide — that crystal ringing sound — and the impact prints a new spin in the dust. They die when they misjudge a tower and discharge all at once. In between, they live in that frantic, sideways-blizzard way, because they must. If a scint stops moving, it loses charge and drops. If it drops onto the salt, it pops. So it darts. Between sails. Through sails. They don't damage the towers; the towers want this. Each time a scint rips between two sails, a violet filament snaps — you heard it — and both participants get a little relief. The tower's sails flutter back from overcharge, the scint gets a push, your teeth ache.

There are not thousands. In any cubic meter there are eight or nine, but your eye only catches one in fifty because their wings go clear when taut. Across the whole bowl there are maybe eighty thousand of them at any moment. In a minute, there are a hundred thousand. They swarm without swarming; they never flock, never lead. Sometimes the wind traps a hundred of them between three towers and they braid themselves into a ball of frantic light, all snapping at once, a chandelier having a seizure, then it bursts and the air is empty again for two seconds. That's as social as they get.

They are not aware of you. You are a big, warm, electrically dull obstacle that weakly bleeds their food.

### The Anchors

And under all of this, holding the tablecloth still so the others can dance —

the tori.

You almost missed them because the eye forgives what the mind can't categorize. Four of them. No, five, the fifth half-buried on the north slope like a dropped bracelet. A meter across, warm-matte, heavy as a memory. They hover. Not much — a hand's width. Just enough to show that gravity was asked politely to ignore them and said yes.

They are alive the way a dam is alive. Not moving, but working.

When whatever built the bowl left — or became the bowl — it needed something to keep the pressure from equalizing and whistling all the forest away. The tori compress physics. You felt it in your molars. The air inside their perfect swept circles is still. The salt beneath them is untouched. Lichen grows on them, except it's not lichen; it's tower-sail material that has learned to grow on something that will not die and flake.

The towers lean, imperceptibly, toward them. The scint never fly through the hole of a torus. Ever.

They don't need maintenance. They are maintenance.

Stand in the middle long enough, mouth open so your chest doesn't crack from the organ roar of wind through living reeds and snapping glass animals, and you will understand the arrangement: The Anchors hold the Bowl, the Bowl holds the Pressure, the Pressure holds the Wind, the Towers comb the Wind into Charge, the Charge feeds the Scint, the Scint keep the Towers from cooking themselves.

It is a closed cathedral. Lush, furious, and entirely self-praying.

It has no use for a congregation.
