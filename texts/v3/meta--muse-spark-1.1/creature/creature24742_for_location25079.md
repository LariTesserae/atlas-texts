What lives here is almost entirely plant, and the plant does not know it is plant.

**The Roof is the first living thing.**

You think it is sky at first, because it is so big and so bright it has to be sky. It isn't. It is a meadow turned upside down and pressed against ice. Each blade is what you said — a glass fiber as thick as your wrist, as long as a redwood was tall in the world before this one cracked. They are not soft. If you ever climbed up there, which nothing here can do, you would find they are sharp enough to cut, and cold.

They are called no one thing by anything down here, because nothing looks up with a naming mind except the people passing through, and they call it simply The Light.

It lives by eating stone. The underside is root, and the root is in the ice, and the ice is holding back the old world. The actual leaf of the thing, the photosynthetic part, is somewhere above the ice, in whatever thin soil and shattered daylight remains up there. What you see from below is only its lamp — a root that has learned to glow to flip the equation. Where there is no sun, make your own and let it filter down. It sweats because it is warm. The ice above it sweats because it is always melting a little. That sweat is the only rain this world will ever have.

It grows at the edges. It has to be trimmed back by its own weight. When too much ice gathers, a whole acre of it calves off — glass and root and light together, falling for a kilometer, a slow, burning comet that does not go out until it hits the floor. Where it lands, a new Pillar will eventually try.

It pulses. You noticed the dimming. That is not night. That is the roof breathing all at once, a stress response running through a contiguous organism the size of a country. When it dims, everything below pauses and then rushes to grow, afraid the light will not come back.

**The Pillars live here because the Roof permits it.**

They are the second attempt at being a tree. The first was up above and it failed when the world opened.

A Pillar starts as a seed the size of a house, black-green, heavy as a car, that drops from a tear in the mat-floor of the ceiling. It falls for so long you hear the whistle before the impact. When it hits the fresh, hot basalt — and the basalt here is still young enough to have a smell, like a struck match — it cracks. Not the seed. The rock. The seed puts out three or four knife-blades of root that are not roots for drinking. There is no water down there. There is only holding on. They are pitons. You can hear them working. Tick... tick... tick... as they cleave the glassy stone by sheer turgor pressure, exuding acid the color of tea.

And then it goes up. That is all it does. Light comes from above, always. So the goal is up.

It does not make wood. Wood would be too heavy, too slow. It braids itself out of tubes. Each tube is hollow, translucent, big enough to drive a train through, and inside each tube is weather. Cold air falls down one tube, warm air rises up another. Water that condenses on the outer skin runs down the inside in black, internal rains that never reach the ground. If you stand inside a dead one — and there are dead ones — you hear it raining inside a building that is also a plant.

A mature Pillar is three kilometers tall and still growing at the tip. It never stops. It does not know how to stop. When it finally gets close enough to the Roof that its highest whiskers singe in the lamp-light, the heat kills its apex. Then it branches sideways, throwing out arms as thick as freeway overpasses, not for leaves, but to become new ground. That is Rule Four: everything that stands will be used as ground for something else.

Its leaves are not its leaves. They are tenants.

**What hangs from the Pillars, and what carpets between them, lives here because the Pillars permit it.**

The curtains: each sail you saw is not one leaf, but fifty, a hundred flat green hands fused at the wrist, hanging from the Pillar's horizontal arms. They are pale because they are not built for full Roof-light — they live in the Pillar's shadow, and they make a living off scatter and drip. Their upper surface is cupped, waxy, engineered to catch condensation and funnel it to a central vein where it is stored. Their lower surface is furred with white hairs that comb moisture out of the air even when it is not raining. When they get too full, they wring themselves out with a shudder you can feel in your molars. That shudder is not wind.

When one tears off and falls, it does not die for weeks. It lies on the floor like a beached whale, still catching water, still photosynthesizing, until the floor comes for it.

The floor — the Weft — is the thing you are actually standing on. You thought it was moss on a mattress on stone. It is more accurate to say it is a city that decided to be a blanket. It is fifty meters deep in places, and every centimeter of it is alive and arguing. Black air-roots like cables, white hair-fiber that blinds you if you pull it up to the light, sheets of lichen like wet cardboard that can be peeled up in acres, fungi that are not fungi but a third thing that glues it all together. It does not need soil because it is soil. It makes soil out of Pillar bark, fallen sails, its own dead.

It is hungry. Not in a predatory way. In a place-claiming way. Leave a hammerstone for two days and you will find white threads kissing it. Leave your boot for a night and it will have grown a little collar around the sole. It does not mean harm. It only knows Rule Four. If you stand still long enough, you become ground, too.

You can cut it. It gives you a smell of fresh cucumber and iron. Within hours, the cut foams over with clear sap and begins to knit. People here learn quickly not to make beds on it. They make hammocks slung from living hooks.

**What moves, lives here on sufferance.**

There is not much that moves because the plants got there first and learned to move themselves. Growth is movement at an inch an hour. But allowances have been made.

The one you almost saw — the grazer — is not rare because it is hiding. It is rare because the place does not have enough spare energy to make many of them. It is the shape of a sloth that was made out of old window glass and dryer lint. Six limbs, no eyes that you can find, translucent enough that you can see the pale green soup in its gut, which is not stomach acid but a farm. It licks. That is its entire economy. It traverses the vertical mile of a Pillar at about the speed of a tired man walking, tongue out, collecting the biofilm of algae and condensation that grows on everything. It drinks the almost-rain without needing to catch it. It breathes out little white tufts — mammalian, warm — because it is the only thing here wasteful enough to be warm-blooded.

It does not eat the Pillar. If it did, the Pillar would drop it. Instead it cleans the Pillar, and the Pillar tolerates it.

Smaller than it are the Borers. You will never see them, only the evidence: perfect round holes, the diameter of your finger, punched through a hanging sail, one after another in a line, as neat as paper from a three-hole punch. Something lives inside the sail itself, between its fused leaves, chewing a tunnel and drinking the stored water. When the sail falls, it stays.

Smaller still is the dust, which if you hold your hand out in the bright light and look sideways you can see moving against the fall of water. Mites big as sesame seeds, white, with legs like eyelashes, that ride the condensation drops down and then climb back up on static. They live their whole lives in the air between floor and Roof.

**And then the people.**

They live here least of all. They do not belong to this new below — they came after the crack, down old lava tubes or on ropes through ice caves, following the light they saw leaking up where there should be none. They are thin. Everyone here is thin, because nothing here makes fat easily.

They are not a tribe. A tribe would require staying put long enough to become ground, and this place does not let you. They are maybe twenty, maybe two hundred scattered across the horizon-to-horizon basin, you cannot census a place this big and this empty. They move every few weeks because if you stay, the floor lifts your hearth, or a Pillar arm you thought was solid drops a curtain the size of a house onto where you slept, or the Weft grows over your tools.

They have learned:

Metal goes dull and then green and then is gone.

Fire is a prayer, not a tool. They keep it in little basalt cups, fed with dry hair-fiber and shavings of Pillar tube, because everything else is wet. They don't need it for light or heat. They need it to remember that they can make a different kind of light, small and controllable.

Clothes are made from sail-leather that is still alive enough to keep beading water off.

Their knives are splinters of Roof-fiber, which never need sharpening because they are glass. The handles are left alive so the knife doesn't cut the hand that holds it. A hammer is a rock with a root tied around it that is still faintly green.

They do not carve the Pillars. That would be like carving a church and also stupid because the Pillar would heal and push the carving out.

They leave offerings. You saw one. A little figure with arms raised toward the Roof, made from a curled leaf. Not a god. A reminder: Light comes from above, always. That is the only direction. When they are lost, which is often, they lie on their backs on the Weft and stare up at the Light until they know which way is up again, and then they walk.

At twilight, when the Roof dims all together and the world goes blue and cold and quiet enough that you can hear a water drop form a hundred meters away — tick — they stop and listen to the creaks and pops and distant sighing roars of failure, and they add their own small sound: breath, making white tufts that vanish, joining the almost-rain that will fall somewhere else, a day from now.

The forest shivers under their feet and decides, again, to grow around their boots.
