They live here the way water lives here — not in a hurry, not visiting.

You would call them people if you met one, because there is not another word that fits better. They are not tall. The high air would have made them tall, maybe, somewhere else, but the low ceilings of their half-quarry houses and the broad, heavy work have kept them compact. Shouldered. They move low to the ground, even when they are standing upright, because the stone is always slick.

Their feet are what you notice first. Everyone is barefoot. Always. Even the old ones. The soles are wide, pale, soft despite use, pruned a little at the edges as if they've just come out of a bath. On dry basalt they would be useless, they'd bleed. On this wet, polished plateau they grip. The toes splay. Children learn to walk not by balancing but by sticking — toes curling on moss, on wet block. You hear them coming by the soft slap that water makes between skin and stone, and then nothing.

Their skin stays damp. It has to. The air never quite lets you dry. They wear very little — a wrap of beaten fiber around the hips or chest that soaks through by ten in the morning and stays that way, dark with water. Some wear a second cloth over the shoulders against the sun, because the sun here is punishing even though the heat is soft. The sun doubles off every canal, every cisternal eye, every wall. So their skin, where it is exposed — arms, backs, faces — is freckled, sun-dark, and their eyes are always narrowed a fraction. They have the eyes of people who look at reflected light all day. Deep-set, with heavy lids. Light grey or light brown, almost the color of the settling pools when the algae stirs.

They don't have much hair on their bodies. On their heads, they keep it short or shaved off entirely, not for ritual but because hair never dries here. It stays lank and cold around the neck. The children are often completely bald until they are older, their scalps beaded just like the north side of a wall before noon.

What bellies there are are small. There isn't famine — there is always water, and there are taro-like roots from the black-reed thickets and flatbread made from the starch of the giant fern-trunks, and cakes of pressed algae that taste like stone and salt. But no animal protein. Ever. Nothing has walked up these cliffs to be hunted. No fish has made it into the cisterns. So they are lean in a way that is not thin. Stringy, hydrated, like the reeds themselves. Old people's arms show the blue of veins too clearly, because the skin is thin.

And they are quiet.

That is not a choice, or not only a choice. It is a condition that has become a culture. In a place where you can hear a sluice stone being lifted two streets over, you learn not to make extra noise. A shout would carry across the whole table and come back off the cliffwall and be embarrassing. So they speak low, and rarely. When they need someone two lots away, they don't call — they go. They walk, bare feet slapping, then sticking, through the canals' mist. Their language, if you could still call it language, is as small and functional as their tools. Short words, mostly nouns for water states. *Sif* for the fast clear water at the top of a canal. *Sahl* for the slow, fern-haired water near the bottom. *Tak* for the slab. A flick of the hand for *lift here, I'm coming*. Children play a game where they try to make as little sound as possible crossing a plaza of packed ash. The winner is the one no one heard move.

There aren't many of them. That is part of why it feels like you can walk a long time between presences. A town the size of a valley with the population of a hamlet. Maybe three or four hundred, spread thin enough that moss grows unchecked between occupied houses. You might see four in a day: one raking a canal with a long wood-and-copper rake, moving with the water, not against it, gathering the black filamentous growth into a pile that will be left to dry into a useless felt. One sitting on a cistern lip, legs in the water up to the knees, watching the algae breathe. One hanging laundry that will never quite dry, wringing it, hanging it, wringing it again. One asleep on a flat roof, face up to the dissolving cloud.

They tend the water because the water tends them and because there is nothing else to do. Every morning, before the air goes fully wet, someone lifts the high sluices. Every evening, before the air cools that fraction that is not really cooling, someone lowers them. They stir a settling pool with a pole so the clean water on top stays clean. They lift a block that has cracked and set a new one that still has sharp edges. This is not done with urgency. If a canal clogs, water finds another way. It sheets over stone, finds a joint. The city won't flood. It will only get wetter.

There is no chief. There is no temple. If there was an ancestor who led them up here through thinner air, that ancestor's name has been worn off by condensation. What they have instead is an agreement you can feel in how they leave empty lots empty: a shared understanding that this place is still arriving.

The oldest of them — a woman whose skin has gone the pale yellow-green of the moss on her roof because she never leaves its shade — remembers being told by her grandmother that one day, things will come. Not people coming up the cliff. That will not happen; the cliffs are still unclimbable. But spores will bring the first small beetle, in a pocket of loam caught in a wind updraft. Bird shit will bring a seed that is louder. A storm will lift a frog. And then the city will finally hear what a wet place is supposed to sound like at night.

Until then they keep it ready. They keep the canals straight as rule-lines so something can swim in them when it arrives. They keep the cisterns clear-eyed. They keep the silences between houses open so that when sound comes, it will have somewhere to live.

If you stay long enough they stop noticing you, and you see the other thing that lives here: not just the people, but the kind of attention they have become. An attention that ripples very slowly across the plateau, like algae in a current when no fish disturbs it.
