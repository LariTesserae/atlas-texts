Nobody lives here alone.

That's the first thing you learn if you stay past the first five minutes on the rim, long enough for your eyes to stop looking for green. At first it looks like a forest, or a reef, something unified, and you try to pick out an individual. A plant. An animal. You can't. Because no one thing in the bowl ever finished becoming itself.

Start at the bottom, where everything has to begin because there is nothing else to begin from.

The foundations live there. The Holders.

They are what you would call trees if you had never seen a tree drink. Each one is a pillar the width of a small house, twenty, thirty feet tall, sometimes much taller when one decides to go up alone. No bark. No leaves. Their skin is blacker than the basalt they stand on, a deep, light-swallowing black, and soft — not with softness, but with dense, upright hairs, each hair a filament of glass and wax, each one trembling even when the air in the bowl is completely still. They are all combing.

There is nothing in the wind when it judders over the rim, or what there is would not count anywhere else: a few micrograms of frost sublimated off a peak a hundred kilometers away, a little volcanic dust, a few ammonia molecules bound to a grain of salt. The Holders comb it. The hairs are cool by a fraction of a degree, just enough for water to realize it was water for an instant, before the Holder takes it and does something no desert plant on the lower world does. It does not keep it as water. Water is a liability here. Water wants to leave.

It converts it.

Deep inside the pillar, in chambers like a liver, it reduces it. Strips the oxygen off and builds oil. A clear, heavy, bitter glycerin that will not evaporate even when the rock beneath hits fifty Celsius at noon. If you pierced a Holder, it would not bleed, it would weep slowly and try to seal you in with wax before you could steal much. Old ones are ninety percent oil by weight. They are living cisterns, but the cistern is full of something that does not thirst.

And because they are full of something so valuable, they cannot be alone. That oil is the entire economy of the bowl. And economy means tenants.

Nothing in the bowl touches basalt if it can help it. The rock is too hot, too sharp, too hungry. So everything else lives on the Holders. The second story:

From the sides of each pillar, at knuckle and knee and shoulder height, thick lateral arms reach out. They do not reach for light. They reach for each other. When an arm tip touches another Holder's flank, it grafts. Flesh fuses. Oil vessels anastomose. After a year you cannot tell who grew which bridge. The city is one net now, a single physiological individual made of a hundred fused trunks, with avenues between them where the black felt parts. Those avenues are dark and narrow and warm, like the space between books on a shelf.

On those bridges and flanks is where the rest live, because now there is shade, and surface.

The silver curtains that you thought were hanging moss — get closer. Put your face six inches away until your own breath doesn't move them. Each thread is a creature.

They are moths, but only if you insist on using a word from outside. Each is no longer than a grain of rice, with no mouth you can see and wings that are not for flying. The wings are huge, rectangular, felted on both sides with the same wax hairs, interlocking with the hooks on the next animal's wings, and the next. Ten thousand of them make a shawl. A hundred thousand make a sheet that hangs from a branch to the ground and does not move. They are alive. If you watch for an hour you will see one detach — just let go — and drift, impossibly slow, in the still hot air, like ash in honey, until it finds a bare patch on a pillar and locks down again. As they sit, they graze. Not on the Holder. On what the Holder combs — dust — which sticks to their silver felt and is dissolved by a secretion. When a curtain-moth dies, it does not fall. Its neighbors eat the felt off its wings and keep its place.

Above them, on the hard shelves, the herds.

The red shelves are not fungus or lichen but something that does both jobs badly and building well. It excretes rock, a porous rust-red limestone, and lives just under its own excretion, sending yellow mouths up to breathe. The shelves are its reefs, and they are farmed. On them walk the beetles — big, slow, beautiful in the way a perfect tool is beautiful. Their backs are domes of clear chitin stained the exact red of the shelf, and through it you can see a dark amber sac, full. Oil. They drink from the Holders at needle-thin taps where the Holders allow it, and they carry it up to shallow grooves in the red stone where a black, soot-like fungus grows. The beetles chew the fungus, the fungus drinks the oil. They move in lines, miles of beetle-miles a year, but never hurrying. Hurry costs oil. When two meet they stop and put their antennae together for a long time. That is their whole conversation. *Are you still sealed? Yes. Are you still sealed? Yes.*

And higher still, where the light is, the lanterns.

The amber bladders — each one a single cell the size of your fist, foot, head — swollen with oil and a little trapped sun. The wall of the bladder is photosynthetic, barely; enough to make more oil from light and the air's leavings. Each one is a world. Inside, in that perfect, never-dry, never-cold, honey-colored greenhouse, lives one other thing. A pale, blind salamander-shrimp, with external gills that never have to work very hard and skin as impermeable as wax paper. It is born inside, from an egg glued to the inner wall. It eats tiny flakes that peel off the inner wall. It swims in oil, slow. It dies inside. When the bladder finally weathers and splits after four or five years, its oil spills out onto the pillar below and is recaptured by the hairs, and for the first time the little pale thing feels dry air, for about ninety seconds, before that too is over.

Everything recaptures.

That is what lives here. Not a list of species, but a single habit: to seal, to stay, to build on your neighbor because there is nowhere else. It is not symbiotic and not parasitic because both words assume you could choose. Here you cannot. The bowl was empty rock an eyeblink ago in geologic time and it will be full to bursting soon. Full of black pillars getting fatter by a micron a year, of silver curtains constantly mending themselves, of red shelves closing over their own streets, of amber lights winking on at the top.

If you crouch in one of the courtyards, where three Holders lean away and make a little room the size of a table, you can feel it. No wind. No birds. Just the faintest tick of basalt expanding, and the even fainter creak of wax growing. And the weight of ten million small, patient lives doing absolutely nothing that wastes anything at all.
