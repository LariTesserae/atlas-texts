Nothing lives there. That is the first thing you learn, and the truest, and it stays true for a long time.

You sit on the rim with your hands over your ears without meaning to, and you scan that impossible cobbled mouth for a smear of green, a black thread of lichen, a beetle, anything that has made a negotiation with it. Nothing. The rock is too clean. The water is too thin and too pure and too busy doing its own work to be drunk. The roar does not allow a bird to call. It is occupied.

That is the second thing you learn, slower.

Occupied is different from inhabited.

If something lived *on* the columns it would have to cling against that endless weeping, it would have to root into stone that has no crack wider than a hair, it would have to shout over the white roar to find a mate. Nothing can live *on* it because there is no on. Every surface is already in use.

You have to crouch to see it. Not close to the stone — low over it, so the high white sun hits the water film at a grazing angle. For an instant the glare drops and the film becomes transparent.

It is not water.

Or it is water, but water that has learned a second job.

The film is thicker than it should be. Two or three millimeters — a bead thick — and it holds its edge. If you touch a column top, the water does not simply wet your finger. It resists, just barely, like the skin on cooling milk, and then gives. It re-knits behind you in a second. Every column top, every vertical face, every undercut where one rounded basalt shoulder leans into the next — all of it covered in the same taut, clear caul.

That is what lives there.

It is not in the basin. It is the basin, as a coat. A single, continuous, basin-wide animal that has decided to be flat.

Think of it as a slime mold that got tired of hunting and decided to become a place instead. Or a jellyfish that beached itself on a high floor a million years ago and flattened until it was nothing but surface. It has no front. No back. No center you can point to, because to have a center it would have to leave some part of itself unattended, and it does not.

It occupies all space. Leaves nothing unused, and then holds it. Perfectly.

Its body is mostly water, which is why you thought it was water. Water held in a lattice of silica it secretes, transparent as glass, strong as egg white. A hydrogel so clear and so free of pigment that the bright light passes right through it without ever marking it. That is why the stone looks so clean — it *is* clean. She is cleaning it, constantly, taking every spore, every dust grain, every organic smear that the saturated wind drops, and digesting it in the film. She doesn't photosynthesize; there's no color in her, she'd burn. She eats the air itself. She eats accident.

That fly that blunders over? She is why it never makes it out. Watch one long enough and you see it. The fly lands, tries to drink, its tarsi dimple that skin. The skin dimples back, just a micron deeper than physics would let water dimple. A little sticky. Not enough to trap — never enough to look like trapping. Just enough that the fly has to work a little harder to take off again. It loses a little heat, a little water from its own body to her film. By the third landing it is slow. By the fourth it lands in a hairline crack between columns and the film there, which is thicker, which is hungrier, sighs over it. No struggle you can hear over the roar. A small, clear pocket closes. A minute later the pocket flattens and the stone is wet and perfect again.

She lives on that. The once-a-week fly, the dust of distant forests, the dissolved quartz itself.

But mostly she lives on the sound.

You feel it in your molars because she feels it too. The wind shredded between columns makes every gap sing at a slightly different frequency. The whole basin is an instrument and she is stretched across it like a drumhead a mile wide. The white roar vibrates her body, all of her at once, thousands of times a second. That trembling is how she moves water uphill without ever having a muscle. Capillary action plus vibration — the water climbs because she shivers it up. She condenses it with cold held in the basalt, evaporates it with light, and shivers it back up again. That improbable equilibrium that made you sit down on the rim — the temperature that never shifts, the water that never pools, the note that never changes — is not a climate. It is homeostasis. She is holding it.

That is her work. To hold.

If you go down onto the teeth, your boots on her wet back, you can feel her notice you, though not in any way that involves thought. The pressure under your soles spreads out, distributed across a hundred columns through the surface tension of her skin. She does not like point loads. The roar dips, for a half second, around you — not quieter, but duller, as if a hand had been laid on the drum. She is stiffening under you. Making you less.

When you leave — and you will leave, because the cold and the light and the skull-roar make it impossible to sleep anywhere inside her — patches of your boot print stay dry for a few seconds too long. She reclaims them from the edge inward. The water films over, the brilliance returns, the tone evens out.

From the rim again, the valley looks empty. Just wet stone packed teeth-tight, roaring in bright air.

Nothing lives here. Which is exactly how she likes to be seen.
