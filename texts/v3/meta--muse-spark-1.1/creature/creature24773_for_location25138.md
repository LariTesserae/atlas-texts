They are not a community that moved in. They are what was left when the water moved in and decided to stay.

At first, from the lip of the street, you think nobody could live down there at all. It's too full. There is nowhere to put a person in that press of leaf and iron and seep. And then you see that a person *is* put there, sideways, like a bookmark.

There are about thirty of them. Not enough to be a village. Enough to have names for every root.

They are all old in the way that people who have always worked wet work get old early — not lined, but softened. Bath-warm air, year-round, has taken any hardness out of them. Their skin never really dries. It stays pale and slightly swollen, like something kept too long in water, at the knuckles, at the knees. They keep their hair cropped to nothing because hair rots here. Men's chins and women's legs grow in patchy, because the skin underneath is always colonized by a faint white bloom they don't bother to wash off anymore. It itches if you think about it.

They dress like the pipes do. Nothing woven tightly survives, it stays wet and goes black. So it's all loose, open, sacrificial — singlets cut from burlap rice sacks, shorts that were once work pants cut higher and higher every year, rubber boots that everyone has slit up the sides for ventilation and then laced with vine. Most of them are barefoot on the duckboards because you need to feel if a board is going soft. Your feet learn to read rot.

You can tell how long someone has lived below grade by their shoulders. A new person still tries to walk forward. A person who was born down there — and now two or three of them were — walks with one shoulder forward always, crabbing through the two-foot gaps where a fig trunk has swallowed half a walkway and a prop root has taken the other half. Their backs are permanently damp. Their hands are the only hard part of them. Constant pruning without gloves in a place where everything is mildly caustic from concrete leachate: their palms are callused and green-black under the nails. Fingernails thick as horn, for splitting suckers off banana corms.

They don't talk much, because the place eats sound. The leaves are acoustic tile, the drip is too close. If you try to have a conversation at normal volume your voice dies two feet out. So they have a close language. Two inches from the ear, loud, almost shouting, over the chuff-chk-chk of the Listers. Or silent: they touch. A hand on a forearm to say *stop, this root is load-bearing.* A knuckle rap on a pipe, twice, which means *listen to this bearing knock*. A palm flat on a chest to say *wait, screw's chewing grit*. Children down here — the few — learn to read interruption before they learn to read letters. You do not step past someone without putting a hand on their back first, or you'll push them into a stand of taro that will cut you.

That's how the rules hold. The rules aren't posted. They are grown in.

They wake by the engines, not by light. Light never really changes — it's always that late-afternoon green, the amber bare bulbs burning all night. But the engines change tone when they're cold. First person up — whoever sleeps closest to the southeast screw — pulls the rag from the flywheel and cranks. The whole block shudders and then settles into its day-rhythm: chuff... chuff... chuff... steady enough you feel it in your molars. The second job is gutter. Everyone has a stretch. Twenty feet of tin at the top of the retaining wall where the seep is lifted and dumped. You climb the iron balcony that has become a trellis, push aside the curtain of aroid leaves that have sealed overnight, and clear it with your hands. Leaf mush, calcite clots, a dead frond that fell and made a dam. If you don't, your corner floods, and flood here doesn't mean water — there's never open water — it means your feet sink into the root mat and the mat goes anaerobic and stinks sweet and your taro drowns from the bottom up. So you clear your gutter stretch every morning. You check the oil.

Oil is religion. The three Lister engines are down in a pit where the original slab dips, in a cage of mangrove roots that have grown politely around them because the heat keeps them back. The engines are cleaner than anything else down here. Wiped down twice a day, brass fittings polished by palms. They sit on cut tires to damp their shudder so the roots don't strangle them in self-defense. Each has a name — not cute names, location names: Gutter One, Bend, Deep. They can tell from the sound of Bend if someone on the far side cut too deep into a water pipe-root. They keep them alive because if they stop, the seep wins, and in three days the duckboards are under soft mud and in ten the lower walkways are gone. They have been running longer than any of the people. They will run after.

Other than that, what they do is thin.

That's the whole economy. Not harvest, not farming, though they eat. Thin. The greenhouse was planted to produce biomass fast a hundred years ago and it learned. If they don't cut, it closes. A hallway that was open in March is a wall by June — not because a plant moved, but because five plants leaned into the gap and then locked arms. So each person has routes, and they patrol them with a machete that is more like a butter knife now, dull and pitted, used to shove rather than slash. They cut suckers, they pull aerial roots back through windows, they train the tomato that became a tree away from the light bulb it wants to swallow. They never cut a load-bearing root. Everyone can tell by looking which roots are bearing. They are the ones with concrete flecks grown into bark, the ones creaking under the weight of the old third floor.

They eat what thins. There is no animal down there at all, not even a roach, not a fly. Something in the old fungicides and the hot iron water made sure of that. A fallen banana leaf never gets chewed — it sits until a white film eats it from below. So there is no meat, no eggs, no honey, no rot with maggots. Only plant. Perennial plantain banana that must be cooked twice to be edible, but they eat it raw anyway. Sour woody tomato from the shrub that was an annual in '64. Taro from the mat that tastes of pipe rust. Heart of palm, but only from the heads that have already failed — to take a healthy heart would be cutting a column. They are all slightly anemic. Their teeth are stained orange from the water.

They sleep not in houses. The houses dissolved. The iron balconies and the second floors are still there, but the walls are hedges. Someone stretched a tarp between two banana stems, hung a hammock made of braided cloth wire insulation and nylon sack, and that's a room. A curtain of living vine, kept trimmed so it doesn't seal. Because it will seal. If you close your door and don't tend it for a week, it will knit shut, and you'll have to hack your way out from inside. Everyone trims their door every morning after the gutter.

They don't leave much. From the top step the world is dry and too loud and too bright. Up there the air doesn't have iron in it. Two or three of them go up to trade for oil and lightbulbs and salt. They come back fast, because up there people stare at their swollen feet and their green hands. And because down here, for all its loud drip and grind, it's steady. It's slow. 

In a place that changes so slowly that a rust stain can be a decade's clock, you stop measuring time forward. You measure around. That root by the north wall holds more this year. That bulb burned out nearer the east corner than last time. The tomato shrub dropped more leaves than usual, so mulch your walk.

From the sidewalk you hear the chuff and the drip and you think it's abandoned. Down inside, thirty wet shoulders move sideways through the leaves, one after another, checking oil, clearing gutters, patting each root that holds the world up like you'd pat the flank of a big, patient animal that you live inside of.
