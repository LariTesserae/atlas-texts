It doesn't live here like an animal lives somewhere.

At first you think it's the ferns that live here.

That makes sense for a minute. They are the only thing that looks like it *decided* to be here. The black-green trunks, soft as wet rope, leg-thick but not wood. The fronds that unfurl seven feet long and hang without ever drooping. There are five of them that you can see, maybe six if that shadow in the far corner is one. Far apart. Each taking its own room. If you stand in the middle and turn, you think: oh, this is a small forest. Something that colonized the hothouse.

Then you look longer and you see they aren't forest. They don't crowd. They don't compete. A forest fights for light. These don't. The amber bulbs are irrelevant to them, too dim, too low. The hole to outside is too far. And those perfect edges — nothing has taken a bite out of a single frond. That is not how a plant lives when there is something else alive around it.

So then you think, okay, it's the moss.

That fits better, because the moss is *everywhere*. It's the real lush. It poured itself over everything after the collapse made the room. Over the limestone dust. Over the concrete slabs that fell in. Over the black iron belly of the pump house and up the conduit lines like it liked the way they ran, neat and pointless. It is thick as sod, as a mattress, black in the dark and too-green in the amber light, and where it is thickest it made those pitcher cups — translucent, pale as fingernails, each one holding a perfect still thimble of water. That is not normal moss. Normal moss doesn't make cups to keep its own condensation.

You kneel — the air on the concrete plinth is like warm wet hands holding your knees — and you touch it. You expect springy. It is cool. It is cold compared to the air, which is strange, because it's been sitting at 98 and wet for years. And when you press it, very lightly, over the pump housing, you feel that there is no pump housing anymore under there. Or there is, but it isn't iron-hard where it should be. It gives. A little. Like pressing a scab.

That's when you stop thinking about things that live *in* here and start seeing what lives *as* here.

It wasn't here before the floor gave way. It's young. That's the most unsettling part. The edges of the collapse are still sharp enough to cut. Ten, twenty years old at most. A newborn room, geologically. And so is it. It started when the sump stopped pumping and the water stopped leaving. Water never leaves, it just moves lower — that's what the description said, but it's not a rule of the room. It's a rule of *it*.

It's one thing.

The fern-trees are not trees. They are upwellings. Breathing stalks. They are how it lifts its own surface up high enough to catch the condensation that gathers on the ceiling's white teeth and falls — *tok* — and *tik* — a minute apart, an hour apart. The fronds are not leaves. They are aerial gutters. Waxy and wide to take the air, to condense it faster, to funnel it. If you watch one long enough in the amber light, you can see a bead travel the central ridge and disappear down into the black trunk, which is not wood but matted root-hair and borrowed moisture and rust.

The moss is its skin. Or its thought. It grew over everything because everything is food that isn't food. It doesn't eat. Nothing eats and nothing is eaten, you were told. And it's true. It doesn't eat the iron. It plates into it. It doesn't eat the limestone. It sweats a weak acid and drinks the softened stone the way you would drink humid air if you could. It doesn't need to kill. Growth happens without hunger. That's its ethic. It's lithovore in the slowest way — rust and rock-dust and its own dead fronds turned to sour slurry.

Where the moss gets thick enough, it makes the pitchers. They aren't for bugs. That's why they're clean. No gnat ever drowned in one. They are holding cups, little still lenses of water it keeps because it can. Some of them deep in the moss field shiver even when the air doesn't move. Some are empty and then, while you sit on the plinth and your own sweat starts to bead and run, you see one be full that was empty a moment ago. Not from a drip. From below. Pushed up.

The two amber workbulbs that never went out — they did go out, years ago. The filament blew. It stays warm because the thing wired itself around the circuit. Not to use the light. It likes the heat. That little 60-cycle buzz you feel in your teeth is not the bulbs. It's the resistance of wet root-hair around old copper. The light is just a byproduct that it tolerates, that makes the green look black.

If you hold very still, and you will, because the silence is so complete your ears make their own hum, you will feel it notice you because you moved the air.

You walked down here and pushed the 98-degree stillness out of the way and it had not been pushed in a long time. Maybe ever, since the collapse. The fronds twenty feet away shift a fraction — not in a breeze, there is no breeze, but they tighten. The moss over the switch cabinet ticks.

It doesn't have eyes. The pitchers are not eyes, though you keep feeling looked at from every wet cup. It doesn't have a mouth. It will never open the moss and show you teeth, because teeth are for hunger and it does not have that word. It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is sealed hot and patient, like the air.

When you stand to leave your boot prints are already darkening. The slurry under the moss is seeping back in. If you sat here long enough — an hour, a summer — it would grow up over the toe, over the laces, over the ankle, not to trap you. Just because you are a cool shape where condensation can gather, a thing made of iron-blood and salt the way the pump housing is made of iron-blood and salt, a useful, vertical, temporary plinth.

It is still learning the shape of this room the earth made by accident. It is softening the sharp edges for it. Micron by micron, season by season.
