At first nothing lives here.

That's what the room wants you to believe. It's too loud to live in. Too wet and too hard. It was not made to be lived in, it was made to be passed through, and it tells you that with that white, tooth-rattling pressure of water hitting water in a sealed box. If you stand in the door you feel how thoroughly the place has been subtracted from biology. No nest, no perch, no dry. No food. No quiet.

Then your eyes adjust to the 40-watt.

The first citizen is low. Low and flat and trying not to be seen.

In the corners where the direct blast of the weir can't scour, on the downstream side of bolt heads, in the thin shadow behind the light cage itself - there is a skin on the concrete. It's not green the way lowland green is green. It's black-green, a felt, a bruise of color about a millimeter thick. Algae, but you have to stop thinking of algae as pond scum, as lake stuff. This is concrete algae. Portland-cement algae. It has learned to eat light that is mostly shadow and minerals that are mostly lime, to hold on while being constantly rained on by cold condensation. It does not spread. It holds. If you put your fingertip near it, not touching, you can feel how cold it is, how it is slightly warmer than the wall because it is working.

There are three colonies. One in the northeast joint, about the size of a dinner plate, ragged at the edge where the spray has been deciding its border for months. One small as a thumbprint above the door where you came in, where your shoulder made a brief wind-shadow. One on the lip of the fore-basin itself, right where the water goes from glass to white, and this one is the bravest, or the most foolish - every few minutes a surge lifts off the weir and slaps it and half of it goes pale with trauma and then darkens again.

It lives so slowly you can't watch it live. You can only see that it has not died.

The second citizen is the reason you notice the first.

In the junction of wall and floor, on the left as you come in, where the broom grooves in the concrete all point like a watershed to the singing floor grate, there is a hairline failure in the pour. Not structural. Just a crack where two days of concrete work met and didn't quite marry. White has bled from it - lime leached, calcite re-precipitated, a tiny, sour chalk. And out of that white, out of that single chemical gift in a room that otherwise offers nothing but water and stone, grows a moss.

It's the size of your open palm. True moss, not industrial felt. Little starry leaves, as bright and wrong here as a parrot. In the bulkhead light it is the only thing in the room that has a color that wasn't assigned by engineers. It shivers.

It lives because of what the room does. Every time the black iron throat in the ceiling bangs with cavitation - *thud-thud-thud* under the white roar - the whole box flexes by a fraction of a millimeter and this tuft shivers and sheds a bead of water that has been sitting on it. That bead runs down the anti-slip grooves to the grate. The moss is being watered by weather made of spray, and fed by the wall itself bleeding. Its rhizoids aren't roots; they have no soil to hold. They are fingers dug directly into raw concrete, prying apart sand grains, dissolving cement paste atom by atom.

It has never set spore. It will never need to. It is perfectly alone and perfectly sufficient. It is probably ten years old. It will probably be thirty. Its entire life is to be brilliant, cold, and to tremble each time the pipe hammers.

And if you are patient enough to kneel - if you can stand to put your knees into that cold, ankle-deep churning on the floor and let the condensation drip down your collar and let your ears go half-deaf - you see that the moss itself is a place.

Inside it lives the third citizenry.

Because no hand has ever washed this moss. No sun has ever disinfected it. Inside its leaves there are pockets that stay almost still, even in this room where nothing is still. The hum that rattles your molars can't quite get in there. And in that stillness live the ones that can truly live here.

Water bears. Rotifers. Nematodes so clear you would need a lens to see them. They live in the film of water that the moss holds around itself like a thought. To them, the Stilling Chamber is not deafening or dim. It is a rainforest of gigantic proportions. The weir is a continental waterfall that never stops. The 40-watt sun comes and never moves. When a fat drop falls from the low ceiling and lands on the moss, it is, for them, a meteor, a flood, a season. They boil and then they settle. They dry out when the spray thins and they swell back when it returns. They do not know about the dam. They do not know that they are 2,800 meters up in a made mountain. They know moss, and moss is enough.

That is all that lives here, and it is not small.

If you stand up again, your legs numb, you realize the other thing that lives here is not a thing at all but the agreement between them. The moss anchors the water bears. The algae softens the concrete so the moss can keep its grip. The water which is not alive and never will be, which is just snow and cloud being moved downhill through iron, is the only reason any of them get to keep their bargain. And the concrete, which is not alive either and most certainly does not want to be alive, keeps weeping lime and holding a shape and making a shadow for them to live inside.

This is what lives in Stilling Chamber 4-B: three dark felts that refuse to die, one bright green hermit anchored in a bleeding crack, and a city of invisible, patient animals inside that hermit's leaves, who have never heard silence and never needed to.
