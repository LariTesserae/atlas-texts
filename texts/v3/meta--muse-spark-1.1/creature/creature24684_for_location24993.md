What lives here is not here.

Not anymore. That's the first thing you understand when you push the leaves aside and get your face in.

The builders are gone. But they lived here so completely, for so long, with such tight, shoulder-pressed intimacy, that their living is still happening.

They were small. Not small like children — small like people who have been edited down to only what is useful at 5,000 meters where there should be nothing. Low, broad women, mostly. Broad hands, broad feet. The kind of People who make things in the low islands — barkcloth beaters, basket women. Their skin was the color of wet thatch. They did not belong up here. You can tell by how they built.

Everything in the pocket is built at hand-height. No ladders. No standing. All the lashing is done with the tension you get sitting cross-legged, elbows in. The knots are all thumb-knots, pulled tight with teeth. You can still see the prints in the pitch — not fingerprints, the whole pad of the thumb, pressed over and over while the seal was still warm to keep the mountain's sweat out. They breathed shallow, in this heat. Their hair was always wet. They must have tended this place not like a house but like a wound dressing. Unpick, reweave, retie. Because the mountain keeps being born behind the back wall.

You can feel how many of them there were. One. Maybe two at a time. Never more. You can't fit more. The pocket was built for one sitter and her replacement. She sat in the middle where the frog sits now, in the one shallow depression where the woven floor has been polished dark by buttocks and heels. From there she could reach everything without standing. The taro on the left for eating, the vine on the right that brings water, the gutter that needed clearing, the seam where the rock intrudes — all within arm's reach. She was a maintenance saint. Her entire job was to keep weaving the room bigger around itself so it would not be crushed.

And she taught the plants to do it.

That's what lives here now, primarily. Not the frog.

The plants.

They are not wild. Not anymore. The taro, the vine, the moss-mortar, the waxy flower that opens against your mouth — they were recruited. Generations of being unpicked and re-threaded through split cane has made them understand cane. You watch it if you sit long enough. The broadest taro leaf in the corner has given up on pretending to be a leaf. Its edge has frayed into a dozen thin, bleached fibers. They are reaching, blindly but with purpose, for the loose lashing above it. The aerial roots no longer seek soil; they seek knots. They know how to tie. This is a hothouse where domestication went too far and looped around. The vegetation is learning thatch. It is now the inhabitant *and* the builder. It sweats and pops and grows with a soft relentless push, and every push is a stitch.

It has no mind. It has a practice. Keep the pocket. Keep reweaving. Keep ducting the water in circles so it never leaves. The old women are gone — maybe they climbed down when the air below cleared, maybe they died and were immediately composted because there was no space not to be — but their hand-motion stayed in the green.

And then there is him.

The frog.

He is the only animal that could solve the conditions: 2.8 hot, 2.6 wet, no space, no light, nothing to eat except rot and petal, nowhere to hop. So he solved them by agreeing to become part of the architecture.

He is big. Two of your fists, cupped. That is too big for how crowded it is. So he does not move. He was not built to be here — you can tell by his eyes, huge and high-altitude adapted, built for distance, for seeing far over the cloud sea — but now he sees only leaf, six centimeters away, forever. So he has gone opaque with boredom and translucence with heat. His skin is the color of old barkcloth left in water. You can see his heart. You can see the water he holds. He is full of water that he carried up here from below in his throat and belly when this place was briefly connected to the world, and he has not let it go. He is a cistern. The plants drink from the humidity he makes.

He is wedged. The taro and the vine have grown around him so tightly he holds his shape because they hold him. If you tried to pull him out the whole north wall would come with him. His legs are folded permanently. His mouth is slightly open to let condensation run in. He does not eat flies — there are no flies this high. He eats the shedding of leaves. He eats rot-sweat. Very slowly.

Every seven or eleven or twenty minutes — time goes soft here — he fills his whole body and lets out a *BONK*.

It is a stupid, deep, holy sound. A temple drum made of wet bark. It is too loud for the room. That is the point.

When he bonks, all those suspended bars of cold trapped light shiver and shatter. Every leaf that has been holding a perfect bead of water on its tip loses its grip at once. The whole ceiling rains. The whole room is re-wetted, re-washed, re-sealed. The plants sigh. The weave tightens with moisture. The embryonic basalt behind the wall hisses as it is cooled for another few minutes and stops growing quite so fast.

Then he goes back to holding.

He is not a god. He is a maintenance saint, like the women. The last monk of a monastery that got too small and too hot and too full and folded itself in half to survive. He lives here because if he leaves, the place dies. And if the place dies, there is — for him — literally nowhere else.

If you leave your hand near him, he will eventually put one translucent, too-warm foot on you, because you are also a surface that is briefly not covered in leaf, and something ought to be woven over you.
