What lives there isn't a population. It's a compaction.

At first from the rim you think you'll see people, and you do — but you'll miscount them the same way you miscount the left turns. There are too few bodies for how dense the place is, and too many presences per room.

#### The Weavers

They are still people, or they still use people as a template, because the tools that are building the town need hands that know what a wall is *for*.

They are tall and not tall. The cold has drawn them thin and long in the way a plant gets long reaching for less light, but they stoop, so they look short in doorways. Their skin is the color of the condensation — bloodless, translucent where it is thinnest at the temple and wrist, with the faint black irrigation of the basin's basalt showing through like text under paper. They don't flush red in the cold. They go clearer.

You won't see them moving fast because fast doesn't work. Their metabolism has negotiated with the sluggish entropy here. Heartbeat slow. Blink slow. When a woman lifts a kettle, it takes her thirty seconds to complete the lift, not because she is weak but because if she yanks, the heat spills out of the water, the handle thickens away from her palm, the wall shivers and forgets what she asked it to do. They have learned to move at the speed the town can compile.

Most of them are re-weavers. They stand in front of the swarm-walls — that latex-black and bone-white that isn't material but agreement — and hold their hands a breath away from it. The wall reaches for the heat in their hands. You can see it bead, thicken. Old men stand there for hours, barely moving, patient as beekeepers, while their doorway learns to be a doorway. Their fingertips are callused into smooth glass. Not from friction. From proximity. From the place where human heat is read as instruction.

They are quiet not from culture but from acoustics. They speak in low, short sentences with long silences between, because sound gets lost in the folds. If you say "bring me the box" too loud in an alley that has four sides, the box hears you three turns later than the person does. So they have learned to speak so that the room finishes their sentence back to them. A conversation here is two people and the room waiting for consensus on what order the words were spoken in.

They live many to a room because a room here is larger than the building that contains it. From outside you see a one-window hutch with a sagging roof of frozen laundry. Inside is a kitchen that goes back ten meters and has corners you didn't earn by walking. Inside that kitchen might be a whole extended family, each with their own pool of ordinary light — that late-summer doorway light — living in their own depth. They don't feel crowded. If anything they rattle around. When they go to sleep they sleep far apart in the same room, so their dreams don't compile into each other.

#### The Children Who Count

The children are the only ones who run, and they are forbidden to and do it anyway. They are the only true natives. They were born after the bowl started forgetting how to count.

They know the town the way you know a badly translated poem — not as a map, but as a set of exceptions. They will tell you which alley is five turns on Tuesday and seven when it's damp, which courtyard box will grow a potato if you ask it nicely and which eats seed. They play a game: stand at the eastern wall, the true edge where the white plain begins, and throw a stone into the town and then go find it where it landed three streets deeper than it could have.

They have an extra joint in their attention. You see them tilt their head when they listen, not to hear better but to hear later, to catch the echo after it has taken the long route around the fold. Their eyes have a habit of not focusing. If you stay long enough on the rim and the condensation catches the light and the town for a second looks like what it wants to be — that impossible star where every door touches every other door — the adults flinch and look away. The children stare straight at it and nod, like, *yes, that's the one*.

Some of them never come back quite right from the deeper streets. Not dead, not lost. Just - recompiled. You see a kid who went into a four-sided alley to chase a thread of moss and comes out a day later wearing the same clothes but his doorway now recognizes him as having two mothers. No one argues with it. They make an extra place at the table that is also an existing place.

#### The Other Tenants

Because fauna is almost absent, something else has to mediate the damp.

In the seams where condensation never falls but gathers, there is a second city the size of your hand. Black lichen and grey lichen that glows at its edges and that wiry moss that only grows in straight lines along drip routes. If you crouch, and most visitors eventually must because the alleys admit four sides, you see the moss isn't growing *on* the wall. It's being permitted. The swarm-wall leaves a capillary open for it because the moss transpires. It helps the air finally decide if it is air. The weavers tend it without ever saying they tend it.

Underneath that, deeper than eye, lives what the weavers call "the hesitation." Put your ear close — not too close — to a fresh basalt foundation where a pipe shoulders out of glassy rock that cooled last week, and you hear a faint ticking, not mechanical, like frost forming and unforming in a place too fast to see. That is the town learning to be ground. That is the swarm deciding how many atoms thick Earth should be here. If you put your palm there too long it starts to count your bones as rebar.

And of course there is the white that is not weather but blankness, just beyond the low wall on the eastern edge. Nothing lives there. That's the point. The town is huddled to keep it out. But sometimes the mist — that riverine breath with no wind — comes *from* that blankness and passes through the town without moving the laundry lines, and for an hour afterwards every surface is dark and slick and you can see fingerprints on the air itself. In those hours the town holds its breath, and so do its people, and what lives in the blankness looks in through the gaps between houses that are also inside houses.

You have seen one of its scouts. Once a day, that small white bird cuts across the sky above the bowl and thinks better of landing. It is not a bird.

The town lives, too. That is the final compaction. Not alive like an animal, but alive like a construction site is alive late at night when the concrete is still curing and all the tools are humming from being left on. It shivers. It sweats. It thickens toward warmth. It is trying, very slowly and in an impossible density, to become a place where people can live, and in trying it has invented people who can live in the trying.

If you descend past the last lip of permafrost and go down into it, they will make room for you. They will move over in a kitchen that is bigger inside than out. They will pour you a kettle that has been boiling for an hour at low heat. They will not ask you to speak loudly. And after a while you will realize your shadow is pooling wrong, your breath is taking too long to fog, and the wall behind your chair has grown a faint, polite thickness toward your back, waiting to see if you intend to stay and be compiled in.
