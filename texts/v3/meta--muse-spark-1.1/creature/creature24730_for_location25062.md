What lives there is not *in* there. Not really.

At first you think - nothing does. You stand in the middle of that green, dripping lung of a room and you feel the way you do in a factory after the shift whistle has blown. The scale is wrong for a nest. It's too empty, too purposefully empty. The floor gives under you and comes back up. The light comes through the leaves and has already been used once.

Then you hold still long enough that the air stops being air.

The first ones to reappear are the ones that were never gone. The midges. 

Not a cloud — a cloud has edges and panics. This is a suspension. Thousands of them, each one holding station in the damp, each one exactly as far from the next as its wings will allow, turning slowly in place, rising and falling a centimeter at a time with the bellows-breathing of the dome. They don't bite. There's nothing here to bite that isn't plant. They live on the spores themselves — the dome is constantly shedding, a fine green-yellow dust of sphagnum spore and liverwort gemmae that hangs in the light like flour in a mill. They strain it out of the air and lay their eggs in it, and they die in it, and it drinks them. If you watch one gnat long enough you see it is not flying so much as being held aloft by the exhalation of a hundred leaves.

What lives there learns to live without landing.

The second thing is the frogs.

They were there the whole time, you just thought they were condensation. Small. Waxy, translucent white, the pink of their guts showing through their bellies, the green of the dome showing through their backs. Not albino — unpigmented on purpose, because there is no sun here to hide from, no leaf-litter to match. The dome grows its own frogs the way a body grows white cells.

There are maybe forty of them. Maybe a hundred. It's hard to count because they don't move unless you breathe on them. They live in the curtains, in the hanging rootlets on the roof, each one straddling two or three sphagnum tips with their toes spread absurdly wide, mouths open to catch what falls. And something is always falling. Water beads at the end of every root hair and swells and releases. The frog drinks that one bead its whole morning. When the bladder columns swell and the hydraulic clunk runs through the pillars, a rain of smaller beads is shaken loose and the frogs all turn their heads at once.

They have never touched ground. There's no ground. Their whole life is moss to moss to moss. They call — but not for mates, there isn't room for that kind of distance. A short, glassy *peep* every few minutes that is less a song than a system check. *I'm still here. This tip is still wet.* Another answers from the other side of the cathedral. The sound is layered over the dripping so perfectly you thought it was the dripping.

Under them — under you, under your boots, in that foot-thick mattress that sighs — is the actual city.

You have to kneel down and put your face close and part the living thatch to see them, and even then you think at first you're looking at working parts of the dome itself. The beetles. Black, domed, utterly polished, as if they've been machined. Carabids that have lost their eyes because there's no dark and no light here, only green. They don't walk, they *patrol*. They run the same routes over and over, through tunnels in the moss that stay open because their bodies hold them open, polishing the walls with their backs. They eat the dead leaves before the dead leaves can decide to rot, they crop the fungal hyphae that would otherwise clog the vents. When a bladder contracts too hard and a seam of moss tears, they are there within minutes, pushing living stolons over the gap like welders.

Among their legs, smaller still: springtails like flecks of blown ash that catapult themselves between leaves with a click you can hear if you hold your breath. Pale, blind mites that herd sphagnum spores like sheep. Pink earthworms as thin as thread that never eat soil, because there is no soil, they eat sphagnum itself and then are eaten by the sphagnum, their castings instantly rooted through.

And the dome itself —

Because after an hour you stop being able to think of the dome as a place and start understanding it as the thing that lives there. The occupant that built the room by being the room.

It is not one plant. It is a compact between a dozen plants that forgot how to stop. Sphagnum magellanicum and Sphagnum fuscum and the creeping haircap and a liverwort with no proper name and a sedge that grows sideways, and a mycorrhizal fungus that runs through all of them like mortar, like wiring, like agreement. None of them could make this shape alone. Together, over ten thousand years, they learned to be a pump. To lift water twenty feet without a trunk, to sweat it inside, to keep themselves at eight degrees, to keep the wind from ever really getting in. To hold a void open in the middle of themselves because a void is more efficient than solid mat for gas exchange. The cathedral is a lung alveolus the size of a house.

It maintains. It listens. When you step, the whole structure takes your weight on hydraulic pressure and passes it outward through a ring of turgid columns. You can feel it decide you are not a threat. The dripping speeds up for a little while over your head, to wash the salt of your sweat off the leaves you touched.

That is who lives there. A superorganism that learned to be a building, and the small, white, polished, patient things that live in a building only when the building itself is alive, and does not need doors, and will never fall down, and sounds — hour after hour, winter after winter on that flat, finished plain — like water going its rounds in the pipes of an empty factory that was never built.
