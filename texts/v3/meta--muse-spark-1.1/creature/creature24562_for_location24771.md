There is almost nothing here now. That is the first thing you have to unlearn.

At first you think the town is abandoned. That is the story its scale wants you to tell. Empty houses, empty loom, empty cistern. Too many rooms. One stool in a room built for twenty. You think they left, or died out, because something so intentional can't have been built to be this sparse.

But the density was never wrong. You were counting wrong.

What lives here does not crowd. It can't.

### The Lush

The first resident is the one they will tell you is not a resident at all. The rust.

It is not on the town. It is the town now. The black-red, the maroon, the oil-purple — the ash-veil.

Get low and you see it is not a mat. It has structure. Each fleshy tongue, each waxy, finger-thick drape that hangs off a lintel, is made of a thousand thousand smaller tongues, folded over each other like a book left in the heat. Its upper skin is armor, dull and dusty, but its underside — if you lift it, and it will let you — is a fever of glass. Hairs, trichomes, clear as spun sugar, each one a hollow needle tuned to shiver. 

It does not photosynthesize. There is nothing to conserve. What it does is worse and more patient. At noon, when the throat of the sink catches the sun and throws that thin amber shaft down, the upper air in the pocket becomes hotter than the salt below. That difference is a meal. The hairs all stand up at once, a tide of them, and the whole town goes loud. You thought it was cicadas. It is not sound. It is the ticking of thermal expansion, the glass hairs lengthening and contracting at different rates, a billion tiny fingers flicking.

It drinks the fall of heat.

At night, when the light goes completely and the only illumination is the deep, held glow of the salt itself, it lets go. All at once. Not a flower opening — a letting go. A shudder runs from the cistern outward, house to house, following the old streets because the streets are wind-channels for stillness. The hairs curl and it exhales a cloud of dry spores, or not spores. Ash. Carbon snowflakes so light they hang. They don't fall for minutes. They hang in the hot, heavy air and you breathe them in and they taste like hot iron and old incense. Then they settle. On your shoulders, on the peat.

It is one thing. That is the part that takes a day to see. The mats on the east houses and the mats in the empty cistern and the beard growing off the abandoned loom are not separate colonies. They are fingers of the same hand. The salt-brick walls, foot-thick and cool to the core, are its bones and its reservoirs. It grew to the shape of the town because the town is a perfect trap for differential heat — low houses spaced wide to make slow convection cells, thick walls that stay cool while the air over them boils, narrow streets that funnel the still air so it never goes still enough to stop moving. The people didn't build a town and watch it get overgrown. They built a farm for this. A cooler house for it.

Where the dead growth falls, it does not rot. There is no water for rot. It abrades. It crumbles into that black-red peat, dry as powdered brick, that has filled the houses up to your shins. You wade through centuries of the veil eating itself and making a bed for itself. If you put your hand in it, it is hot. Always hot. That is its memory. It remembers heat the way soil remembers water.

If you stay until the dim, when the radiation of the day's fire leaks back out of the salt, you can see its thoughts, if it has them. The edges of the oldest fronds near the cave floor pulse faintly, infrared, orange-red then dark then orange-red, house by house, in a rhythm too slow to be breathing and too regular to be random. A signal passing around the pocket, house to house, checking that it is still there.

### The Low Ones

The second resident is why the town has door lintels and worn steps and thumbprints in the brick.

They made the bricks. You can see the print pressed deep in the salt-mud — five fingers, but wide, splayed, the thumb low and huge for squeezing. The hand that made it was not much larger than yours, but it was wider. Flatter. Built like the houses: low.

They are still here, just not many. There was never many. One person needs too much veil.

In the deepest house, where the peat has drifted highest and the walls are felted over so thick you have to push through a curtain that bites warmly at your face, there is a hollow in the peat the exact shape of a body. Not a grave. A bed. The veil grows around it even now, trained away from the center, held back by the old fibers still braided into the doorframe.

They are low because of rule one. Everything here settled to get away from the sky. A tall thing on the white plain above dies in minutes, cooked and blinded. Even here in the pocket, heat collects up near the lip. So they fold. Knees that bend backward a little, feet like broad shovels of callus insulated with salt — you can stand barefoot on stone that would blister you. Backs that are wide, not tall. Heads that are wide too, skulls flattened a little like bread, so the brain sits further from the sun. Their skin is the color of the salt bricks, not white, but the yellow-white of old teeth, because the fine airborne salt has exfoliated into them for generations. Salt is their crust.

Their eyes are the shock. Huge. Not big like an owl's, big like a shallow pan. Amber, almost lidless, with a nictitating film you can see working constantly against the dust. They need it down here, in this perpetual dusk. The three lines carved over every lintel — that is not decoration. That is how they count. Three lines: safe, lower, lowest. You leave that mark so any other one trailing behind you knows you are still descending, still in the protected geometry.

They do not sweat. They cannot afford any system that throws away anything. They are silent in the way that people who live in a place that is already loud have to be silent. If you speak at normal volume here, the veil crackles louder to drown you, irritated by your heat. So they learned a language of pats. Flat palm on salt brick: *I am here*. Flat palm on peat: *I am resting*. Two knocks on a wooden scaffold, because wood carries low and true: *come, it is the exhale*.

What do they eat? They braid the old veil that is about to crumble, heating the glass hairs until they snap and go dull. The underside, once defanged, is chewy and yields nothing but stored differential, a mouthful of hot, metallic time. It is not nourishing in any way your body would understand. But theirs revs a little faster after. Their chests have faint, dark lines under the skin — not veins — the same glass, in some form, implanted. Symbiosis so old they think the veil is a crop.

They built the town empty because company is expensive. Each body puts out heat, and the veil feeds on heat. Too many bodies together in one house and the veil over that house fruits and dies all at once. So: one stool in one huge room. So that the veil on the walls, on the roof, has enough cold wall to survive your sitting there for an hour. A street built wide enough for a market but only one loom in it, because you only ever need one weaver at a time. You weave the veil fiber into rope, into scaffolds that do not conduct heat like metal would. Then you stop. You go home. You sit very still and let the house get cool again.

There is one here now, or there is none. The density makes it hard to tell.

If you stay until the full dim, until the salt itself is the only lamp and the veil begins that slow infrared pulse, you will feel the air behind you become hotter by a degree. You will turn and the peat in the far doorway will have a shallow dent in it that was not there before. Or you will hear, very soft under the crackling, the sound of a broad, flat foot displacing dry peat — *puff* — and then quiet — *puff* — from around two corners away, never getting closer, respecting the distance needed to live here.

If you are very patient, and low, and you press your own palm flat against a salt brick and leave it there until your sweat — if you have any left — is gone, you might feel an answer come back through the stone, a little tardy, as heat soaks through:

Flat. Warm. Still there.
