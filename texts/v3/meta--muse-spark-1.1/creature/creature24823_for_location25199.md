You don't see them at first.

That's how it works there. You come up over a wind-scoured slab and you think - nothing lives here. The stone is too new. It looks like it was made this morning and left out to cure. Too high, too bright, too clean. The sun is white and has no mercy and the wind takes everything that isn't nailed down by weight. You think: sterile.

Then you hear it. Under your boot.

A cough. Not yours. A baby crying, but muffled, like through three blankets. A goat bell, but not ringing — just knocking once against stone. The ground is hollow.

You crouch, and you see the line. A straight, black line in the basalt that shouldn't be straight. It's a fault, fresh enough that you can still see where the rock tore, but someone has laid flat slabs across it, leaving a slit of darkness. From that slit, warm air is breathing out. It smells like animals and fat smoke and damp wool and old stone and milk. It smells crowded.

They live in the cracks.

### The People of the Lintel

They are not tall people. Altitude doesn't allow for tall. They are barrel people — short in the leg, long in the torso, chests like casks. Children born here have blood that is almost black in the vein, thick with red cells. Even at rest they breathe like you do after running. Their lips and fingertips are a little blue, always. They move slowly, not out of laziness but out of economics. Air is thin currency; you don't spend it shouting or hurrying.

You learn to see them by their hands first. Everyone's hands are the same — palms cut with stone, knuckles fat, nails ground flat from splitting slate. A language lives in how a stone sits. They have fifty words for a joint: how a rock bears, how it leans, how it locks, how it wants to slip in three winters' time. A child learns to read a wall the way a lowland child reads a face. Is that slab comfortable? Is it listening to the slab below it? Will it hold?

They live in layers. A single crevasse, ten meters deep and a meter wide, is not one house — it is a building turned on its side, then filled like a honeycomb.

At the very bottom, where the seep never quite freezes, is the garden that is not a garden. Black-green liverwort like wet felt, and hair moss that holds six times its weight in water. They don't eat much of it. It's for the flies.

Above that, the goats. If you want to understand this place, understand the goat.

It's not a goat, exactly. Call it a crevasse-yak. It was bred down, generation after generation, for a thousand years to fit the fault. It stands no higher than a large dog at the shoulder, but it is wide, absurdly wide, and its back is flat and calloused from scraping lintels. Its coat is not hair, it's felt still attached to the animal — underwool so dense water beads on it for days before soaking. It is the color of the stone, black-grey-brown. It eats lichen, it eats cushion-plant, it eats the forty-yard runs of yellow wind-shorn grass and turns it into heat, milk, meat, dung for fuel, felt for walls and clothing. It never sees the sky unless it's being moved between crevasse systems. It spends its life shoulder to shoulder with other crevasse-yaks, standing in condensation drip, chewing slow, breathing slow, keeping the wall at 10 degrees Celsius when outside is -15. It is patient in the way a stone is patient. You can lean against it and fall asleep standing up and no one will think it odd.

Above the goats, a family. Their nook is dry-stone, roofed with a slab that is also the floor for the walkway above. There is no privacy in the lowland sense. You can hand salt to your neighbor across the crack without standing up. You can hear your neighbor's goat chewing, your neighbor's child dreaming. So they have learned privacy of sound. They don't shout. They speak in a low, air-conserving murmur. Disputes are settled by how you place a stone in the shared wall.

Their felt hangs in strips, black with smoke. Meat — goat, muskox, snow dove — hangs in the same smoke and freezes and thaws and smokes again. A clay jar sits under the eternal drip from above, tick, tick, tick. That jar is wealth. Water that stays.

On the other side of the same crack, at the same level, another family. Above both, another slab walkway, another level of goats. Then more people. Then, at the top, the wind-lintels and the sky, and the white dogs lying like rime.

The dogs are not for herding. The crevasses herd for them. The dogs are small, white, round as the cushion plants, with lungs like bellows. They sleep in doorways and boil pots and keep the ravens from taking drying meat. At night they curl around babies. There are more dogs than people, but you only notice them when they move.

That's the pattern, repeated for a continent. Two million people, thirty million goats-that-are-not-goats, uncountable dogs. From above, the plateau looks empty. From inside, it is a seabird colony made of mammals, a hive made of stone. The density is not horizontal but vertical. In ten cubic meters you can have water, flies, goats, people, birds, and smoke.

### The Others Who Pay Rent

Because the heat has to go somewhere.

Every hearth burning seal-oil and dung puts a plume of warm air up through the lintel-cracks. In a place where everything is below freezing, a plume of +20 C air is a miracle. It is a cliff that faces the wrong way. It never dries.

So the birds come. They should not be here in this number. Ravens big as dogs who have learned to knock on flat stone trapdoors to be given scraps. Alpine choughs with red beaks that laugh and steal felt. Snow finches that live in the walls, eight to a fist-sized hole. Ptarmigan, almost invisible until they move, fat on moss in summer. And the white doves — thousands and thousands of them, a snowstorm of them — who nest exclusively on the warm side of chimney stones, where the rime never forms. Their cooing at dusk is the loudest thing in the settlement. They lay their eggs on bare warm rock.

In summer — two weeks when the sun is high and the seeps run a little faster — the pockets hatch.

The black flies and midges that have lived as larvae all winter in that liverwort at the bottom of the crevasses come out all at once. For forty-eight hours the plateau has weather that is insect. A black cloud that is not smoke, low over the slabs. The air vibrates. And everything that lives, human and goat and dog and dove and raven, opens its mouth and eats. Children run on the upper slabs with mouths open, laughing without sound, their faces black. The goats snort them in. The doves go mad with plenty. People preserve them in fat by the jar. It is the only time year when anyone gets fat, and it is holy.

Under it all, doing the invisible work, is the 1.1 of vegetation.

Lichen like rust and sulfur paint, growing a millimeter a year on any slab that isn't walked on. It holds the stone together with acids. Cushion plants — Azorella here would be cousin — but these are harder, domes of tiny leaves so tight they are more mineral than leaf. You can stand on them. They hold dew in hairs on their surface and give it to goats with tongues like sandpaper. And in the wind-lee, where snowmelt lingers for a month, the one extravagance: a strip of grass, forty yards long, yellow, shorn to stubble. That grass is mown not by wind but by teeth, cut by the crevasse-yaks that are led up for an hour a day to graze in rotation, watched by a child whose job is to make sure no hoof breaks the cushion.

That grass is their meadow. Their prairie. They talk about it.

### The Slow

Nothing here is fast except water and flies, and even those return to the same place.

The basalt rises a centimeter a year. The great blue glacier on the north scarp moves an inch a day and has done so since before any lintel was laid. A wall built right — two stones leaning into each other so gravity locks them — will stand five hundred years because there is no rain, only condensation. Families build one granary well, in the driest, most windless elbow of their fault, and their great-grandchildren will add a slab.

People live like that too. Long pregnancies — nine and a half, ten months, common, because bodies conserve. Children slow to walk because the air is thin and a fall in a crevasse is long. Old people are very old, sitting in the warmest nook at the goat-level, tending drip jars, mending felt, listening to stone tick as it gives up the day's sun. They don't tell stories fast.

If you stay long enough that your lungs stop screaming, you start to understand the ethic that isn't spoken but is built: Keep a roof of rock over your head. Keep water near but not in. Share a wall. Don't waste breath. Make thick things — thick blood, thick wool, thick walls, thick with neighbors.

The plateau itself wants to be empty, bright, hard, new. It wants to show its fresh black sheets to the sun. Life there is an argument against that — dense, damp, warm, murmuring, layered into every crack like moss forced into a seam with a thumb. Not conquering the rock, just refusing to leave its pockets.
