What lives here arrived after. Nothing was invited.

First there is the film. That's the base layer, the true ground cover of this range. It's not alive in any way you'd mourn, but it is alive.

On every north face that never sees direct sun, a black-green lichen lays itself down like spray paint, thin enough you think you could wipe it off, thick enough to hold a bead of condensation for a day. On every south face, where the light is too white and the concrete too hot at noon, a white salt blooms instead - calcium leaching out of the fresh pour, drying into a fine crust. It tastes like nothing and everything, like the smell of wet sidewalk made solid. Between them, in every joint where a wall almost touches another wall, there is moss. Real, deep, impossible moss, lime green and rust red, as thick as a mattress where the sweat collects. If you lift its edge it comes up in a sheet, and underneath the concrete is dark and pitted, already being eaten.

That is the pasture.

On the pasture live the grazers. They don't look like much at first because they look like the place.

The most common is a woodlouse thing, but wrong-sized. As long as your palm, as wide as two fingers, the color of damp concrete dust, with a back made of overlapping plates that scrape when it walks. It lives under everything that can be lifted - loose corrugated sheeting, pallets that were left with nothing on them, cable trays - and it spends its whole month-long life going back and forth across a single square meter of lichen, rasping it off with mouthparts you can't see. When you flip its rock it doesn't run. It just tightens slightly. Air is thin here and moving fast costs too much. Everything here has learned to be slow. Dynamic 1.4.

Above it, tending the water, are the skaters. In every sag, every oil-drum lid, every boot-print filled with dark condensation water that never quite evaporates, something lives in the meniscus. Long legs, so long they look like cracks in the water, a body no bigger than a grain of rice. They don't hunt. They farm. They poke and prod at the dust that falls on the surface until a bacterial skin forms, and then they drink the skin. If you breathe on the pool they shatter and re-form a few centimeters away, undisturbed. In the deeper pans — the ones in the V's where two warehouse blocks lean toward each other and make permanent shade — there are larvae. Wriggling commas of black in the brown water, breathing through a snorkel, taking a whole season to become anything.

The moths belong to the light. That was the strangest niche to fill. Because the light here has no source, or has a hundred sources - sun bounced off whitewashed brick off galvanized decking off an amber worklight left on in 1998 - every shadow is a little too light, and every illuminated surface is a little too bright. The moths decided to become the dust in the light. Their wings are the exact gray of air-entrained concrete, speckled with the orange of tie-wire rust. At rest they are invisible against a poured wall. In flight they are clumsy in this thin air, working too hard, whistling faintly like the pigeons do. They don't fly toward light. They fly toward the places where light pools incorrectly, where a sodium lamp in a stairwell with no stairs has reflected itself seven times into a corner and made a little amber puddle that should not be holding together. They sit in it. They warm their blood in light that has no heat.

Then the vertebrates, because this place is so full of holes that a mouse could imagine it's a savanna.

Mice use the cable trays. That is not a metaphor. The whole range is laced with tray and conduit and ladder rack, continuous for kilometers, an elevated highway system at ankle or head height, dry, warm from the low-voltage hum inside, safe from the wet. They have packed the trays with shredded mineral wool and concrete dust and the white fluff from the lichen, made nests that look like failed insulation jobs. They run them at night when the human worklights buzz. They are pale - not white, but the faded tan of unpainted drywall - and they are slow. Fat. They don't need to be fast. Nothing needs to be fast.

The pigeons are the keystone. In any other industrial ruin there would be dozens. Here there are thousands, but you don't see thousands at once because there are thousands of ledges. Every I-beam flange is a cliff roost. Every open electrical cabinet is a cave full of white guano and iridescent feathers. They have adapted to the thin, mean, bright air. Their chests are huge now, bellows. When they take off they don't launch, they heave, beating hard with a hollow whistle from the gaps in their primaries. They eat everything: moss, larvae, woodlice, each other's eggs, the mineral crust, the crumbs under a break table in a tenement that was poured directly against a rolling mill. They circulate nutrients from roof to roof, from damp V to dry tar-top. Their cooing is the only sound that carries correctly, because they've pitched it low enough that the hard right angles can't eat it. At noon, when the black roofs exhale and the sweat starts, the whole range thrums with them.

And the cats that eat the pigeons, or mostly eat the mice that eat what the pigeons drop. There is a specific cat to this altitude. Farm cats are lean. City cats are mean. High-cordillera cats are broad and low and move like liquid poured down stairs. Their fur is thick with that same concrete dust that never settles. They are the color of rebar rust and old galvanizing. They don't stalk; they wait in the slot canyons where two straight walls never meet and let the wrong geometry bring prey to them. You will find one sitting for hours staring at a corner that doesn't connect, because in this range - the 2.8 - a corner can be a hallway if you don't look at it hard. The cats look at it soft.

Because the last residents aren't animals at all. Or they're animals that only exist because of the tolerance error.

Call them holders. Interstitials. You only notice them peripherally, the way you noticed the perspective shift when you first walked here.

There are places where plumb diverges, where if you hang a string it points a degree off from the next string over. In those seams, dust hangs in the air longer than it should. Light makes a faint step, like water over a ledge. If you crouch there long enough, you see things using the step. A beetle that is walking along one wall is suddenly on the opposite wall without turning. A pigeon flies into a shadow that should be thirty meters away and emerges behind you. Things live in that offset - tiny, flat, almost-transparent things made mostly of legs, that cling to the very edge where space disagrees with itself and feed on the shear. You can scrape one off with a fingernail and it smears like static.

And sometimes, a person. Not many. Squatters who came up before the range finished finishing itself, maintenance men who never left, kids born in the tenement blocks that have no outside-facing windows because they were poured against another block. They are thin-lunged and photo-bleached, wearing three layers against the cold bright, eyes narrowed to slits. They know how to walk here without forcing the world to correct them. They don't use straight lines. They allow themselves to be slightly lost at all times, so the place doesn't have to notice them. They drink the sweat water, filtered through moss. They keep the amber worklights burning, not for light, but because the little motors that turn the big fans at three revolutions per minute will seize if they stop, and if they seize, the air will stop moving, and they know - without ever saying it - that if the air here stops moving, the whole high table will finally remember that it has no bedrock, and will forget to hold itself up.
