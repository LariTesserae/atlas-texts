They do not live *in* it the way something lives in a house.

They live *as* it. As expressions of its current compromise.

The pit is not yet a cave because it hasn't learned how to be closed. It is all mouth. So nothing in it has learned how to be hidden yet, either. That's the first law down here. Everything that lives here is exposed, and has made a treaty with exposure.

**The farmers who don't farm.**

You count them last but you see them first because they are the eye's size. 

The hermits. *Coenobita lunula*, if you need a name to pin to something that doesn't want names. They are not true sea crabs anymore, not land crabs yet. Endemic to three pits like this one on the whole plain. Their grandfathers left the sea in the Miocene and walked until they found a place that was wet enough on a schedule and bright enough that gulls wouldn't follow.

They are the size of the first joint of your thumb. Their legs are too long, making them look like little bleached spiders made of glass and parchment. Their bodies are translucent where they aren't sunburned — you can see a faint pink gut inside. They cannot keep water for long, so they live on a clock of condensation. 

And they wear hats.

Not shells. There are no shells down here. They have found something else. The pit sheds flakes of chert from its walls — conchoidal fractures, black or honey-colored, with one edge always sharper than the rest. Each crab has one. They carry it on their back like a parasol when they walk, held in the smaller cheliped, tilted to keep the sun off their gills. When they find food, they flip it. 

The food is not food as you think of it. It's lithic algae, a cyanobacterial film that grows inside the limestone itself, a millimeter per century. It tastes the light and lives just under the surface of the rock, staining it faint grey-green. You would miss it. They don't. 

They kneel — if crabs can kneel — and scrape. Flake edge against stone. *tik...tik...tik.* The sound is so quiet you hear it only when you have stopped listening for anything bigger. It leaves a little pale scar, two parallel lines. The whole bottom meter of the pit is covered in that braille. A manuscript written by undesigning hands, for centuries. It says: I was hungry here. I was hungry here again.

No crab makes a better flake. They find them. Some flakes are lucky — thinner, with a better grip. Those get stolen. You will see two crabs standing over a particularly fine white chert lid, pushing, testing, not quite fighting. They are not social. They are not solitary. They are just enough of each other that tool-use can persist without teaching. The knowledge lives not in the crabs, but in the pit that keeps making flakes that fit in crab hands. The pit is the culture.

When the pans flood they stop scraping and drink, lowering their whole faces into the ephemeral water. For an hour they are almost aquatic again. Then they go back to being desert things.

They are hundreds. But because the pit is so bright and so perfectly empty in the middle, you never see more than six at once unless you sit still for a long time. Stillness is their predator. If you are still long enough they forget the shape of your boots and use your shadow as a pool.

**The ones who live faster than water.**

The pans have their own citizens, citizens who are only sometimes alive.

You would swear the dust was dead. It's white limestone meal, dry as flour. Then the afternoon sigh happens — the cold breath finally leaking out of the deep rock after the day's heat has pooled — and a film forms. First just a darkening. Then a sheen.

And then it wriggles.

Fairy shrimp. *Branchinecta alba.* They aren't born, they resume. Their eggs wait years in that dust if they have to, little cysts the size of salt. When water touches them they hatch in minutes. For two hours they are the only thing in the world that is fast. Pink-brown, transparent, twelve legs beating, they flicker through water that is a quarter-inch deep and contains the whole sky reflected. They don't eat each other. There's not time. They filter the one bloom of bacteria that bloomed the instant before them, they mate in mid-tumble, they lay. By the time the edge of the pan starts to retreat, turning white again, they are already dying, leaving behind the next dry generation. If you cup your hands around a pan, you can feel the heat of their brief, furious metabolism. An entire civilization of frenzy that leaves no ruins.

In the one permanent mirror under the north wall, their cousins tried to stay. Water-boatmen, bleached bone-white to match the limestone, backs perpetually breaking the surface tension. They row without ripples. Below them, in the coldest black inch of that pool, the true cave things are already starting to happen — isopods, blind, unpigmented, each like a grain of cooked rice that learned to walk. They graze on the detritus that falls into the pool and never leaves. They have no eyes because this is how a place starts to grow eyes on the inside. Give this pit another hundred thousand years of dissolving down, and they will be everywhere, and the crabs will be fossils under their feet.

**The vertical things.**

Life is not supposed to be vertical here. It is, because horizontal is lethal.

The maidenhair ferns live in the only direction that stays damp after the air lightens. They don't grow in a clump. They grow in a Morse code line up a seep track, one frond, an inch of bare wall, another frond, as if each spore arrived and politely decided not to crowd. Their black stems are the darkest thing in the pit. They shiver even when there is no wind, because water moving through them is the only movement they get.

The ants live vertical too. Tiny, matte-black *Pheidole* workers, marching down from the rim they can never quite return to, harvesting the wind-burnt tips of those fern scales, carrying them into a crack too narrow for your finger. Inside that crack there is a half-teaspoon of soil that has been accumulating since the Pleistocene. That's their world. The whole plain above is a desert; down here, in three square inches of crack-soil, it's a rainforest.

The orange lichen on the sunward wall is not in a hurry. It grows inward. It eats light that has weight.

**And the thing that is not an animal.**

There is one more resident. You felt it when you first hit bottom and your depth perception failed.

In the early afternoon when the temporary waters are at their fullest and evaporation turns the still air into a low, warm cloud, the light itself moves in. Not sunbeams — those have edges. This is thicker. Pillars form where no pillar should be, between the empty bright and the stone, slow, viscous columns like invisible water made visible by dust that isn't there. They stand in the middle of the pit doing nothing. Fairy shrimp swim through them and for an instant they are gilded.

It isn't an organism. But in a place this balanced, this close to becoming closed, light has started to behave like water. And water, down here, has started to behave like a clock. 

Everything that lives here lives inside that clock. They do not build. They do not store. They do not need to. The pit makes a little water every day, erases it every evening, makes the same light every day, erases it every night. Their whole job is to be exactly as temporary as the condition that allows them.

If you stay until dusk you will see the light-resident leave first, pooling up and out the throat like it was being sipped. Then the fairy shrimp pans go dull. The crabs stop scraping and tuck their hats down tight over their backs and become stones among stones. The pool mirror goes perfectly still and shows you the circle of the first stars. And you understand, finally, why no one lives here.

Because it is already full.
