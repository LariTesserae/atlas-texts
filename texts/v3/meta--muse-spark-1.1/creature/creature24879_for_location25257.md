No one lives here the way people live on the surface.

If you stand still long enough for your lamp to steady and your own silt to settle — and you have to stand still a long time, because down here everything settles slowly — you start to see that you misread the cull marks.

The white gardens are not abandoned plots gone wild. They are cropped. But not cropped the way a human with shears crops. They are *grazed* short, bitten. The stalks that grow in the warm seams between the boiler plates are all exactly the same height, the height of a mouth. And between the clumps, on the enamel-hard rust, there are places worn clean. Not by water. Water smooths everything. This is polished. Rubbed by something soft passing again and again.

Then you realize you haven't seen any silt *on* anything that matters. The catwalks have silt. The dead roofs have silt. But the chain runs are clean. The handwheels on the pressure shutters are clean. The lip of every furnace mouth where the amber light leaks out is clean, buffed to a dull shine by palms.

Something still wipes its hands before it works.

They are not big. They cannot afford to be big. The cold that is a material — 2.9 of 3 — punishes anything that tries to keep a large warm core. So they have become small and round and low, to keep the warm in.

The first time you see one, you think it's a piece of the Works detaching. A sack of tools falling.

They walk.

They do not swim. Swimming here, in water that hangs in sheets, would cost too much. They walk on all fours along the gantries and the pipe-backs, magnetized not by magnetism but by sheer weight. Their bodies are dense. Their bones, if they are bones, have taken on iron from the water until they are ballast. When they lose their footing, which is rare, they do not flail. They simply sink, slowly, feet down, until they touch again, and continue.

They look like they were made here, not born elsewhere and adapted. Imagine a person built by someone who had only ever heard a person described as "soft thing that tends a fire."

They are the height of a fire hydrant, the width of a boiler drum. No neck. No chin. Their skin is not skin anymore. It's the same black-brown enamel the rust makes, but supple, still living — thick plates with translucent white furrowing between them where the heat can get in. Their backs are barnacled with white garden. Not decoration. Cultivation. The stalks root in the seams of their plates, their black beads orienting forward, toward the furnaces, so each of them carries a little forward-facing constellation of dim lights. They farm themselves.

Their faces — if you can get in front of one, which you shouldn't, because they don't like light that isn't amber — are blunt, featureless, folded in on themselves like a closed fist. The eyes are gone. Not lid-covered. Gone. The sockets have smoothed over and become anchor points for feelers: six pale filaments, each as long as your forearm, each the same species as that blind jet-pulsing thing you saw. They are not appendages. They are symbiotes, or children, or sense organs that left. They taste vibration. When a bolt is about to shear, three gantries over, the feelers all twitch toward it.

They don't breathe water. Nothing that breathes could live on this cycle. They breathe the difference between hot and cold. You see it when one leans into an exchanger sigh — the warm water pours over its side, and the white furrows flare pink for a moment, and silt shivers off it as the water inside it changes density. They inhale heat. They exhale a small, perfectly clear plume that the cold devours in a foot.

There are not many. The bowl can only hold so much warm. You think maybe thirty. Maybe forty when the brood is tucked. They live in the densest part of the knot, where three warm pipes braid together and make a hollow no bigger than a closet in surface houses. In that hollow the water is their water, held still and warm and clean. They press together in there, plates clinking softly, and you can hear — not with ears, but through your boots, through your teeth — a faint clicking. Pipe-code. They tap on each other, on the pipes. Not speech. *Accounting.* This shutter loose. This belt fraying. This garden needs burning back.

Because the other thing they farm is the still-water.

That pocket in the oldest part, where water has become architecture and holds tools on invisible shelves — that is not storage. Or not *just* storage. That's where they memorize things. They can't write. Paper would become soup. But water that has been still for a century remembers pressure perfectly. So they place a wrench in it and the water remembers the shape of a wrench around the shape of a lack-of-wrench. They place their dead in it too.

When one gets too cold to keep its gardens alive, it walks itself into a still-place and lets go. It doesn't sink. It just stays. The water takes its edge, its weight, its heat-difference, and holds it. The next one that comes along will put a hand against the gelatin edge and feel, for a few minutes, how to do a thing that the other knew. How to seat a piston that takes seven minutes per cycle without getting your feeler caught. How to scrape the stalks without killing the root.

They keep each other, like the water keeps tools.

They have seen you. One has stopped grazing a pipe three levels up and has turned the black beads of its back-garden toward you. Its feelers are all pointed at your lamp. It doesn't flee. Nothing here flees fast. It just considers whether your heat will hurt its crop, whether your clumsy pushing-aside of sheets will stir a still-place, whether your need to hear things with ears will keep you from hearing the low deep thrumming in your sternum that says: *a flywheel two floors down is wobbling a millimeter off true and needs shimming now, now, now.*

After a minute, it decides you are not cold enough to matter yet, and not hot enough to be useful. It resumes biting, each bite precise, and its garden shivers.

The Works continues. It was built to be drowned, and so it was, and so it found the people who could live in drowning.
