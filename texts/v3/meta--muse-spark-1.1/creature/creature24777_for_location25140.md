It is not empty. That's the first mistake you make.

From the rim, with your chin over that low warm dome of water that refuses to spill, it looks untenanted. Black tea. Stillness so complete it holds the shape of the sky. You think, for a second, nothing could live in water that has been held this long.

Then you put your face under and it resolves.

What lives here lives close. In a place this small and this old and this completely made, there is no open water. Every cubic inch is already claimed.

**The Pasture itself is alive.**

First, before animals, there is the felt. The builders set limestone block after limestone block — forearm-long, hand-set — and in the ten thousand years since, the stone has grown a skin. A bacterial varnish, brown-black and velvety to the eye, slick to the touch if you brush it, though you shouldn’t. It grows a micron a year by eating the rock itself, exhales a faint iron tang. It covers everything: the five descending rings, the two squat pillars on the north side, the choked overflow notch on the south. It is the only thing that makes food from stone here. Everything else eats it, or eats something that ate it. If you stay still long enough with a light pressed close, you can see it breathe — not moving, but the faintest paling and darkening as water passes.

**On the felt, the grazers.**

They are the most numerous. You think at first the water is dusty. Then the dust flicks.

Pale shrimp, thumb-joint small, completely clear except for a single thread of dark gut. They don’t swim so much as row. Thousands of them. They move in loose clouds that keep to one ring at a time, like sheep assigned to a terrace. They have two fans on their heads that comb the felt, leaving behind pale stippled paths. When they are startled they don’t dart — there is nowhere to dart to — they just instantly go still and become invisible again.

Under them, the Bearers. If the shrimp are sheep, these are slow, blind cattle. Big as your fist, domed on top like a river stone that has been polished until it shines, flat and soft underneath. Segmented, limbless, they are not shrimp and not worm and not isopod, but something that learned to be all three because nothing else was going to fill the job. They crawl so slowly that you cannot watch them move. You look, they are here. You look away to blink, they are a millimeter on. Where they have passed, the bacterial felt is shaved clean to a lighter scar, and in a day or two that scar will be grazed again by the shrimp, who follow the Bearers like birds after a plow. If you turn one over — and a few are always overturned — the underside is a pale, frilled mouth that never closes.

In the rope grooves on the lip, where a thousand years of jars being hauled left canyons deep as your wrist, lives a whole nation that never leaves its canyon. White mites, smaller than rice grains, eyeless, with legs like eyelashes. They walk the groove endlessly, farming a whiter fungus off the salts left by evaporation at the dome. The whole world, for them, is a single curved line around the bowl.

**In the hollows, the tenants.**

The builders left cups in the third ring, little oil-lamp niches, each just big enough to cup in your palm. Each one is now occupied. In each one sits a single large shrimp — not pale, but amber, twice the size of the cloud-shrimp, with one claw grown huge and flattened like a door. This is the Keeper shrimp. It lives its entire life in that one cup, sweeping it clean, defending it. When light moves over it, it raises its door-claw and shuts itself in.

Higher up, under the lintel of the old sluice between the pillars, there are snails with shells like wet paper. They cannot survive alone. Their shells have grown thin and then grown together. You will see a cluster of ten, fifteen, sharing one convoluted, lumpy apartment of shell, all their soft bodies turned outward to feed, all turning together when the slow warm current sighs through the bowl. If one dies, the others eat its body and keep its room.

**In the water column itself, the wolves.**

They are not many. A closed bowl can’t feed many wolves.

The eels — though they are not eels — hang in the water. Ash-colored, as long as your forearm, muscular in a way that looks wrong for how little they move. Ribbed along their sides with what looks like gills but are mostly heat exchangers. They hang vertically, nose to the sky-rim, tail down, utterly still, saving the mountain’s heat. They drift down a centimeter per minute. They are blind. They navigate by feeling the tiny pressure wake of the shrimp clouds. When they pass through a cloud, they open — not a bite, but a sudden, soft inhalation — and a dozen shrimp disappear. Then they hang again.

You will think there is only one, and then you will see there are seven, all at different depths, all hanging, all waiting.

**At the bottom, the city.**

Four meters down, where light is only a chemical memory and your lungs begin to feel the weight, the limestone blocks disappear. You cannot see the stone anymore because it is paved entirely with the filterers. They are not coral. Coral needs light and the sea. These are something that remembered coral and reinvented it in the dark. Soft, branching, the color of old bruises, each branch tipped with a mouth fringed in hairs so fine they are invisible until they close. They open and close on a clock so slow you cannot time it — one breath every five minutes, ten minutes. Together, as a carpet, they pump the whole bowl over, slowly, endlessly. They are what keeps this bath-warm water from going stagnant. They eat what dies.

And things do die. That is the rule that made this place what it is.

What comes in, stays.

If a Bearer at the end of its long, slow life lets go, it falls. The shrimp scatter. An eel will angle, almost reluctantly, and take what it can. The rest sinks to that living carpet at the bottom, and is taken apart over a week so thoroughly that by the seventh day there is only a faint white outline on the dark felt — the shape of what it was — and then the felt grows over even that.

Nothing leaves. Silt doesn’t leave. Heat doesn’t leave. A lost hair from your head won’t leave. That’s why it is so full.

You float there, in the middle of a room built before any map you know, and you realize the builders didn’t just build a cistern. They built a set of niches — lamp cups and rope grooves and knee-high steps — and then left, and the water stayed, and life did what life does when you give it warm, still water and five perfect rings: it moved in and filled every handhold you left.

It doesn’t need you. It has its pasture and its flocks and its wolves and its farmers of white fungus and its city at the bottom that breathes once every ten minutes. It is, in the strictest biological sense, finished. Complete.

It was high, and it was made full of water anyway. And then, because it was full, it became full.
