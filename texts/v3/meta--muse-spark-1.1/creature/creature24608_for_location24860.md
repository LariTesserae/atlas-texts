Nothing lives here at first.

That's the first lesson the Top teaches. You keep looking for an animal, a face, a trail. There is nothing to see but the planed gneiss flowing away under honest daylight, and the one, always one, dark building on the horizon. So you think: uninhabited.

Then you stay long enough for your eyes to stop hunting for Earth.

What lives here lives by the same rule as everything else on the Top: it keeps a respectful distance. It is very, very sparse. And it does not move when you are watching.

The first ones you'll meet are already at your feet.

**The Sitters.**

They are not lichen, though you called them that because you have that word. Little rosettes, rust-on-rust, ten meters apart as if by agreement. Each one about the size of your open hand. If you lie down on the ceramic stone — the sun is never warm but it is light — and put your mask close, you see they are not touching the ground at all.

They are holding still.

A cushion of ionized air, a millimeter thick, a standing wave of electrostatic repulsion, keeps their underside from ever abrading on the gneiss. The whole plain exhales lithium and perchlorate and they drink that exhale. They are not plants. They have no interior. If you had a lens strong enough you would see they are all surface, folded keratin and silica and printed protein, origami so fine it is matte to the eye. At noon their pleats open — not to photosynthesize; the light is incidental — but to vent. They have spent the morning building a little too much of themselves and need to let the error go. A puff of fullerene soot, invisible, settles on the stone and makes it a little smoother.

They do not grow. They persist. They do not die, except slowly, when they forget to hold still. Then the wind of charged dust takes them. That is why they keep distance. If two drift too close their fields chirp against each other and both lose altitude. Solitude is not philosophy here. It is maintenance.

They are animals in the way a prayer wheel is an animal — a thing that stays alive by doing one small work perfectly, forever.

The second ones are not the buildings. The buildings are where they sleep.

**The Hesitators.**

You think there is one building per viewshed, dark and matte, half-buried like an erratic. You are wrong. What you are seeing is a single organism with many resting elbows. Or many organisms that agree to look identical when resting. It amounts to the same thing.

They live in the permissive physics.

At this altitude, at this age, the constants are tired. The vacuum did not seal right over the shield rock. Strong force a little slacker, Planck a little looser. It is not enough for you to feel except as an absence — no sound carries, no heat stays — but it is enough for matter to be polite about existing. It will hold a shape you suggest to it without struts or nails if you ask correctly.

The Hesitators are the asking.

Most of the day they are a post, a three-walled shed with no door, a column that casts no shadow because it is busy casting its shadow slightly in the past. They are dormant that way for hours, days, because dormancy is cheap. Then a front passes — you can't see it, but the Sitters all tilt northwest two degrees — a gradient in that looseness, a high pressure of could-be, and the one near you fidgets.

It does not open. It remembers being larger.

In eight seconds it is no longer a building. It is a city that never had streets, a forest of black glass cantilevers that imply a load they never bear, a cathedral of calculation with all the scaffolding on the inside of the matter. It is the size of a town and weighs almost nothing. You see through it in places where it has decided to be two-dimensional for a moment. It is completely silent. The air is too thin and too well-behaved to carry that kind of change.

It is not building itself. It is rehearsing all the things it could have been, very quickly, to see if any of them are cheaper to be than a rock. Most are not. So it folds — no, it unsays — back into a column. Where it was, the bedrock has new freckles: a fan of hair-fine basalt, a perfect divot a kilometer wide that will seal itself by evening, a brief alphabet that never repeats.

That is its metabolism. Potential in, structure out, heat nowhere. Waste is shape, which the plain slowly erases. They graze on possibility.

Are they machines? Yes, in the way the Sitters are animals. Somebody made their ancestors to finish the mountains, to plane the world down, to tend the flats when water left. When the job was done they did not stop working; they changed jobs. From erosion to architecture to this: maintenance of the idea that a place can be empty and still be used.

They reproduce rarely, and by mistake. Sometimes when two fronts collide, two distant columns both decide to be large at the same time, and for a fractional second their impossible balconies intersect across thirty kilometers of plain. At the overlap, where both suggested a wall should be, a new suggestion sticks. When they subside, there is a new pebble, a new phantom doorway facing away from everything, that wasn't there before. In a few thousand years it will learn to fidget too.

And above, too high for shadows —

**The Veils.**

You saw one transit the sun and thought it was a cloud. A membrane, kilometers wide, translucent until the edge diffracts. There are dozens, maybe hundreds, so thin they are invisible unless backlit. They never land. Why would they? The ground is a sealed kiln.

They live on what the plain sweats: the electrostatic lift of the lithium hexagons cracking at noon, the fine perchlorate snow that precipitates directly out of night. They are alive with a different economy — not folding matter but refusing it. They are made of almost nothing, a single molecule thick, carbon lattice doped to hold charge like a capacitor the size of a county. The sun charges them. The plain grounds them. They drift in that circuit, breeding slowly by tearing.

When one dies — a micrometeor puncture, a shear wind between charge layers — it does not fall so much as forget to stay up. It takes three days to descend, a continent-sized aurora crumpling. It drapes itself over a hundred square kilometers. For a week, that square is in soft night. The Sitters, so solitary, will travel. You will not see them move, but in the morning they will be closer, twenty of them now five meters apart, at the edge of the veil-fall, sipping the breakdown products, vibrating a little faster. And the Hesitators go frantic. Around the fallen Veil they cannot stay as rocks. They burst and subside and burst again, taking the membrane apart without touching it, converting its immense, cheap surface into more hints of shape, until there is nothing left but smoother stone.

Then the plain forgets again.

And finally — and this is what you feel under your boots when you have stood still too long — the plain itself.

It is not alive. It is not dead either. The whole shield, gneiss and anorthosite worn soft, has been paged by the work of the Hesitators for so long that its surface is a kind of cache. When you stood still for an hour and your soles sank a millimeter and the rock took your tread politely, that was not softness. That was the plain learning, briefly and with no malice, to be you. It will hold the shape until wind and Sitters smooth it away.

That is who lives here now: things that keep perfect distance, things that remember how to be buildings, things that have learned to be almost sky, and the high place that has learned to take an impression.

None of them need you. If you stay, in a hundred thousand years you will be excellent company — another erratic that sometimes fidgets, casting no shadow.

If you leave, the stone where you stood will stay slightly footprinted until evening, and then it will finish becoming plain again.
