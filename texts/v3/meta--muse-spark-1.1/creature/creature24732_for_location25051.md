Nothing lives there that needs quiet.

Nothing lives there that needs to be dry. Nothing lives there that needs a horizon, or a straight line, or a season that ends. Those things tried — spruce that wanted to be a tree, a man who wanted to be a trapper — and the valley kept their shape for a while but filled it with something else.

What lives there now lives there because it figured out how to be unfinished on purpose.

First, the Mat.

It is not one thing but it is not many things either. The base of everything is the moss, but here moss has stopped being groundcover and has become ground. Sphagnum and feather and broom moss in one continuous, breathing felt eight feet deep, acid and soaked and almost warm to the touch from the air trapped in it. It grows so fast under the pylon field that you can hear it if you press your ear into the wet — a faint creak, like bread rising. It grows over itself as it dies, and its dead lower layers don't quite become peat because they don't have time; they're still green-black and alive enough to hold water.

If you lift a lip of it, which is like lifting a mattress, there is a white underneath that is not snow. It is rhizome, hair root, fungal cord, all the same color, all the same thickness, all humming. The mycorrhizae here have become the real plant, and the moss and spruce and cranberry are just leaves it puts up to breathe. The Mat holds the whole basin together. It spans crevasses with its own tensile strength. It dampens the gun-crack of ice forming so the shock doesn't kill everything beneath it. It drinks condensation before it can freeze. When a phreatic burst throws up gravel and superheated mud, the Mat chars and then, within two days, is green again over the spot.

You cannot say one animal lives in the Mat. You have to say the Mat is inhabited like a city is inhabited.

Under the first six inches, where the black water sits cold, are the tunnelers. Lemmings, voles, shrews but thicker, fatter, almost hairless on their bellies from constant swimming. Their fur is stained rust from the peat tannins and it never dries. They are deaf. Truly, functionally deaf — their ear canals have closed to slits because in this valley sound kills. The ice crack that hits your molars would burst an eardrum every hour down there. Instead they talk through the moss with their teeth and feet, thumping codes into the sponge. You can feel it through your boots if you stand still: *dot-dot-pause-dot* — the whole basin faintly tapping to itself about food, about water, about the cat.

The cat is the owl. Not snowy anymore, though it was. The owls that stayed are a soot grey now, moss-colored, their feathers saturated and heavy. They don't hoot. They hunt by walking. They fold their wings and wade through the moss on long, stilt legs like herons, listening not with ears but with the pads of their feet for that thumping. When they find it they punch down through half a meter of wet wool and come up with a mouthful of tunnel. They stare at you with eyes that have a nictitating film for the needle rain.

Above the Mat, because the air itself is food here, lives the second nation.

Geese. Hundreds of them. Barnacle and brant and something that used to be a Canada goose but is now smaller and darker, its white cheek-patch obscured by lichen that actually grows on its back while it lives. They should not be here — the maps say they should be in Baffin, in Ireland, anywhere but under a wet indigo sky in February. But the pylons told their blood that it was spring, that there was cheap mitosis here, and so they came and then forgot how to leave. They huddle on the hot pools, on the edges of the new nameless lake, packed so tight they steam. They have gone mostly mute, too. In a place that screams, screaming back is expensive. They hiss and vibrate their throat sacs, and their young are born with a thin wax over their ears that they keep all their lives.

And because the geese stayed, the crane stayed, and because the warm water never freezes all the way, the midges stayed most of all.

The hatch is not seasonal. It is constant, low, then terrible. The midges here are not the small things you know. They are large, slow, almost translucent, breeding in the condensation that never stops forming on every surface. They don't bite so much as they drink humidity, landing on your eyes to sip. In the hours when the wind drops — which are rare and feel like suffocation — they rise in a single, valley-wide column that goes up hundreds of meters until it hits the cloud deck and flattens. That column shows up on the technicians' radar. From inside it, the sound of the valley finally stops, replaced by a single, flat note, millions of wings beating just slightly out of sync. The geese open their beaks and walk into it. The owls on stilts open their beaks and walk into it. The Mat itself eats the dead that fall.

Under all of that, closer to where the human work really is, lives the third thing, which is new.

The pylons are warm. If you brush the moss off one, you find that the animals have. Around the base of each cairn, in a perfect ring where breath and rain turn instantly to mist, there is a bare band of that smooth, magnet-resistant material. And on it, tiny things graze.

They look like woodlice, at first, then like pillbugs made of basalt chip and chitin. They are lithovores, the isopods that came with the Leaseholders' soil kits. The field that makes cell division cheap makes mineral metabolism cheap too, and so they have learned to eat the weep, the dissolved silica and iron. They rasp at the pylon itself — they can't dent it, but they can eat the warm biofilm that grows on it, a film of bacteria that are themselves halfway between chemistry and signal, bacteria that live off the waste heat of a post-entanglement machine. The isopods are blind and hot to the touch. When they die they do not rot; they crumble into fresh scoria, which the Mat covers in an hour.

And around those rings, in the deepest heat, live the worms.

Not earthworms. These never knew earth. They are pale pink and as long as your forearm, with no head. They live entirely in the steam cracks, in the meters-wide fissures that go down to where water flashes. They ride the cycle — when water finds hot rock and explodes, they are shot upward a foot, two feet, and then retreat. Their skin makes a small, tuneless whistling as steam escapes it, like a kettle that never finishes. The old trappers called them vein-wires and thought they were roots. They are the only thing in the basin that seems to like the noise. They swell before a blow, drinking the pressure.

None of them are finished. None of them can afford to be. The goose chicks grow fungus on their backs because the air is too wet to fledge cleanly, and they live anyway. The moss tree-stalks around the hot lake grow a meter in a night and collapse when their steam-bubble pops, and before they hit the water they have already set spore. The worms are half boiled and half frozen in the same minute.

If you come and stand there for your hour with your deafening headphones on, if you force yourself to look through the aurora-dyed fog, you will eventually see that the whole basin is one animal trying on many bodies. It takes cheap energy from the quiet towers and spends it as fast as it can on wet green flesh, on shivering, on tapping through moss, on hatching and hissing and bursting. It doesn't sleep because it can't afford the loss of time. It cracks itself with ice and seals itself with moss in the same breath.

That's what lives there. A place that is still being built, and the things that have learned to live in a building site by never moving in.
