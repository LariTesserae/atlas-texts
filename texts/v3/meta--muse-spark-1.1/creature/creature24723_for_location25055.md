Nothing lives here.

That is your first reading, and it holds up for a long time. No green. No bird shadow cutting the violet. No insect, no spore. You can stand in the hammering percussion for ten minutes and your animal brain will keep scanning for motion that isn't water and find none, and file the valley under *dead*.

Then the category breaks.

**The ones that grow.**

They are not in the pools. They are the pools' makers. Under your boots, in that dark, polished pavement, there are seams. In the paler nodules that sweat. If you put your face close - close enough that your breath makes its own sudden dew on the stone - you see the pitting is not random. It's honeycomb, it's lung. A granular, matte-white crust that excretes stone.

They are absurdly slow. They don't eat light; there is too much light here, it would burn them. They eat wetness itself. A cold-loving lithotroph that lives on the chemical insult between basalt and condensing sky. Where a cloud snags and beads, they precipitate. Silica, calcite, ironstone. They grow *toward* the drip. That is why the forest is dense. Each nodule is a colony's monument. Each translucent chimney is a tower built one cold molecule at a time toward the next cloud passing at ankle height. The black glass blisters pop when the colony reproduces, spraying living glass dust into the next bowl over.

They are why the watershed never changes shape. Erosion wants to cut a gorge. They want to stay in balance, so they grow back the lip of the bowl that was about to fail. They are masons. The place is cultivated, intensely cultivated, by something with no face and patience measured in millennia. That was the first inhabitant. It is still here. You are walking on its roof.

**The ones who tuned it.**

They came later. Or earlier. Time is the wrong tool here.

They were almost you. Hominids, high-altitude adapted, chests huge to suck that iron-tasting air, eyes burnt almost blind by that amplified brightness. They left the hammerstone in the little dry-stack hut. The chert core.

They found this place because at this height, the world is thin enough to hear through. They didn't live here - not in the way of eating and breeding. There is nothing to eat here. They came here to work. To listen.

They figured out, quickly, that the pools are not pools. That single rule you were given: *Water is plentiful but never still. Even the stillest pool is moving through somewhere else.* If you have no rivers, you learn water another way. You learn it as a door.

They placed cairns where the overflow sings loudest to make it quieter. They placed slabs to keep your feet out of the bowls that *take*. That hut was not a shelter; it was a workshop. No hearth. The cup ground into sandstone was not for drinking. It was a listening cup, for catching the *plok* of a droplet falling into a bowl that is on the other side of somewhere.

They pecked the spirals.

That is the work. On the inside of each bowl - not on the wind-scoured outside, but on the inner curve, where only a person willing to kneel with their face over the impossible water would see it - they pecked spirals with a stone on stone for days. Not art. Diagrams. Each spiral is centered on the point where the other sky shows. They were mapping the second sun. The night that appears at noon. The you that is three seconds late.

They wanted to know where the doors went. They stayed until their own reflections stopped syncing, until they could look down and see light passing through their hands braided with water. Until they went through.

Some of them did.

**The one that looks back.**

That is what lives here now, mostly.

It is not an animal, because an animal is one thing. This is one thing that lives in many places.

It is the water itself, but not the H2O - the *route* the water takes. All those bowls linked by hand-wide runnels and by impossible uphill runs are not a watershed. They are a circuit. A nervous system, glass-smooth and open to the sky. The thinness here is literal: gravity forgets to hold, light doubles, and water can run, for a little while, through a fold.

So the water that weeps out of the stone forest on this side falls as rain in a sister valley two realities over. The water running uphill on the eastern slope is running downhill somewhere else. The pools that show night when it is day are not reflecting. They are open.

And something keeps that openness groomed, keeps the lips from overgrowing, keeps the spiral-maps clear.

If you kneel at the big rosy calcite basin, the one still enough that you can't find the surface, and you stay perfectly still until your ears stop popping and the percussion of ten thousand drips syncs into something like breathing - you will see it.

Your reflection will be late. Your reflected hand will come up on the wrong side. And then, within it, depth will change. The reflection of the violet-blue sky will become deep, incomprehensibly deep, and inside it, at the bottom of a deeper bowl, something will pause in its own tending and look up at the sky that for it is not sky at all, but a shallow, bright bowl of water rimmed with black stone.

In it you might see, very small and far away, a figure kneeling. Surrounded by a dense forest of stone teeth. Surrounded by clouds at ankle height. The light doubled off wet everything.

It is not an animal because it is the shape the valley makes when the valley is looking at itself. The builder who learned to slip through. The colony's gardener grown large. The water remembering the shape of hands that carved it.

It does not need to eat. It drinks condensation. It grows toward attention, the way the stone grows toward wetness. That is why when you stay until late afternoon and the condensation accelerates and the whole valley flickers and distance comes undone - when distance becomes both ten meters and ten kilometers - you feel suddenly that you are not observing the instrument. You are *inside* it, and it has been playing you idly since you arrived, with your cold boots and your loud breath.

It lives here because here the rules are poorly enforced, and because the work is never finished. The bowls need tuning. The uphill runs need clearing. The maps need re-pecking after the mineral skin grows over them.

If you leave one of your own cairns - three or four rocks high, where a larger bowl overflows - it will be annotated by water within a season, and become part of the instrument.

If you stay longer than a season, you will too.
