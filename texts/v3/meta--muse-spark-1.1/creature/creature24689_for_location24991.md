They don't live in it. They *are* it.

At first you think the pool is empty because your brain is looking for the wrong shape of life. You're looking for green. For fringe and softness. For something that wants the sun.

There is nothing that wants the sun here.

If you brace your hands on one of those glass teeth — and it will cut you, the basalt is still all edge — and you hold still long enough for your eyes to stop watering from the engine-noise and the cold, you start to see that the water isn't clear. It's crowded.

The commonest one makes the water crowded. 

Billions is too many, but it's close. They are small, smaller than your thumb, and they have decided to be perfect. A barrel, a pill, a ball-bearing with a seam. Their shell isn't shell, it's precipitated chrome — iron and silica laid down so thin and so even it shines like new machinery. Each one has six oars underneath, not legs, not swimmerets, but fringed cilia that beat out of phase so the animal never wobbles, it just hangs. And on top, set like a rivet head, a single black eye. No lid. No expression.

They stack in the water. That's the word. Not school, not swarm — stack. In columns twenty, thirty deep, all facing into the current, all hanging in that specific, cold water. When one of the rock engines below chuffs and is about to fire, they know a full minute before you do. The whole column tightens. The gap between one chrome barrel and the next goes from a finger's width to nothing. Carapace kisses carapace with a soft *tick*. You can hear it if the big vents are between beats. *Tick tick tick* like someone pouring out hardware.

Then the vent blows — a white geyser of boiling silica — and they open. You didn't know they could open. The seam around their middle splits a millimeter and they sieve that eruption straight through their bodies. They are eating the chemistry of it, the dissolved iron and sulfur and the bacteria too small to see that eat the chemistry first. Then they close, and loosen, and hang again. Ball bearings in water.

Below them, where the pressure gets heavy and the light starts to bend wrong, are the red ones.

You called them shrimp-things because you have shrimp in your head, but shrimp are fast and nervous. These aren't. These are forearm-long and slow on purpose, because fast costs heat and heat is work. Their blood is out on the outside: they are translucent except for where they aren't, and where they aren't is a brutal, rusty red — hemoglobin by the liter, to carry oxygen in water that is half boiled away by vents, to carry sulfur without being poisoned. Their backs are armored in what looks like rusted plate mail, but it's magnetite. They pull it out of the vent water grain by grain and lay it as scale. If you could lift one — you can't, the water is too cold to stay in — it would stick to a magnet.

They don't swim. They commute. They find the piston current that a particular vent makes — every chuff sends a toroidal vortex rolling up and out — and they slide into it and ride. No flick, no effort. The vortex carries them ten meters in a slow arc. At the end they step off with two heavy forelimbs and wait for the next one. They graze. Not on the chrome barrels, never that. They graze on the vent throats themselves, scraping the bacterial felt with a mouth that is more like a rasp, leaving bright scratches on the white silica floor. When two meet, they clink. Magnetite on magnetite. A dull bell sound under water.

And the ones that don't move at all.

On the lip of every vent, on the shoulders of the bigger reds, on any piece of basalt that has been underwater for more than a week, the third kind grows. Fists. You thought barnacle at first and it's close — they have that same stubborn, bolted-down look — but these open and close like lungs. A thick, black iron shell, hinged, held shut by a muscle that has no business being that strong. For twenty seconds they are closed, and you can see pressure build around them because the superheated water wants in. Then the muscle lets go and — *hiss* — they gape. Water hammers in. Particles, larvae, sloughed magnetite scale, everything. They slam shut again, *clang*, and the sound joins the bigger chuffing so that the whole bowl sounds like a shuttered mill.

If you watch one for an hour — your hands will be too numb for it, but if you do — you see it grow a micron. You see the faintest red blush of new shell at its lip, laid down in that instant of open. They are the clock in here.

None of them see you. The chrome barrels look, but their eye is not for you; it's for the gradation of chemical light, the shadow of silica snow. The reds have eyes on stalks but they are cataracted with mineral growth after a month, and they navigate by feeling the tug of each other's magnetic backs. The fists don't have eyes at all.

There is no leader. No builder. No queen in the bottom. There is no inside where they go to sleep because they don't sleep. The economy is all outside. Rock exhales heat, bacteria eat heat, barrels eat bacteria, reds scrape bacteria and get cleaned by barrels, fists filter what they all shed. The iron goes from water to blood to plate to silt to water again. The silica goes from dissolved to floor to snow to floor. 

And they all listen to the engines. Six or seven diesel hearts in the rock, firing out of sync, *chuff— —chuffCHUFF—scream-clank*. When one fires strong, the whole stacked city of ball-bearings above it dimples inward, and the reds pause in their arcs, and the fists all hold shut for a second longer.

A factory floor with no foreman. An industry that never needed to invent industry. 

It was not here last year. It will not be here when that rim finally gives way and this hanging sea pours itself down the mountain in one terrible, steaming spill. 

Until then it runs. Cold, overfull, deafeningly loud, packed to the meniscus with bright, patient, magnetic things doing their work under a white sun that never quite reaches them.
