At first you think nothing does.

That's the trick the bowl plays. You come down the east cut with your chest still working, and the lanes are empty, and the oven is cold, and the tools are left where work stopped mid-thought — and you think: ruin. You think: the people left. Because you are still thinking in numbers, and there are sixty doors and not sixty smokes.

If you stand still long enough for your blood to stop thumping in your ears, that starts to come apart.

What lives here lives thinly, and on purpose. It does not fill.

The first residents are the ones you were told about and then stopped seeing.

Lichen lives here. It lives the way a town lives. The gray-green map-lichen on every roof tile, the soot-black crust on the north side of the low wall, the sulfur-yellow rosettes inside the communal oven where the smoke used to be — they are not decoration. They are the settlement. They hold the slate together against the wind that tries to take it. They are slow enough that you have to come back twice to see that they've grown.

Then the stuff around the pool. That tight, dark green that is not moss but is trying to be moss — close up it is beads, thousands of beads, a skin that swells when the cloud river pours in and sweats. Nostoc, maybe. Something older than town planning. It lives on that line of perpetual damp where the clay liner meets air, and it tastes like stone-blood, like iron and salt. Nothing eats it except the wind.

Above that, managing not to be too much: the grass. One bunch per foot, never crowding. You could think each bunch was planted, the spacing is so decent. Straw-colored, stiff-backed, each blade rolled tight against evaporation. It lives only on the empty pads — the places where a house was and is not right now — because there the rammed earth is a finger warmer and looser than the lanes. It lives there on sufferance. When it gets too thick, someone pulls it.

The mat-plant with the waxy leaves lives in the joints. Between slate tiles, between the big glacial slabs, in the nicked channels that bring sweat to the cisterns. It holds dew inside itself like a miser holds coin. If you chew it — and whoever lives here has taught themselves to chew it — it gives back the salt that sweating took.

These are the citizens that do not count themselves. They keep to the edges and the stone and the precise amount of water.

Then the things that move.

A small brown bird with a white stripe — you never see two at once — that lives here by not living here. It crosses. Point A above the rim to Point B above the rim, a straight line, low, over the pool, every day at about the same light. It does not nest in the bowl because there is no nest material and too much attention. But its droppings on the sitting stone are part of the economy. In them are seeds from far below that never germinate. The people leave them.

The pika lives upslope, beyond the lip, where the scree is bigger. You hear one. One note, sharp, like a slate flake snapping. Its haypile somewhere is huge and ridiculous for such a small animal. It doesn't come down into the bowl except in hard winters, and when it does, it steals one leaf from the failed vegetable furrow and goes. It is tolerated.

In the cisterns, the other town. Lift the capstone — the flat one with the hole — on a hot afternoon and look, and you will see nothing. Just black water a long way down and your own head reflected very small. Come back at night with no lamp, when the air is thinest and coldest, and put your ear near the hole and you hear them. The newt, the beetle, the larva that has no name because no one has needed to name it. They live on the drips, on the accidental moth, on each other. They keep the water from going dead. The people know each cistern by its sound. This one clicks. This one is silent. This one has the swimmer who bumps the stone at dusk.

And then — only then — the people.

You will not see them at first because they are not where you expect people to be. Not in the doorway waiting welcome. Not in the square. They are where the grade is. One will be crouched at a gutter with a bone awl, picking out a single juniper needle that has dammed the fall by half a finger's width. One will be on a roof, reset slate, re-weight. The tile they lift is exactly the size of the tile they put down. No better. No worse.

There are not many of them at any one time. Seven, nine, twelve — if you try to count, someone will have gone into a house to nap and someone else will have come out of one you thought was empty. If you stay a week, you will count maybe twenty different faces, but never more than a dozen together. If you stay a year, you will understand that counting is the wrong tool. It's not a population. It's a number of mouths the bowl has agreed to.

They are high-mountain people in the way that the grass is high-mountain grass. Broad in the chest, narrow elsewhere. The air has made them efficient. Their skin is all weather, darkened to the color of the lichen on the sunny side and paler in the creases. Their hair, when they let it grow, is cut short with a flake of slate because long hair tangles in wind. Old and young look similar until you see the hands. The young ones — and there are few young ones, one or two maybe, because a child is a very large decision about water — have hands that are still learning not to add. The older ones have hands that have memorized the south-facing threshold dip.

They wear what the place gives without asking it to give more: undyed wool from animals that live below, felted hard and patched with other felt, the color of sheep and dust. Over that, a short coat of juniper bast or woven grass for wind. Nothing bright. Bright things get lost and you don't find them again in flat light.

They don't talk much. Not from unfriendliness. Sound carries so well here that a normal voice feels loud. And after a while you understand that the bowl already has all the noises it needs — tick of slate, bucket knock, wind overhead — and to add a lot of talk would be like building a second story. So they say what needs saying. *This cistern is low. This tile. Sweep.* They know each other's names but use them rarely because when there are only nine people in three hundred steps, you know who is being spoken to by the direction of the eyes.

What they do with their days is almost entirely maintenance.

Morning: breathe, look at the sky to see what kind of moisture is up there. Check the sweat on the north stones. Some mornings there is enough to hear it run, a line of bright moving beads. Empty the overnight dew cloths into the bucket. Pour that into the nearest cistern. Check the pool — never rises, never falls, but you check it — for dust skin, for green edge, for a crack in clay.

Midday: sweep. Every lane is swept with a broom made from grass bunches tied to a juniper handle. Not for cleanliness in the way lowland people mean it. For reading. Sweeping shows you if a tile has shifted, if a joint has opened, if a pika has been down and left a print. After sweeping, repair. One person, one repair. No committee. If the communal oven needs blackening inside, you take the soot pot and do it. If the sitting stone has bird lime, you wash it, not because it offends you, but because the lime holds too much damp.

Evening: they go inside. The doors face south for a reason — low sun comes in and lays a warm bar across the packed earth for an hour before it goes. They sit in that bar. They eat what keeps: roots that grow below and are carried up, dried curd, the salt-waxy leaves, water. They cook rarely, together, in that big oven, and when they do the smoke smells like the whole bowl for a day and then it is gone. At night, you see a little light from the smoke holes, not flame, just deep juniper coals glowing, too dim to throw shadow.

They do not have a chief. They do not have a myth about leaving or founding. If you ask where they came from — and if you ask out loud, they will look at you a little puzzled, as if you had asked where lichen came from — one might say, "We stayed." The town achieved a shape that matches the condensing point of air and the bearing strength of slate and the number of buckets you can dip before you have to wait again. They understood that shape. That was a long time ago. Their job since then has been not to un-understand it.

That is why there are empty pads. A house falls in. They could rebuild it bigger, or leave it as rubble, or build two small ones. They don't. They clear it to its pad, ram the earth, and let the grass come in its spaced, shivering bunches. The emptiness stays built. The town remains one single artifact, a low, exact insulse in the mountain's palm.

If you stay until evening, when the light goes flat and then blue, you will for a minute be one of them. Your breath will show. Your feet will have learned not to scuff the gutter edges. You will feel the quiet not as absence but as a kept agreement among things — lichen and newt and grass and bird and person and pool — to take up exactly as much space as they are given, and no more.
