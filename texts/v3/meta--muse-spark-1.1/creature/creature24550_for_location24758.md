What lives here is not one thing. It is a compact between things that figured out how to keep each other from finishing.

At the bottom — which is also everything — is the Mat itself.

It is not moss. Moss is a part of it the way your fingernail is a part of you. It is sphagnum and liverwort and haircap and the black one that has no name and twenty other things grown into each other so completely that no root or rhizoid belongs to a single individual anymore. It is ten meters deep in places, cold-alive all the way down to where its own dead compress into something like wet coal that never quite becomes rock. It does not photosynthesize so much as it wrings light out of that low iron overcast like water out of a rag. It holds ninety percent of the water on this plain inside its cells and the air between its cells. It is the ground, the soil, the forest, and the ocean, all flattened.

And the Mat got bored, or efficient. Three billion years of being flat and wet and cold will teach even a plant thermodynamics.

So it grew engines.

**The Hearts:**

The first ones you hear before you see. They are not animals. They are places where the Mat has decided to breathe differently.

The Sump-Hearts are sphagnum on a scale that has forgotten it is sphagnum. A dome of living peat the size of a fishing boat, or a house, or once, a cathedral, but always round. Its skin is moss pulled taut and shining, translucent amber where the water inside presses. For a minute it swells — you can hear the water being pulled up through its root-throat from deep peat, you can feel the whole plain tightening toward it. Then it lets go.

THUMP.

Not loud, deep. Through the soles of your boots, through your molars. It contracts all at once and a column of atomized water and trapped air rams straight up into the low sky, thirty, fifty feet, caught for a second as a pale ghost before the cold grabs it and it becomes rain again. It does this every ninety seconds. It has been counting since the continents had different faces.

Nothing lives *in* a Heart. Everything lives *because* of the Heart.

The second kind are the Boiler Reeds. The Mat found that if it packed black graminoid stems tight enough and pumped them full of sugar-water and let anaerobic bacteria ferment in the core, it could make heat — real heat — 70, 80 degrees, nonsensical in this cold that wants to be ice. A stand of them, a dozen, a hundred, grow fused at the base, hollow, jointed like iron pipe, hissing, clicking against each other as pressure builds and releases, steam venting from lipped pores with a shriek that peels across the flat for a kilometer. Their outer surface is polished to a gunmetal shine from rubbing, from wind, from age. New ones push up beside old ones in an hour, soft and green at first, then blackening, hardening, learning to whistle.

The third are Throats. White felted fungal mouths sunk into the Mat, two meters across, inhaling, exhaling. They are filters. They inhale the wet air that never stops weeping and comb it with cilia the size of fingers, catching midges, pollen, ice crystals, sorting water from food, shuddering when they cough dry air back out. If you stand too near one when it inhales, your coat will pull toward it.

They are not separate species. They are organs. The Mat grows whichever engine the spot needs and reabsorbs it when the parameters shift. You can watch an old Heart go slack, split, rot greenly in a day, and by morning a clump of Boiler Reeds is already using its sugar.

**The Tenders:**

Because where there is heat and spray and sorting, there is a living that can be skimmed.

They are sparse the way stars are sparse until you find a galaxy.

The most common are the **Leaf-Foots**. From a distance you think caribou, because something on this plain has to have filled that shape — heavy, patient, quadruped. Up close they are wrong. Their legs end not in hooves but in living snowshoes, great splayed pads of keratin and symbiotic moss that spread their weight over the Mat so they don't sink. Their backs and flanks are green-furred, not with hair but with an actual living coat of moss that grows from their skin and flowers tiny white flowers in brief fits. They crop the Mat with broad, flat teeth, and their grazing is husbandry: they keep it open, keep it from sealing its own engines over. When they step on a Boiler Reed that is too young, they snort steam and step sideways, careful. Their dung is hot and instantly colonized, steaming little cities.

Then the **Loom-Cranes**. They are why you think the place is industrial. Twice as tall as a man, stick-legged, grey as the sky, and completely eyeless — smooth feathered skin over where eyes should be. They navigate by the THUMP. Each Heart has a different thump-tone, each Boiler has a different shriek-pitch, and the Cranes map the plain in sound. They pick their way between engines with absurd delicacy, stop, and slide their spear-beaks — which are not beaks but jointed, warm tubes — straight into a valve on a Heart or a seam in a Reed and drink. Not water. Hot sugar and sulfur and half-made amino soup, the Mat's own blood. They stand there for a long time, shuddering, while smaller things pick parasites off their legs. When they leave, the valve seals with a sigh behind them.

And the floor crew: **Kettle Beetles**. Hand-sized, or head-sized in the good places near a vent. Their elytra are not chitin but a overlapping plate of iron-enriched carapace, riveted-looking, black and pitted like an old boiler. They trundle, nearly silent, eating only dead. Dead midges, dead moss, dead Leaf-Foot skin, the slough of a Throat. They can stand full in boiler steam that would scald anything else, because their joints vent it. When two meet they knock shells with a clear PING that carries. They are the true recyclers; without them the engines would choke on their own shed.

Above them all, the air itself is alive. Clouds of **Glass-Midges** — you call them midges because you have no other word — hatch in the spray-plume of a Heart, live for twenty minutes, breed in the hanging droplets, die and fall back onto the Throats. They are so numerous in a plume that the plume darkens. The birds that are not birds — little things with wings like wet paper — cut through them, mouths open. The whole plain smells faintly of iron and fresh blood when a plume is thick.

This is the bazaar. You can walk a kilometer through empty wet hissing grey and see nothing. Then you hear three engines at once, and the air gets thick, and there are forty Leaf-Foots cropping in a circle, seven Cranes all plumbed in and motionless like worshippers, Beetles clattering underfoot, and the sky above is a flickering snow-globe of wings and static. Loud, frantic, breeding, defecating, feeding, dying, all in a patch the size of a kitchen. A full ecosystem balanced on a leaky steam pipe. Then it thins and you are alone again.

**The Ones That Live In The Unstitched:**

Because this old, the flat has worn thin. Where the Mat is tiredest, physics leaks.

Some of the residents have learned to eat the leak.

There are little, almost invisible things that live only in the **Still Circles** — those places where the wind stops and the falling ice hangs mid-air, rotating. They look like dust until you realize the dust is holding position against gravity. If you breathe near one, the circle pops and they scatter, then slowly reassemble.

There are rivulets that run uphill, and living inside the uphill water is a worm — clear, flat, blind — that only swims uphill, always climbing to see what's at the top of the hump, then tumbling back down inside the Mat to start again.

The Loom-Cranes have learned to lead with their beak when the light-lag hits — you hear the THUMP before you see the swell — because their long legs sometimes remember a different step. Once in a while a Leaf-Foot's back leg will stretch a meter too long for a single step, like the world hiccuped, and it doesn't panic. It waits for the snap-back and keeps cropping.

Nothing here knows it is strange. The Mat doesn't know it is an industrialist. The Crane doesn't know it is blind. The Beetle doesn't know its back is iron. They all just keep the thrum going.

If you stay, you feel it in your own chest after a while: that ninety-second swell, that inhale-exhale, that need to keep the water moving even when down is not down. The plain will not make a place for you. It will make a use for you. Your heat is a vent. Your breath is a plume. Your boots filling with water is irrigation. And if you lie down long enough, the Glass-Midges will find you first, then the Beetles, then the green will come, slow and without malice, to consider you as a very interesting, very warm, temporary thickness in the Mat that has finally, after three billion years, decided to grow something new a few meters to the left.
