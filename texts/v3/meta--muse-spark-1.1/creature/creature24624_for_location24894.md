They don't look like people who climbed up here.

They look like people who were left behind when the mountain closed over.

The first one you see is also the first to see you, because nothing approaches this bowl without shaking every leaf on its rim. She is on top of her house — which is to say, she is kneeling in the living roof of her house — ankle-deep in the leaf-mash that her house makes to feed itself. She is small.

That is the first law written on their bodies. Altitude and a diet that is all water and starch makes small people. The men, the women, the bent-backed grandmothers — none of them would come to your shoulder. Broad across the chest, though. You can hear it when the girl on the roof shouts — *someone's here, someone's come up!* — her voice has to rip itself out of a chest built for thin air. A barrel chest on a child.

That is the second thing: they are wet the way you are not wet yet. Not damp. *Thoroughly* wet. The water that never stops weeping off the basalt never stops landing on them either, and they stopped trying to fight it generations back.

Their skin is pallid underneath — mountain milk-pale, almost blue at the temple — but you barely see the pallor for the freckles and the patina on top of it. Their hair is cut brutally short with those mussel-shell shears, both for the midges and for the rot, and what hair they have is felted and always beaded. Their clothes are not clothes, not really. Woven bamboo bark that has gone soft as old linen, capes and aprons of pressed, living moss that actually wick water away from the skin and drip it off in an orderly way. The women scrub the rock with wads of moss — the old women wear the scrubbing wads. On their shoulders, on their heads, thick stoles of sphagnum they have encouraged to grow onto a net of twine. It keeps the cloudflies from their necks, and it keeps them warm when the wind spills over the rim.

Their feet. You'll notice the feet. No shoes would last a week down in that acid mattress and no foot that wore shoes could work here anyway. The soles are broad, splayed, the toes long and prehensile as fingers, nails black and always soft at the edge. Between the toes, a faint dark line of ingrained moss that will never wash out. The children run across vine-bridges on those feet, sure as geckos. An old man — maybe sixty, maybe ninety, hard to tell when everyone is pruned short — comes toward you along a platform of green wood that is still sprouting leaves, and his instep leaves a momentary white print in the algae before it beads over.

Their hands are the opposite of their feet. If their feet are made to grip and spread their weight, their hands are made to stop. Everything about this town is growth that wants to close. A doorway left alone from morning until evening will be a wall of thin white shoots by nightfall. So their hands are never still, and they are all callus and little white scars. The bone-knife woman you thought you saw — she is there, she was always there, just behind a curtain of striped leaves — her thumb and forefinger have a permanent groove where she pinches and snaps. She has a thumb-nail grown long and hard as horn, filed to a point on basalt, for lifting grubs out from under leaves. Everybody's hands look like that. Tools they have, yes — antler adzes, cherrywood wedges that swell tight when wet — but mostly the tool is the hand that knows exactly how hard you have to pull a living cable before the bamboo weeps and lets go.

They are not silent with each other. They can't afford to be. The bowl is so loud — drip, croak, warble, the endless chewing of a billion insects — that to be heard you have to be just slightly too loud, all the time. So they talk in a bright, clipped, overlapping shout, even when they're close. A mother leans over a moss-crib where a baby is sleeping on a bed of dry lichen — the only dry thing in the whole caldera, changed hourly — and she shouts her lullaby. Two boys repair a ladder by shouting *hold, hold, HOLD* while the ladder tries to root under their hands. It isn't anger. It's just how you have to live when the frogs bellow like dogs.

You begin to understand that there are not very many of them. Thirty souls? Forty? The bowl can't feed more, and more would have to speak louder than the birds, which is impossible. But because they are always moving — pruning, lifting, tying, plucking the new shoots off the top of their own homes — they look like twice that number. Anthill energy, you thought from a distance, and up close it's true, but with a gentleness to it. No one is building. No one is making anything new. Every person here is a gardener who lives inside the thing he's gardening. If you stand, as they do, knee-deep in your own roof with a shell in your hand, trying to keep your door from becoming solid again, you can't afford pride. Their faces when they finally gather to look at you — because of course they've gathered now, dripping, steaming faintly in the cool air — aren't suspicious. They're curious in the way a person is curious about a deer that has walked into their kitchen. *How did you keep that thing dry?* one of the children asks, pointing at your pack. *Why?*

And under their feet, over their heads, on their shoulders and in their hair, the other citizens.

The orange thumbnail frogs use their moss-capes as ponds. A girl has one tucked behind her ear like a flower, its throat pulsing. The cloudflies — they do bite them, you see now, the backs of their necks are dotted with old bites they do not scratch — but they also feed the warblers, and the warblers feed them in return with eggs. A slug as big as a loaf moves along the main walkway and nobody moves it; three women step over it with perfect familiarity, because its slime keeps the bamboo from splintering underfoot. On the central vines, fat iridescent beetles are being knocked into gourd-baskets — not to kill, but to keep. They'll be boiled.

If you stay until dusk, which you will because no one climbs down from this height in failing light, you'll see them uncurl. All day they have been tended outward. At night, the light finally, truly dies inside the stone cup, and the cloud that has been tangled all day settles as cold wool, and then they fold. Whole families fold into a single house-clump, curling into hammocks woven from aerial root that have to be re-tied every third day or they'll become part of the wall. They sleep wet, so wet they steam slightly. They sleep listening to water move over their roof and through their floor. And in the deepest hour, when the frogs sync for a moment before falling out of sync again, you can put your hand on the bamboo that is also a wall and feel, very faintly, a pulse that isn't yours.

They are not living in the mountain. They are what the mountain does to keep the rain from drowning itself.
