What lives here didn't arrive after. It was included in the blueprint.

At first you think it's the goats. God knows there are enough of them to be a population.

They are everywhere you look when you stop expecting to see people. Shaggy mountain goats the color of old snow and wet slate, with coats so long and heavy that the moss compresses under the drag of them. They stand on the terraces between the towers and on the towers themselves - on the low walls, on the second turn of the external stairs, on the slate roofs, balanced on stone that should not hold a hoof. Dozens. Hundreds if you walk the bowl.

They aren't grazing. The moss is thick enough to lose a knife in and none of it is cropped, none of it is torn. They are just standing. If you watch one goat for a long time, a minute, five, you see the micro-movements that tell you it is alive and not taxidermy. The flank lifts a fraction. The enormous, dog-like ears twitch against no fly. And the eyes.

They have two eyes, until you count again. They have two forward, dark and amber and wet and goat-like, patient. Then there is a pair set a little higher and wider on the skull, under the sweep of the horns, that do not blink at the same time. And another pair even higher, smaller, almost hidden in the shaggy hair, matte black, always open. Six. You keep trying to make it two and the arrangement keeps resolving into six, no matter what angle you come at it from. Not monstrous. Just... excessive. As if the valley needed more seeing than two eyes could do.

And they are all looking at slightly different things, which should be impossible to coordinate. One pair looks at you. One pair looks at the water pocket nearest to it. One pair looks at the sky directly above, at the sun that doesn't move. Every goat in the valley is doing this. The city has built itself a distributed array of attention. Nothing moves through the bowl without being seen six times by every living post.

Above them, the ravens live here too.

White ravens. Not albino - their eyes are black as the water pockets, and their beaks are iron-dark, like they've been tapping at slate. Two or three per tower peak, so regular you could think they were ornaments. They never leave. You can count forty in a piece of sky and then realize you have been counting the same forty for an hour, their wings locked open in a high glide that uses no energy, eats no wind. The ones on the roofs are not perched so much as placed. They settle their feathers with a soft *shuk* that is loud as a bell here and then they do not shift again for a very long time.

If the goats are the eyes, the ravens are the memory of sound. Every so often one opens its beak, not to caw, but as if it has forgotten what its throat is for. No noise comes out. It closes it again. They remember that this is a place where calls used to carry between towers.

Lower down, at ankle height where the air is dampest, the marmots live. Big as dogs, big as pigs, their brown pelts gone grey at the muzzle with an age that never culminates. They sit upright at the lips of the black pools, paws folded on bellies, like old men at a bathhouse. They stare into water that reflects a tower that isn't quite where the real tower is. They never drink. Condensation beads on their whiskers and stays there. You can kneel and look into a pool with a marmot and you both see nothing moving at the bottom, because there is nothing to move, only a perfect, depthless black holding the sky open.

And above all those, the little birds - larks, swifts, things you don't have names for - drawn in ink against the rinsed air. They are not flying. Flying is work and transit. They are suspended in the act of flying, like the stone chips suspended in the act of falling next to the mason's hammer. Their wings are out. The air holds them.

None of this eats. None of this shits. None of it breeds. That is not what living means here.

What lives here is a state of being witnessed without interruption.

The people who built the towers with hand-tools and patience understood that. That is why they spaced them so far apart, why they left bare rock between every decision, why they built them for looking out and drying things and being alone while still being inside a city. They were not building houses. They were building perches. The towers are not dwellings, they are brackets for a kind of attention that needs altitude, coolness, damp stone, thin light, and quiet.

The people left when it was finished. Or they stayed and became very good at it.

You can see it in a tower if you climb the external stairs that wrap it like a vine. Inside, no furniture. No hearth that was ever lit, though there is a place for a hearth. Instead, on the third floor, the floorboards - adzed dark wood, fitted tight - have taken the shape of someone sitting cross-legged for a very long time. Not a stain. A slight dish in the wood. Facing the window that frames the next tower exactly, and the goat standing on its roof, and the white raven behind the goat. In another tower, on another floor, the same dish. In another.

They learned how to stop changing and called that living.

So now the city lives. Not the goats. Not the ravens. The equilibrium itself lives. It keeps the water at the lip of the basin and no further. It keeps the moss at the precise height of lush and no higher. It keeps the stone chips a half-inch above the ground where they were when time decided to hold its breath. It keeps the goats with six eyes open, the birds with wings open, the pools with sky open.

If you stay long enough on the rim you feel it draft through you, too, that cool that makes breath visible and then holds it a second too long. Your blood goes quiet enough to hear. Your thoughts stop propagating. You become another animal that the city has made room for without crowding. And you understand that your footprint will stay in the moss not because the moss is dead, but because the valley has finally, perfectly, included you in its count.
