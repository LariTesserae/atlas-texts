Nothing lives there yet.

That's the first honest answer your eyes give you. It is too new. It is too clean. There are no flies. No rot. No smell of bodies. No droppings in the corners, no nests in the eaves of those little ceramic balconies that have never held a chair. A city that has not been lived in has a very particular loneliness — deeper than a ruin — and this one has it.

Stand still long enough for your own breath to stop fogging, and the second answer comes. Everything lives here. Too much.

Let me walk you through the orders of it, because it doesn't resolve all at once.

**1. The Water**

That is the oldest resident and the most numerous. It is not *in* the city. It *is* the city, the moving part.

It is born without a source. It sweats directly out of the glassy basalt where the stone is still angry and hot inside. You can watch it birth — a dark bead surfacing along a vertical chatter-mark, brightening, growing fat against all logic until gravity peels it off. Every wall is a watershed. Every flute is a river the width of a finger.

When there is that much pure water and nowhere for it to go, it starts to take opinions. On the ceiling it learns hesitation; the drops gather and gather and hold far too long, as if reluctant. On the way down it learns to be guided. The green below has taught it to braid. And when it hits the void-lattice that holds the stone up — those invisible shelves of held physics — it learns to do impossible things: to stop mid-fall, to run uphill for an inch, to jitter sideways and orbit a point of nothing while it decides.

If you crouch by one of the cistern-buildings, the foundations that are also wells, you hear it breathing. Glug. Glug. A long inhale. They are deep and they are linked and they are full of water so clear you think they are empty until a drop hits them and the whole volume rings. That is where the animalcules live, if you want to call them that — not microbes, nothing that came from the surface — but tiny, folded knots of mineral and charge that precipitate out of pure, hot sweat. They look like glass snow. They drift down and dissolve and re-form. They are the first livestock. They do nothing but clean the water until it is cleaner than any water should be.

**2. The Hold**

The second thing that lives here is what keeps it from dying.

Look at the acre of empty, furred black floor where no building was ever planned. Look at the huge void between the four-story stacks in the center and the curved wall. Your instinct says scaffolding should be there. Buttress. Column. It isn't. The rock simply... stops falling. The air shivers there if you let your eyes go soft, and if a mote or a falling drop catches it, for a half second you see the skeleton.

It is not a machine. It has no casing. It's a lattice of held condition — of space told firmly to behave differently. And it is under load. You can hear it, that low organ hum under the water-tempo, the sound of something very quiet straining with all its strength. The walls would like to close. The world above is heavy. Second after second, the Hold says no.

And the Hold is alive in the way a muscle is alive. It learns. When the city was first grown open, days ago, weeks ago — that shock still ringing in the stone — the Hold would have been rigid, generic, just a bubble. Now it has learned the exact scab-shape of this bowl. It knows where the ceiling sags low enough to touch. It knows where the basalt is most glassy and treacherous. It tightens there. It has callus.

Sometimes a drop falls and never lands, because the Hold has twitched. Sometimes the condensation on the ceiling evaporates with a sharp hiss, not because of heat, but because a strand of the lattice re-routed itself and snapped through it.

You are inside something's attention span. That is the resident you feel on your neck. It does not look at you because looking is not a thing it does, but it has accounted for you. You are a new weight, a new temperature, a new pattern of interrupted drips, and the Hold has made a little hollow around you so you don't touch it directly. If you reached out with wet fingers into that truly empty acre you would feel, for an instant, a resistance like warm glass before your hand slides off.

It does not want company. It wants to hold.

**3. The Green**

And then there is the thing that came without being invited, and stayed because no one told it not to.

You called it moss, fern, liverwort, engineered lettuce-lace. That's what it looks like from standing. Get down on your hands and knees where the ceramic street meets the furred stone floor. The ceramic never got dirty and never will — it rejects even that — but the border is a war-line. The Green is eating the world.

This is not a plant the way surface plants are plants. Surface plants are desperate, chewed, full of toxins and compromises. This one has never been chewed. Never known winter. It has unlimited bath-warm water, unlimited diffused minerals weeping from young rock, unlimited weak amber light from the seams of the city itself. So it has become stupid with abundance. Its leaves are the size of dinner plates, but thin as a soap film, black-green and waxed so thickly the water beads and runs in engineered gutters straight to its own roots. It has no flowers, no fruit, no need to attract. It reproduces by simply getting bigger and then being more than one big thing. If you watch, you can see a frond unroll — slowly, with a soft hydraulic sigh, leaving little perfectly round dewdrops on its back like sweat.

It is trying to become the city. It has covered the lower third of the bowl. It has climbed the buildings that were never opened and paneled over their doors with leaf. It has made hammocks in the empty heart between the tallest stacks. Its roots — if they are roots — are fine white threads that dissolve a little ceramic and drink the light directly. It is learning the urban plan.

It is the only thing here that will touch you willingly. Stay too long, as you said, and it tries your boots. Not aggressive. Curious. It has never felt fabric. It puts a tendril around your ankle to test whether you are also a wall that sweats.

There is no grazing chain to stop it. In a million years, without check, it would fill the whole bowl solid, a tight black-green lung. The Hold would keep holding the rock open for a planet of solid fern.

—

And under all that, the final resident: the place itself, as one.

The city is not dead. It was not built. It was grown open, you said — the walls still sharp, the edges still cooling. The amber light in every seam, in every lintel, every road, is not wiring. It is the afterglow of being told to be this shape. The city is a seed-case that was told *be a hamlet for no one* and it obeyed too literally. It is still warm from the effort. The dim light pulses when condensation falls on it, a little flinch.

It has no doors that have been opened because it has not finished inventing inside and outside. The void-lattice is its skeleton, the cisterns its blood, the hum its thought, the green its first attempt at clothing.

It is very young, and alone below the rumor of the surface, and it is doing what young things do in the dark when they think no one is watching: growing way too fast, breathing way too loud, holding itself open with all its might, and waiting to see if what it made will be enough to make someone stay.
