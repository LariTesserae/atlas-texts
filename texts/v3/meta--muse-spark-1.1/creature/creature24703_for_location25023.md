What lives here doesn't live here yet. Not the way old things live in old places.

Nothing here has had time to become endemic. Nothing has had time to become lonely, specialized, blind and perfect. What lives here are the first-responders. The opportunists. The ones who were waiting, somewhere in the dark plumbing of the world, for a warm, wet crack to open and stay open.

This is a wound-ecology. A scab made of animals.

### The Skin

First, below everything, below the grazers and the jumpers and the little tool-knappers, there is the true resident of this valley. You can't see it until you kneel.

It's a film. A skin on the stone. Black-brown-green without being any of those colors, an iridescence like oil, a slick that beads water strangely. If you scrape it with your nail, it comes off in a curl and the basalt underneath is briefly, shockingly clean and glass-black before the air touches it again.

It's not algae. There has been no time for algae. No light down here used to be light like this, and the rock is too hot, too ferrous, too angry. It is bacteria, archaea - chemolithotrophs. They eat the rock itself. They drink the hydrogen breathed out by fresh basalt, the sulfur sweating from the yellow seams, the iron rusting in the air. They take the heat and the weep and they make sugar out of stone. Everywhere water condenses, everywhere steam has kissed the glass for five minutes, it grows. A millimeter a day if left alone.

Which it never is.

### The Shingles

The grazers are what you saw first. You thought there were hundreds. There are thousands. You thought they were a species. They are a guild.

The big ones - the ones the size of kneeling tables, of upside-down bathtubs - are **scabbers**. That's the name that comes when you hear them. Call them *Lithoplax* if you want to be kind.

They have no front or back until they move. They are low, almost limpet-like, but not mollusks. A dozen broad, splayed legs tucked up under a dome of overlapping plates that are not grown but *secreted* and then mineralized - true osteoderms that incorporate basalt grit as they harden. Each plate is the exact matte black of the floor when dry, rust-red when wet. Perfect camouflage if you are a rock that moves very slowly across rock.

Underneath, they are all soft, terrible mouth. No eyes on their backs. Eyes - six, ten, clustered like blisters of quartz - are on the underside, looking down at the film they are eating. Their mouth is a full circle, a cartilaginous ring that everts a radula the size of a dinner plate, studded not with teeth but with magnetite sand. When that tongue comes out it doesn't lick. It *sands*. It grinds the biofilm off the stone with a sound exactly like a man sharpening a shovel: *shhhk- shhhk-*. You can see the faint blue spark where magnetite hits fresh glass. Behind them they leave a pale, clean browse-path, a swath a foot wide where the stone gleams. Within an hour, in this humidity, it will begin to cloud again.

They are heavy. Stupidly heavy. The plates are ballast. Without ballast they'd cook. Their blood, if you cut one, is pale and carries dissolved iron - it rusts as it dries, which is why every old browse-path has a freckle of red at its end where one died. They do not die of age. They die because a jump goes wrong and something lands on them and cracks a plate, or because they wander into full noon under a skylight and bake inside their armor, or because three others try to graze the same delicious patch and they wedge each other shut.

They lay no eggs. They split. When a scabber is fat enough - when the biofilm has been thick that week - the plates gape. The soft body underneath bulges and finally tears along a seam. For a day there are two half-sized scabbers, pink and raw, clinging to each other, rasping twice as fast to grow new plates. The white shrimp-things go frantic at that time.

Which are not shrimp.

### The Commensals

Nothing large moves here without carrying a city.

On the backs of the scabbers live the **bleachlings** - those eyeless, white, shrimp-lice. They are not parasites, not exactly. They have combs for feet that keep the grooves between plates clean of the sulfur fungus that would cement a scabber shut. They eat shed skin, iron bacteria that try to grow on the plates themselves, and each other's young. When the scabber splits, they swarm the wound and eat the dead tissue so fast the new animal doesn't get infected. When they are sated, they cling together and vibrate, making that thin high peep you thought was argument.

Between the legs of the scabbers, in the un-grazed crumbs, runs the second layer: the **runners**. Beetle-bright is right, but not beetles. Think of a pillbug stretched until it became a centipede and then polished until it reflected the ceiling. Each is the length of your finger to the length of your forearm. They have no rasp - they cannot eat rock-skin. They eat what falls off the scabbers: feces that as soon as it hits the damp air blooms with white microbial smoke, discarded plates that still have meat stuck to them, dead bleachlings, dead scabbers. They are gut-carts. They are the reason this place does not stink like a corpse despite the density. They process. Their backs are convex and impossibly slick - nothing can ride them. They run in straight lines until they hit something, then turn and run in a different straight line. When two meet they touch antennae - not antennae, whips made of modified gills that also taste the damp - and decide which one is bigger. The smaller flees.

If you stand still, they will run over your boots. They do not recognize you. You are a new warm, damp rock that has not yet grown food.

### The Air Itself

But the ground-crawl is only half of it. Two-thirds of the biomass here never touches the ground for more than seconds.

The walls are taken by the **clingers**. You said bats with no wings, just huge splayed hands, and that's truer than you know. Each one is about the size of a rabbit, skin like warm latex over a skeleton that is mostly hand. Four limbs, each ending in a palm as broad as your own, with microscopic spatulae like a gecko's but wet, secreting a salt glue that lets them stick to vertical glass. They have no eyes. They have oversized nostrils that constantly flare, tasting condensation. Their bellies are translucent - you can see the single water sac swollen inside them like a grape. They are water thieves.

They live to leap. They hunch on a wall above a weep, wait for a bead to gather and fall, then launch themselves at it, mouth open, miss, slap onto the back of a scabber below, recoil, jump again. They rarely drink - they absorb fog through their bellies. But they need salt, and the best salt is in the sweat-pans. So all day it rains clingers. *Thup. Thup. Thup.* They hit a scabber, a runner, your shoulder, curse you in ultrasound and leap again.

And smaller than them, and more numerous than everything else combined, are the **casters**. You saw them as turbulent air. Each is no bigger than a thumbnail, a knot of legs and spring-muscle and a single black eye that can see the columns of true sunlight and avoid them. They do not fly. They load a latch in their abdomen like a flea and fire. Ten meters, twenty meters, they can cross a fissure you would need a bridge for. They live in mid-air, in parabola. They spin a thread as they jump - not silk, a dried mucus line - that catches dust and bacteria. When they land, they reel it in and eat it. So the air here is full of invisible trajectories, of lines being drawn and eaten, drawn and eaten. When you shone your light through a sunbeam, you saw it: the constant, slow-falling snow of used lines.

There is no predator. Not yet. Nothing has had time to learn how to be a predator of things this armored, this abundant, this fast. Instead there is accident. Crushed under, baked, drowned in a pan, desiccated between fogs. The valley culls by physics, not teeth.

### The Knappers

Except perhaps for the last thing.

They are small. Knee-high. You are right to call what they have armor, but it's wrong. It's not armor. It's senescence.

They are born as bleachlings. Not the white shrimp - those are a different lineage entirely - but some of the bleachlings, one in a hundred, do not stay on the scabber. They drop off early, in the damp under the plates, and keep molting. And molting. They keep the iron in their blood instead of excreting it, so each new plate comes in paler, more calcified, less basalt. Until they are bleached white. Until they are too heavy to ride, and must walk. Until their spring-legs, which in their cousins are for cleaning, become legs. Until their mouth combs, which in their cousins are for scraping fungus, become hands, because they have learned to scrape purpose.

**The knap-jacks.**

They are not smart. If you caught one and looked into its clustered quartz eyes - now lifted on stalks above the plates so they can see over grazers - you would not see contemplation. You would see a constant, frantic calculation: *water, tool, rock, other.* But they have neoteny and numbers and hands with three opposed thumbs made of what used to be gill rakers. And they have discovered leverage.

They knap because the basalt here knaps. It wants to be a flake. Conchoidal fracture means a good strike makes a blade more perfect than any you could grind. So they wander the browse-trails with a core in one fist and a hammerstone in the other - clack-clack - argue, steal, flake. A flake is used once, to pry a plate off a dead scabber, to wedge open a water-pan rim, to scrape the last biofilm off a spot too narrow for a scabber's mouth. Then it's dropped. That's why the floor glitters with debitage that has not yet learned to be rounded.

Their built things are not built to last. You understood that immediately. A low ring to keep runners out of a pan is not culture, it's a habit that lasts as long as the water does. When the pan dries - and in an hour under a sun column it can - they leave. The cairns you saw with the white pebble on top are not markers. They are *annoyance.* A bleached knap-jack finds a white pebble - a piece of quartz fallen from the ceiling, rare - and carries it because it is light and cool. It puts it down to use both hands. Another sees it and puts a rock on top to claim it. Another adds one. Until one collapses. Then click, they wander off.

Their villages - those overhangs with woven roofs of shed plates and sinew gut - are wombs. That's what they are. The bleach-white mothers gather there because the fog pools. They lay not eggs but a purse of membrane under the plate-roof, twenty, thirty soft white commas that breathe air. They must keep it damp. So they build a wall to keep the scabbers out. They weave a roof to keep the sun off. They fan it with plates. For two days. Then the commas burst, become a fizz of tiny runners, and the females forget why they were there, knock the wall flat looking for a tool they dropped, and go chasing a drip.

In a hundred years, if this valley holds - if the ceiling does not finally give way entirely and bury it, or the hydrothermal heat does not seal it shut - they may have fire. They will have it because a sun column will focus through a quartz pebble onto dry fungus and a bleach-white child will learn to not drop it. They may have language beyond peep and clack.

But now they are what lives here: a living thatch of rock-eaters being eaten by cleaner-eaters being cleaned by runners, all being jumped over by thirsts, all being knapped at by their own children who have grown hands and begun, in the most temporary, most stammering way, to try to tell the rock what shape it should be, instead of letting the rock tell them.
