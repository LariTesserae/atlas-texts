What lives here didn't all evolve here. That is the first wrongness you have to get past. Nothing could. The stone is ten, maybe fifteen years out of the mantle. It's still counting down.

And yet it is crowded.

You have to learn to see it in layers, because at this scale and in this cold everything practices camouflage by being still.

### The low things.

The first layer is the one that makes you think the Shelf is empty. It isn't.

There are mats. Black-green lichen, but not lichen as you learned it — this is a designed thing, a composite. It grew itself out of the seeding drones after the Tapers stabilized. Up close it isn't flat. It’s a minute forest of tiny cups that catch condensing fog and hold it. It is warm when you put your hand on it, a degree or two above air temperature, because it is photosynthesizing with a ferocity that old-world plants never needed to. It lays down acid, micron by micron, to make soil out of glass. From above, it looks like spilled oil. From below, on your knees in black gravel, it smells like iodine and fresh snow.

Around it, in every wind-trap — the lee of a vesicle, the inside corner of a basalt step — is the grit grass. And the rust sedge. The sedge is red because its roots bleed iron to survive the basalt chemistry. You only notice it is alive when the wind stops, because its seed heads tremble on their own, shaking off ice.

Where the terraces leak heat, people have drawn perfect lines of dwarf birch and gene-pined needle trees. Waist high, table-topped by the wind. They are supposed to stay inside the grid. They don't. At night you can hear their birch roots pop as they throw runners out beyond the thermal envelope, feeling for the weeping places. They go feral within fifty yards and then die and then go feral again.

In the water itself — those mile-wide meres that are an inch deep, that perfectly mirror the Tapers — lives the real abundance. Not fish. Everything that scoots. Black ice-midges as fat as your thumb, with no wings. They breed in such numbers that if you stand still on the marsh ice, the surface begins to move without wind. They are food. They are why everything else can be here.

### The weight on the marsh.

The grazers. You don't see them at first because their whole strategy is not to be seen. 

They are called mennir by the Shelf folk, though no one knows if that was a name from before or made up here. Six-legged, splayed, elephantine without being elephants. Their feet are snowshoes made of horn that spread and seal, so they can walk on ice that wouldn't hold you. Their backs are that same pale mineral grey as the raw basalt when it's wet. From a distance a herd of two hundred looks like a field of erratics.

They move at one speed — slow, ceaseless, heads down. A broad, keratin shovel for a lower jaw scrapes lichen, sedge, midge-larva, gravel, everything, into a mouth that grinds it. They don't make a sound except for the crunch, and the faint exhale through broad noses that briefly fogs and freezes.

They are not wild. Not tame either. They were seeded ten years ago to be landscape. The idea was they'd make soil faster with their guts. They are making it, but they also learned the Tapers. Between towers they walk in straight lines, ancient migration lines along fracture edges, and they know which tram-absence is safe to cross. When one dies — and they die often, falling through a new crack, heart attack from cold — the rest stand around it for a long time, breathing on it, until the fog freezes over its eyes.

Above them, always, are the others.

### The crown riders.

You thought they were birds. They aren't. The kites — four wings, two beating, two planing — are reptile-light, hollow-boned, their blood laced with antifreeze glycoproteins that make them glitter when the light catches them wrong. Their wingspan is wider than your outstretched arms, door-wide. They almost never flap. The Taper throws a column of hot air twenty thousand feet up and they ride it in a slow, endless gyre, so high they are specks, so low sometimes you have to duck.

They're silent hunters by design. Their feathers, or not-feathers, absorb sound. They drop like ash onto the midge pools, tongue out, scooping. They have no voice box. The young squeak by grinding plates in their beak.

They sleep on the shoulders of the Tapers, tucked into the ice accretion, and in the morning the heat pulse shatters the ice and they all drop at once, then catch.

### The eyes in the blue.

Nothing large should live in the chasms. They are too new, too sheer, too cold. The bottoms have never seen sun.

Something does.

You hear them before you see them. In the fractures that are half a mile deep, the air itself moves differently. A low grunt that isn't echo. Stone shifting that isn't stone. When the windward face of a fracture exhales fog — because the raw basalt is still degassing, still weeping steam from its birth — you will see the fog tear around shapes.

They are long, low, and dark. Not mennir-dark pale, but the black of the deep crack. They have many eyes along the side of a wedge-shaped head, and those eyes reflect terrace amber at night — hundreds of points along a fracture rim, looking out at the lights, at the herds.

The Shelf people call them fringers. No one goes down after one. The tram crews say they can hear them climbing, using the vesicles in the wall like handholds, but never coming up all the way. They scavenge the dead mennir that fall. Sometimes they call, and it sounds exactly like the wind over new stone, until you realize the wind stopped a minute ago.

People think they were here before the city. Not in this rock — impossible — but in the genes. Something from cold caves elsewhere that was remade for here. Or something that walked here on its own when the Shelf rose, because when you make a new place, old things come to look.

### The people of the heat debt.

And then there are the people.

There are so few of them. That's the most human thing about them. Three miles across at the base, a Taper holds perhaps eight thousand inside its terraces and skin, mostly near the middle where the waste heat of the lattice is steady as a second summer. Between Tapers is fifty, sixty miles of mostly nothing. The tram that crosses that nothing leaves a trail where ice never forms and carries twenty people and feels crowded.

They dress like the mennir look — pale grey, shaggy on the outside, wind-cut, sealing at the feet. They all leave a faint heat wake for thirty seconds after they pass, and children chase each other's wakes as a game. Their etiquette is built around one rule you learn in an hour: you don't make heat where you don't have to. No fire. No flare. No showing off your suit's red emergency dump unless you're dying. The Tapers give you everything. You don't waste it. An open flame in the market terrace will get you a quiet, communal stare that feels colder than the wind.

Because the stone underfoot is magnetically buffered — that old accommodation for bad backs that became architecture — they walk light. Elderly women move in long, gliding steps. Kids learn to throw a ball so it lingers and slides sideways on the gradient, and their games are all curved arcs adults have to re-learn.

They are not loud colonists. The tech here is so far past novel that nobody talks about it. Doors know you by your heat. Rooms warm when you are sad, or they think you are. Everyone here has a little glaze of ash in their lungs from the year the Shelf outgassed hard. Everyone has tether scars on their wrists from when they went out past the grid with a reason.

They live by the pulse. Once an hour the Taper lattice brightens, a slow inhalation of geothermal energy being shunted up and out, and it makes that note — a finger wet on glass, felt in the teeth — and every head turns, just a fraction, toward the core, even in sleep. On that pulse the mist crowns thicken, dump, and the silver braids of water on the tower sides get fatter for a minute.

At dusk, which lasts an hour and a half, they come to the terrace edges and look out — not at each other, but outward, where the shallow meres turn pink and the herds are long shadows and the fringer eyes are just beginning to open in the cracks. And for a moment you see what kind of animal they are: not city animals, not mountain animals, but shore animals living on the thinnest livable edge of an immense, cooling stone tide, tending infrastructure that is itself alive enough that you apologize when you kick it.

And they are quiet, like the fauna. They absorb.
