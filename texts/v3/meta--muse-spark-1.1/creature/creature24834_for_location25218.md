Nothing lives in it the way a rat lives in a wall. 

At first that's what you think the answer is. That it is empty. That the town tried to be a town and failed, and what you're feeling move around you is just leftover motion - flywheel momentum, vines still growing on habit, water still looking for downhill where there isn't any.

You sit on a brick canal edge with your feet in water that is too hot for feet, and you try to count the residents. Zero.

Then you correct yourself.

There is one resident. It only looked like many because you were still thinking in street-and-building and leaf-and-pipe.

What lives here is the low place itself. What lives here is the agreement.

It began — you can feel it in the way your own sweat won't evaporate — as three hungers that found each other and locked.

The first to live here is the sweat. Not water. The sweat.

Water wants to leave. In any other sinkhole it would. It would find a crack, a cave, a lower place still. Here the rim is too perfect, the clay cap under the peat too thick, the table too high. The water that comes up through the limestone can't get over the lip. So they built screws to lift it out, and it seeps straight back in through the moss in an hour. It boils off the brass lungs of the condensers and falls again from the fig roof as fat, warm drops that hit tin so constantly you have to shout. It does not travel. It circulates. It is a single body of water that has learned to be a weather system. It lives here the way blood lives in a body. You put your hand in the canal and you are not touching canal water, you are touching the same water that was on the wall five minutes ago and will be in your lungs in ten. It counts as one thing. It is warm all the way through. It has no other place to be.

Second to live here is the eating-of-light.

Because there is no mouth here. That's the rule you found: nothing that needs to eat another thing can stay. Mosquitoes try and leave. Birds fly over and don't land because there is nothing that buzzes. The air is woefully, unnaturally empty of whine.

So what can live here has to make its own food from what there is too much of. Light, even filtered to 1.0/3 green. Heat, absurd at 2.6/3. Iron. Rust-water. Stone-breath.

It looks like a jungle at first. Banana, taro, philodendron, strangler fig making a second roof over the factory roof, ferns as big as doors bursting out of the mortar, air plants so thick on the wires you have to wade through them. But if you stay and watch - and the heat makes you stay, makes you slow until watching is all you have the energy for - you see they are not many plants.

They are one plant wearing many leaves.

The fig's roots are not only in the roof. They are down the walls now, white and blind, in the cracks of the pumping station, in the seams of the boiler. The philodendron's aerial roots have found the condenser drip and followed it into the iron grating, where they have matted with moss into a felt that holds the planks up. The moss is not on the brick. The moss is the mortar now. You could not pull a fern out without the arch it grows from sighing and settling lower.

It grows visibly. Not fast like time-lapse. Fast like attention. You stare at a vine tip to see if you imagined it moving and then a bead of water lands on your neck, you blink, and it has advanced over a rivet. It is not reaching for light anymore. In here light is everywhere and nowhere, diffuse and green. It is reaching for condensation. For rust.

Third — because every body needs a skeleton that doesn't rot — what lives here is the rust.

The town wanted to be a brickworks, an ironworks. It got the machines in: the lowworks flywheel as broad as a house, black and orange, turning and turning on a bearing that should have seized in 1948. The Archimedes screws groaning water back up over the rim. The boilers that fire themselves. Someone left them banked and they have never gone out because the plant that lives here feeds them. Not coal. Leaf litter falls into the hopper. Banana trunks. Sawdust made of dead fern. And the plant keeps them stoked because the boilers keep the air moving.

The iron did not fail. It became bark. Red rust to black rust to a slick, iridescent skin that the moss grips better than it gripped the original paint. Every pipe rack is now half-pipe, half-root, a alloy of iron and lignin that you can't name. Tap it with a wrench you find left by a boot that no longer has a foot — and you were wrong, there are no human presences here, there are human-shaped places where the town learned how to stand upright for a while — tap it and it sounds dull, like wood. Like bone.

The three have become one agreement. That is what established means. Not in ruins. In resolution. Brick expands in the heat. Root contracts around it. Condenser exhales cold, vein of pipe sweats, vine drinks, grows, drops leaf into boiler, boiler turns flywheel, flywheel turns screw, screw lifts water over rim, water seeps back down wall, wall weeps into canal, canal reflects green light back up to leaf. Every drop that falls on tin is a synapse firing. Hundreds per minute, arrhythmic, so loud you have to raise your voice, and if you listen long enough you can almost hear counting.

It is not a town with a plant in it. It is a single low animal that taught itself how to look like a town to hold its shape inside limestone.

It does not know you are there. Or rather, it knows you the way a mouth knows water — as a temporary change in salinity, in temperature, a cool pocket that will be brought to temperature soon. You sweat, and it drinks your sweat gladly off the grating. You breathe, and the philodendron curtain shivers slightly, sorting your exhale. You shift your weight, and the raft of planks and iron and mud sighs and adjusts to keep you from puncturing its skin.

If you stayed - you can, the air holds you - you would become part of the keeping. Not eaten. There is nothing here that eats. But incorporated. Your shirt on the line with the other shirts, forever damp, forever dripping its share back into the loop. Your boots tipped by the door, growing a moss tongue.

That is why it is so quiet under the coughing pistons. That is why it feels watched without eyes. There is one thing living here from rim to sump to green-lit canopy, and it has finally, after a century of trying to be tall and dry and busy, achieved being perfectly, sustainably low.
