No one lives here.

Not the way that word usually works.

There's nothing to count. Nothing that arrived. Nothing that will leave. Nothing that ever decided to stay.

What lives here grows here, because it *is* here. The ground extruded it.

---

At first, from far away, you think it's a forest. You think: population. You think: canopy and understory and individuals competing for light. That is the right shape for the wrong reason.

Closer, you realize there are not individuals at all, not really. There are updrafts that have learned how to cool themselves.

Each one begins not as a seed but as a soft spot. A place where the vesicular basalt thins enough that the pressure underneath can sigh through. The ground blisters. A pimple of iron slag pushes up, hollow. Hot vapor — anhydrous, sulfurous, metal-heavy, hotter than any oven — rushes up through the throat of it and for a moment there is only a wheezing vent in the stone, a little waist-high chimney that hasn't figured out its shape yet.

Then it starts to make its first leaf, because it has to.

If it doesn't dump heat, it chokes.

So it smokes itself into a radiator. The vapor carries iron and carbon and silica in solution, and as it cools at the rim of the vent, they plate out. Molecule by molecule, plate by plate. It builds its own throat taller this way, weld-seam laid on weld-seam in a slow, blind spiral with no welder, like a wasp building a paper nest out of its own breath. It is self-extruding. Self-wrought.

That first leaf is coppery and soft and curled in on itself like a fist. Like the head of a fern before it unfurls. It trembles. Not in wind — there is no wind, just the vertical draft — it trembles because hot air is flexing it as it learns its angle.

This is the childhood of what lives here: a solitary, knee-high spire ticking bravely to itself a kilometer from anything else, out on the raw table where the sky is too large. It doesn't know it's lonely. Loneliness requires water.

What lives in the middle of the valley is older.

There it is too hot and too thick for loneliness. The upwellings are so close together that their cooling organs tangle. They become what from a distance looks like jungle.

A mature one is twenty to forty meters tall. It will never get taller, because it has found the balance. Its trunk is not bark, not wood, but chimney — laminated slag, iron oxide and iron silicate laid down in matte black and tar-red layers, pocked, delaminating. You can put your hand on it and feel the deep warmth coming not from the sun but from the thing itself, from the mantle moving through it. It ticks. The metal contracting as it cools. *Tink.* A long pause. *Tink.* Like a tin roof at dusk after the sun is gone.

On top it spills open. Not branches. A crown of vanes.

That is the real organism, if you want to call it organism. Hundreds of fins, some broad as doors, some delicate as fingers, some feathered again into fractal ferns of graphite, all matte, slightly oily, that deep iron-red and verdigris black. They do not photosynthesize. There is nothing to photo. They thermosynthesize — which is to say they exist in the gradient between 1400 degrees below and 40 degrees above, and make more of themselves out of the difference. Hot vapor up, cool metal down. The vanes shiver in the draft and turn it, slow the air just enough that it drops its load of metal sulfide as a fine, glittering dust. The dust settles on the vane, is re-absorbed, thickens the vane.

When a vane is done, it spalls. It sheds. A plate of oxide as big as a dinner plate, as big as a shield, detaches with a soft sigh and helicopters down to lie in a perfect circle around the base. For a while the ground around each trunk is armored with its own fallen leaves — except they are not leaves, they are rust. And then, over years, because the ground is still foam, still porous and warm and unfinished, they sink. They are swallowed and melted and will be extruded again.

Nothing is lost. Nothing is made. That's why it is equilibrium.

So what lives here is not a forest. It is a single thermodynamic habit that has found a thousand throats. An industrial process with no industry. The plateau exhales, and where it exhales it must grow a lung, and the lungs all look like trees because a tree is the best shape to cool something.

They don't communicate. They don't need to. Their ticking is out of sync on purpose — if they were in sync something would be driving them. They don't reproduce. The windless column carries a little extra nickel vapor downwind and where it touches cool basalt, a blister forms. A new chimney begins, a kilometer away, alone. Not a child. Just another place where the same thing became necessary.

There is no understory. There is no fauna to live in the canopy. No insect has ever bored into a trunk, no bird has ever perched on a vane. No animal could; there is no water in this place, not one molecule bargained loose. The air is so dry it would suck the water out of your eyes before you could blink. The tower doesn't want water. Water would crack it.

If you stand in the deep part of the valley at noon, in the slatted light under the interlocked crowns, with the dust falling slow-motion around you and the whole place ticking like a cooling foundry the size of a city that was never built, you understand: this place is inhabited the way a furnace is inhabited by flame.

The inhabitants have no faces, no front, no back, no will. They have only an orientation: up. They have only a job: cool.

And they do it well enough that they are lush.
