The first thing that lives here is not an animal.

If you stand long enough on one of those lifts - that two-foot island of white sand - you'll think nothing lives here at all, because what lives here is precisely timed to not be seen. It crouches in the glitter.

Then it blinks.

On the dry, if you can call it dry:

They live shallow. That is the first law. Nothing here ever learned to go deep, because deep is ironstone, and ironstone is a roof over the earth. So they go wide.

The banksia lives here, but not the banksia you know from pictures. Not a tree. A stubborn knuckle of wood the height of your knee, with bark like burnt paper, old enough that its trunk has grown around the lightning strike that tried to kill it thirty years ago and failed. Its roots are fingers spread flat under the sand like a hand pressed on a table, ten, fifteen feet across, drinking the dew before the sun can take it. It flowers only when it has to - after fire - a cone of yellow that opens over days and drops one seed at a time into the white. Tea-tree clings near it, even smaller, its leaves rolled tight as needles to keep the water it has. Between them the wire-grass lives wire by wire, each clump an isolated city, its blades so thin you can see light through them, yellow-green and rattling. It doesn't grow fast. It grows forever. A clump you step over could be older than you.

And under that, barely living because they live so slowly, the crust itself lives. Lichen on the iron pan where it shows through the sand. Black, orange, ash-grey, a map so tight to the rock you think it is the rock until rain comes and it swells and lifts a millimeter. Cyanobacteria in the white sand, gluing the grains together so the wind, which has miles to gather speed, can't take them.

In the water that is not quite water:

Everything that lives here knows how to wait in mud.

It is not empty because it is clear. Look down into that sheet that is three inches deep and a hundred yards across, and at first you see only your own feet, and sand, and reflected sky. Then you see the movement that is not your shadow.

There are fish here, but they are not the fish of rivers. They are small and perfect and old. Rainbowfish the length of your thumb, all fin and light, hovering in the warmer hollows. In the deeper pans - where deeper means eighteen inches, enough to drown a boot - there are salamanderfish no longer than your finger, and lungfish that should not exist anymore; brown, lunged creatures that can sit in damp sand for months breathing air through their mouths when a pan dries to a mirror-sheen and then to cracked iron. They have been here since the rock was a mountain.

And between their legs, so many legs: The freshwater cray. Cherax, blue-black, burrowing down until it hits ironstone and then sideways, building wet chimney mounds of pale sand pellets on the rises. Its tunnels are the plumbing of this place, carrying water under the pans when the surface looks still. Under the lily pads - each pad small as your palm, with a beetle asleep in its cup - swims a cloud of tadpoles, black as iron, that will not become frogs for a year because there is not enough to eat to become a frog quickly. Dragonfly nymphs live among them for two, three years underwater, monsters with hinged jaws, before they decide to climb a rush stem and split their own back open.

They all live on almost nothing. The water is tea-colored where the tea-tree drops leaves, and that tea is food. Algae hangs in it like green smoke, and that's food. The ironstone itself rusts and is food, for bacteria that live in threads of red in the seep lips on the sandstone domes. That red weep you saw at eye level - that is alive. Iron bacteria making a living by eating rock.

On top of the water, in the air that is too loud:

This is where it shows itself. Because the country is so flat and open, sound is territory.

The frogs are the lords of it. Not one kind. Ten, twenty kinds that have divided the humidity into pitches. The western banjo frog that sounds like a dropped wire. The slender tree-frog that clicks like two stones. The corroboree frogs that bury themselves in the sedge on the very edge of wet and dry and call from underground, so the sand vibrates around your ankle. They are all small. All brown and green as tea. You will never see most of them. You will hear the shape of the water by where they call. One big male in the middle of a pan, and his rivals spaced exactly far enough apart that their calls don't cancel.

The cicadas stitch the high end of the sound together, from every banksia. They live seven years underground for seven days in the air, all of them screaming. Fairy-wrens live under that scream, two pairs to a clump, the males blue so violent in the white-and-yellow that it hurts your eyes, quick, always vertical, taking spiders. Cisticolas and reed-warblers zip low, sewing the rushes together with nests of spiderweb. And then the walkers: the brolgas that belong here because they look like the place organized itself into a bird - tall, grey as vapor, legs so long they can stand in the middle of a glittering pan and still look dry. Jabirus and yellow-billed spoonbills walk circuits that are fifty miles wide, beak-deep, filtering shrimp you can't see. Stilts, on legs like black wire, pick their way because the water is never deep enough for ducks.

And because it is so open, the hunters have to be air.

Dragonflies own it. So many you feel their wings as pressure. Red skimmers, blue damsels that hover like needles, big brown hawkers that have been patrolling this exact pan since morning, never landing. They eat everything that hatches. At dusk, bats take over the same station, tiny fifteen-gram bats that live in the paper bark of the tea-tree and come out when the frogs are loudest and catch moths that live only to lay in water and die.

What else lives here? No one who stays in one way.

In the late afternoon, when the rain comes - and it comes almost every day, quick, warm, falling straight down without wind - everything that lives here lifts its face up. The banksia opens its pores. The moss on the sandstone domes, which looked dead, turns green in a minute. The white sand darkens, and for ten minutes you see how many footprints there actually are: turtle tracks, three-toed bird, mouse-light marks of a dunnart that lives in grass, all preserved briefly in wet sand before the water spreads and erases them again. That is how they live here: without leaving sign. A country that has finished becoming asks that of you. Live long, live thin, leave no stack, no cut, no path.

If you stay till the evening, when the air goes cool and the water starts to smoke, you will see it breathe. One turtle head up, then another, far off. A ripple that is not wind. And still you may not understand who the most common resident is: the thing that is everywhere and nowhere. It is not a creature. It is the patience between creatures. The agreement that water will come, that it will not go fast, that food will be scarce but constant, that fire will be gentle, that nothing will need to be tall ever again.
