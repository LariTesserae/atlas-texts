They don't live here the way things live in a field.

Nothing here has the luxury of staying put.

What lives here lives in the *between* — between lift and slam, between spray and drip, between whistle and hush. You thought at first, when the light hit the glass of the rock, that it was empty. Then you thought it was moss. Then you realized the moss was only the skin.

**The upholsterers are first.**

They had to be. Nothing else could have held on.

Where the piston-plates throw their cold fountain up to the ceiling, the rock is not black anymore. It is swaddled. Pillows of moss, but you have no good name for them - not the starved, flat arctic moss that clings like lichen. These are monstrously fat. Engorged. Each shoot swollen like a finger left too long in water, leaves turgid and translucent at the edges, black-green going to deep wine-red where they are oldest and wettest. They grow outward, not up, they cant over the edge of every plate like wet upholstery, they make a lip, a gasket. When the plate below them lifts - Plok - they compress, and when it slams they sigh and release what they've been holding.

If you put your hand under one without meaning to, it will wring itself out on you. A half a cup of cold, clean water it made from fog.

Between them, the liverworts have gone leathery. Great slick sheets of them, liver-colored, almost black, overlapping like roofing shingles but soft as soaked gloves. And from every vertical sheet where water weeps steadily, the algae grows not as a film but as hair. Long, hair. Two feet long in places, green-black filament, each strand straight out from the wall into the air because the wind from the keyhole at the back holds it out. It sways. It pulses. It combs the spray. You can see the water running down each hair to its tip and beading and dropping. The whole room is loomed. It is manufacturing peat about ten thousand years too fast. It is trying to make soil out of nothing and cold and noise, and it is succeeding an inch at a time.

That is the base. That is the factory floor.

**The conveyors live inside the lift.**

You hear them before you remember to look down. A ticking, like rice hitting a drum.

When the sea jacks the false floor, water knifes up through the seams under pressure and with it comes the krill of the shallows that got caught in the surge chamber. Not krill, smaller. Amphipods. Thousands of them. No, tens of thousands, but they keep their interval so you think thousands.

Each one the size of your thumbnail to the last knuckle, and almost entirely clear except for a dark gut and two black pin-eye dots. They ride the piston. They are *thrown*. Every lift - Plok-THOOM - they are launched into the air of the room like sparks struck off the anvil of the sea. They hit the low ceiling and stick for a second, legs kicking, then fall into the moss pillows. They kick again. Then the water drains, sucking back down through the plates, and they go with it, dragged back into the black surge below to be thrown again.

They don't mean to be here. No part of them wants basalt and cold air. But they have learned to eat while airborne. In that one-second arc they graze the hair-algae. In the moss they snatch detritus. They are the only thing moving marine protein up onto new rock. The rock is being fed by repetition. The moss pillows are full of their shed skins - a snow of clear husks that never dries.

**The sparks live in the air that shouldn't be breathable.**

Above the highest splash, in the dead pocket where the air goes warm from being pressed again and again through the keyhole, there is a cloud that holds.

Winter midges. At first you thought it was steam. It is too cold outside for midges, you know that. And it is too cold in here. But they make their own heat. If you stand still long enough you feel it - a faint fever coming off the cloud.

Each one is smaller than a mosquito, humpbacked, flightless-looking but flying anyway, in that tight, frantic dance. They never leave the room. They were born in the liverwort leather, in a film of water that never freezes because the rock underneath is still faintly warm from being born last winter. They live three days. They spend all three in the air. Their whine is a second whistle laid over the first - higher, electrical, fraying at the edge. When the basalt inhales through the back and screams, they scatter and re-form. When a storm-petrel snaps, they part like smoke.

At night - though it's always a kind of night-blue in here - you can see their eyes catch the twice-filtered light. A hundred tiny reflected points.

**And the supervisors.**

You will not see them until you stop looking for them. Because they do not move when you move.

In the back wall, where the plates bucked highest and made a dry-ish V above the spray line, there is a nest that is not a nest. Two of them. Maybe three, if the third is tucked tighter.

Storm-petrels. But not the sea-going ones you picture over waves. These are the ones that have decided to live inside the whistle. They are small, the size of your fist, ash-gray with a soot-black V across their wings, and their eyes are absurdly large, too large for an animal that fishes by day. They have to be. They live in submarine dusk.

They have built with what the factory offers - stolen strands of that long green-black hair, and their own down plucked, and, strangely, fragments of amphipod shell pressed in like aggregate. Their cups are wedged into the sharpest crevice where a mammalian foot would never fit, where the rock if you touched it would cut to bone. They fit.

They do not fly in here. There's no room. They shuffle. They hunch. They tuck their heads and watch with those open, wet eyes while the entire room hammers and rains on everything but them. Every seven lifts, or ten, one of them will shift its weight and a claw will scrape - *skrit* - bright and loud against glass.

When they call, rarely, it is not a birdcall. The stone makes it something else. It is a ripped-metal shriek, a saw-toothed thing that bounces three times and comes back smaller. The midges freeze for half a second after. The amphipods kick harder.

That is who lives here. A nursing, swollen moss that became a gasket. An alga that became hair that combs water. A shrimplet that has become a shuttle, thrown and thrown and never learning. A midge that lives its whole frantic life in a single cold breath. And two small gray birds who have learned to be still while the world goes Plok-THOOM around them, supervising a factory with no owner.

None of them are at home. All of them are staying.

If you sit, carefully, on the one scooped plate that is almost dry, they will decide you are just another plate, slow-pistoned, and they will go back to work around you. The moss will drip onto your head. An amphipod will land on your sleeve and look up at you with its two black dots and kick off again. A petrel will blink, slowly, with a membrane, and keep watching the water come up through the floor as if checking a gauge.
