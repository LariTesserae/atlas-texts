At first, nothing lives there. That's the point of the place. That's what it wants you to think.

You hang in the mouth for a long time looking in, with your filters hissing and your suit ticking like the rock, and you see no movement at all except water. The amber worklights don't flicker when someone passes them. The terraces show no footprints because ceramic doesn't take footprints and the scree below is too hot. The cistern in the middle hangs, shivering. You think: they left. Or they died trying to grow something here.

Then a light on the east cluster blinks. Twice. Long. Long. Short. Not a power flutter - a stitch-anchor catching a drip makes a different blue. This is an old hand-lamp-behind-a-shutter blink. And when you answer wrong, with your white suit light, three more open further down. And you realize the windows were dark not because they were empty, but because they were watching.

The people who live here now are not the builders.

There are maybe thirty of them. Maybe eleven. It's hard to count because they don't keep time together and they don't keep together, period. They came afterward. Squatters, caretakers, pilgrims, stranded. Every low-orbit crew that had to put down near the spire after it pushed and found the blister exhaling cool wet air in the morning. They could not leave.

You see one cross a gap.

She - it reads as she - doesn't walk. No one walks here. The air is 11 kilopascals and 60C and will kill you open-mouthed in six minutes, so they are always suited, but the suits are ruined, patched with grown ceramic that has no seams. She steps off a balcony that has no railing into nothing, into fifty meters of empty hot air, and she doesn't fall. She *leans*. Where your stomach drops because two stitch-fields overlap and pull, she finds a ridge in that topography and puts her weight on it. She slides down an invisible hill, feet not moving, arms out just a little for balance, like someone on very thin ice. Halfway across, a sphere of water the size of her head is hanging, trembling. She cups it as she passes without breaking stride, lets it roll over her helmet and back and shoulders - drinking through the suit? Cooling? Baptism? - and it keeps hanging after she's gone, a little smaller.

Up close they are thin in a way that has nothing to do with rationing. Long-legged, long in the chest. They were not born here, but they have been here long enough that their bodies have learned the negotiation. In a stitch-field you can be light. Outside it you are very, very heavy. Bone remodels if you give it a reason. Their suits tell you they have no hair anywhere anymore; hair traps heat you can't afford. Their skin, where you see it at a seal, is mottled pale and flushed permanently red, and it doesn't sweat right - because sweat doesn't evaporate right. Instead they have these faint, subdermal lines, heat-pipes, along collarbone and thigh where their modifications dump infrared. They are almost deaf. There's so little air for sound, and what there is is eaten by the hanging cistern. So they talk with lamps. Even face to face they will flash at each other rather than try to shout through filters clogged with iron dust. Blink. Blink. Pause.

They don't live off the city. They live off its leak.

Their real work is water. All day - except there is no day, only the forge-glow of cooling rock - they adjust lips and gutters by a millimeter, scrape glass dust out of a channel, wait. When the wind comes through the mouth and sprays the hanging cistern across the void, they all come out onto what they call the porches, those stitched nowhere balconies, and open every container they have. They hold up sheets of black fabric. They stand in it. That is their rain. That is their farming. The rest of the time they are very still, because moving in 60C heat in thin air is death, so they sit in dimples where gravity is 0.3g and watch condensation bead and race.

They think the city is empty, too.

They haven't seen the builders. Nobody has. Because the builders are still here, and they are too small to see, and they were never people.

Put your faceplate right up to one of those seamless ceramic walls, past where the amber light stops, and let your eyes adjust past glare. The surface isn't smooth. It is made of thread. Not laid. Grown.

It moves.

If you hold your breath - which in here you do without meaning to, because the suit recycled air tastes like hot metal - you will see it crawling. A film, a soot, a grey-black lichen that is not lichen. Thousands, millions of motes, each the size of a salt grain, each with a black body that is mostly stitch-anchor and a ceramic jaw. They pick up a molecule of glass where the wall has micro-fractured. They carry it three millimeters. They put it down. They pick up another. The wall extends one lattice at a time.

That's the city. Not the buildings. The builders are the city. The terraces, the balconies, the frames that hold themselves up with no strut, the gutters that re-carve themselves overnight because a new crack appeared higher up - that isn't maintenance mode. That's them alive. They farm the people the way the people farm the water. The people exhale a little more moisture, drip a little more salt and skin oil, shed a little more heat. That changes the condensation pattern. That gives the builders new instructions.

When a human dies up here - and they do, of heat stroke, of a stitch-shear stroke where your blood tries to go two ways, of just misplacing a step in the invisible terrain - the others don't bury them. There's nowhere to bury. They take the body to the far end of the blister, where the geometry is wrong and the height is hard to measure. They leave it on the scree. By morning the film has been over it. By evening there is a new, small, seamless balcony where there wasn't one before. By next week, a new channel starts a centimeter from it.

At night, when the outside drops to -70C and the rock contracts and pings like a drum, when the city dims itself to cherry-red and the cistern shivers hardest, if you turn off every lamp you have and you wait long enough for your eyes to learn a new kind of dark, you see the other light.

It is not amber. It is not blue-white anchor flare.

It is everywhere at the edge of vision, a lattice of faint, matched photons running through the walls themselves, through the air itself, through the hanging water. The stitch-fields talking to each other. Adjusting. Deciding how heavy you are going to be tomorrow. Deciding how far away the back wall is. Deciding whether your suit has always been part of the rock.

That is what lives in the mountain that is not a mountain.

A water trap that taught itself to make space for hands to maintain it, and the hands that came because anything that catches water at twenty-three kilometers is by definition inhabited, and underneath both, a slow, patient, soot-colored tide of builders who have never understood they were building a city at all.
