They are hard to see at first.

Not because they hide. Because they look like what they tend. When everything is moving a little - tarp, vine, crane, water - a person in dark rubber and dark wool standing still on a walkway becomes part of the infrastructure. Your eye slides past them. Then you blink and the walkway is empty and someone else is on the next terrace over, bent over.

After a few minutes you stop trying to count them and you start trying to see the pattern of them. There are a lot.

They are short. Or they seem short, because they are almost always crouched, or stooped, or kneeling. Nobody stands up straight for long in a place where everything worth doing is at ankle height or overhead. Their knees are bad old before they are old. You see a woman who cannot be more than thirty get up from a cress tray with both hands on her thigh and a soft grunt that is not complaint, just hydraulics. Their breath shows. Even the children. The air is thin enough that you never quite fill your lungs, so you learn a small, efficient breath and you work inside it.

They are wet all the way through. Not miserable-wet. They gave up on dry years ago. The ones you meet have skin that has made a treaty with the damp. Their hands are pale on top and darker on the underside, stained walnut from wirevine tannin and moss, cuticles always black. The beds of their nails are soft. They wear layer on layer of things that never fully dry: felted wool that has shrunk and reshrunk until it's practically boiled, canvas that was once yellow or blue, oilcloth cut down and patched, rubber boots that everyone has, none that match. Most of them have a coat that is too big - an old site coat, with too many pockets - because pockets are where you keep occupancy. A bolt. A seed. A short length of chain. Pliers.

Their hair is lank. It has to be cut short or tied back severely because everything snags. The older men are nearly bald, not from age but from keeping it shaved for the midges and the drip. The older women keep theirs under a cap made from a welded collar or a plastic bucket cut down. They don't look uncared for. They look like they spend all day caring for other things and the care has worn off backwards onto them. Lichen-colored.

They are gardeners who know tolerances. They are maintenance men who know propagation. The distinction has collapsed.

You watch a man for a long time because you think he is repairing a pipe. He isn't. He is weeding it. He has a little hook and he is pulling out the hair-roots that have found their way into the joint and will, left alone for a month, pry it open and make a new spring where you don't want one. Next to him a girl who might be his daughter is doing the inverse - she is planting *into* a crack. She has a spoon and a little baggie of gritty compost and she is packing it into a shrinkage fracture in a concrete retaining wall, then pressing in a cutting of something low and tight and silver-green. She waters it from a cup that has been catching drip from the terrace above. In a week that plant will hold that wall together better than rebar.

That is how they live. Each person holds maybe twenty or thirty square meters of vertical and horizontal — not owns, holds — in an understanding that shifts whenever someone dies or gives up or has a baby or inherits a new leak. The holding is not a wall, it's a circuit. Their plot is a route for water and light and movement. If they dam too much, the person above them floods and comes down with a prybar to negotiate. If they let too much through, the person below loses their rice and does the same. So they are always out there adjusting. Lifting a gutter board an inch. Wiring a sheet of corrugated just so that it steers the weep from that cold spot on the boiler where it condenses most, over to the gourd trellis.

They talk quietly. The moss eats loud sound and they need to hear the water. A lot of their talk is numbers with no units, because everyone knows the units. "I got three off that one yesterday." "Open a half, it's pushing." They call out measurements across alleys, and children act as human repeaters. A child comes screaming down a ladder made from rebar: "Mama says open a half!" and the man two terraces down pulls a handle.

Children are everywhere, because there is no edge to fall off that isn't a five-foot fall to another roof that is also someone's kitchen. They are the only real fauna. They run in bare feet where adults need boots because their feet are still new enough not to be sodden all the way to bone. They are apprentices with no school - they carry, they watch where drip is strong. You see a boy lying on his stomach for an hour, chin on his arms, just watching how water beads and joins and runs on a sheet of steel, memorizing it the way other children memorize roads.

Old people are not less busy, they are less mobile. They get the highest, most sheltered holds, up against the warm side of the machine rooms where the old screw-turbines turn and put a low heat into the concrete. They sit on upturned buckets and do the small, endless fiber work. Stripping wirevine down to its inner cord — strong as aircraft cable, thin as twine. Mending gloves. Shelling cold beans. Their eyes are bad in the dim, so they work by feel. They are anchors. If you want to move your stall, you go talk to the old woman above you whose cistern chain clinks near your bed at night. You bring her a handful of leeks from inside a boiler.

They are not thin, though they should be at this altitude and work. Starch — that cold rice, the tubers that grow in the felt mats, the leeks — and fat. They render something, I don't know what, and keep it in old grease drums. They eat standing up, often with water running over their wrists. They are not healthy by lowland standards. Their lungs rattle a little in winter when the cloud freezes into everything. Their teeth are bad where the water is soft. But they are durable. The rust reached stasis with the oil, and they have too.

And there is a second population, which they have made some tolerance with.

Moss first. Not as decoration. As another citizen. They speak about it as a material: "That green is good holding green, that green is eating through." They farm the good ones, scrape the bad ones to feed the rice. Then the wirevine, which they both curse and let live because it finds the warm runs and shows you where the structure is still carrying current. The pigeons — wet, slate-colored, almost indistinguishable from the concrete until they move — are not kept but are allowed because they eat the black midges that would otherwise take your blood all summer. The midges themselves come and go, a cloud at knee height at dusk that the people walk through with their mouths shut. Rats, so rarely you point them out to each other when seen, as an event.

At night — which is to say, when the dim gets a little dimmer and the caged bulbs all over the mountainside become the main light, amber and buzzing — they do not stop. Some of the work can't be done in daylight anyway, because daytime is for plants. Nighttime is for iron. You hear the soft ting ting of a hammer on a wedge, a low, contented argument about whether a slope is draining or creeping. Light falls on wet faces and makes them shine. They look happy then, or at least fully located.

If you stay long enough you will be put to work. Not invited. Just a hand will put a handle in your hand while a man holds a piece of rusted grating up with his shoulder. You will hold it while he bolts it, and afterwards your palm will be cold and creased and your shoulder will ache from the thin air. That is how you become part of the place for a little while. Not housed, but held. Inclined, like everything else here, slightly back into the hill.
