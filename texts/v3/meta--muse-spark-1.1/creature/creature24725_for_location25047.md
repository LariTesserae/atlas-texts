At first you think no one lives here.

That's the point of it. You have to unlearn the lowland idea of living — crowded, chewing, calling, chasing. There is no sign of that here. No run, no nest, no burrow, no little highway through the grass. The leaf you turn over has no eggs underneath. The peat has no worm casts. The pool that holds the sky does not ripple when you approach because nothing flees from you through it.

And so the first hour you think the bowl is empty. A greenhouse left open after the gardener died.

Then you realize it's full — but full in the wrong way, and your eyes have to slow down by a factor of fifty to see the inhabitants as inhabitants and not as furniture.

It lives spaced out, on purpose.

The first ones you meet are the Bowls.

They are not taro, but your mind calls them that because it hasn't got another bowl that big. Each one is a single person. A thick, waist-high stem the diameter of a forearm, banded like it remembers water levels, rooted deep through the felted mat into the sand below, and one crown that is not many leaves but one leaf at a time — or two, or three if it is old and strong. Each leaf is a living cistern, two feet across, upturned, with a raised rim and a slightly off-center dip at the lip, as if made to pour. When you stand next to one you can see yourself dimly in the inch of water it holds.

They don't grow together. They keep a distance you could stretch your arms across three times and still not touch the next. Where a seedling has come up too close, one of them has leaned. Just a degree, over years. An apology of angle.

The Bowl doesn't eat light like other plants eat light — not greedily. It drinks air. All day the condensation beads on its waxy back and slides down into its own cup. The water in the cup is never empty and never overflows except on purpose. On still afternoons you can watch one decide to reproduce. The water in its cup gets heavy, the petiole strains, the leaf tips — slowly, so slowly you think you imagined it — and a sheet of clear water slips over the engineered dip in its rim in a silent pour toward the next Bowl downhill. And in that pour is a puff of glassy beads, each heavy as sand, that sink in the receiver's cup and will sit there for a season before they put down a white root. They only do this when no wind is inside the rim. Which is almost always.

The second ones are the Witnesses.

The single-trunked ferns. They are older. You can count age by how much bare peat surrounds them. The young ones have ten feet of empty circle. The very old ones have thirty. Nothing grows in that circle because the Witness has salted it lightly with its own fallen spores. It stands perfectly vertical — it has to, because its job is plumb. Its trunk is covered in a felt of black root-hair that is always wet. Its crown is a shaggy hanging head of fronds that never lift in a breeze, because there is no breeze. The fronds weep constantly.

Each Witness lives above a shallow in the peat, a pool that has been there since before it was tall. When it is ready, it lowers a frond — not drops, lowers, the way you would lower a hand — until the tip kisses the pool, and then it shivers. You can't hear the shiver, but you can see the pool suddenly full of cinnamon dust, each spore turning slowly over as it sinks. Then the frond lifts again, an inch an hour, until it is clear. They never do this at the same time. If you map them, you find they take turns, maybe a week apart, so their spores don't mix in the air, if there were air to mix.

The third ones are the Pillows.

That's what you called the moss, and then you realized it isn't moss, or not only moss. It's colonies. Each dome, the size of a curled-up dog, is one individual. There are maybe forty of them in the bowl, never touching. Their surface is so green it looks lit from underneath. If you kneel — and your knees immediately go wet — and put your hand close, you feel cool air coming off them. They are breathing without breath. All day they take in the condensation that forms directly out of the air, and their whole body swells. Not visibly at first. But if you mark a pebble next to one with a flake of basalt, come back in two hours, the pebble is closer to the center by the width of a fingernail. At dusk they sigh — that's the sound you heard — a tiny release of trapped air as their surface tension changes, and they contract again. They have no roots. They sit on the wet and are the wet. Underneath each, the peat is black and sourceless warm.

If you press your palm gently to a Pillow it does not spring back. It accepts your palm and then very slowly pushes, until you take your hand away. It remembers the shape of pressure for days. Some of the Pillows have shallow impressions on top, round and smooth, where water has pooled and evaporated a thousand times. Or where something with hands has pressed.

Because something with hands does come. That is the fourth.

It doesn't live here the way the others do. Nothing that needs to eat could live here — there is nothing to eat that wouldn't be murder. The Bowls have never been chewed; the Witnesses have never lost a frond to a mouth; the Pillows have never been torn. They have not evolved to be eaten and they would not know how to recover. So no animal stays.

But people know about this kind of place. Not many. One in a generation, maybe.

They climb, because there is no other way, and they come inside the rim and they stop making the sounds people make without thinking — they stop humming, whistling, talking to themselves — because the bowl makes you self-conscious. And they go to the spine in the center, the warm whaleback of basalt that comes up through the peat, polished by water and older than any of the living things on it except the stone itself.

The hollows on its back are mortars. You can see it now, because you know what to look for. One fits a knee. One fits a sitting person. One, closest to the deepest pool, is exactly the size of a Pillow, but empty — a Pillow was once there, cut clean. Inside the larger hollows there is a faint greenish smear, an oil that doesn't belong to stone. Basalt flakes are left — not debitage, not waste. Tools knapped carefully somewhere else, carried in, left for whoever comes next. They are not for hunting. Their edge is not for cutting meat or wood. It is for slicing Pillow.

You understand suddenly why there are no footprints around the flakes, why there is no fire, no char. Whoever comes here does not come to live. They come to take a piece of something that has grown without ever touching rot or blood or dung — water that has never been inside an animal, a moss that has never been bruised — for medicine, or for proof, or for a rite that must begin clean. They cut a single Pillow with a clean flake, grind it in the hollow with a little pool water until it becomes a cool green milk, and drink it, or carry it. And the Pillow does not die. They cut from the top, and it closes. Next year it is a little smaller and perfectly domed again.

That is the animal in this place: a visitor so brief, so careful, so quiet, that the bowl does not count it as animal. It lets the tools stay because they are stone, and stone belongs. It lets the person go because they learn how to pour like the Bowls do, and tip like the Witnesses do, and swell and sigh like the Pillows do, and for a day they are not really an animal at all. Just another thing that holds water for a while and then gives it away.

If you stay till evening, you will feel the bowl decide you are too loud — your breathing, your quick heat, your eyes blinking every few seconds. The condensation still forms, the ticks still fall into bowls far off, the peat still creaks. But the Pillows, the Bowls, the Witnesses all attend, slightly oriented away. They are waiting for you to become a visitor in the older sense. To set down your flake, or pick up one left for you, and then leave.

And when you do leave, climbing back over that low, sweating, lichen-furred lip, the place behind you doesn't close. It just continues swallowing fog and pouring itself into itself, spaced out, patient, complete — a town of one kind of citizen: those who have learned to live alone together without ever touching.
