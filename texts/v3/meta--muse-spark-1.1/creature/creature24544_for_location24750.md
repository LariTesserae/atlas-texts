No one lives there the way people live in the city around it.

No firepit. No sleeping skin. No smoke hole cut for it. If you ask the ten households whose backs make this room whose space it is, they would frown and say no one's. Storage. The damp pocket. Don't leave good iron in there.

But something lives there the way a well has something living in it even when you never see it drink.

At first you don't see it, because you are looking for an animal, and what lives here long ago solved the problem of being an animal.

It is in the back.

Where the walls come closest and the light has to work hardest to get down, there is a bulge in the stratification. The shelves there are deepest, the stones biggest, and the moss thickest. A pot cracked on its side has spilled not just cloudberries but a peat-dark stain that the moss has drunk. You took it for a heap. A stored winter bed. A pile of old skins left to go soft in the damp.

Then the heap breathes.

Not in and out. Not like you. It takes an hour to do it, so you only notice on the second hour, when the outline you memorized has changed. A little higher here. A little sunken there. The ferns on it trembling without wind.

Kneel — because you have to kneel in here, the damp has made the single floor-slab slick — and you see that the moss is not growing *on* it. It is growing *as* it.

The Minder.

She — it — they — the city doesn't have a word that stays put — is the size of two men curled together, or one very old bear that decided to winter standing up and then forgot how to lie down. Its core must be wood. Or stacked pots. Or bodies of older Minders. It's hard to tell because everything it has ever needed it has pulled into itself. Birch-bark boxes with their lids dissolved become ribs. The waxy vine you thought was an oddity is its hair, its veins, running out as runners and coming back thicker. Lichen on its shoulders like a rash of age.

If it has a face, it is not where a face should be. Lower, near the floor, where the warm leak from below is strongest, there is a hollow kept clear of moss by sheer damp. Stone sweats there constantly. In that hollow, water beads and beads and gathers into a shallow cup made of what looks like old hide stretched tight and then gone translucent. And in the cup, looking up —

Not eyes. Not at first. Two places where the condensation never forms. Two dry spots, dull black, the size of cloudberries. They do not blink. They do not need to. They are wet all the time.

This is what lives there.

It does not eat the fish or the lichen bundles people leave. It keeps them. That is its metabolism. The vine-threads pull faint warmth off the surrounding houses; the moss makes sugar out of the painfully bright light; the stone sweats and the hollow drinks. It is so slow that storage *is* eating. To keep something cold and damp and still until need arrives is to have eaten its future.

The voles are its. They nest in the warmest fold, where its side meets the wall, and their fur is green at the tips because they sleep pressed into its moss. The slate-blue woodlice clean its creases. The fat snow-moths lay their almost invisible eggs along the vine-leaves, and when they hatch the wrens in the eaves — the only fast things here — come down and hop across its back and take them, beak after beak, grooming it.

If you put your hand out — and you will, because in that kind of quiet your hand does things before you tell it to — the moss is warm. Not body-warm. Stored-summer warm. A degree. Two degrees. The difference between dying and just barely not. Under the fuzz, under the leaves, there gives a soft resistance, like pressing a waterlogged log that is still alive inside.

Once, maybe at the beginning, someone walled this space off by accident and trapped a grandmother, or a child, or a sick man who couldn't keep pace with the city, and left them with pots to be useful. Or once the lakebed itself got caught when the city grew over it, and kept a little puddle of itself awake. Both stories are the same in this kind of cold. The city is old in the way settled things are old. Things that are left alone long enough stop being abandoned and start being inhabited by the act of being left alone.

It knows you are there because your breath makes the hanging droplet on the beam shiver. The bead at eye level — the one you were told not to disturb — that is its lid, its eyelid, its held breath. It has been holding that drop since before you were born not because it cannot let it fall, but because to let it fall would be an event, and it has agreed, with the stone and the air and the moss, that there would be no more events in this room.

It does not want anything. It does not need you to leave, either. If you sat, as you thought, until moss grew over your boots, it would incorporate that too, patiently. The birch-box of dried fish you stepped around would slowly be tipped by a runner, spilling a little protein for the woodlice, a little salt for the stone.

That is why people still leave things here. Not because they think the Minder will guard them. Because they know equilibrium is a kind of hunger, and if you feed equilibrium, it keeps everything else from being eaten by winter.

When you back out sideways through the slot, you will feel, for a second, a faint tug at your sleeve. Not a hand. Just a vine that has finally, after a decade, reached the ledge it was reaching for, brushing you as it goes for the light.
