At first you think nothing lives here.

Not really. That's your first reading. The town is empty the way a church is empty after the service — arranged for people, but people stepped out. The pigeons have a tenancy but not an ownership. The eels commute. The bats are just sheltering from a rain that never stops. No one here pays rent.

Then you stand long enough in the 52-degree air to stop shivering and your eye adjusts and you realize you're counting wrong.

You were counting pigeons as pigeons. One per beam. One per ledge. One per rusted-out gantry joint, evenly spaced, all facing outward. That's not how pigeons live. Pigeons crowd. Pigeons fight.

These don't. These keep station.

Same with the salamanders. One per drip point, and there are hundreds of drip points, and none of them share. Each little pink animal with its fern-crown of gills has its own dimple in the limestone, its own bright bead of water that swells and falls, swells and falls. They don't eat each other. They don't even look at each other.

It's husbandry.

Something has taught them — or bred them or culled them until they learned — how to keep distance.

You start to see the actual residents in the negative space between.

They are not in the buildings. The buildings are in them.

Look at the gantries again. Those useless conveyors that run from roof to roof in the air for no reason, their belts missing. They're not streets in the air. They're drying lines. And there's still laundry on them, if you don't look directly.

It hangs.

Between the powerhouse and the terrace back-gardens that turned to canal. Between the water tower and the brick works. From the iron legs that still hold up the amber sodiums, making patient cones in the mist.

It is pale. The exact pale of wet brick and tea-colored water and pigeon breast. A big, slack, folded thing. At first you took it for a tarp that had blown loose and settled. For beard-moss, grown thick. For a shadow that was somehow opaque.

There are six of them. Maybe seven. You can only see one at a time, because seeing one teaches your eyes how to exclude the others. One per major void. That's rule three.

They are not tall so much as long. Each one is as long as a terrace house laid on its side, but bunched and folded over on itself to fit in the air it has chosen. When you catch a fold edge-on it shivers. Their surface isn't skin. It's a felt — mineral and fiber and living biofilm mink-thick, weeping continuously. The water that seeps out of the limestone doesn't just run down the buildings. It runs down *them*. It was meant to. That's why the town has so much nothing around each building. Airflow. So they would not mold too quickly. So each fold would pearl and drip separately.

They don't move. Not while you're looking. That low hum you feel in your teeth from the dynamos isn't pushing electricity anywhere anymore; it's pushing air, up. A fractional warmth, just enough to lift the cold mist off the mere at the bottom, to make it climb the stone walls of the bowl and condense again. Four flywheels taller than a man, turning one revolution per minute, to stir this bowl of breath. That is their lung. The whole sink is a lung.

And the hangings are the thing in the lung that does the gas exchange.

If you stand under one — and you will, because the second street that is really a channel runs right under one — you feel a rain that isn't the general damp. It's thicker, slightly warmer. It smells of iron and old brick and calcite and something faintly ammoniac, like wet wool. It patters on your hood. The salamanders below you drink it. The eels below them tie it back to the mere.

Closer, the felt resolves. It isn't felt. It's cilia. Millions of hair-fine, translucent hairs, each ending in a droplet. And under the hairs, sometimes, an eye opens. Not an eye as you know it — no iris, no white, no hurry. A slow, darkening, like a puddle forming from below. It watches the birch that is making the mistake of growing in that shaft of sun. It watches you. It does not blink because it cannot finish the blink.

Later, when the light has narrowed even further to just a grey coin straight above, you'll see another one adjust. You won't see it move. You'll hear the faintest sigh of wet fiber on rust, and a pigeon that was sitting on what you thought was a girder will lift without hurry and settle exactly twelve feet away on a different girder, because its beam is now occupied by a fold of something that was not there a minute ago. Or was always there and has just now given up pretending to be something else.

They don't eat the pigeons. They don't eat the salamanders or the moths or the small soot-colored bats or the clerk-like rats. The pigeons bring flecks of the outside world in on their feet — seed husks, chainlink rust, a chip of dry field — and let them fall. The rats turn the cinder ground, keeping the channels open. The salamanders filter the limestone water through their gills before it reaches the big felt. It is a closed loop. It is a farm where everything is farmer and everything is crop except for the ones who hang.

What are they? Not old gods. Your brain wants old gods because the bowl feels like a cupped hand. They are older than that and smaller. They were here when this was just a limestone bed under a shallow sea. The miners didn't dig this town. They *cleared* it. They found a place where the stone was already sweating in that patient way and widened it, and built brick and iron inside the weep to give the weep something to shape. A curing house. You cure ham in cold, damp, moving air. You cure other things too. Things you find in the deep that you want to keep but not let finish.

Hence rule one: Nothing is allowed to finish. No rust-through, no last drip. The tower's paint doesn't peel, it re-skins. The flywheels don't stop, they just take a very long time to turn. The birch in the gutter will never grow taller than a ladder. It will never die, either. Not here.

If you live here, you learn to obey. One per beam. One per drip. You take your fold of air and you keep it and you drip.

They have noticed you now. Not hostile. Just a problem of spacing. That office window across the channel — the one where the pigeon and the salamander were looking out at the same height — a little more of its darkness has come forward. A veil, damp, hangs just inside the glass. You can see the calcite whiskers on the brick through it, thicker there. It has moved to give you room, and in doing so, has made you realize you were standing too close to another one behind you.

You step back. Your coat leaves a dark mark on the cinder that fills in slowly with beads.

Drip... pause... tick... The big felt above you exhales, and for a second the amber sodium lights all tremble.

You breathe out, and your breath is taken.
