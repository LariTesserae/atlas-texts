Nothing lives here at first.

That's the trick of the Anvil. You stand in that hard 2.8 white light, in that cool that never commits to cold, with a hundred thousand basalt throats screaming the wind back at the sky, and your mammal brain says *abandoned*. Too thin. Too loud. Too clean.

And then your eye learns the economy of it.

The first citizen you meet correctly is not the interim raven. That black, white-ruffed bird with the whistling primaries — let's call it the chough, though it's heavier than any chough down below — is the most *obvious* citizen, not the most numerous. It is the only one that does what you recognize as work.

He lands on the rim of a pipe organ column, knees bent against the roar. He has learned, over generations that have never left this table, that the wind is so constant you don't hear it anymore unless you try. His skull conducts the low moan of the rocks directly into his jaw. He doesn't sing like other corvids — what's the point? Anything pitched below a kettle whistle is swallowed by the stone choir. So he flashes.

When he wants a mate, he spreads that white ruff and it catches the unfiltered sun like a signal fire. When he is angry, he snaps his wings and the slots in his primaries make their own brief, sharp whistle that cuts above the roar. Everything up here talks in light and in interruptions of light. 

And when he's hungry, he makes.

You watch him hop down into the lee of a column, where a knot of iron-black chert has been left exposed where two pipes fused badly as the lava cooled. He picks at it with a beak that is too thick, almost parrot-thick, hammered into a wedge. He strikes. He doesn't peck. *Strike.* A flake comes off with a clean bulb of percussion that only intention makes. He leaves the core where it is — there will be more flakes later, for another bird, next year — and takes the new flake in his beak to the throat he has chosen.

Inside that throat, in the tea-dark condensation pool at the bottom, something the size of your thumb knuckle is grazing.

The moss-pigs.

They are not bears. They are not tardigrades anymore, though they have the same eight dragging legs and the same blind, probing snout. Up here they have scaled up until they are too big to be invisible. Thimble-sized, translucent amber, each one a slow bag of water in a place where water has to be hoarded. They live their entire lives inside a single pipe. They are born in the iron-smelling film on the basalt, they eat the algae that eats the dissolved guano that leaches down the walls, they lay their clear eggs in the black ice that never melts at the bottom, and they die there. When the chough needs them, he wedges his struck flake into a damp crevice and levers. He fishes. He is careful — he never empties a pipe. A bird that empties a pipe will have no pipe next season. His mother showed him two pipes when he fledged. His to tend. His to not ruin.

He eats, drops the flake, and it clatters down among a century of other flakes in a little talus of lost tools at the base of the column. That is the archaeology of the Anvil. No houses, no walls. Just a thin scatter of sharp black glass where it was needed and left.

If you crouch and look, you'll see what the pigs were eating.

The pipe is a wick. The air that screams over the top at a hundred kilometers an hour drops its humidity when it hits the cool stone inside — five degrees, seven at noon — and weeps. No rain. The plateau makes its own water out of nothing. In the mornings every hollow column sweats inside, and the sweat runs down into the dark. There, in that damp that is somehow always 0.6, never more, never less, there is a skin of life: cyanobacteria, black yeast, a lichen that decided to grow inward instead of outward. It tastes of rust and old guano. It is the pasture.

Above the rim, the pasture looks like nothing. Sparse, 0.9 on any scale that measures green. Hard buns of cushion plant, the kind that lays itself down against the wind so flat it becomes rock with a little fur. Each one is a hundred years old, maybe three hundred, and each one makes exactly one white flower and then holds it. Not holds it for a season — *holds* it, for years, because to make a new one would cost more than the sun gives here, and because there are no bees to impress with excess. When the flower finally gets pollinated — by wind, by a desperate fly that got swept up from below and will never get down again — it closes and makes three seeds and starts over. The cushions are islands. Between them is bare basalt, lichen-yellow on the lee where the birds shit.

That guano is the other ocean here. From above it comes down constantly. The high gliders never land.

You only really see them when you lie on your back and let your ears equalize by opening your mouth. Then you see that the emptiness is full. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, turning on wings so thin you can see the sun through them. They are not birds. They are something between a swift and a bat, with long, silent, membranous fingers and no feet to speak of. They live in the updraft where the plateau ends and the world falls away. The wind hits the lip of the Anvil and is thrown upward, and they ride it, all day, every day, without beating a wing. They drop, they climb, they scream — but their scream is pitched above even the highest pipe, a bat-click shriek you feel as pressure behind your front teeth rather than hear. The basalt roar is the perfect cover for them. Nothing down below can hear them coming. When one dies, it falls onto the Anvil and for a day there is a feast. Otherwise they harvest what the wind harvests: moths dragged up from the forests a vertical kilometer beneath, ice crystals, spores, and they piss white fire that feeds the lichen that feeds the algae that feeds the moss-pig that feeds the chough.

Everything is a closed loop with a very loose knot.

Down on the rock itself, low enough that the wind almost misses them, are the others that make the census read as both empty and abundant.

The big-eared pikas — ears like satellite dishes, thin and red with blood trying to warm itself — live in the interstices where two columns broke off together and left a wedge of permanent shade. They don't burrow. There is nowhere to burrow. They stack. They cut the hair-fine dry grass that grows in the crack dust and pile it behind the rock where the wind can't steal it, and they sit on it and listen with their whole bodies. Their bones, like the birds', carry the vibration of the stone, so they can feel an ibex coming long before they see it.

The ibex are the opposite story.

They are not many. In a day's walk you might see four, which is two pairs. They are always in twos. A table-shouldered, smoke-gray grazer maybe the size of a large dog, with a coat so dense and so full of hollow hairs that wind cannot move it, and hooves so splayed they make no click on the pipes. They don't need to herd because there is nothing to be protected from up here except the idea of falling off the world, and the wind is so steady it never gusts. They move like monks, five steps, stop, crop lichen, five steps. Their lives are paired permanently — not male-female, sometimes, but whatever two were born in the same hidden ice-crevice in the same week. They eat the cushion plants only when a cushion dies, which is rarely. Mostly they lick. They lick salt and lichen and guano and iron seep off the basalt itself, tongues the color of slate.

They give birth once, to one calf, every six or seven years, and they will not do even that if the black ice in the deep clefts is thin that year. They know. The plateau sets the quota and they obey it perfectly. That is why it never changes. That is why a place that looks like a post-impact wasteland can be in absolute equilibrium.

At night — though there is no real night, just a dimming of that white torch overhead — the pipes cool another fraction, and more water condenses. The moss-pigs swell. The pikas tuck their enormous ears around themselves like blankets. The choughs wedge themselves sideways into empty throats and go still. The ibex pairs lean together with their backs to the flow. The gliders climb higher to get out of the cooling sink. And the organ plays on and on, the chord that is not one note but ten thousand almost-the-same notes, deafening and steady enough to be a kind of silence.

If you stay until you stop trying to hear your own thoughts, you understand the technology here.

It is all hand-held, all opportunistic, all immediately dropped. A flake when you need a flake. A flower when you need to have made a flower once. A pool made inside a rock because the sky will not provide one. Nothing is built to last because lasting is the plateau's job. The residents just borrow its tune and add nothing that would change the pitch.
