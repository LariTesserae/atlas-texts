At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the trick of how crowded it is. You push through a wall of clubmoss that doesn't part so much as reluctantly give way, you sink to the shin into Racomitrium that has the spring of deep carpet padding, and you think: this *is* the life. A botanical accident. Too much green for the altitude, but still just green. No eyes in it. No intention.

Then you sit because the air is too thin for that much work, and you get wet, as per the first rule here - nothing leaves dry - and the whole room starts to work on you, and you realize it is occupied from the rock outward.

What lives here is not *in* the moss. It *is* the moss, but not how you think of moss.

Call it the Thresherman. Because that's what it does.

This whole cut - forty meters long, twenty wide - is one thing. A single colonial mat, a chimera of a hundred species that have forgotten how to be separate up here in the permanent blue dusk. The forked spleenwort and the filmy ferns are its fingers. The clubmoss that runs in scaly green ropes up the sharp new basalt is its cable. The woolly Racomitrium - that vast, ankle-deep, chest-high sponge - is its body, its lung, its stomach. 

It should not be able to live here. There is almost no light - that bruised 0.2 light that never lands directly, just bounces down off the greater wall above like light at the bottom of a well. There is no soil. The rock is too young to have rotted into anything. Miocene basalt that broke yesterday and still has the glass edge of its cooling.

So it found the other way to eat. 

Because young rock bleeds. This basalt is full of unmixed, unweathered feldspar and iron, apatite, trace phosphorus that older mountains have already had washed out of them. The Thresherman exudes acid - not much, a slow, patient sweat of oxalic acid from a million root-hairs and rhizoids. You can smell it if you put your face close, a faint metallic tang under the wet. It etches the sharp faces. You can actually hear it on very quiet moments, or you think you can - a faint fizzing under the louder sounds of water and wind, like alka-seltzer. It is mining the mountain while the mountain is still trying to decide what shape to be.

And it has industrialized the weather.

That is the real life here, the reason this impossible meadow exists at 4,000 meters in a north-facing knife-cut that should be bare ice.

When the wind hits the outer columns - those fluted organ-pipes of columnar jointing - it huffs, it hammers. The whole cut breathes. The Thresherman catches that breath. Every frond, every feathered moss leaf, is tipped with a hydathode, a microscopic cup. Cloud is pulled in over the lip and shredded, and it condenses. Not rain. Never rain. Capture. A million million cold droplets born directly onto the surface of the thing itself. That is why you never hear insects here, why fauna is 0.2 and essentially zero - insects need free water, moving water, standing water. Here water exists only as a *held* thing. It is owned the instant it exists.

You watch it happen, sitting against the sharp rock getting soaked: a fern frond in front of your nose is dry for a second, then it beads, then the beads merge, then the whole frond bows under the weight and pours in a precise, channeled runnel into the moss tank below, which swells, which sighs.

It is a factory line. Cloud in, leaf capture, tank hold, acid leach from the rock below, drip down, filter down, exhale. The Thresherman runs wet and cold and loud.

And it is always moving. That is what that turbulent 2.9 dynamic means. This is not a peaceful, old-growth cushion. This is a boomtown. In this constant cold damp and mineral broth, growth is obscenely fast. A few millimeters a day. You can literally hear the mat tearing itself apart under its own hunger. Soft *paks* and *tiks* all around you as moss colonies grow so thick they peel away from the vertical basalt under their own wet weight and slump. Then a small sharp rock, newly frost-spalled from the back wall - *thok* - falls into the thatch and is swallowed. The rule is the second rule: every surface must be occupied. The instant a new tooth of black glass is exposed, a protonema, a green film, is already on it.

Underneath you, in the black, under the living top foot, is ten feet of dead, cold-composting previous Thresherman, pickling slowly because it's too cold for anything to rot properly. The cut is filling itself in with itself.

If you lift a handful of the wettest moss and squeeze, the water that runs out is not clear. It's amber with tannins and suspended peat. And if you let your eyes go wide in the gloom, you see the only animals that have ever gotten tenure here.

Not birds. Not marmots. Nothing that needs to leave. Fauna 0.2 is generous because you have to count these as fauna, and they barely qualify.

In every cupped leaf, in every moss tank the size of your thumbnail, there is a pocket-world. Tardigrades - water bears - in their thousands, fat and translucent as raw glass beads, paddling in the held water. White oribatid mites so slow they look like moving mineral grains. A blind, unpigmented springtail, no bigger than this letter *o*, that does nothing but shuttle spores from fern to moss and back. They never dry. They never leave their spoonful. They are born, graze, die, and are eaten by the moss, which digests them too. They are the maintenance crew.

And then there is the listening part. Because a place this dense and this loud can't afford rustle. Rustle is wasted information.

When the wind huffs through and makes the wet thatch slap - that hissing, wet clattering roar, that 1.7 sound - the Thresherman goes still in a wave. Watch it: a gust hits the lip, the outer spleenworts shudder, and then a ripple of stillness runs down the back wall as fronds curl slightly inward, to keep their water from being flung. It is not wind response. It is too coordinated. It is the colonial mat bracing. The only nervous system it needs is hydraulic - pressure passed leaf to leaf. It hears the wind with its whole body.

It will outlast you there. You are too dry for its taste, too big, too much empty surface. You sit and you become damp as per rule one, and the basalt cuts into your back through your soaked coat, and the whole small room of this mountain that makes only itself, endlessly, out of rock and cloud, thumps and drips and huffs around you.

It doesn't notice you, exactly. But it has already started growing over your boots.
