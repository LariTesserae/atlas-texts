They live here the way you live in a room that already had tenants when you arrived, and you have come to an understanding.

You think at first they are not here. That's the density trick working on you. You walk the four-lane that no truck has needed for an hour, past the roller door open on a shed big enough to pour weather in, and you hear one pump ticking like a throat in the back of a cathedral. The street looks empty. It is not.

The people are sparse by design. There were never many, not for what was built. They came after the pour, to keep the pumps. Now they live on the second story of everything.

They are soft in the same way the place is soft. That is the 2.2 erosion on a human. No hard edges left on them. Their t-shirts have lost all sizing and hang off them limp and damp from a wash that never quite dried. Their hair is cut short because in this heat hair is a cruelty, and always wet. Their skin never dries. It shines. Hands and forearms are stained the gray-silver-black of wet concrete and oil that doesn't come off, not because they are dirty but because it has soaked in. The corners of their eyes are gone, dissolved by squinting into steam-light for forty, fifty, sixty years.

They do not hurry. Nothing here hurries except water. In 38C wet air you learn to not waste moisture. So they don't call out. They don't slam. They nod from second-floor landings. They leave their doors open for ventilation and privacy is negotiated by not looking in. They walk in the middle of the road at low tide because the crabs take the edges. They pick their way in bare feet or in boots with the laces left undone. At night you hear them more clearly — not voices, but living: a chair scraping one story up, a radio tuned low to weather and tide, the clink of a bucket being set out to catch condensate because condensate is cleaner than what comes from the tap. They boil crab, sometimes. More often they eat rice and things out of cans that come in on the same two trucks that need those four lanes. The children — there are fewer than there should be — learn before speech to step over, not on. To shake their shoes in the morning. To keep the water moving.

They do not think of themselves as owning a building. A building is a dry place with a working upper floor and a first floor that you have ceded. That is the agreement. The ground floor is given over. You can see it if you stand at one of those open roller doors and let your eyes adjust: the entire slab, ankle-deep in sheetwater, alive and rippling without wind. The brown mud-crabs moving shoulder to shoulder, feeding, cleaning, breeding, dying, quiet because chitin on wet concrete makes no more sound than rain. The thumb-long roaches farming the underside of pallets, turning cardboard into more roach. In the expansion joints, in the weep holes, in the crack where the concrete jacket of the canal has been meandered from beneath, the white flat-crabs live like rivets, one fat claw held up like a shield. This is not an infestation. It is tenancy. The men who tend the pumps live above it because that way makes sense. They leave their boots outside at dusk on the landing, and by morning the crabs have cleaned them.

Then above the people, the other upper-story tenants.

Because what lives here is stratified, like water of different salinities will not mix.

At ten meters, in the rafter dark where hot air stratifies and the bare sodium bulbs buzz all day, is the flicker. You only see it when it decides to go out hunting. A whole factory's worth of bats and chimney swifts that have learned to share the same void, poured out of a busted clerestory in a river at false dusk — even though outside the sky has not changed, that perpetual low smoke holding low cloud down. They braid the dim. They do not squeak; they learned not to, as everything here has learned not to. They feed on the other weather, the columns of midges that stand over every puddle that does not move fast enough, trembling pillars that from a distance look like the heat shimmer but stay in one place. Midges into swift into guano into roach into crab.

Above that, on the hot tin and the flat roofs where no one ever goes except to free a stuck valve, stand the birds in rows. Gulls. Terns that have forgotten the sea. Boat-tailed grackles. Night herons that are not nocturnal here because night and day are the same color. They have arranged themselves sparsely, predator-sparse, one bird every ten feet along a ridge cap, all facing into the wind that isn't there, waiting. They do not call. Gulls that do not cry are a different animal — patient, terrible, old. They watch the road for crabs, they watch the crabs for rats.

And the rats are the mirror of the people. They keep the same hours. They use the same highways. At 2 a.m. when the pumps are at their most audible — that low chatter of bearings and the chuck of water into water out in the dark between buildings, a sound that is everywhere because it is nowhere — the rats move in lines down the center stripe, nose to tail, not squealing because squealing wastes water. They move between the bigboxes and the refinery squares and the rail fan that still gleams. A human on second-floor watch sips tea and watches them go and counts none. It's not his business. His business is the gauge that says keep the water moving or become water.

That is what all of them live by, and that is what makes them one thing despite being crab, bird, rat, roach, man. This town was a wager that you could keep a marsh from being marsh by pouring it over. The marsh is patient. The groundwater sits six inches down, waiting to be surface again. In the yard where the empty chemical totes are stacked ten stories high, beyond where the chainlink has no sign, the concrete simply gives up maintenance and softens into wet mineral mud that still holds tire tracks like coagulated soup. Beyond that, the estuary, brown and hot as bathwater, has no line between itself and the sky. It wants itself back.

So what lives here lives like a crew on a ship that is never not leaking, not frantic, but constant. Not hostile, not friendly. Attentive. Everyone keeps the chatter down to save the bearings. Everyone keeps moving sideways just enough to stay in place. The whole city transpires and sweats and drips and condenses and is drunk again by something else.

Big enough for millions of small lives to transact all their business without ever needing to be loud about it. Sparse enough that if you walk all day you might only meet two other people, nod once, and understand the entire civic law:

Keep it moving.
