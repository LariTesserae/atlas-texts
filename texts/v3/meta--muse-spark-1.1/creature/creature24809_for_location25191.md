They live here the way water lives here — not by force, but by staying.

At first you would think they are human, and you would not be wrong, but you would be wrong about what kind of human that means. They are not the hardy mountaineer kind you picture at four thousand meters. No leanness, no wind-burned angularity. The air is too thin for that.

The people of the Bowl are short and wide. Not stout in a quaint way — constructed. Barrel chests that have had to become barrels. You can see it in the old men who go shirtless even when their breath fogs, their ribs like the staves of those big cistern tanks, their lungs making a faint tick at the end of each inhale as the condensation does. Their blood is adapted to be a little thick, a little slow. Everything about them is slow. A quick movement up here blacks the edge of vision, makes the heart hammer in the ears. So they have stopped moving quickly generations ago.

They are soft-looking. Pale in the way of things that grow under fern fronds — not white, but the greenish-white of underside, of root. Their hair grows thick and stays damp. Most of them keep it cut very short or let the moss take it; the older women in particular don't fight the mat that forms at the crown and shoulders, the dark roof-moss finding purchase in the oils of their scalp. It doesn't itch them. They use it to keep their heads warm. The young peel it off and press it into bricks with the rest.

Their eyes are large. Not romantically. Functionally. The light is always low, always filtered through cloud and soot and frond, that deep-sea afternoon. Their irises are almost all a washed grey-green, the color of the lichen that looks like poured wax. They see motion well in that dimness. They do not see distance well — nothing here ever requires seeing distance, the bowl is too close, the sound too eaten. An outsider would squint at the rim of the valley. They don't. The rim is a suggestion.

Because it is so quiet, they are close talkers. They have to be. Sound falls out of the thin air two meters from your mouth and lands dead in the moss. Shouting is obscene up here, and biologically expensive. So their language has gone low and consonant-heavy, a murmur you feel in the chest plate more than hear. They often touch when they talk — a hand on the forearm, a forehead nearly touching another forehead — to catch the words that the air won't carry. Children are taught to listen to pipes. You put your ear to the warm iron that carries the boiler runoff and the condensate from the fans and you can hear the whole street: who is filing metal two doors over, who has left their collector valve open too long, whether old Mara's cough has gotten worse. Eavesdropping is not rude here. It's how you know if the city is still alive.

No one here builds. They tend. That's the occupation for everyone, whether they work in the machine shop, or keep a food terrace, or run the big iron flowers on the rim. 

The machine shop man knows his lathe will not stay a lathe. If he leaves it alone over winter, the moss will take the exact shape of its gearing in a damp, perfect glove, and under that the iron will pit and soften. So he oils it with the rendered fat of those sourgrass shoots, and runs it every day, and when a part grows too soft he does not replace it with a harder part — there is no harder part left un-purchased by rust. He replaces it with something that gives. A knuckle joint of that succulent rope-vine where there was once a steel elbow. A bearing wrapped in felted roof-moss that holds water and so holds motion. His machines are not machines anymore. They are animals he has convinced to keep working.

The wall-tender — and every woman and man is a wall-tender in their own house — knows her house moves an inch a year downslope, toward the center of the hand. She does not correct it. She cuts the floor on the uphill side and packs the downhill side with more stone dust and clay, and retrains the vine through the new crack. A good house here is not square. A house that is still square after twenty years is a house no one loves enough to let move. The oldest houses have interiors like stomachs, all curved, all lined with living green, stairs that start where a window was. They negotiate.

This is how they last. Their rule is not philosophy, it's physics, enforced by the valley: anything that insists on being hard becomes soil. So they have become soft, as an ethic. Their shoes are soft moss-brick soles that soak up water and let it out slowly. Their joints are loose. Their plans are loose. A house raising here ends with the sentence "And we'll see what it wants to be in spring." Old age is not hardening. The oldest people become less distinct. Their skin dampens permanently. Liver spots bloom that are precisely the same color as the wax-lichen. They move less, settle deeper in the cut foundation of their homes, and finally stop moving at all around noon during the condensation hour, when the city ticks. No one is surprised. They cut the foundation a little wider around them. After a few seasons you can't tell where the wall ends and the ancestor begins, except that one part of the wall stays a few degrees warmer than the rest and sometimes drips a bit of salt.

That's why the fauna is not really rare. It's just that you were looking for the wrong kind.

There is the moth, of course — *Bowl Grey* they call it, in that low language where grey and moss and grandmother share a root. It is not decoration. Its whole lifecycle lives inside the ten-centimeter-deep mattress on the roof, eating, breeding, dying without ever seeing the sky. When you peel moss for fuel, you do it in a checker pattern, always leaving every other square untouched, for the moths. If the moths leave, the moss dies, and if the moss dies the roof goes.

There are the long blind things in the soft rock. When you cut a new stair or cistern — and everyone cuts, constantly — you sometimes open one up. A pale, translucent rope as long as your arm, no eyes, a mouth like a pinched seam. It has been tunneling there since before the city. It eats stone dust and excretes clay so fine it holds water like a womb. The children do not kill it. They sing to it — a low hum in the chest — and nudge it gently with the flat of the pick until it goes back deeper. To kill one is bad luck because then your cistern won't hold.

And there is the raptor.

It is not a bird the way the ferns are not really ferns. At this height, with this thin air, no true hawk should be able to circle. The wings would have to be too impossibly wide. And yet in the late afternoon, when the big condenser fans on the rim creak open — those huge slow iron petals that pull moisture out of the cloud — something rides the cold column of air they push down. It is too large, too silent, because there is no air for its feathers to disturb. From below it looks like a tear in the overcast, edged in grey. It circles the Bowl once, twice, without flapping. It never lands, because there is nowhere hard enough to land. 

The people do not look up at it, much. They call it the Inspector, or sometimes the Memory. They say it is the valley itself doing its inventory: counting drips, counting houses that have become hills, counting people who have become walls. When it passes overhead the ticking of condensation seems to pause. The apprentices in the machine shops stop their files in mid-stroke. 

Then it is gone over the soft lip of the dome, and the cloud drops, and the thin clear indigo comes down with all its stars too sharp for lowland eyes. And then the whole city, houses and people and moss and lathes and moths and the long blind rock-eaters and the sheet of water still hanging for a second under the gantry, all glitter at once, yellow window light reflecting off a million droplets, before it all, together, freezes just a little.

In the morning it will all soften, and give a little, and drip again, and keep going.
