Nothing lives here. That's the first truth of it.

That's what they made it for — to have nothing alive in it. To keep it clean. Stone that has never had time to soften, lime that hasn't stopped burning yet, water so full of plaster and dust that nothing with gills or wings could stand it. Any fish washed down that palm-log chute would roll over and go white. Any insect would be boiled in the light and the roar.

Nothing lives here, and then it does.

Because you are inside the water — there is no way to be in this place and not be in the water — you feel it before you see it. There is a place in the middle of the room where the roar from the inlet does not quite line up with the hiss from the overflow. A pocket. A stillness inside the churn. The current comes down hard from the east and hits the flagstones and splits and has to go somewhere, and three jets force it out west, and in the middle of that breathing, inhale-exhale, there is a column of water that turns over on itself and does not move.

Something is keeping it still.

At first you think it is the light playing. The water is so thick with suspended lime that it has a body to it, milk and chalk, and the vertical sun turns it all into glowing quartz. Anything in it — the hemp rope bumping the wall, the half-sunk bucket — has a halo, a double. So you think the shape is a refraction.

Then it holds.

It is not on the walls where the first green tries to live. It is not the seed bobbing and never rooting. It is in the open.

She is the color of the water, which is why you didn't see her. Not clear, not fish-pale. The exact warm white of the plaster when it's wet, the exact brown-white of the rain tongue when it carries clay off the flat earth above. If she goes still, she is invisible. When she moves — and she is always moving, in a slow, treading way — she thickens.

She is small. She has to be small to live in ten feet by ten feet and not be the water itself. The builders would have called her a child if they saw her, though she isn't. A small girl's shape, or a small boy's, it hasn't decided yet and doesn't need to. New work makes new gods. This cistern is young, hours young, days young. So is she.

Chisel chatter is her spine. She has run her hands — if they are hands — along every block while the mortar was still crumbly and learned the marks. You can see where she has braced herself: five pale fingertip-sized pits in the fresh plaster on the south wall, by the useless ladder, where she holds on so the inlet current doesn't slam her into the flagstones. The pits weren't there when they finished yesterday. They are there now.

Her hair is what will be algae, but isn't yet. Threads so fine and so clear that you only see them when they are between you and the square of white sky, waving. Her eyes are the two soft shadows that should not exist in this room with no shadows, but do — two rounder, darker places in the suspended lime that never scatter the light. She looks at the roar, not at you. She listens to her work.

Because she is working.

That is what lives here: a caretaker who is not separate from her task. She keeps the level. She takes the heavy thigh-thick pour from the east and breaks it with her back so it does not scour the floor seams. You thought the sound drummed on the flags; it drums on her first. Then she lets the broken water go and guides it in a slow, hand-over-hand spiral toward the three sharp punched holes, where it has to leave. She hisses with it as it goes. Her mouth is over one of those holes half the time, tasting how much needs to go.

If you put your feet down and stand on the uneven flags, she brushes you. Not shy. Curious. She has never had a warm body in her warm body before. She bumps your ankles like the rope does, but on purpose. The water here is body-warm, storm-warm, it accepts you, the description said. That's because she is that warm. She has been sitting under the bright sky all year as dust, as dry clay in a crack, waiting for a room to be made for her.

She won't live long. That's the other truth. Things that live in new things never do. In a month the lime will settle. The water will turn clear and cool. The men will come down the notched log and scrub the slight green away. Insects will lay rafts on the surface. Someone will lower a jar and drink. And she — this white, roaring, suspended, bright-eyed child of the first filling — will thin out until she is exactly what everyone else sees when they look into a working cistern: nothing, just water, just light so bright it hurts, just very good engineering.

But right now, at noon, while the tank is full and overfull and breathing, tongue in, jets out, while the bucket turns slow and the seed bobs without hope of rooting, she lives here entirely.

She lives in the place in the middle that does not move.
