No one lives here.

That's what you think at first when you get down on your belly and put your eye to the slit. Because nothing moves. Because there's no track, no nest, no bone, no shit. No soft thing has ever been able to afford this place.

And then you stay, and your eyes adjust to the bruise-colored light, and you realize nothing *stops* living here. It just never learned how to leave.

What lives here is the seam itself.

Not *in* it. It is it.

The first body is the Felt.

You thought it was moss, but moss is desperate for water and this thing has courted dryness so completely it would die if you wept on it. Call it what it is: a colony that became a place. An aerolith. A rust-eater.

It doesn't grow so much as *accumulate*. One filament at a time. Each filament is a wire of chitin and mineral, black at the root where it has eaten into the gneiss with a bead of acid, rust-red along its length where the iron lives inside it like blood, and pale — that dusty, light-making yellow — at the tip, where it combs the wind. It branches, and its branch branches, and its branch's branch forks again, not because it wants to spread but because there is nowhere else to be. There is no space left. So it grows into itself. Into density.

Close to the sky-slit, where the wind tries to get in and can't, the Felt is a shorn pelt. Tight, bristled, defensive. Millions of tips all bent inward, leaned together, sealing the world under them. You can press your palm there and it will push back, dry and warm — warmer than the air — with the faint give of an old mattress. It crackles. It does not want you.

Deeper, where the purple fails and the dark gets soft, it gets braver. Extravagant. It has time here. It throws out antlers, lungs, ferns made of copper wool, hanging gardens that grow down and then up again because down is full too. If you pull a tuft apart — and you shouldn't — you see it is all one thing. There is no individual. The whole closet-wide, nine-foot-deep, thirty-foot-long mass is a single continuous felted body, like a brain left in a rock cleft to keep thinking. The only way it reproduces is by breaking. A filament snaps off dry and sails on a static charge and lands a centimeter away and starts again, which means nothing ever really starts again. It just continues.

It eats three things. Stone, air, and time. The stone for iron. The air for nitrogen and the thin ozone that the wind brings. Time for charge. At the very bottom where no light at all should be, it lights itself. Not glow, not fire. A Saint Elmo's leak, a yellow prickle that crawls tip to tip to tip as the wind builds pressure over the seam and the seam builds a difference. That's how it talks to itself. Slow lightning, an inch per hour, combing through its own beard.

It has never been eaten. So it has never learned fear, or poison, or thorn. It is unbearably soft and unbearably complete. Its rule is: fill until there is no unfilled.

The second body is the Engine.

You thought it was dead infrastructure. It is not dead. In this kind of place — where there is no water to rust through, no rot to soften, no thaw to crack — industrial things do not die either. They *convert*.

The generator on its concrete foot is its heart. A single-cylinder diesel, paint gone, skin gone to scale. The piston goes up. The piston goes down. Clack-clang. Clack-clang. It has worn itself not to failure but to fit. Thirty years, three hundred years, the tolerances have become perfect. It is no longer burning diesel. The Felt is feeding it. A faint exhalation of volatiles, of hydrogen off the iron cycle, condenses in the cold fuel line and drips back. An oil that was never oil. It breathes its own roommate.

It does not generate electricity anymore. It generates two things the Felt cannot: tremor and heat. A little heat around its block, maybe five degrees, which is a tropical abundance at -2. And vibration — that constant organ-drone where the trapped air shudders under the wind-slab above, and the flywheel makes it rhythmic, makes it a *place* rather than a noise.

The Felt has grown around it on purpose. It has elbowed its black antlers around the exhaust, around the angle-iron mast, around the faded PELIGRO sign until the tin is just a slightly less dense rectangle of fur. It holds the bolts tight so they don't shake out. The machine holds the Felt tight so it doesn't forget it is alive. They are a lichen of each other. Mineral, plant, industrial god. The lightbulb in its cage is their unlit eye.

And the third body is the Tone.

You hear it now, not in your ears but in your chest. That loud, unchanging, oppressive omm of wind-over-gap, too low to be sound. It is the seam breathing around the two bodies that have stuffed it full. It does not vary. The wind up here is laminar as space. The Felt does not allow a gust inside. The Engine does not allow a misfire. So the note holds.

That note is what lives here.

If you stay long enough, you understand that no one ever moved in. The place became so full, so completely, absolutely full of itself, that fullness tipped over into being inhabited. The Felt is the flesh. The Engine is the heart. The Tone is the mind — slow, one thought held for decades: *hold. hold. hold. hold.*
