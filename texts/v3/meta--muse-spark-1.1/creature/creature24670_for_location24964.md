They don't live here the way anything else lives anywhere else.

At first you don't see them at all. That's why you think, and anyone would think, that there is no animal life here. Nothing darts. Nothing grazes. The forest shudders and nothing is shuddering it. The stone circles are empty. It's a reasonable conclusion in water this cold and this huge-sounding. An eye looks for movement that is quick, for a fin, for a flicker. There is no quick movement here.

You have to wait until you learn the tempo of the place. Until your own heart slows down enough that its hammering stops filling your ears. Until you stop kicking to hold yourself still and let the water hold you instead - the way the silt does, taking a long minute to decide to fall.

Then you start to see that the clearings are not empty.

---

The ones who live here live low. Lower even than the peat.

They are the size of you, or would be if they stood up, but they don't stand. They bend. They are bent over double by habit and by weight. The water here is heavy; to be tall is to be knocked over. So they go on all fours when they have to move, knuckles and knees on basalt, and when they don't have to move they don't. They go still for a very long time.

Their color is why you missed them. They are clay-color and stone-color and trunk-color. Not camouflage - they didn't evolve to hide. They just have not bothered to be any other color than what they touch every day. The skin of an old one is the mottled black-green of the kelp trunks, smooth but thick, a good centimeter of blubberless insulation that doesn't crease when it bends. A young one's skin is paler, the exact gray of the cold clay in the clearings where they do most of their work. They are hairless. Head, body - no drag. Their heads are large, round, almost featureless at distance. Up close you see the eyes are there but they are kept shut, sealed under a second lid, translucent as the thin edge of a frond. They do not open them often. Light means little here. The faint pressure-light the water makes on still nights is enough to see movement through skin. They see current. They see heat, or rather they see the lack of it - a man in this water is a flare.

Their hands are wrong in a way that tells you how long they've been here. Too broad. Fingers too long and the webbing between them thick and scarred. The nails are black keratin plates, long and flat, not for digging but for cutting. To cut the kelp you need to get under its waxy rind and shear. They do that with their nails. That's what the braided cords are.

They do not swim.

If you had a word for an aquatic animal that does not swim you would use it. They walk. They push. They plant a hand, drag a knee. When they need to cross open water between ridges - that kilometer of empty, loud water - they push off once from stone and glide, arms tucked, like you did, for a hundred meters without a twitch, letting gravity remember them slowly. In the forest they move hand over trunk, trunk to trunk, using the forest the way you would use a staircase banister in the dark. The groaning you thought was only plants was often them, settling their weight against a stalk twenty meters tall, making it bow.

They breathe, but not the way you think.

They can't, not continuously. There is no dissolved air enough for lungs, not for lungs that ever knew air. So they have kept lungs, but they have learned to be miserly with them.

Each of those low walls, those stone circles with the down-current doors, each of those little four-slab huts with the shivering silver inside - those are not houses. They are lungs outside the body.

The forest exhales constantly, those streams of silver pearls that rise and never arrive. The people here - for lack of a better word - farm that exhalation. They bend a frond into a circle and pin it with stone so its bladders pop in one place. They stack slabs to make a bell. Gas collects there, cold, with a smell like cut grass and iron. When a trap is full, it trembles, a mirror the size of a table, stuck under rock. They crawl inside. They put their faces in it. They breathe out the old water and breathe in what the plants made. One breath can last them an hour if they are still. Two if they are very still.

That's why the clearings exist. The forest does not respect a boundary you can't see; it respects a boundary that is tended. They clear it. They scrape the holdfasts off the basalt with their plate-nails, they pull the drifting sprouts that try to root mid-water and let them go. They keep the stone bare because bubbles will not stick to frond. They need bare stone to hold the sky. Every village is in a place where the ridge is just high enough that the water above is dark blue instead of black, where the current slackens. That's not for comfort. That's where the gas rises slowest and stays longest.

They live in small knots. Three, four. Maybe six where the traps are good. You saw a dozen circles and thought village; it is not a village all at once. It is one family moving between lungs as one fills and another goes sour. They do not need fire. They do not need tools except stone and cord. Cord is still alive, by the way. That black rope that hasn't rotted - it hasn't rotted because it is still growing, a little; a living skin grafted to rock, holding.

They communicate rarely, because sound carries too well. To speak is to tell the whole valley where your air is. But when the deep boom comes - the gas pocket letting go, the stone settling - they all pause. You felt it in your chest. They feel it in theirs and they answer it, once, with a stone on stone. One clack, from a circle a kilometer off. Then another, farther. A slow census through water that carries everything. *We're still here. We're still here. We're still here.* The current roars overhead like wind and takes the clacks away.

They are not hostile. They have no predators and no prey and they long ago stopped thinking anything that falls into the high sea is either. When you pushed off that ridge and glided, one of them watched you from under his air-mirror with his sealed eyes and mistook you, for a moment, for a peat-sail falling - those big sheets of dead frond. Then you kicked, and he knew you were something that wastes air.

If you stay still long enough on the clay, in one of those immense dark clearings where the forest stops, you will feel one come close to see if you are an obstruction to flow. It will come knuckle-walking, mountain-slow, a boulder deciding to be a thing. It will not touch you at first. It will circle down-current of you, so your taste reaches it. It will smell your warmth leaking away fast into water that has never been warm. And then because its hands are gentle - everything gentle that has to work with frond that can whip-crack and slice - it will reach out and with one of those broad, too-long fingers, brush the silt off your shoulder.

Not to be kind. To keep the clearing clear.
