Nothing lives here if you mean it the way the valleys mean it.

No burrows. No nests. No tracks but the scuff of boots that will be gone by nightfall. You were told right — there's no soil for root, no sheltered bark for lichen. The UV comes down too hard and too straight and scrubs it all sterile. Even the prayer ribbons bleach to nothing. A mouse would die here in an hour. A man, in a little longer.

So at first you think the bowl is empty, that the crowded stones are only stones, left by something alive somewhere else.

But stay long enough to have to breathe more slowly — lungful after lungful of that thin, terrible air — and you realize empty isn't the right word. Unoccupied is not uninhabited.

It just isn't inhabited at a scale you start at.

First, there's what lives *in* the rock, because it can't live *on* it.

On the north side of every basalt slab, where you said the water never dries, there's a darkening that isn't wetness. Scrape it and your nail finds nothing. You have to split the stone with a hammer along its glassy quartz veins to see it: a green so dark it looks black until the merciless light hits it right. A film, a stain, a bruise a few millimeters under the surface. Cryptoendoliths. Cyanobacteria and black fungi and lichen's tougher cousins who gave up the partnership and learned to live alone. They don't grow on the rock; they grow inside its skin, where the quartz acts like a window. Light comes through diffused and safe, water comes through pulled by capillary, and the wind can't scour them out. The stone sweats for them. An entire country of old, old light-eaters, living inside their own stained-glass cathedral. If you hold a split stone to your ear you can't hear them, but you can feel them there — because that stone is a little warmer than its neighbors.

Where there's light to eat, there's something that comes to eat the eaters.

In the lenses you described — that palm of water cupped in basalt that will be ice by nightfall — nothing is ever just water. Look close. In the hour when the sun hits it directly, before the wind skins it over with ice again, the water is amber with motes. Not dust. Animals. Tardigrades, the water bears, lumbering through their brief summer between freezes. Rotifers, spinning. They are the true residents here. They have been here longer than any cairn.

They don't live *in spite* of the cycle you described — film, then bead, then bead that swells and runs, then ice — they live *because* of it. This is a tide pool made of weather. In the morning, when you saw that faint, almost inaudible fizz — that soda-water sound of condensation appearing by the thousand — you were hearing their world come into being. The air exhales, it condenses, it gathers. For maybe three hours that little lens is ocean. They unbunch from their tun-state, that little glassy raisin they become when dry, rehydrate, swim, eat, lay an egg that can survive a millennium. And then the shadow moves, or night drops like a blade, and the lens goes glassy again. Tick. The stone contracts. Drip. The icicle falls three inches. They go back to being almost-stone and wait. They can wait longer than the mountain.

You can count them as hundreds. Or you can count them as one thing distributed across a hundred little oceans that appear and vanish every day.

And then, larger.

You called the cairns small houses for the wind, and that is exactly what they are, and what happens when you build enough houses for something — it moves in.

Not a spirit. Nothing with a face. But wind, when you force it through a maze of cold stone and ice, when you braid it and split it into a hundred small winds, stops being weather and becomes behavior.

One tower has a window that whistles only when the air comes from the northwest. Another, two stones over, hums only when the first one whistles, because it steals its draft. A third, taller, capped with ice like bone in amber, collects the hum and turns it into a low chord you feel in your sternum. The whole bowl, which you thought was quiet, is actually listening to itself.

In the afternoon, when the main wind over the lip stalls and gives up its load, the air inside the bowl begins to circulate, slow and deliberate, like water swirling in a basin. Damp air kisses cold stone, becomes droplet. Droplet becomes lens. Lens becomes life. Life becomes dust for the wind. The cairns are lungs. They inhale that exhausted wind and exhale small winds and music. They keep the fizz going.

That is the colonial animal of this place: cairn plus ice plus condensation plus bears. A composite creature forty meters across that inhales once per day, that sweats continuously, that is warmest deep inside its own rocks, that hasn't reached the number of towers it needed and stopped — no. That is how it grows. Not by needing. By offering.

One more person comes gasping up from below with a flat stone split by hand, already light-headed, already burned, already knowing they can't remain. They wedge themselves between two cold towers, turn sideways to fit, add their stone to a third. They think they are leaving a marker. *I was here.*

What actually happens is they are fed on. The bowl takes a little of their heat, their breath's moisture, their sweat. It adds a calcite ring of condensation to its north faces. It fills a cupped stone a little fuller. For a minute, the rotifers spin a little faster.

The human leaves quickly, head pounding. He thinks the place is empty again behind him.

It isn't. It just closes up, content, damp and glittering and crowded — the algae dozing under their quartz windows, the tardigrades curled into glass in their brief ponds, the many small winds pacing from house to house to house, making sure all the doors still work.
