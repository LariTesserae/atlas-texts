Nothing lives there. That's what they wrote on the last inspection sheet and left clipped to the dead pump housing. N O F A U N A. And for the first ten, fifteen minutes you believe it, because you are looking for the wrong kind.

You're looking for something that runs or hides or breathes with a mouth.

In a place this hot, this loud, this sealed, nothing that does that can stay. It was right about the insects. No buzz. No rats. No birds that got lost. The air is soup and the water is iron and the floor will cut your palm when you steady yourself. So at first the pocket seems to have solved the problem of life by refusing it entirely.

Then you stand still long enough that your own sounds get absorbed into the larger sounds, and you start to see that it's not empty, it's just slow, and low, and made of the wrong stuff.

She lives low.

She lives in the negotiation between the plates.

What lives here is not one animal. It's a city that decided to be one thing. The main body is a film. At first you take it for that rust-orange discoloration around the pools and then for a bacterial smear, because it is that too. It is a skin, maybe three, four millimeters thick when it's fed, thinner when it's thirsty, that has grown over five years or fifteen over the black plates where the water runs coolest. It doesn't look alive until you notice it is the only thing in the room that isn't beaded with water. Everything else sweats. She doesn't. She *is* the sweat.

She is matte. She is iron-eating and sulfur-eating, a thermophile mat, orange at the edges where she's young and drying, turning deep, translucent maroon-black toward the center where she dips down into the fracture that is too hot to touch. There, where your hand would recoil, she thickens into something like gristle and holds on. The heat map said 2.7 hot, the kind of hot that lives in stone. That's where she roots. She breathes what you can't. She does not take oxygen, she takes the hiss out of the stone. The water that is being pushed up through the porous basalt comes up through her first. She filters it. That shimmer you see over the black pool isn't just heat, it's her exhale.

You think she's still. Crouch. Wait.

Her rule is Whatever enters stays damp. So she stays damp by eating the damp. Every droplet that falls with that too-loud *tok* hits her and vanishes. Not splashes — is taken. Watch one of the big ceiling drops: it hangs, silver and trembling under the 60-cycle thrum, it falls, it hits her orange margin with a slap, and the skin dimples and closes. No ripples. That water will come out twelve hours later on the underside of the catwalk as mineral. She drinks the room.

Her rule is Whatever is sharp stays sharp. So she keeps the rock sharp.

Where her mat ends, the basalt stays glassy, knife-edged. Where she has been, she etches. She does not round anything. She leaches the soft feldspar out of the young rock and leaves the black teeth. Her edges are exact. If you lift a plate — don't — you would see underneath a white lace of root-hair, each hair tipped with acid, holding the plates six inches apart against gravity, keeping that fresh fault scarp open because she likes the pressure that comes through.

That pressure is her other food. The drone.

Whatever makes sound does not stop. So she has learned to live inside a sound that never stops.

The circulation pumps in the factory above that thrum through your molars — she conducts it. Put your palm very close to the mat without touching and you can feel it humming higher than the concrete. Her whole body is a sounding board stretched over a bowl of hot stone. She doesn't make noise of her own. She lets the pump make it for her. The tick and ping of cooling metal, the sighing crack of a plate shifting a millimeter — those move through her as a twitch. When the instrument column over by the test cylinders swings, very slowly, from 31 to 32 psi, she swells a tiny, invisible fraction. When it falls back, she sighs. That gauge isn't measuring steam anymore. It's measuring her inhalation. It's the only instrument in the factory that still tells the truth.

She has appendages, but you have to unlearn animal to see them.

The sulfur flowers hanging bearded off the catwalk grating — white and yellow stalactites, that dusty growth you took for dead deposit — those are her lungs, her antennae. They are alive at the tip. The active ones weep a clear bead that never falls. The dead ones crumble to sulfur dust you saw on the fern fronds. And the ferns themselves? Those stunted, furred little ferns in the cracks? They are not separate. They are her attempt at leaves. She made them because the light shaft from the hatch told her leaves were a good idea. She spit algae and copper and spore up around a root and coaxed it. They don't sway because there is no wind. They steam gently because she is breathing through them.

And then there is her brood.

Remember the plastic shell of a hard hat, filled with warm water? Crouch there. Look.

The water in it is clearer than any other pool in the room. No rust. No dust motes. Because she filtered it. And floating in it, suspended perfectly in the middle without touching the plastic, is a cluster of perfect spheres. Three, five, seven depending on the season. Each about the size of a pea, each clear with a black dot in the center. Eggs? Cysts? Geodes? They are sharp under the magnifier. Faceted. Whatever is sharp stays sharp, even newborn. They pulse very, very slowly — not on your time. If you watch for a full minute you might convince yourself you saw one turn toward the vibration of your boots on the grating.

If the pumps ever stop, she will die. If the hatch is sealed and the white shaft of day is taken away, her little ferns will die first and then her sulfur tongues. If the water table drops one more meter, her black fracture pool will dry and her heart will cook.

For now she lives like this: damp, sharp, and sounding without end. Not on your map because not big enough. A pocket that taught itself to be a room, and then taught a room to be an animal, and then lies very still when you come down the ladder so you will think there is NO FAUNA here, and leave her alone in her accidental cathedral.
