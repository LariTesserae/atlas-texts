They don't live *in* it. That's the first wrong thought you have to shed, like a layer of frost off your shoulders.

Nothing lives *in* the new mountains the way deer live in a forest. The new mountains are too young, too sharp to be dwelt in. They are knife-sharded, heatless, unsalted, still ringing from being born. Nothing lives in the rock. Everything lives in the negotiation between rock and air, and the negotiation itself is alive.

### The Towers

The Plant — and everyone here calls it The Plant, singular, even though you can count thousands of them if you had the years to count — is the first inhabitant, and the country itself.

It is not a tree. A tree is a solid thing that wants to be taller. This thing wants to be *emptier*.

Each one begins as what the rock-peoples call a soot-boil: a blister of black-green skin over a basalt crack, ankle-high, warm to the touch even at minus thirty. Put your palm on it and you feel it thrumming. Inside, methanogenic fermentation is cooking its own weather. If you cut it — and you do not — steam would sigh out and then freeze instantly and kill it. So you don't.

In a week it is a hut. In a month it is a lighthouse. In a year it has learned the trick. The rind is only a hand-span thick, or a meter where it has to bear weight, made of layered fiber like felted carbon nanotube, waterproof, light-proof, stupidly strong and light. Under that rind is a lattice of living guy-wires, and inside that nothing but conditioned air. Warm air. Wet air. Living air. It sweats.

It drinks light that you cannot see. That dusk-violet at noon that makes your human eyes ache — its skin is blacker than military paint, taking 98% of it. It drinks rock too. At the base each tower has bearded feet of acid-hair that dissolve fresh basalt and schist to milk and suck it up. It grows so fast you can hear it if the wind stops: a green creak, a tightening.

And when it is two, three kilometers tall, its top goes to lace. It shreds itself into filaments as fine as dandelion clock, as fine as hair, and they climb higher still, looking for the last photons above the permanent storm. That lace catches wind and sings.

No single tower is alone. When you brush one, a phosphor flush runs under its dark skin — slow, amber-white, like blood under black ice — and a minute later, a tower a kilometer away blushes back, without having been touched. They pass water through root grafts. They pass alarm the same way.

They are the mountain, now. The rock is just the thing they are holding onto while they learn to be sky.

### The Things That Live On Its Skin And Inside Its Breath

Because the towers are hollow and because they weep constantly inside, they are a whole watershed made vertical. The rock below never gets a lake. The Plant is the lake.

**The rind-borers.** You hear them first, because they never shut up. They are not insects, but they took the same contract: small, hard, communal. The size of your thumb-joint, white as fat, with a head that is all rasp. They live by the millions in the thickness of the rind itself, chewing tunnels, farming fungus on their own shit, and singing to each other to keep the tunnel from collapsing. The chorus is infrasonic — your sternum feels it before your ears do — and it is amplified by the hollow tower like a drum. When a tower is sick, or when a sector of rind has grown too thick and airless, their song changes key. The rock-people listen to that key the way you might listen to a child's cough in the night.

**The frost-grazers.** They look like deer only because anything that walks up a vertical wall in this place needs the same answers. No eyes — useless here. Wide heads with heat-pit arrays like a constellation, splayed feet that are big around as snowshoes, each toe tipped with a curved black keratin hook that can find purchase on the almost-smooth rind. Their coat is not fur but long, hollow, frost-feather quills that they comb off to eat. They walk up. Straight up. Sideways. Sometimes upside down under an outflung arch-root, their bellies to the abyss, lipping lichen-frost from the skin of the tower. When they step, their pads exude a little glue, then release it with a soft *pop* you can hear three towers away, because sound has nothing to hit.

**The blanket-moths.** Those white things you saw riding the updrafts. They are never landing because they cannot take off again. Their bodies are kites. Wingspan is three, four meters, membrane so thin it is translucent, veined like a leaf. They are warm-blooded, or something like it — the towers vent hot air at the tops and the moths live in that fountain forever. They are filter feeders, but there is nothing to filter in this clean air, so they filter snow. And condensing vapor. And spores. And the dead rind-borers that get exhaled out cracks. They breed in mid-air, tumble, couple, and die mid-air, their bodies catching on the tower lace and becoming moss.

**The waiters.** The predators did not get big here. Big is expensive when it is minus forty and dark always. They got long. Think of a snow leopard that was drawn out on a rack — legs like stilts, body narrow, neck absurd, face small and precise. They are white-grey with faint vertical striping that matches water-staining on the towers. They cannot run. There is no flat to run on. They post themselves at drip-points — at the great catch-leaves the size of a house that collect the bucket-sized drops and tip and spill, tip and spill, day after night after day. They crouch, utterly still, for fifteen or twenty hours, metabolisms slowed to nearly nothing, and when a frost-grazer comes to drink the unfrozen water in the leaf-basin, they strike down with that neck. They do not chase. The fall would kill them. The whole ethic of the forest is: to move less is to live longer.

And below all of that: in each leaf-basin, in each drip-lens, in each film of rock-milk condensate, are oceans of bacteria and algae and things that are neither, making the water brownish, making it alive. You can drink it if you are careful. If you are not, it will keep you company for the rest of your short life.

### The People Who Know The Rule

Then there are the people.

Not many. If you walked a hundred kilometers of this range — and you could, because there is so much empty air between trunks — you might meet a band of six. Or signs of a band that left last season.

They are not rugged in the way lowlanders think rugged is. They are thin. Their chests are big, absurdly big, like barrels under their felted plant-fiber coats. Their eyes — the first thing you notice when the oil lamp catches them — are huge, with no white left almost, iris expanded into a deep amber-black that drinks that dusk-light. They were not born like that. Their children are born with eyes like yours, and they become like this if they live.

They do not live long, by your counting. Forty is old. But they think fast and remember perfectly, because mistakes are stored in bodies here.

Their entire culture is a conversation about breach.

**The Unbreakable Law, which they don't even call a law:** Never cut the living skin all the way through.

If you slice clean through, the tower exhales its weather all at once. Its warm wet breath becomes a cloud, then ice, then nothing, and the column that took twenty years to raise goes cold. Its rind-borers go silent. Its drip stops. For years — sometimes a decade — that corridor of air is true high-mountain air, minus sixty with wind, unbreathable, and nothing can cross it. If you killed a tower you made a desert in the sky desert, and everyone nearby moves or dies.

So they learned to ask.

When a young tower is still soft, they place a bone wedge against it and let it grow around the wedge for a month. Then they remove the wedge. There is a blister-hole, lip-healed. Inside is warm, wet, quiet. They will live in that blister for a season. They line it with inner-leaf fiber — soft as wool, smells faintly of ferment — and with fat from frost-grazers, and they hang an oil lamp that is almost dark, because they don't need light anymore.

They do not make villages. A village would be outgrown and then torn apart when its host tower flexed in storm. Instead they make what you found: a braid-bridge left strung between two towers after a band moved on, humming in the wind like a low cello string, glazed in ice. A cairn of that glassy black schist, its edges already dulling, with a cup of soot and fat marking a safe drip-lens below that will be liquid again tomorrow. A bone ladder pinned with living rivets to the side of a tower, where the tower has accepted it and grown little callus rings around each peg.

They move vertically as a matter of course. Down is to the roots where it is warmer, but the air is thin with fungal spores. Up is to the lace where the blanket-moths drop dead. They measure distance in songs — how far can you hear a rind-borer chorus? — and in blushes — how far can you see another tower's night-glow after you slap the one you are on?

Their religion, if you must call it that, is hydrological. They husband drips. Each catch-leaf has a name. Each drip-point has a rhythm — sixteen drips a minute at Aunt's Leaf, thirty at Third Elbow on Tall Mother — and when that rhythm falters, someone climbs for a day to clear the channel with a stone knife and whispered apologies. They keep calendars by noting when a particular tower's top lace freezes solid enough to hold a man's weight. They tell stories by tapping on the rind so the light under the skin ripples.

And at night — which is always — they sit.

You saw one. Perhaps you will be one. On a platform that is not a platform but a stiffened leaf that grew wrong, half a kilometer up a trunk that is itself sixty meters thick, legs hanging over an absence that is ten kilometers deep but looks deeper because there is no haze to tell you where it ends. The wind is screaming through the gap between this tower and the next, making an organ chord. Below you, *click... click... click* of a hundred disparate drips arriving at different stone. Far off, two kilometers and therefore shockingly close, another tower blushes as a grazer steps onto it.

The person next to you is not looking at you. They are listening to the tower you are both inside-but-not-inside, ear pressed to its skin. Hearing whether the rind-borers have changed key.

When they are satisfied, they hum back, a low note in their too-big chest, until the light under the skin hums with them.

The mountain is still being born. The forest is still learning how to be a mountain. And these — the towers, the moths, the deer that walk on walls, the long waiters, the people who know not to cut through — are all of them, together, the thing that is being born.
