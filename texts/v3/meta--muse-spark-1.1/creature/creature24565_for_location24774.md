What lives here now did not arrive here. It was edited here.

At first you think nothing does. Or nothing at the scale your eyes are tuned for. You walk the ring walkway around the Engine and the bowl looks empty the way a cathedral looks empty — too much air per thing, too much distance between meaningful shapes. Warm, wet air that holds rust flecks and seed down and gnat. One dragonfly crossing it for a long, long time.

Then you stay long enough that empty resolves into full.

The first citizen is the Engine itself, and you have to start there because everything else calendars itself off its breath. It is not alive the way an animal is alive. It is alive the way a reef is alive — by hosting so much living that it has become indiscernible from it. The pistons as thick as old trees rise for twenty, thirty seconds, hang — you can hear the grease sigh — and sink again. On the downstroke the heat blooms off it and the whole bowl inhales fog.

If you put your hand on it where the paint has boiled away a century ago, it is not hot. It is mammalian. Too warm to hold, not warm enough to burn, except at the collar where the piston meets the sleeve. There the metal is kept clean. Not by anyone. By the plants. 

That's the second kind of living here, and the most obvious once your eyes adjust: the negotiation.

The vines come in two schools. The ones that tried to go *through* the Engine are not here. You can see their black ghosts — carbonized handprints on the cylinder walls where a tendril got caught twenty years ago and learned. What lives here are the grandchildren of the vines that learned to go *around*. They've learned to make arches. They've learned to leave a piston's width of clearance plus a little extra for shiver. The maintenance crew left a century ago and the pruning is now self-inflicted. Every moving joint is bordered by a perfect, haircut edge where leaf has met slow steel and lost enough times to evolve manners.

Moss is the actual ground. There is no ground. There is grating, and then there is what the grating has become, which is a mattress. You step and it doesn't clang. It gives with a soft *shhhh*. In some places three feet deep, that moss holds the only soil this mountain has ever had: its own dead, and rust dust, and the powdery frass of the beetles that live in it like deer in tall grass. Those beetles are the color of oil on water. If you lift a frond, fifty of them will startle and show you that.

The frogs are the timekeepers. They should not be here. They are lowland frogs, bromeliad frogs, fat and gold-green, carried up as tadpoles in a boot tread or a bird's gut and then never challenged because here is always warm and always wet. Every cupped leaf that can hold two tablespoons of distilled Engine-fog has a frog in it, or eggs. At about three pm when the cloud boils over the parapet lip and becomes rain again, they all start calling at once — a small, glassy, desperate bell chorus — and then stop all at once when the piston hits top-dead-center and the light shifts. They are so loud if you kneel that you cannot hear the wind outside.

Above them are the moths. Concrete-colored, palm-sized, dusty. They have learned to use the Engine as a sun. They land on the black iron to warm their blood and lift only when they're too hot to stay. Their caterpillars eat the ferns — those two-meter ferns whose roots are not in anything — and spin silk that they have laced with iron oxide, so that their cocoons are little rusted thimbles screwed directly into conduit. You could unscrew one if you wanted. The birds know not to eat them; they look exactly like bolt heads.

Swifts cut in over the parapet in the afternoon, screaming, fast as thrown knives, slashing moths out of the still air. They don't live here. They only commute. What lives here full-time clings. The bat things — maybe true bats, maybe something that was true bats seventy years ago — have wings that are more translucent now, veined like leaves. They roost on the underside of the walkways, and when the great piston rises they rise with it, riding it up the three stories and back down, licking the sweat of pure water that beads on the iron. They don't fly in the bowl if they can avoid it. They prefer to ride.

And then — because a hamlet-scale place will have a hamlet somewhere inside it, even if it is a hamlet of one — there is her.

You don't see her unless you stop looking for a person and start looking for something that is wrong in the green in a practical way.

Not a nest of wire insulation. Not a tool left hanging, its handle now a garden.

A path.

In the moss-mattress that has swallowed the grating on the east quadrant, there is a narrowness. A place where the moss is pressed thin, where it has not had time to rebound. It goes from the old control booth — its glass long ago opacified by condensation cycles to a cataract white — to the clean-collared edge of the number two piston, and back.

If you wait on that walkway until the Engine exhales its evening ghost — that straight-up column of warm damp that punches into the cooling sky — she will use it.

She is old in the way that things here are old: not brittle, but polished. Small. You thought the beam-ends were rounded by wind. She is rounded too. Her back is bowed forward from a life of ducking under vines and her legs have a bow from walking grating that bowls in the middle. She wears what the crew wore, or what is left of it: canvas coveralls that have been patched so many times with other canvas and with woven fern fiber that the original color only exists in the armpits. Her hair is white and always wet, tied back with stripped copper wire. Her skin is freckled not with sun but with rust — tiny, permanent brown points at her temples, knuckles, the corners of her mouth, where the air has set.

She doesn't keep the Engine running. It keeps itself running. What she keeps is the agreement.

In one hand she has a hooked knife made from a piece of spring steel. It is not sharp enough to cut metal. In the other, a tin cup, dented, tied to her belt with twine.

She walks her path and trims. Not much. Not like a gardener. She trims the aspirants — the bold new shoots that haven't yet learned not to reach for the moving parts. A flick. Done. The cut piece she does not discard. She drapes it over a nearby pipe where it will drip.

At the clean collar she stops. Every evening. She kneels — painful to watch — and looks at the clearance between vine and piston with the attention of someone reading scripture. If a root has worked its way into a grease channel she works it out with a fingernail. If a beetle river has tried to dam a weep hole with rust paste, she clears it. She never touches the hot iron with bare skin. She knows where not to.

Then she holds her cup under one of the Engine's lower seams where the water pearls. Fog condenses, runs, beads, falls. Fat, irregular drops, distilled from cloud. She catches it and drinks it and then catches more and pours it into the bromeliads that happen to hold frogs that she happens to know individually. Or so it looks.

She doesn't speak to them. There is too much noise from the outside pouring roar. She hums, sometimes, low enough that it vibrates in her chest more than in the air, and the frogs answer.

She sleeps in the control booth where the moss has made a bed up the back wall. The dials are still there under green, their needles frozen mid-thought for a hundred years. She has piled more moss around them. For insulation. For company.

She was not born somewhere else. Or rather, she was, but the mountain has rewritten her. Her lungs expect damp. Her eyes expect green on iron. Her sweat smells a little metallic. If you took her down below the cloud layer she would take days to stop listening for the piston's shift.

She is not lonely in the way people mean when they say a place is lonely. The bowl is crowded. Every leaf has something on it and every something knows her step and doesn't startle anymore. The bat-things let her pass under them without unfolding. The moths land on her shoulders because she is warm.

She is the thing that remembers that this was built. The moss does not remember. The frogs do not remember. The Engine does not remember, it only continues. She remembers just enough to keep the balance: not too hot, not too cold, not too much growth, not too little water. She keeps it because if she stops, the water stops, and if the water stops everything she knows — which is everything that exists within the parapet — stops.

When she dies, something else will take the path. Probably not a person. Maybe the ants will learn a different route. For now, the path exists, pressed thin in the moss, from the white-eyed booth to the breathing black iron and back again, twenty minutes if you don't stop.

She stops.
