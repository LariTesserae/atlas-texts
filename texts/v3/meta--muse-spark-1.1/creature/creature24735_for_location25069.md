What lives there does not live there the way deer live in a forest.

Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. Nothing is born young and dies old. It appears, in place, and then continues.

In the air, the first layer up from the mud, from your ankle to your knee, lives the **Cling.**

It isn't a species. It's a job, filled by a thousand things that gave up being separate. The black ground sweats and the Cling drinks the sweat before it can become a puddle. It's isopods the size of your thumb, but flattened so thin they look like wet scales. It's slugs that have lost the idea of a head and are just a muscular foot dragging a stomach. It's beetle larvae, white and translucent as boiled rice, living inside their own shed skins because molting would be a change. They don't move forward. They move in place — a constant milling, mandibles sanding the half-rotten mattress of leaf and frond into finer and finer paste, and then another set of mouths behind them sanding that paste again. If you kneel you can hear them, a soft, wet rustling like paper being chewed forever. They never get full. The food grows back into their mouths as fast as they take it. The ground they make is themselves.

Above them, from knee to chest, where the proto-trees crowd until they are a single wall, lives the **Holding.**

Everything here learned not to have bark, because bark is armor for a future that never comes. So the trunks are open. They are wet rope and living sponge, and they are covered in other things that decided being a plant was too much work, so they became animals that pretend to be plant.

Frogs. But the name is wrong — frogs jump, and nothing jumps here because there is nowhere to land that isn't already occupied. These are fist-sized lumps of wet leaf-muscle that have grown themselves into the trunk. Their backs are bark-textured, lichen-textured, leaf-vein-textured. Their bellies are fused to the fiber. You only know they are animals because every three seconds, all of them at once, they open a slit and breathe out. A warm puff that smells of cucumber and rot. Then they pull the mist back in. Their external gills never retract — great crimson ferns of exposed blood, waving slowly in air that is barely air — because the air is wet enough to breathe water in. They have no eyes, or their eyes are the exact color of water-beads and never blink. Tadpoles never become different; they just swell inside the puddle on the frog's back until they slide off and stick to the next inch of trunk. Some of the larger ones have things living on *them* — smaller frogs, mites the size of poppy seeds with their own mites.

Interspersed with them are the **Bell Keepers.** The fleshy flowers that never open or close? They aren't flowers. They are throats. Each bell is a sac of warm water and sugar and yeast, and inside each bell lives one fat, blind, shrimp-like thing, pale pink, endlessly stirring. It drinks, it excretes, it stirs, keeping the water from stagnating, keeping the sugar from fermenting too far. The bell feeds it light that isn't quite light. If you tear a bell — and the mat makes a soft tearing sound you will feel in your molars — the Keeper doesn't flee. It continues to stir the air where the water was.

Up higher, head-height to just under the cloud, where the leaves are biggest and already rotting as they unfurl, lives the **Chord.**

You thought it was insects. It is, but not as many as you thought. It's one behavior repeated.

Gnats so small you breathe them and never cough them out, because they were already in the breath you exhaled. Beetles with elytra fused shut, because they will never need to fly more than the six inches from one dripping leaf tip to the next, thudding from frond to frond on the exact same trajectory for their whole lives. Glitter-flies that don't eat, their mouthparts vestigial, their whole purpose to catch the sourceless amber light and throw it back down in a flicker, so the understory never goes completely dark. They rise a foot when the humidity thickens, sink a foot when it thins — a single lung, inhaling and exhaling without ever leaving.

They make the sound. Not a chorus. A chord. Every beetle-rasp is phase-locked to the drip of water four leaves over. Every midge whine is tuned to fill the gap between two frog-breaths. It doesn't get louder when there are more of them. There can't be more of them. The air has a carrying capacity and it was reached before you got here. When one dies, mid-air, its body does not fall. It hangs for a half-second in the thick wet until something smaller has already colonized it and is beating its wings in its place, at the same frequency.

And underneath everything, in the black, ruinously hot basalt that still holds the heat of its making, lives the one that actually lives here.

The **Puddle That Never Drains.**

It's not water. Water moves. This is a film, a microbial mat, a single vast organism the size of the whole low country, matte-black-purple-green, a few microns thick but everywhere — under the mud, through the mud, between the fibers of the proto-trunks, slicking every rock that weeps. It photosynthesizes without sun. It respires without air. It sweats the water out of the rock and then drinks it back before it can run. It is the reason erosion never learned its job. It glues the basalt with a skin of itself. It makes the ground give an inch when you step and hold your footprint afterward like a mold, because it remembers pressure.

Everything else is just an organ it grew to keep itself perfectly level.

When you stand still long enough, it will grow up over the soles of your boots. It will send fine, hairlike filaments up through the seams, not to eat you — it doesn't need to eat you, you are already full of water it can cycle — but to incorporate you into the accounting. To make you drip at the right interval. To make your breathing join the frog-breath, your heat join the rock-heat.

If you stay, you will feel your thoughts begin to do what everything else here does: not progress, but persist. The same thought, at the same temperature, at the same volume, forever.
