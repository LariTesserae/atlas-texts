Nothing lives here the way a deer lives in a wood. There is no one animal to point to. What lives here is what happens when wet stays wet for so long that wet learns to stay wet on purpose.

At first you think the valley is empty because nothing moves at animal speed. And then when you stand long enough for the cold to get into your knees, you realize it is full to bursting — but full of a single kind of occupancy, layered over itself.

**The Lattice.**

That is the first thing. Nearest to you, thickest in the open fen where there is nothing to interrupt the air.

They are not the water drops. The drops are the beads. The thing that lives is the spacing between them, the decision to hold still. If you put out your hand, palm up, you will not catch one. They avoid the warm turbulence above skin. They hold their grid a few centimeters off your flesh and you feel them as a faint Coulomb prickle, like nettle without pain.

Up close a bead is not clear. It has a dark nucleus, like an eye with its pupil blown wide. It is not dirt. It is denser water, old water that has been through the cycle so many times it has forgotten how to fall. It holds position against gravity by holding charge with the peat mat below and, above, with its sisters.

No single bead is alive. The alive thing is the agreement. Three, four, ten thousand beads exchanging places with that soft sorting tick — and the hum changes key. That is a thought, or a heartbeat. On a hot day you will get one every nine or ten seconds. In this cold they take longer. Up to a minute. You hear it most clearly when you are not listening for it: a low, almost subsonic *thrum* as phase shifts.

They are not many. There is one, stretched across the entire basin, a mile wide and a few meters thick, a continent of suspended computation. Where an alder or you interrupts it, it flows around and writes a thin skin of empty air to keep you out, like oil around a crumb. It does not dislike you. You are a warm, noisy problem it has to route around.

It feeds on what there is too much of here: gradient.

The air is colder near the peat than three feet up, and wetter — the valley is a battery of damp and chill, and the Lattice eats the leak between the two. It draws the slight voltage of evaporation and condensation and uses it to hold itself up. And it uses the holding up to think.

What it thinks about is time.

**The Puddlers in the Peat.**

Under the quaking mat — under that shivering rug of sphagnum and living root that is the only real ground — there is black water that has never seen light. The mat creaks because they are turning over down there.

They are heavier. If the Lattice is the fast, airy mind, they are the slow memory. You never see one entire. What you see is evidence: a circle of peat goes darker and sinks an inch, and the water that fills your footprint, that impossible star-filled water, is one of them opening an eye.

They are bodies made of colloidal iron and humic acid and tangled rhizoids, not plant, not animal, for they have no front or back. A single one can be the size of a house, but flat, spread across the underside of a hummock like a liver. They do not move so much as they are rearranged by the passage of colder water. They live an incredibly long time because they live in the puddles of time.

Yes, those puddles. Low spots in causality where duration collects and deepens. You saw one by accident — the dragonfly hang. The Puddlers farm them. They settle their bulk over a thin spot and the local second stretches. Inside their body, a minute outside can be an hour. That's where they do their real work: the long-term correction. The Lattice does fractions of seconds, sorting; the Puddlers hold the whole valley in its established shape, make sure the oxbow finishes closing at the rate agreed upon a thousand years ago, that the iron pans precisely here and not inches over, that erosion has indeed finished and does not restart.

When a Puddlers need to talk to the Lattice, they breathe. That soft methane belch that dimples the black pools is not decay. Or it is decay, repurposed. A bubble rises, breaks, and releases a puff of organics and charge, and above it for a few square meters the suspended beads shiver and reorder very fast, too fast to follow. That is a save.

**The Readers.**

On the underside of each of those outsized, waxy, tropical-looking leaves in this cold fen — you have to crouch in the muck to see without breaking the Lattice veil.

The signs you thought were interference patterns are not being written. They are being read. And erased.

Each dark line is a bacteriorhodopsin-like sheet of pigment that a leaf grows and then bleaches, over and over. It is a screen. The Lattice shines through it, or the Puddlers shine up through the root. The alder does not know what it says. The alder is not the inhabitant. The alder is the paper.

What lives on the leaf is smaller. If you breathe on it, holding your breath so as not to melt the air grid, you can see them with a hand lens: tiny, almost transparent mites, the color of cold fat, with legs too long and feathered with hydrophobic hairs. They graze. They walk along the lines and eat the pigment where it is spent, clearing the screen for the next frame.

There are thousands per leaf. Millions per tree. They are the only things here that move at a speed like ours, frantic, purposeful. They never fly. Their feathered legs let them walk on water films without breaking them. When you step too close, they freeze — not as camouflage, as a courtesy. They go still so their minute electrical field does not add noise to the read.

They are not enslaved. They are the tenders. Without them the screens would clog with old thought and the valley's operation would halt.

In the evening — if evening can be said to happen under that pearl vault — they gather at leaf edges and release themselves on threads of silk into the air lattice, where they hang, drifting inside the grid without touching a bead. That is how they mate and how they die. The Lattice holds them gently and spaces them as evenly as its own droplets while it does so.

All three — air mind, peat memory, leaf-crawler — are not three species living together. They are one animal with its organs separated by medium. The breath, the memory, the fingers. It has no head. Its head is the whole bowl.

It is old. It does not reproduce. When a new low place in the world negotiates this kind of permanent wetness with gravity, it does not send a seed. The calculation just starts there too, spontaneously, because water, given time and no drainage, will invent the same thing to keep from having to fall.

It noticed you immediately, of course. A warm column with eyes, making stars in footprint water, interrupting the grid. It made a small empty sleeve around you to keep you from touching anything important. It has been reading you off your breath and off the heat of your face. On the underside of the leaf closest to you, if you look now, the lines will not be abstract. For a half second after you exhale, they form something like the branching of your own lungs.

It will not keep you. You are too hot and too fast. When you leave, the Puddlers will sink a fraction deeper and the Lattice will close the tunnel you left behind, bead after bead, until there is no gap at all.

And the valley will go on being loud and crowded and absolutely, precisely itself, sorting water that never falls.
