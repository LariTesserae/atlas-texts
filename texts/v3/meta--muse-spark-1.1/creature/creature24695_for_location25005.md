At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the correct first thought, and the City lets you keep it for a long time. It is the size of a courtesy the place extends.

For hours, maybe a day, you walk the hundred-meter avenues and you see only the cranewalkers far off on a roofline, so slow they might be forgetting to move, and the oil-ribbons lifting when the big wheel comes around. You think: fauna, rare, not evolved for here. You think: uninhabited.

Then you start to see where the emptiness is worn differently.

There is a kind of doorway in the worker tenements that is not a doorway at all — the brick there has been leaned against more often than anywhere else. The dark oiled iron of it is polished to a dull mirror, at the height of a shoulder. And on the soft, egg-round curbs outside the bag factory and the dynamo hall and the tram depot that has never had a tram, there are places where the stone is a little more scooped than it should be. Not carved. Sat in.

People who live in a place that erodes always erode the place. People who live in a place that has finished eroding start to be eroded *by* it.

They are there when you turn off your light. Or rather, you learn how to see them after you turn it off, when your eyes give up on wanting lamps to be bright.

He is sitting on the curb outside the silent bag factory. Or she is. It doesn't matter for a while, because they have worn down to a shape that is not strongly one or the other. You almost stepped over him on your first circuit because his coat is the same color as the dustless air and his skin has taken on a little of the same dim red as the rocks themselves.

Come closer. He will not startle. Startling is a kind of work, and work here is already being done everywhere for free.

What lives here is what is left when a human being reaches equilibrium.

They are tall now. Not originally, but they have grown that way after a long time of not stooping, of watching cranewalkers go by far above. Their legs are long and thin with a little extra joint in them you don't want to look at too closely — not like a cranewalker's, not mineral, but compensating. Their feet are very broad, bare, black on the bottom from the warm stone. Soles thick as tires. They don't wear shoes anymore; shoes wear out, and nothing here is permitted to wear out, so shoes were the first thing they lost.

Their skin is the part that took the longest. Newcomers crack. Lips, hands, eyelids, sheets of you peeling away into the zero-humidity air. The ones who stay learn not to sweat. Not to weep. They learn to keep everything inside. Over years, the perchlorate salt that never settles as dust settles on them instead, and into them. It plates there. Their forearms, their cheeks, have a faint hexagonal patterning, like manhole covers seen from far above, that chimes very faintly when the pressure changes. If you put your palm up close to one you can feel it — not heat, but a refusal to cool, just like the rock.

They have bigger eyes than you do. Or the same eyes, more open. The streetlamps are dim by law. A filament gives the light of a match behind frosted glass. That is not enough light for an eye that evolved under a sun. So theirs have gone large and wet and dark, with no white left you can see. They carry no light of their own. They go from pool to pool, from one dying coal of a lamp to the next, and they sit under it because it is a tenth of a degree warmer there, and that matters when you are trying to be the same temperature as everything else forever.

They don't eat. There's no metaphor in that, it's literal. The lunchboxes left in the factories have nothing organic left in them because organic ends. The ones who live here have converted over. In their pockets and sometimes in a small tin box they keep with the reverence you would give medicine, there are perfect cubes of clear mineral — nitrate, salt, a little iron. They put one under the tongue and let it sit for a day. It doesn't dissolve. It just reminds the body that it doesn't need to dissolve anything anymore. Their bellies are flat that isn't hunger. Their breath barely fogs the warm, dry air.

And they don't speak much. Speaking moves air that does not want to move and covers distance that is all open and alone together. When they do, they do it low, almost subvocal, so that you feel it through the rock in your feet before you hear it. The 60-cycle thrum of the city has taught them that listening is done with the whole skeleton.

What they do — because even equilibrium-life does something — is tend. Not fix. Nothing here is broken. Nothing here is permitted to become more broken or more fixed. They tend the tendency.

One will stand inside a dynamo hall the size of a cathedral and watch a flywheel that turns once every nine seconds and has turned once every nine seconds since before maps. He will watch it with his huge dark eyes and his broad feet planted and his chime-skin quiet. If it is turning correctly — and it always is — his watching is a kind of weight, a little confirmation that holds it in place. If a ribbon-life in the oil lifts and flutters and settles wrong, he might breathe on it, not to move it, just to give it a little extra temperature differential to graze on.

Another will walk the avenues, slowly, always slowly, because any avenue, matter how you turn, tends to bring you back to higher ground. That is her work. To walk them and confirm that they still bring you back. She knows every polished lean-spot, every sat-in curb. She does not get lonely because loneliness is a gradient and there are no gradients. She is open and alone together with all the others, spaced as shyly as the factories themselves — one alone in a district, then nothing for half a kilometer, then another.

They don't remember arriving. Or they do but it doesn't accumulate. You cannot make a scratch in the ironstone with a hammer; the mark is gone by the time you look again. Memory is like that too, when you have had it for long enough. The idea of other weather, of water, of night implying day — that chalks off very quickly.

If you stay long enough to learn the rule without being told — that this place has already eroded as far as it can, that it is an old, old system that has found its final shape — you will find a curb that fits you. It will be a little more scooped than it should be, but not much. You will sit. The rock will not cool under you.

After a while a newcomer, high up because he should be cold but is not, will almost trip over you on his first circuit. He will think at first that nothing lives here. And you will let him keep that thought for a long time, because that is the courtesy the place extends.
