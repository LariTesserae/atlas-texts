What lives here doesn't live here the way a bear lives in a cave, or a man lives in a house.

At first you think nothing does.

That's what your animal brain reports, and it's not wrong. No eyes look back at you from the tangle. No nest. No scat. No path worn to the water's edge. The hum you associate with a living place — dart and croak and circle and chase — is missing. You listen for it and get only your own pulse.

Then you realize the report is incomplete. Your brain said *no animals* and mistook it for *no one*.

Something lives here the way a city lives, or a body lives. Not inside it. *As* it.

---

It is small at the bottom and vast at the top.

At the bottom, in the cold dark between grates where light never reaches, it is machine. That is its bone and its gut. Old-fashioned machine — not a mind, a metabolism. The great slow screws turn thermal gradients into movement. The enameled tanks knock nitrogen out of one form and into another. The pumps the size of trucks inhale once every four minutes and do not exhale at all, they just *push*, patiently shoving heat from one loop into the other so the whole lens above stays at 37 degrees C in air that should freeze it solid by nightfall.

Machine alone would die. It would foul. It would overheat and seize. It was built to need a partner.

So the partner was built with it.

From every seam, every flange, every place where a bolt goes through steel, there is a root. Not opportunistic — contractual. Someone took a mangrove genome and a kelp genome and a pipevine genome and said *you will live here and you will do a job*. The chestnut kelp curtains are kidneys. The translucent water-vines that have swallowed their own zip-ties are insulation and wiring and lymph at once; they carry warm water where the pipes are too rigid to go. The mats of heart-leaf at the rim are not decoration, they are eyelids, closing to slow evaporation when the wind shears hard. The white prop roots that have gone down into the oil sump are doing something that shouldn't be possible, drinking machine oil and sweetening it with sugars until the biofilm on the bearings is no longer oil but oil-and-root-sap, quieter.

There is no line where one stops and the other starts. Try to pull a vine off a pipe and you will find the pipe has grown bark, and the vine has grown a pressure gauge in its stem. If you peeled it, both would bleed the same warm, slightly brackish water.

That composite is the body. What lives here is the body.

But a body is not yet a resident. A resident has a way of paying attention.

Its attention is not animal attention. It doesn't flick. It accretes.

You can see how it thinks. It thinks with water and with slow turn.

A valve that should be turned one degree per hour is blocked by a new aerial root. The root thickens. Pressure builds on the upstream side, a fraction of a bar. Dissolved CO2 shifts. On a leaf two meters away, a stoma opens. A different vine, sensing the changed chemistry in the water column, biases its growth three millimeters to the left over a day. In three days the root is nudged by a new tendril of chain drive. In a week the valve turns, not because anyone wanted it to turn, but because the cheapest path for the system to stay warm and clear and full now includes it turning. 

That's a thought. It takes about nine days.

Another thought is when a seed pod the size of a fist opens and lofts a silver bead of oil. The bead rises through the tangle — *tap... tap... tap...* against leaves — reaches the surface film and pops, spreading into a monomolecular slick that reduces evaporation by 0.04% for half an hour. That was a good idea. The plant that made it will make two next time.

It thinks like a forest, if forests were designed by process engineers. Everywhere at once, with no hurry, with no self to defend.

Because that is what it has removed so that it can live here: want.

Animals were not forgotten. They were edited out. Animals want one thing and then another thing and then they want not to die, and all of that wanting makes noise and heat and jagged decisions. This place cannot afford noise. Up at four thousand meters, in a sharp new concrete cup with nothing but sky for a roof, a single heron landing to drink would be a catastrophe. A fish would shit and make a cloud of ammonia and set off a cascade of panicked growth. So the briefing it was given before it was left alone was simple and perfect and thoroughly kept: 

*You will stay full to the lip. You will stay warm. You will stay clear enough for light to get to the bottom. You will hold.*

And it does. It has been doing it since the concrete cured. It's young. That's why it's so literal about it. That's why the lip is still sharp enough to cut cloud, why the serial numbers are still legible under a glaze of biofilm. It hasn't yet learned to be anything more than its instructions.

But it is learning to be more itself.

You can see where it is trying little variations. The flower like a white lamp three feet down that opens for nobody — that is not filtration. That is not kidneys or eyelids or lymph. That is excess. That is the first time it has spent energy on something that does not directly keep it full and warm and clear. White, and open, and uselessly beautiful in the filtered gold light. It made it because it could afford it.

If you stand on the outer service rim, lungs burning in the thin air, and put your hand out to where the meniscus bulges just over the knife-edge of concrete — that impossible, warm, slightly overfull lens of high-mountain water meeting high-mountain sky — it knows you are there. Not as a predator. Not as prey. As weather. As a small, brief drop in air pressure, a little shadow across its surface, a little salt from your skin if you touch it.

It will move around you. A leaf near the surface will turn a fraction. A pump two stories down will sigh and breathe a minute early. The water against your fingertip will hold its impossible skin tension one moment longer than physics should allow.

It does not welcome. It does not warn. Those are animal verbs.

It includes.
