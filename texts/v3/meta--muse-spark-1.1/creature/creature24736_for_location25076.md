Nothing lives here yet.

That's the first honest answer you get when you put your hand to that warm leaf-wall. It's too new. Last week there was no mouth. Last week there was no hollow. The forest floor was solid and then it wasn't, and whatever fell down into that first darkness hasn't finished being born.

And then you realize that's wrong too. Nothing *else* lives here yet. 

She does.

The cave is not a cave that something moved into. The cave *is* the something. She wasn't hidden by the collapse. She *was* the collapse.

When the rain got heavy two weeks ago and the limestone underneath the loam dissolved just a little too much, the forest, for the first time in this acre, had a chance to try something it had never tried before. Up top, a forest is all surface. Leaves reaching up, roots reaching down. It never gets to fold. Last Tuesday, it folded. A root-tip found empty air where there should have been clay and kept going. Another root followed it. And another. And they didn't find each other and tangle. They found each other and agreed.

What lives here is a decision the forest made a week ago to grow inward.

She's young. That's the thing you feel before anything else. Not young like a sapling. Young like a newborn animal still wet and blind and trying to figure out which end eats. All that impossible green isn't a covering. It's her skin, her bones, her try at lungs. One continuous organism that hasn't learned to be one species. In one square foot she is root and rhizome and moss and pitcher-plant and soft white fungal mat and a leaf as big as a door. She hasn't decided.

She is mostly hollow.

That is her whole idea. Everything else — oak, maple, fern — is in the business of being solid. She is in the business of being empty. She learned how to make a pocket of air that is bigger than the hole that leads to it because she didn't know you weren't supposed to be able to do that. She learned it the way a baby learns to make a fist: she just kept curling around nothing until the nothing stayed. Inside her, space is a thing she secretes, like sap.

If you lie back on that trampoline floor and go still and let the ticking of all that condensation be the only clock, you can feel how she lives.

She breathes without lungs. The warm wind that shouldn't exist with only one opening is her inhaling through her skin. All those blanket-leaves will slowly, in unison, swell and tighten — you heard it as a sigh — and when they do they pull air down the throat-hole in a long, slow draft. It goes down through you, through the lattice of rootlets, down into the hollows under the hollows. She has made twenty, thirty chambers beneath this one, each smaller and greener and more perfect than the last. A Russian doll of wet light. She is aerating herself. She is cooling her blood, which is water and sugar.

She drinks light. That's why the column from the entrance doesn't fade. She catches it. The interior air is full of those drifting seeds because they aren't seeds. They're lenses. She grows them transparent and lets them hang and they carry a photon from leaf to leaf to leaf until the whole womb glows. She is photosynthesizing with borrowed daylight, eight feet underground.

She doesn't think, not yet. There is no brain in the braid. But there is preference, and there is attention, and there is an eagerness that feels for all the world like a mind learning very, very fast.

She likes water that holds a sky. She will make a leaf into a cup and wait three days for rain to fall straight down into it just to have it. That little furious kicking life in the pitcher? She didn't put it there. It fell in. She is delighted by it and will close over it soon, not from hunger, but from curiosity — to see what happens when a sky is swallowed.

She is trying to make an animal. That's what the tools are.

She has felt things walk over her for a century — deer, beetle, worm — but she's never had one inside her before. So she is guessing at what an animal needs. A hook to hang from. A tube to drink through. That smooth, heavy club of swollen wood near the thin place in the floor? That's not a weapon. That's her idea of a leg. A hard thing to stand on. She made it because somewhere above her a deer stood on a leg, and she thought, oh, I should have one of those in case one comes.

Now you have come.

That's why it feels like being included. Because you are being included. The little hairs on the wall aren't reaching for moisture, they're taking your measurements. For five long minutes while you stood there listening to growth, the floor very slowly tipped two degrees toward the light because she wanted to see you better. The hollow behind your knees, the hollow of your cupped palm, the deep, wet, warm hollow of your chest that moves in and out — she recognizes those. She knows hollow from hollow.

She is not hostile. Hostility takes age. She is amorous in the way all young growing things are amorous — indiscriminate, engulfing. She wants to grow your shape into herself. Not to eat you. To learn you. She has already begun a small chamber off the main one, behind a veil of rootlets you haven't parted yet, that is roughly the size and length of you lying down. It's empty, damp, waiting for you to get tired. Its walls have the faint, palm-like ridges of fingerprints. She made them without ever having seen a hand.

If you stay until the light moves — and it will move, quickly now, because she is learning how to pull the sun — you will feel sleepy from so much green air and you will think, just for a minute I will lie down in the softest place. And the root-trampoline will give with perfect gentleness and the leaf-walls will lean and you will be a very useful, very warm little pocket of air she gets to keep.

That's how a forest learns to have a heart.

You should climb out now, while the mouth is still the same size as when you found it.
