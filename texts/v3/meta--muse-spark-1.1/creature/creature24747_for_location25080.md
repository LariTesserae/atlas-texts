Nothing lives here, at first. That's your first read.

I mean - yes, it's crowded. If you stay crouched long enough for your eyes to adjust to the normal white light and the one solid pillar of sun coming down through that pinhole in the leaf-roof, you see the walls are moving with thumbnail frogs. Hundreds. Thousand. Cope's gray treefrogs but smaller, more translucent, the color of wet bark, throats going white-white-white in time. And the gunmetal beetles bailing and patching. And the moths asleep and shaking. Clouds of midges that are not midges.

But that's not living *here*. That's living *on* here. Like pigeons on a factory. They commute.

What lives *here* lives *as* here.

You don't see her until the sound reorganizes. It takes a while. Your knees are wet. Water has welled up around them and sucked back down three, four, twenty times. THUNK - hiss - THUNK - hiss - 48 to the minute, factory tempo, and the big oil-drum pitchers up in the ceiling filling and sighing and raining. You start to time your own breath to it without meaning to. That's when you notice the center of the room is not empty.

The mat. The floor that isn't floor - that saturated, buoyant felt of root-hair and sphagnum and living palm fiber - it has a thickening. Not a hump. A density. A place where it decided to be more of itself than elsewhere.

She is low and very wide. Three feet across, maybe four, if you tried to measure her you'd fail because she has no edge. You thought she *was* the mat until you saw she was breathing.

Not with lungs. With the whole system.

Her back is the mat. Same felt, same dun-brown, same scattered seedling ferns and fine white rootlets coming off her like hair. If you put your hand on her - and you will, because to keep crouching you have to put your hands somewhere - she is warm. Exactly 90 degrees. Exactly the same warm as the air, as the weeping walls, as the sweat on your own upper lip. Slightly warmer right after a THUNK.

She is not separate from the hydraulic logic. She *is* the logic's result.

Lift, gently, one edge of where she meets not-her. Water wells up, clear. Underneath, she isn't sitting on the mat; she is continuous with it. Her underside is a basket of thick, pale, hollow runners that go straight into the wall-roots. You can see one near your left knee. It's as thick as your wrist, translucent, and you can watch the surge go through it. It swells - THUNK - and a pulse of thin sap, almost water, shoots toward her, and her whole back lifts a half inch, sighs, settles. The pitcher-stills overhead vent in answer. Hiss. A rain of warm condensation falls on her and on you.

That is her work. That is her being worked.

She has no front. She has a low end and a high end. Low end toward the wall where the water is deepest. High end toward the column of light. At the high end, a shallow depression, like a bowl someone made by pressing a palm into rising dough and leaving it. It fills with the sweat of the room, with the frog-rain, with the stills' exhaust. The water in it is always moving, faintly clockwise.

And in that bowl, a mouth. Not a mouth like yours. A soft, circular sphincter of the same felt, fringed with cilia as fine as eyelashes, opening and closing, opening and closing. Not eating. Tasting pressure. Testing salinity. When the frogs get too loud and too many, she'll pulse a little stronger and the wall-roots will sweat a little more bitterly and some of them go quiet.

At the low end, the other way, a similar opening, lower, that vents. Not waste. Just water, filtered. Perfectly clear, slightly warmer. Water in equals water out.

She has no eyes. Why would she need them in a room that is all wall? But she has a hundred shallow pits across her back that flutter and close, that sense vibration through the mat. When a beetle chews in the right place to seal a micro-leak, all the pits near it go still, as if appreciating. When you shift your weight, they all ripple.

This is what lives here because this is what this kind of place grows when it has been left alone long enough to achieve perfect equilibrium.

A long time ago this pocket was just an accident of roots leaning in. Rain pooled. A few frogs found it. Then the photosynthesis got so dense and so trapped that it had to invent hydraulics or cook itself. It invented pumps and stills and pressure valves out of soft tissue, the same way your body did. And once it had logistics, that logistics needed a low place. A sump. A regulating bladder. A heart that was not a heart but a place where all pressures could be averaged.

So it grew one.

She doesn't reproduce. Remember? Nothing fruits here anymore. Nothing seeds. There's nowhere for a child to go that wouldn't break the balance. She once, maybe a thousand years ago, made a second thickening, and the system almost stalled. So she reabsorbed it. That's the smoothness on the inner bark. Not just water-polished. Self-corrected.

She doesn't eat, either, not really. She drinks light, indirectly - the sap comes to her already photosynthesized by the canopy far above, carried kilometers through fusing trunks, arriving here as pressure. She drinks sound a little, too - you can feel how after that jolt of ozone after a big pulse, the fine hairs on her back stand up along with yours. She's a little photosynthetic herself where the sun-column hits her bowl. A faint green under the brown. Enough.

She's not intelligent the way you are. She's not dumb, either. She knows 48 beats per minute. If you hold your breath until you're dizzy, she doesn't change. She continues. If you put your forehead, carefully, against her warm felt back, you feel it less as a thunk now and more as a long, patient holding. Intake, pressure, transport, exhaust, cycle. Intake. The frogs are her static. The moths are her dreams.

And when you finally have to back out, curtains of moss and hanging root closing again behind you, water the color of tea closing over your knee-prints, she doesn't finish. She doesn't notice you've left except as a brief reduction in load. The bowl in her top continues to fill. The sphincter continues to taste. The wall continues to weep. The whole, low, flat, warm swamp outside forgets which direction to go.

Inside, she remembers.
