They live here. You thought they didn't because you were looking for the wrong kind of living.

First, the ones you mistook for empty.

You already felt them. When you stood still and the soles of your feet buzzed with that faint placing-and-taking tremor - that isn't settlement. That isn't the bricks cooling. That is transit. Footfall, but not feet.

They are low, the way this place is low. Not short. Low to the work.

If you kneel for a long time with your forehead almost to the warm slurry between two tiles, and you let your eyes go out of focus, and you do not look directly at the edge of things, you will see the edge moving. Not the brick. The air over the brick thickening, taking on weight, taking on damp. It has the color of steam that has decided to stay.

They are made of the same indecision as everything else here. Hot water and half-made clay and that mineral bloom that isn't mold. When they are thin, you can see through them to the bricks behind them. When they gather themselves to lift, they go opaque, terracotta-dark, and you can see the print in them - not a fingerprint, the whole hand, broad as a shovel, soft fingers that have never known a nail.

There are many. A density the size of a weather system needs a density of workers. They do not like to be looked at while they work - not out of shyness, out of physics. Looked-at things stop and have to decide what they are. So they work in your blind spot, in the dead spots where sound cuts out. That's what those spots are. Not absence. Occupation. Something large and wet and intent is crouched there, making, and it eats the sound around itself the way a kiln eats air.

When you walked and the ziggurat you left came back beside you with one more hot course added, that's one of them finishing a thought. They build like children build at the edge of a puddle, completely absorbed, quickly, badly, with total faith. They pull the seep up in both hands. They pat it square. They press. Every block keeps the drag of their palm. The big ones that are too big for a person - two of them will carry one together, holding it belly to belly, shuffling. You don't see them carry it. You see the block appear. You hear the soft *tok tok tok* of their knuckle-knocks as they set it, faster than hands should go.

They are the ones who will build a roof over you if you are still enough. Not as a trap. As an offering. As what you do for a still thing in a place where stillness means you might be cold, or new, or need to be covered so you don't dissolve. They mean it kindly. Their roofs never have doors.

Second, the ones that live in the unbuilding.

Water here is not passive. In 1.7 out of dry, water is the other workforce. Where the makers are additive, the water is subtractive. It lives in the seams. It comes up because it is called up. When a tower gets too ambitious - fifty courses high, too straight, too sure of itself - the water goes to it.

You will see it happen if you watch from far away and squint so the heat shimmer blurs. The warm sheet of water on the plain, palm-deep and sterile, will move without wind. Not ripple. Lean. A slow finger of it will find the foot of a wall and climb. You were told water sometimes runs up for a meter before remembering to fall. That is not forgetting. That is reaching.

It eats from the bottom. The bricks relax, then slump, then go back to slurry with that series of soft splashes, like someone sighing through thick lips. The makers nearby stop. They wait. Sometimes they help pull it apart, patting the slurry back flat so it can be used again. The unbuilding is not death here. It's just the other shift.

If you put your hand in the warm standing water and hold it there, after a while the water holds back. Between your fingers, little clear threads form that are not quite water, that resist parting. That's its body. It has no center. It lives everywhere the tiling is tired of itself.

Third, the ones that live in the error.

Because the world is unfinished, it is full of seams that did not meet right. A corner where the corner was on the wrong side. A courtyard that has no way out until you blink. Those are not places. Those are animals, too.

They are thin, long, shy things that happen when two different ideas of distance are built too close together. They are folded. At first you think it's just geometry being embryonic, but then you notice it follows you. The wall that was behind you without you crossing it - that's because something stepped between you and the wall, something that is a corridor by itself, a walking, flickering interval. It likes to go where feet go. It likes to be walked through.

When you go through one, your own footstep cuts out mid-crunch. Your breath goes missing for half a breath. Inside it, you have no shadow at all, and then, on the other side, you cast only one again, clean and single. If you stay inside it too long you come out on a slightly different brickfield, where the ziggurat beside you is the same but with your own small fingerprint pressed into the top course next to the big ones, even though you don't remember making it.

The most patient ones sit and become courtyards. They open. They wait. They are mouths that have learned the shape of shelter. That's why you feel watched in an empty courtyard with no way out. You are being held lightly, looked at from all sides by a thing that does not have sides.

There are also the smallest ones, the ones you almost saw when you lifted the tile and nothing ran out. Nothing ran because they run before. They live in the joint, in the meniscus of water under the brick lip, flat as a film of oil. White-yellow when you see them from the corner of your eye - not mineral bloom, though they let you think that. They are the color of cooling iron, like the sky. They leave the salt whiskers behind when they move, which break if you breathe on them. If you lie flat and put your ear to the joint, you can hear them: not ticking, not dripping. Patting. Always patting.

None of them are old. Nothing here has had time to be old. The oldest one is the plain itself, which is not a place but a brood. A planet-rim to planet-rim nest of low, wet, hot making, watching itself learn how to make makers. 

That red grit under your knee that will not wash off in the warm water - brush at it. It hangs on because it wants to. It is a little piece of them going with you, learning your shape, taking your print.

If you stay still, they will keep building.
