They live here as stone lives.

Not *in* it. Not *on* it. You have to unlearn that distinction or you will never see them.

At first you think the Top is abandoned. That is how slow they are, and how perfectly their bodies have learned the color of their own work. You will drift for an hour over a bone-yellow terrace pitted with rain-drop holes and think: empty. Dead city. Then the current shifts, and the shadowless light changes by a fraction, and you see that one of those pits is breathing.

The ones you notice first are not the builders.

**The Sweepers.**

They come as weather. A cloud detaches from a lee behind a causeway and resolves, when you are too close, into ten thousand individuals. Each one no longer than your thumbnail, if you had a hand that could survive here. They are almost nothing - clear jelly with a gut, and that gut is always full. They have one eye. Not two, which would be for depth. Just one, black and absolute, turned outward. Inside the gut: dust. Fine black powder they have rasped off the old stone.

They never stop. They comb. Their bodies beat with hair-fine frills and they drag a soft mouth across the stonematter and take the thinnest shaving of it - the dead layer, the film that tries to settle out of the endless current. They eat age itself. And they excrete it a little further on as clean. Their clouds are stretched and torn by the current, pulled into ribbons a kilometer long, then they clot again behind a chimney spire, patient. They do not communicate except by being where the stone is dirtiest. If you put your face down close, you could hear them: a faint dry hiss, like a brush on sandstone.

They are not many kinds. They are one kind, repeated until it looks like weather.

Then you see that the terrace you thought was empty is not empty, it is *occupied* by something that takes up the same amount of space as the terrace.

**The Masons.**

They are the reason there is no ground anymore.

When they are still, they are invisible. A door laid flat. An oval, maybe two meters across at their largest, a meter at their smallest. Their backs are stone already - not stone that they carry, stone that they have *become*. New ones are the pearly white you described. Old ones are bone-yellow, and they have a faint pattern on their backs like lichen, except lichen can't live here, that pattern is just more of them being lived on. The oldest ones are that glassy black, and they do not move anymore. You can tell the living ones because their edge lifts.

Around the whole rim, twenty, thirty cilia - not legs, not arms - translucent, thick as ropes, that beat with a slowness that makes you anxious. One stroke every twenty seconds. They don't swim with them. They hold. In this current, holding is everything.

When it is time to work, the Mason lifts its front just enough. Underneath there is no face. There is a softness, a pinkish-gray apron of tissue and, in the middle, a single opening that tightens and loosens. Nothing comes out of it that you can see at first. Then, if you watch long enough to go half-blind with cold, you see the wet gloss appear on the stone under that lip. One millimeter. White. Chemically hot, even in four-degree water. Limestone and silica and a protein glue that will outlast centuries. It lays it down and settles its weight on it, and rocks very slightly, side to side, polishing it flat.

That's all. That's the entire life. Grip. Lift. Exude. Press. Wait.

It will do that same thing in the same square meter for eighty years. Then it will die, its soft belly turning hard last, its black back becoming part of the terrace. You could core through a hundred meters of this place and you would not find bedrock. You would find Masons, in order.

They are not eusocial. They do not take orders. They smell each other. That milky puff you saw - that's a Mason saying *I have broken ground here, the calcium is good,* or *I am rotting, do not build on me,* or *current is coming, hold*. The words are chemistry and they are ripped apart by the jets that tear over the escarpment almost before they are finished. It doesn't matter. Another, a mile downcurrent, will catch a fragment hours later and lay white.

They are sparse because cold will not permit density. There cannot be many mouths this big in water this poor. For a long time there is nothing, then one door, then a long emptiness, then another. The pattern is not random. If you map them from high up - from that glassy knoll you cannot stand on - you see they have made constellations: arcs and long lines that follow the stress fractures in the old black stone, following a logic no single one understands.

**The Lids.**

The scallop-things are not builders. They are something older that the builders have tolerated. A meter across, a perfect disc with a hinge you can only find if you wait for it to open. Most of the time they are shut and look like just another blister in the Yellow. When the current drops for a minute - it does, between surges - they gape.

Inside is not meat. Inside is water that is different water. Colder still, if you can believe it. Loaded with whatever the spires suck up from below. They clap. Open and shut and open. It is not breathing. It's tasting. Sampling the entire ocean over this plateau one mouthful at a time. When something is wrong - a foreign chemistry, a cord of warm water from an unseen vent upcurrent - they clap faster, a stutter that sends a thrum into the stone, and that bone-conducted *hrum* you felt in your teeth spikes just enough to tell every Mason in a hundred meters: *stop laying. Wait.*

And there is one more category, which you only recognize after you have watched the avalanche.

When a section of the black stone gives way - when that slow black hillside finally frees itself and lifts and becomes a slow, silent tumble of plates down into a bowl - the Sweepers vanish. They know not to be under it. The Lids all clamp shut at once. And then they come.

You didn't see where from. The current is too strong for anything to swim against it quickly. But there are suddenly twelve, twenty Masons on that wound, where before there was none. Their cilia beat fast, frantic, a blur for the first time. Their bellies are not white. They are raw, pink, extruding far too fast, laying down thick, sloppy, brilliant white stone over black ruin. They crowd each other. They lay on top of each other's edges. It is the only time you ever see them touch.

This is the animal, really. Not the single door-flap laying its millimeter per decade. The *convergence*. The place has hurt itself, and the place has rushed to close. Individual Masons are cells. What lives here is not in any one of them.

Because if you stay long enough on that high knoll, through a full cycle of dimming and less-dimming, you see it: the Top keeps its shape. Terraces erode on the windward side and grow on the lee. Bowls fill in and new bowls are ground out somewhere else. Causeways wander. The city is a standing wave in rock. No single Mason lives long enough to see it. No single Mason intends it. But the shape persists.

It persists because this shape is the only shape that does not get ripped off the side of the world by the current.

So that is what lives here. A cleaning weather made of eyes. A thousand flat mouths that think they are alone but are a skin. A field of lids that taste the water for trouble. And over them all, a slow, black, mindless body the size of a country that spends its entire existence feeling the water tear at it and healing around the tear, forever, under a sky that is just more water that never ends.
