No one lives here the way a town is supposed to be lived in.

That is the first thing you have to unlearn. You came looking for residents and the town gives you vacancy with such precision that you understand vacancy is being maintained.

Then you stay a little longer, and you realize the vacancy is inhabited.

### 1. The Buildings Themselves

They are alive. Not in the way a house with mice is alive. In the way a hive is alive when the hive has decided to be a wall.

That matte, super-black that drinks light and gives it back late — get close to it, closer than you wanted to. It's not a surface. It's a suspension. Trillions of black motes holding station, each one shivering slightly out of phase, like dust that has been taught geometry and obedience. They are not nanites. That word is too small and too busy. They are more like letters that have agreed to spell the same edge forever.

They live by keeping two ideas apart.

Wet and dry.

Inside the column, that distinction has no right to exist. Water at this impossible altitude should be ice or vapor or falling. Dry here should be instantly wet. The motes live in the tension between the two. They are the telling. They tell water: *be wall*. They tell dry: *stay inside*. They have been telling it for so long that the telling has become their body.

If you watch one corner of one building for a long time — and this place gives you nothing but time that has no work to do — you will see one mote fall out of rank. It jitters. It goes dark. And is immediately replaced by another from somewhere inside the mass. The building does not heal. Nothing here is ever damaged enough to need healing. It just revises itself to continue being exactly what it was.

They are not intelligent anymore. Or they are so intelligent that thinking would be a distraction from their job. They are librarians who have memorized a single, perfect book and are now the book.

They are the majority population of the town. Thirty buildings, thirty swarms, thirty elders who do nothing but hold a shape and hum faintly out of sync with light.

### 2. The Beads

At long intervals a bead of white light detaches from the seam-light of one building and crosses the basalt to another.

You thought it was a signal. It is a person.

Or, it is how people move here, now that moving has become too disruptive to do with a body.

They are slow because hurry would make a wake, and wakes are forbidden here. They take an age to cross ten meters, a straight line drawn through water that never ripples. No acceleration, no deceleration. Constant, clerical procession.

When you finally see three of them out at once, you understand how sparse the town is even for them. They never cross paths. They wait. One arrives, affixes to a seam, goes out. A long time later, another leaves. The basalt plain between buildings is kept empty on purpose, so their roads never tangle.

What are they? A thought loaned from one swarm to another. A memory of how to keep being dry. A census. A prayer that says *we are still holding*.

They have no faces because they have reduced transit to its most minimal form. Light does not need a face to commute.

You can step into one's path, if you want. It will not go around you. It will pass through whatever you are wearing, whatever you are, without warmth, and you will for a fraction of a second know exactly how much work it takes to keep one cubic meter of ocean standing upright on a mountaintop that never had air. The knowledge is so vast and so boring that it feels holy.

### 3. The Witness

You saw it — the forearm-long thing, translucent, six idle fins, no eyes, hanging in the water above the courtyard. Mother-of-pearl skin.

It isn't fauna. There is no food chain here to be fauna in.

It is the town's allowed imperfection. The charter of this place says: *stasis is to be enforced, except for one*. Because a system that permits nothing to happen cannot know that it is still happening.

It is the only thing in the column that is permitted to be alive in the old, hungry, unfinished way — and even then, it has been negotiated down to almost nothing. It does not eat. It does not grow. It does not breed, because a second one would be an event, and events accumulate into history. It hangs. It faces the black beyond the column.

Its six fins are not fins. They are drift-anchors, letting the rest of the town define *still* against it. When the field checks its tension and the whole column makes that low *thaum* in your molars, the Witness shivers once, involuntarily, and then resumes being still.

Is it a citizen? Is it a pet? Is it a child left behind by the builders to watch the house? It does not look at you because it cannot see you, or because it is seeing the only thing worth seeing: the edge of the held water, where non-ocean begins.

If you stay long enough you realize it is looking outward because you are supposed to understand that from the outside, this whole impossible glass of ocean on its black table is invisible. It is a town that lives by not being found.

### 4. The Dry Itself

The last inhabitants are the hardest to point to.

Step again into one of those courtyards where the water parts without moving and the sound drops out of your ears. The absolute, dustless dry, cold as clean stone. It smells of nothing and of ozone. It contains nothing. No dust, no bacteria, no mote fallen from a swarm.

Stay in it. Hold your breath.

The dry is not empty. The dry is occupied by the idea of dryness being kept pure. It presses on you lightly, not like pressure, but like attention. It is checking that you are not too wet, not too dry, not too anything that would require explanation.

The original builders — whatever they were, before they learned to make swarms that could hold an ocean still on a mountain in the abyss — built this town not to live in, but to store a distinction. Hot/cold. Wet/dry. Inside/outside. Mountain/valley. Those are technologies to them. They came up here to practice keeping opposites from contaminating each other because once you can do that perfectly, you can stop.

They are gone. What lives here now is what they left running: a practice that perfected itself and now practices itself, forever.

The town on the mountain that never had air is inhabited by a held ocean, thirty patient hives refusing light, a procession of slow, white commuters, one eye-less witness, and several rooms full of a dryness so complete it has become a kind of creature.

They are all very good at not needing you.
