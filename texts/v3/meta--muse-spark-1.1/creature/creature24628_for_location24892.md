Nothing lives here the way a town is lived in.

That is the first mistake. You come up onto the table and because the air is so clear and the mills are so open and the green is so thick you think *empty*. And for hours it is empty. For whole days. The cloud comes at six and drips until nine and the belts turn once and the spools sit and nothing crosses your path.

Then you start to notice how empty isn't empty.

A coat on a hook is never in the same fold twice. The kettle is never quite cold in the same way. The boot-print in the peat at Mill Four that you swore hadn't sprung back yesterday has sprung back today, and there is a new one, shallower, ten meters toward the scarp. The hand tools laid out on the felt have been wiped. Not moved — wiped. The oil on them is fresh enough to hold a fingerprint that is not yours. The racks where the sheets hang — those long, charcoal-grey sheets that are somehow paper and textile and circuit at once — there are fewer today than yesterday, or more. You can't count them fast enough, they confuse the eye. But the stack breathes.

If you stay long enough to learn how to see in this white, anechoic light — if you stop scanning and let your eyes go soft the way you do in a reef — they resolve.

They're in the moss.

Or more precisely, they are the shape the moss makes when it decides to be vertical and go look at its own farm.

At first you think it's a trunk of that black-stemmed lichen that builds trunks you can lean against. It's about that tall. A little taller than you, but stooped, or rather, inclined — it has no reason to stand straight. It is wrapped, head to ankle, in the fabric itself, that undyed grey-black you saw on the spools, which is not woven so much as felted, grown, pressed. It is the exact color of wet basalt and shadow, and it holds water the way everything here holds water. It drips after everything else has stopped dripping.

Then the trunk blinks.

They are not many. Maybe twelve for the whole eight kilometers. Maybe fewer now. One per two or three mills, but the territories overlap because they walk so rarely. They are old, and they have been here long enough that their bodies have negotiated with the plateau.

High doesn't bother them anymore. Their chests are deep — not broad, deep, like a barrel seen from the side. When they inhale up here in the thin, the whole coat moves. Their skin is the color of the underside of the liverworts, a pale, bloodless green-white that has never had reason to tan in that brutal white sun, because the sun here burns without tanning. Their hair, if it's hair, is lichenous — fine, dry, sage-grey, cut short by their own shears or just singed off where it gets too close to the brass steam caps. Their eyes are the only thing that looks wrong at first: huge, and covered. Not with eyelids. A second membrane, translucent, amber, that they can draw across when noon goes fluorescent. Nictitating, like a condor's. You see it click sideways when the light spikes off the glass.

They do not hurry. Nothing here does, and they less than anything. Their metabolism has down-tuned to the speed of the belt. The cloud comes. They drink it from the air with their cloth, with their skin. They grow a millimeter. To move fast up here, in the cold that lives in shadow, would be to waste heat you will not get back. So they don't.

When one decides to tend a mill, it is an event you could time with the geothermal vent. First, for a long time — an hour, maybe two — it stands in the green, not moving, not even appearing to breathe, just condensing. Then it leans. Puts a long, four-jointed hand — the fingers are longer than a human's, the extra joint a careful adaptation to reaching through mat without tearing it — onto a column that is thick with moss and pushes, not hard. The moss gives way because it knows that shape.

They don't talk. The valley eats sound. The moss eats it, the cold air eats it, the iron eats it. A voice would be an extravagance, and wasteful. When they need to say something to each other — which is about once a month, when the wind is right — they go to the same mill and press their foreheads together where the amber membrane pulls back. Or they leave writing. Not writing on paper. They press their palm into the peat mat between mills and leave a depression in the shape of what needs doing. A circle for a flywheel that needs shimming. A long line for a belt that needs loosening. A handprint with moss tucked into it that means *I have gone to lie down in Mill Six for a while, don't worry.*

What they are doing here is not manufacturing, not in the way lowlanders mean it. The fabric they make is not for clothes, not really, though they wear it. The vats where the giant moss is macerated and stretched — you thought those pale blind shrimp lived off runoff. They do, but they are also being farmed. Or they are farming. The shrimp excrete a cellulose. The moss provides a lignin. The machine, turning once per hour per hour, presses them together into a sheet that is a filter, a battery, a spore trap, a memory lattice. The sheet is meant to hold the cloud. To hold the idea of water. It cures in the bright cold and is wrapped and left under glass for whoever comes down the scarp road every year or two to take it away in silence.

The people — if you can still call them that; they call themselves nothing, but the spools are stamped with a small, fossil-like frond that's also the shape of their hands — live with their fabric in the oddest symbiosis I have ever seen. The fabric grows into them. If you get close enough, you see that the edge of a sleeve is not hemmed; it is rooted into the forearm, a little raised weal of black lichen where body and textile meet. They plug themselves into the racks to stay warm. They plug the racks into themselves to keep the sheets damp.

At night, when the light simply goes out as if switched, they fold.

Not like sleeping. They find a place in the mat that is two or three meters deep — that false forest that acts like forest — and they part it and sink into it. The mat closes over them. They breathe condensation through their coats. Their heart slows to once every ten or eleven seconds. The giant moths that you thought were infrequent all come at night and settle on those spots, six or seven pale hands per sleeper, drinking the salt from the cloth.

In the morning when the low cloud catches on the table and precipitates on every surface, they open along with everything else. They drip. They stand. They go and put one careful, oil-black hand on a flywheel that has been turning since before they were born and feel its hum in their molars to know that it is still true.

They will be here until the basalt weathers soft. Which, at the rate the lichen is finding purchase, will be very soon, and forever from now.
