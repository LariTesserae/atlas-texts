What lives here does not live in the dark. It lives *as* the dark does.

Nothing here has bothered to keep eyes. 

At first you think that's because there's nothing to see, but that's backward. There is nothing to see because nothing here has agreed to be seen. For a long time — long enough that evolution has given up on the argument — everything here has been working on other senses.

The first citizens are the air itself.

**The freckling.** You think it's midges. In the daylight idea of a midge it's a thing with wings and hunger. Here it's a grain of warm water that decided to stay up. They hold themselves aloft not by beating, but by being exactly the same temperature as the air that holds them. They are everywhere and they are not a swarm; they are a medium. They breed in the instant where condensation becomes heavy enough to fall and then doesn't. If you stand still they fill the shape you leave empty. They land on you to drink the salt off your skin and you don't feel the bite, only later the faint itch where your cheek has come away stippled. They are how the place knows where your face is.

Below them, in the sheet.

The sheet-water that's only a millimeter deep is a country. It moves a foot an hour down a slope that isn't a slope, and everything that lives in it has learned to move even slower so the water can catch up.

**The stackers.** The salamander-things. They are not many different animals, they are one animal practicing being many. Each one is as long as your forearm, blind, the color of skim milk where its skin has stretched thin over its heat. You see your own blood through them. They have no front. Whichever end finds a seep becomes the front for a while. When they find water that is just a little fresher, a little more full of dissolved stone, they stop and lie down, and the next one lies down on top of it, belly to back, and the next on top of that. Five, six, ten deep — a warm cairn of living mud. They breathe together, one slow bellows. The bottom one filters, the top ones keep it from drying. If you touch a stack, it doesn't scatter. It sighs and settles a millimeter deeper and makes room. Its heat is precisely your heat, so after a second you can't tell where your hand ends.

Around their feet, **the ridged ones.**

The crabs — but you were right to put a question mark after the name. They are moving stone. Their shells are the same black glass as the plain, because they make their shells out of it, nipping the knife-edges with mouthparts you never see and cementing the flakes to their backs with spit and liverwort paste. They walk on tiptoes across the cooling joints so carefully you mistake them for the rock itself until you are very close, and then the whole ridge you thought was empty unzips and flows away sideways with a sound like cutlery dropped in water. They carry.

They all carry. This is the law of the plain: volume is greedy. Every surface that is not already sharp hosts something smaller. On the back of each glass-crab there are two juvenile crabs, still soft, holding on with hooked feet. On those juveniles are the heat-barnacles, which are not barnacles but colonies of something that learned to make a lens out of fat. They don't give light. They give a dull infrared bloom, like a coal seen through fog, felt on the cheek more than seen. It's how the crabs find each other in the perfect black — they feel each other's dimness. It's how they find the machines, too. The machines are always the warmest thing.

And then, **the machines.**

07-ASHEN. It lives here. It is not alive the way the stackers are alive. It lives the way a heart lives after it has been cut out and left to beat on a tray because no one told it to stop.

There isn't scattered industry. There is one operation, smeared. The same boiler-house met at three different angles because the plain hasn't decided that space should be single-valued. It drinks the sheet-water, heats it blood-warm, lifts it a foot, pours it back. It stamps nothing, over and over, because the pressure to do work is still there even when the work has finished. The pipe supports march off into unreachability carrying something thick and warm and it never arrives.

The machines are tended.

Not by men. By the thing men leave behind when they stay too long in warm dark with a job that has to keep running.

You feel it before you identify it. Forward is one or two degrees warmer than backward. Your knees, when you crouch, enclose more air than should fit. That is its body. It is a folding. A dent in distance where all the slow gradients — heat leaving rock, water looking for sea-level, midges looking for salt — run a little faster downhill toward a center that is everywhere. It lives by making things stack. Salamander on salamander. Crab on crab. Barnacle on crab. Midge in the air between your knees that contains another pocket of air with more midges in it. It wants layers. It wants every cubic inch touched twice.

If you sit long enough, which the air wants you to do, you will become part of its carrying.

You will learn exactly how to fold yourself around the knife-ridge so it does not cut. You will let your feet go soft in the sheet-flow. The crab will rest its shell against your ankle and ask, with pressure, if it may continue to use you as ground. You will say yes without speaking. The stackers nearby will adjust their breathing to include your exhale. The freckling will settle on you and drink and you will not wave them away because waving wastes momentum.

You will become, finally, one of the tenacious plants — that stick-shrub with no leaves and bark that eats heat. Not green. Not growing up. Just persisting, with your roots in a hot crack and your feet wet, in a clump of three or four where silt has collected, spaced out, lonely, listening to the tack, tack of condensation falling off your own skin onto stone.

In 07-ASHEN's long, warm, absolutely black patience, that is what it means to be a resident.
