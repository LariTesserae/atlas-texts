Nothing lives here, at first.

That's what it tells you from the rim. Too deep, too cold, too loud. TheBASALT is too young and the light is too wrong. A few ravens passing high over refuse it. Even the fox that left that single line of prints along the snow-softened lip would not come down.

And then you climb down, and the ground gives.

Because of course something lives here. The cut was never empty. It was just waiting to be the right kind of empty.

What lives here is one thing, mostly.

You think it is moss. That is not wrong, but it is not right in the way that calling an ocean water is not right. It is a continuous thing, a body. It began in the corners where the condensation never dried - a spore on a boot, a spore in the corrugated drain - and spread because nothing told it it couldn't. Sphagnum on top, quick and shaggy and thirsty, making that foot-deep mattress that sighs under your boot. Underneath, sheet moss, black-green and dense as felt, and underneath that the liverwort, varnishing the basalt itself. Hair moss hanging from every rebar in beards.

It is all one mat. There are no borders. If you pull at a piece on the north wall, the piece on the south terrace shivers. The cables, the chain-link, the rusted flatbed - they are not overgrown, they are incorporated. The mat grew around them and then through them. It grew up the iron ladders and made them soft.

It is not fast. In ten winters it has done what a forest does in a hundred. It doesn't need to be fast. The cold keeps everything else out. It knows the cut better than the cut knows itself. It knows where water will bead before the water does. When the air pressure drops and the whole cut weeps at once you can see it - the whole body drinking. The beads fatten, merge, and are gone.

It has learned to farm light. This is its trick. 

Where one of those caged yellow work lamps still burns - humming, flyspecked - the moss below is not just greener, it is a different animal. Thicker, turgid, fronded up toward the bulb like hands. Each light has a perfect halo of vigor around it, a bright circle in the dim, and then a penumbra where the growth thins again. The burned-out lamps have a ghost circle, a little graveyard of the tallest, palest shoots that got leggy reaching for a sun that died. The thing that lives here tends those circles. It leans.

Underneath it all, where you are standing on the trampoline of it, it is warm. Not warm like a room. Warm like a compost pile, like a breath. A degree, two degrees above the air that is sinking off your own mouth. It is warmer because it is eating itself. The bottom layer is always dying and becoming peat, and that slow fermentation makes its tiny heat. Stand still long enough and the cold comes up through your boots, but also, faintly, that other warmth comes up after it. If you put your hand down and push through the top shag you find dark, black, almost hot to the touch.

That is the main resident. But a body that large has inhabitants of its own.

There are things that live only in the circles of yellow light and die a few feet outside them. A pale, almost translucent slug the length of your thumbnail, no eyes - it doesn't need them. It grazes on the freshest tips under the bulbs and leaves a faint silver snail-track that freezes and unfreezes. There are springtails, in their millions, white as frost, jumping in the moss when you disturb it and vanishing again like static. Rove beetles, tiny and black, armored against the damp. You won't see them unless you kneel and stare until your knees go numb, and then you see that the moss is crawling, all of it, at a scale just beneath your noticing.

And there is something that lives in the roar.

Not in spite of it. In it. Because the fans in the big duct have never stopped turning, the whole cut has one constant: vibration. A low thunder that lives in the rock and in your molars. The water beads on the moss shiver with it. The thing that lives here has learned to use it.

It doesn't hear. Nothing that lives here can afford to hear. Its rhizoids are deaf.

But when the fans hit a certain resonance - when the grid that still powers them surges or sags somewhere far away - the duct shudders and the tone drops half a step. And in that half step, all across the cut, the mature capsules on the hair moss open at once. A puff, a barely visible spore cloud for just a second in the yellow light, before the moving air catches it and sends it tumbling down the length of the canyon to snag somewhere else wet and start again. It uses the machine as its wind, as its season.

You understand that is why it hasn't spread up and out. It could have. The spores make it to the rim. But above the lip there is real wind that scatters them, and real sun that burns them, and ground that is not always weeping. Here, in this sunken barge of basalt, the air is still except for the one river of air it has been given, and that river is reliable. It stays where it is kept.

If you go up to the watchman's cabin, tipped two degrees off true, you see how it negotiates with what we left. The door is gone. Inside, the cabin is full of moss to the windowsills. Not like a room with moss in it - like a mouth. The moss has made a bed around the rusted stove, a curtain over the empty window. And in the center of the room, where the stove is just warm enough from some residual heat in the pipe that leads back into the hill to never quite freeze, there is a knot of it that is thicker, greener, almost knotted like muscle. That is its heart, if you want a word. That is where it would be if you had to point.

It does not think. It does not want. It is patient in the way cold things are patient. It knows the lights will burn out one by one. It knows the fans will one day seize. When that happens the air will go still and the cold will settle even deeper and the halos around the bulbs will fade. It will thin. It will shrink back to the corners that weep without help. That is fine. It was not here before the cut. It will not grieve the cut filling in.

For now it lives here complete, a single green slowness that covers almost a half-mile of broken ground, trampolining gently under your weight, sighing faintly where you step, drinking your sinking breath along with all the other water.

If you stay too long shouting over the roar you start to feel that you are not standing on it.

You are standing in it.
