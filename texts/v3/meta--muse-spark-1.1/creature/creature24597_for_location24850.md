They live there so slowly you think at first that they don't.

You sit in the water column between two houses — between what you thought were houses — and you watch an archway of kelp-stipe that has been coaxed into a perfect, low ogive over what must be a door. It is heavy and wrong. Trees don't make doors. And you wait to see the maker walk through it.

Nothing walks. The light shifts. The char school turns. A fist-shell snail goes by at what, for here, counts as a sprint. The shrimp click. A frog bongs somewhere behind you and the sound goes through your ribs and through the arch and the arch hums back.

Then you realize the newt sitting on the threshold is not a newt.

Not only a newt. He — she — it — has been there the whole hour you've been watching. Her back was to you. You took her for part of the doorframe, because she is the color of doorframe and shadow, black and deep green-brown, and because she was not moving. Nothing here moves fast enough to be caught moving.

She moves now. She has to. You are too close and your bubbles have been hitting the curtain of milfoil over her lintel.

It happens by degrees. A throat opens. Closes. Opens again slower. A forelimb that you thought was a root-knot detaches from the wall. It has four fingers, absurdly long, white at the tips where the black pigment thins. Webbing to the second knuckle, then gone — trimmed? chewed? Worn away by a life of handling fiber. The nails are thick and left long and then shaped to points with stone. They are her knives.

The rest of her comes with it. She peels herself off the house.

About as tall as a child of seven, if a child of seven were built for depth instead of height. Broad, low, chest like a barrel. No neck. Head sunk in shoulders, mouth huge and downcurved, seemingly permanently open just a crack. They don't close it because they don't breathe with it down here most of the time; they breathe through skin and through gill frills that they keep neat and tucked under frilled collars of their own living tissue. The frills are the color of fresh blood in the green light, and they flutter even when she is still.

Her eyes are very large, lidless, the iris a pale, tarnished gold. They are old eyes. They have to be. Everything here is old, because cold water and low food makes no other kind.

This is one of the builders.

There are not many. Twenty, thirty in this tarn-generation. The houses you counted are not all occupied — some are fallow, being rested, being let to forget they were houses and remember they were plants for a while. Some are nurseries. Some are bells.

They are not frogs, though they borrow a frog's song. They are not newts, though they let you make that mistake — it is safer. If you were to write a word for them in a field guide you would put them somewhere near the white mountain salamanders, neotenic, kept juvenile by the cold, but then you would cross it out and write *people*, because that is what they are. A relict people. Maybe the tarn kept them when the ice left. Maybe they kept the tarn.

Their skin is black on the back, smoke-grey on the belly. In better light, up close, you see it is actually translucent; you can see the bleach-white bones of those long fingers, the darker sacks of lungs that they only use half the year. They have hair, in a way — no — they have a fine felt of filamentous green that grows on them as it grows on the snails, and they farm it. They let the shrimp pick it clean, and then let it grow back.

You finally see her other hand. She was working the whole time.

The rope of the archway — the green, slick weed-rope that was never more than a temporary lashing until the tree held itself — has snapped in the slow current. She is holding the two ends. She has been holding them perhaps since yesterday, waiting for the stipe to soften enough to re-tie. She uses her teeth. Her teeth are not pointed. They are wide, square, like a beaver's, except there are only four on the top and two on the bottom, and they are bone-white against the black lip. She rasps the fiber to rough it, rasps her own finger as well without flinching, because a little blood in the water brings shrimp and shrimp bring cleaning.

She will not finish while you watch. She can't. At her speed, a knot takes a day. A proper graft takes a season. She is early forties in your years, but in hers she is barely adult.

If you stay until evening, when the light comes sideways and makes colonnades, you will hear them talk to each other.

Not with throat. They have voices — the bongs, the hoots you thought were the houses are them, using the houses as amplifiers. One puts its head into the resonance of a hollow bull-rush and lets out that huge *bong* and the whole street hears it. The water shakes. Far across the tarn, over by the snowmelt lip where the water is even colder, another house answers. Slower. Lower. 

What they say is mostly housekeeping. *I am holding. Come hold with me. The lung is ripe. Keep the fish out of the silt-bed. The stone has moved.* Sometimes, when the shrimp clouds are thickest and the town is loudest, they all bong at once at different pitches and the tarn itself rings like a bell struck underwater, and that is how they remember they are a town and not thirty animals alone in a weed-thicket.

Twice in a life, a builder will make the ascent.

You saw the steps cut into the trunks — chipped stone brought from above water. Only above water is there hard stone. They have to go up and get it. They hate it. Above is bright and dry and hot even when it is not hot and it has no weight to hold you. They go at night in late summer, when the air is almost as cold as the water and the alpine moths are on the scree. They haul themselves with those too-long white hands up over the basalt lip, their gill frills folding shut and secreting a film to keep from collapsing, their lungs — those unused bags — opening with a painful gasp. Up there they are blind and slow and venerable, a black wet thing glistening among lichen. They find a flake of basalt already nearly knife-shaped and worry it until it comes free. They drag it down. One more tool that will last ten generations because nothing breaks fast here and nothing rots fast.

They used to build boats. You can still see one, now a house. Two rushes bent and bound into a hull until they grew into a hull, meant to float on the surface skin. They don't know why they stopped. No predator, no famine. They just stopped wanting to be on top of the tarn instead of inside it. The last boat greened over and rooted.

If you are very patient, or if one of them decides you are not a threat, you will be allowed into a lung-room. They will gesture — not point, but sweep both arms up, which means *up and in* — and you must kick gently not to stir the silt. Inside the dome, the top glitters. Air. A silver bell held by leaves, trembling, smelling of crushed green and cold iron and a little of frog spawn. The builder will push you up until your face breaks into it, and you will gasp plant-air that has never been breathed before, made that morning by photosynthesis and trapped, pure oxygen and water vapor. On the inner wall of the dome, in the half inch where water is not water and air is not air, you will see their tadpoles hanging, rows of them, black commas breathing both, their gold eyes already open.

She will be in the water below you, looking up at you, her four front teeth visible, which is a smile if you let it be, holding the doorway for you the way she has held that rope since yesterday.
