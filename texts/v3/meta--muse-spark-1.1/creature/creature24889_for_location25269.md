They are not people who live on a mountain.

They are people who live *with* a mountain that is still being born, and it has made them strange.

At first you think you won't see anyone. For a place so dense - where every cubic foot is pipe or mesh or corrugated wall or steam-slick stair - there is so little movement. You hear the city before you ever see its citizens: the long exhale of a press, the single clank of a chain carrying ore, the tick-tick-tick of condensation falling from the nets into the funnels. The human sound is missing. Then you learn how to look for them.

They have learned to not move until they need to.

The first one you see is standing perfectly still in the shadow of a cistern, waiting for it to move. He is tall and thin because everyone is tall and thin here - long lungs for air that is fifteen thousand feet thin, long limbs to radiate heat in an oven where the rock itself is 40 degrees Celsius. His skin is brick-red, not from sun but from the water. Everyone's skin is. The warm mineral drip that they drink and wash in leaves a faint precipitate of iron and sulfur on everything. After a year your forearms, your neck, your scalp under close-cropped hair are dyed that same rust-black. They look like they were carved out of the basalt and left just a little unfinished.

They don't hurry. They can't. 

There is a word they use for outsiders: *tight*. Tight lungs, tight joints, tight schedules. You come here trying to walk at sea-level speed and you faint in three steps, slick with sweat that won't evaporate in the saturated air, gasping in heat that should not exist at this altitude. They walk like they are underwater. One economical step. Pause to breathe. One more. When they carry something heavy, two of them carry it on a sling, taking a minute per stair, resting their weight on the warm rock between lifts. They speak the same way - a few words, low and spaced, because speaking is work. Their conversations are mostly standing next to each other looking at the same valve, nodding.

That is their genius. Patience as a technology.

This isn't a young city. The children you see are old-eyed. Kids here don't run. They learn before they learn to talk how to read rock-heat with the back of the hand, how to ring the dew off a polymer net without tearing it, how to leave slack. Their toys are oversized washers, slip-joints, loops of chain they leave deliberately loose. They are taught to build a tower and then push it until it leans and then shim it, not to keep it straight.

Because the rock is alive, in the slow way that matters here.

Down in the lowest cuts, where the heat is almost unbearable and the air shimmers before it's even noon, there are men and women who are not miners. They are Midwives. That's what they call themselves. They sit for hours on warm stone that deforms under their boots over the course of a shift, watching the rebar they drove yesterday now being swallowed. They don't drill so much as they negotiate. They know that if you bolt a factory floor tight to this uplift, the uplift will take it. So they mount everything on skids. On rollers. On hinges that can take ten degrees of new tilt a year. The entire city is a raft floating on a sea that is hardening into continent. Their central prayer, stenciled on every cistern in dripping white paint, is an engineering spec: LEAVE SLACK.

So their bodies have slack in them, too. Loose clothes, always - long, dark, polymer-weave aprons and trousers that never dry, that are cut huge to catch any draft between buildings. Sandals strapped over salt-caked socks, because boots would cook your feet. Hair shaved or bound tight. Big hats made from condensation mesh itself that double as personal collectors.

They harvest twice. In the morning, they harvest water. You see them at 4 a.m., when the cold clouds slam into the hot mountain and the whole city weeps. They walk the catwalks with long hooked poles, plucking the black nets so the beads run. They shake the chain railings. They wring out laundry that was hung out to *collect* water, not to dry. They guide the trickle into runnels with a flick of a neoprene flap. No one owns water here; they own the maintenance of a cistern. A family is a group of people who keep the same sump from silting with bacterial slime.

And in the afternoon, when the water has steamed away, they harvest the other crop.

In the dark under the dye works, in the warm sumps, there is a felt that grows - not plant, not quite anything. A bacterial mat that feeds on the mineral water, orange and white and slick. And eating it are the natives. The original inhabitants. Pillbugs the size of a hand, blind, pale as boiled fat, with segmented backs that click softly against concrete. They came up with the uplift, locked in the rock for a million years and now awake in the damp dark. The people tend them the way other cultures tend bees. Not with love. With respect. A Tender will crouch for an hour, scraping felt with a wooden blade to give them fresh pasture, then with a practiced flip, scoop one that is gravid and fat and drop it into a bucket where it will be rendered down for the only lubricant that doesn't flash off in this heat. The bucket clicks all night.

Otherwise? No pets. No birds that land. High above, always too high, are the soarers - something like condors, something like kites made of ash, that ride the heat column off the whole range and never come down. The people don't look up at them. They are irrelevant.

If you ask one of them why they live here, impossibly high, hot, unfinished - she will not understand the question. She will look out over the layer-cake of lights at night, each bulb haloed in mist, reflecting off glassy, unweathered basalt that has never seen a river, and she will see a perfectly sensible place. A place that is still soft enough to be bargained with. Everywhere else in the world, she would tell you if she had the breath, the mountains are already dead. Cold, eroded, finished. Here, you can still leave a handprint in the world and the world will leave one back, warm, in you.
