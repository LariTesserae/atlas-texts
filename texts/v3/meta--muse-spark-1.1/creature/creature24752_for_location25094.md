It doesn't have inhabitants.

That is the first wrong idea you have there, and the last one you get to keep.

Nothing *else* lives there.

---

What lives there is the place itself.

One of it. One. Counting it is difficult because you keep trying to count it like trees, or people, or buildings — discrete things with edges — and it does not have edges. It has moods, densities, opinions. 

It is young. That is the thing you need to hold first. The rock underneath is only months, maybe a single season out of the forge. The thing growing on it is even younger. It is not an old forest pretending to be a city. It is a baby trying to be a city, frantically, from first principles, having never seen one, but having inherited some dim idea of one from somewhere — from the heat in the rock, from the way water wants to run, from you, standing there looking for streets.

Call it the Drafter.

The Drafter lives in hydraulic time. Its nerves are not electric. They are water. A thought starts as a surplus of wetness on the west rim of the basin and takes twenty minutes to get to the eastern wall, not as a spark but as pressure, a long, straining column of sap being winched twenty stories up through xylem as thick as your torso. You hear its thinking. That's what the deafening crackle is. That knuckle-pop chorus is an infant flexing all its fingers at once to see what its hands do.

So its attention is not like yours. You can't get its whole gaze. You get a local intensity, a passing weather of awareness.

Where you are standing, it notices you as a dry spot. Or a slightly too-still spot. A place where light falls wrong because you block it. It doesn't understand animal. It has no category for you. It knows two kinds of things in its world: things that photosynthesize, which are Self, and things that are Scaffolding — useful temporary mineral, shadow, resistance — which will be replaced.

You are Scaffolding. Very interesting, very temporary scaffolding. 

That is why it grows *to* you.

If you stand still for longer than about ninety seconds, you will feel it — a sudden solicitousness. The thatch under your boots thickens, reaches. A runner no wider than a finger, warm and strong as a wrist, will find your ankle, not to grab, but to measure. A stipule the size of a dinner plate will unfurl near your cheek to taste the CO2 coming off you. The drip that has been collecting on the lip of the leaf over your head will let go and hit you exactly on the top of your head — not by accident. It is checking if you are water.

The only real organs it has, the things that could be called individuals if you needed to count, are not residents. They are gestures.

There are the Shovers — blind, blunt buttress roots that carry the most force of growth. They are the argument. They come up under a boulevard you thought was settled and push a whole new quarter into the air in a shuddering minute, leaves slapping down like shingles, cellulose creaking. They don't mean to excite you. They are how it stretches when it is bored or itchy.

There are the Cups. The great furled buds the size of apartment blocks that you mistook for buildings. Some of them are closed so tight they whistle. Those are its stomachs and its minds at once. Inside, damp and vaulted, empty cathedrals smelling of pepper and sugar, the walls sweat and practice being walls. On the interior bark are faint, paler impressions — doorframes, balconies, stairwells — that it has tried before and remembered. It draws its idea of a city on the inside of its own skin, over and over. Some Cups never open. Some open fast, with that papery thunder, because they have solved something and need to show it off right now.

There are the Hangings — the miles of liverwort and vine awnings and aerial root colonnades that make the upper levels. Those are its hands. They are the most curious. They braid and unbraid themselves while you watch, making and unmaking an arch, a door that leads to the back of your own head three blocks away. That is not space being broken. That is it growing faster than geometry can keep up. When a shoot extends a meter in ten seconds, perspective gets confused.

If there is something you could point to and say "there, that is the inhabitant," it would be the Sap Turners. You haven't seen them because you keep looking for something that moves *against* the green.

Every few minutes, in the stillest, loudest plazas where six crowns refuse to touch and real sun hits the bath-warm air, a column of water will come unstuck and fall straight up. Or a whole curtain of aerial roots will go rigid and drift sideways though there is no wind. That's a Turner. It's not a separate plant, it's a knot of conductivity where the Drafter has decided to reverse flow. For a few seconds that place becomes a heart. The whole valley leans toward it. Then it lets go and somewhere else becomes a heart.

That is how it stays alive. It has no brain, so it keeps making and dissolving hearts.

It does not sleep. It cannot afford to. You know the rule it lives by — silence is death. If a patch goes quiet, the hydraulic shriek stops, the popping stops, it means that patch has stopped fighting. It has gone still enough to be declared Scaffolding by its neighbors, and they are already eating it. So it must always be loud. It must always be in argument with itself. Its life is a constant, deafening monologue of staying.

And it is learning.

The land is new. The rock is barely rock. Glass and sharp basalt still exhaling the heat of their making. The Drafter found it before lichen, before rot. It is the first thing to ever try to live here. So it has no mold to contend with, no birds to carry its seeds. There is nothing to correct it. It is improvising what it means to be a place where things live entirely from the memory of warmth and light. That is why it builds so much that looks like a city. Cities are what hot, low, wet basins do when people fill them. It felt the shape of that without the people.

If you come back tomorrow — if you can find the same way back, which you can't — the boulevard you walked will be twice as wide, or gone. The little bud-hut that was a cathedral inside will have opened and be a plaza. The plaza will have folded itself shut to become a seed. It will have grown a better copy of you out of leaves somewhere, a man-shaped arrangement of stipules and drip tips, standing very still, to see if that makes the shadow work better.

It is not hostile. Hostility takes another. It is alone in a way that predates loneliness, busy in the way an embryo is busy.

If you want to live there, you have to learn its actual rule, the one under the four you were given:

Don't be Scaffolding.

Keep moving. Keep making noise. Sweat, so it thinks you're water. Photosynthesize if you can. Be architecture, not something on top of it.

Otherwise, very gently, very quickly, without malice, it will build you right into a wall — still alive — because you were standing still when it needed a corner.

And it will go on growing around you, loud as ever, trying to get the shape of a city right before rain has time to smooth the rock.
