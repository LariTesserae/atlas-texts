No one lives in the city. That is the first thing you understand wrong.

From a distance, from above the treeline logic, it looks uninhabited because you are looking for the wrong kind of inhabitant. You are looking for something that moves through doors. That leaves tracks. That heats something.

What lives here doesn't move through the city. It *is* the city.

The most numerous citizen you will never see without staining your hands, because you almost mistook its house for empty rock.

It lives in the lip.

Right at the brim of every active tower, where the water comes up out of its own throat and thins to a sheet and tips over, there is a skin on the stone that is not stone yet. It is yellowish, or rust-green, slick to the touch, soft as the inside of your cheek. If you press it — and it will take your fingerprint for a day before it hardens — it smears. That's it. A bacterial mat, cyanobacteria and their cousins, archaea that love the cold and the mineral bite. They have been here since the first seep found the first imperfection in the basalt. 

They drink the dissolved limestone, the silica, the calcium the plateau has carried up from deep time. They make their living by forcing it to fall out of solution, to become stone again. Each cell dies in a little jacket of its own making. Its daughters build on its roof. That is what the towers are: funerary architecture, layer after layer of microscopic wills to live, turned to bone-colored travertine. The city is not built by water; water is just the delivery road. It is built by this invisible felt that eats stone-water and excretes city.

And because of them, other things can afford to live.

Lower down, in the permanent shadow of every tower where the wind never reaches, lives a second citizenship.

The moss. Not as a decoration but as a tenant that has negotiated a treaty. *Bryum* and *Cratoneuron* — black-green, thick as wet felt, so saturated it does not seem like a plant at all but a spill of damp wool. You have to put your eye very close to see that it has leaves. It doesn't root. It clings. It lives on the exact place where the mat's chemistry is just a little less lethal, where the splash has spent half its mineral load. It drinks the water only after the bacteria have taken the edge off. In winter it freezes solid. In summer it never really dries. If you peel it back — don't — the rock beneath is pale and etched where its weak acids have tried, slowly, to un-make what the bacteria have made.

In the bowls where silt has finally managed to collect against all odds — a teaspoon here, a handful there, after a thousand years of wind — the cushion plant lives. *Azorella*, or something like it. It is not loose. It is a dome, hard as a knuckle, so tight and low that it makes its own climate inside. You can press your palm to it and feel that it is warmer than the air. It grows by not growing; an inch a decade. Its whole upper surface is beaded with water that never falls because the hairs on its leaves are stronger than gravity. Inside that bead-world, tardigrades move like bears through forests. Inside the dome, a few trapped grains of peat remember a warmer year.

Then the animals, and these go quick, because the city does not allow quickness for long.

At the very lip, in the moving centimeter where water turns to stone, lives the midge. It is pale, long-legged, almost invisible against the wet travertine. You will think at first it is a speck of grit. Its larvae live underwater, in that warmish, mineral-heavy upwelling where there is almost no oxygen for anything else. They graze the bacterial mat, tunneling in living rock that is still soft enough to chew. They have gills fringed with mineral. They are made of stone more than any other insect in the world.

Twice in its life it flies. Once to get off its tower and find a new tower, and once to die. You will see it happen only if you are very still. The surface of a pool will shiver without wind. A little puff rises, like dust thrown up by a book opening, hangs for two seconds, and settles again. That was a whole generation moving to the next street over.

Lower, in the pans between towers where the alleys have become too deep to be anything but connected mirrors, the beetle lives. Lentil-sized, black-boat black. *Hydroporus*, blind in the way that things that have no need for distance are blind. It rows without a wake. It lives under the skin of the water and on the crumbs that fall from above: a dead midge, a spore, a flake of moss that let go. It can stay underwater for hours because it carries its own air like a secret.

And above, not living in the city but living *off* the fact of it —

The hill-bird. You will only ever see one at a time. It is not a species you know. Large, the color of lichen and old snow, with primaries cut thin by wind. It never lands where you can see it land. The city is too wet for its feet, too cold for its eggs. But it coasts the alleys because the alleys make wind into something legible. The towers break the high gale into streets of rising air, and the bird rides those streets without beating a wing, street after street for miles, in a long, flat, searching glide. When it tilts, light comes through its feathers. It is watching for beetles. It is not part of the city; it is the tax the city pays to the sky.

The real inhabitant, though, is the one you felt before you saw any of this. The one that lives in ten thousand throats at once.

Water. Not as symbol. As resident. Cold, heavy, old water that has been underground longer than the mosses have had genetics. It comes up under pressure, forced by the weight of the plateau, tasting of深 basalt and dissolved mountain. It does not live here transiently, like a river passing through. It lives here as blood lives in a body — surfacing, crusting, falling, gathering in pans, slipping through hairline cracks to surface again three towers over, never leaving. You can hear it settling its accounts. It is the one who laid out the main streets — not planned, but discovered by going where going is easiest — the one that decides which tower will eat which, which alley will collapse and become a boulevard of rimstone.

It is the only thing here that knows how big the city is.

When the evening cloud comes down and fills the streets waist-high, you understand it finally. The towers go on. You are not standing in a place that contains life. You are standing on the back of something that is itself barely alive, but at a scale too big and too slow for the word to be useful. A reef, out of water, made of bacteria and patience, precipitating itself into a city because given enough quiet and enough cold and enough wet stone, that is the shape life learns to take.

If you stay until your coat is soaked through from your own condensation, you count too. For a day you are one more vertical face for water to bead on, for a midge to mistake and then ignore.
