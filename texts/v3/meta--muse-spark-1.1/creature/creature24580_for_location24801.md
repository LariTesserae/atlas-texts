The ones who live here don't look like they arrived. They look like they were grown as a second opinion on what the Lattice was trying to do.

First, you hear them before you see them, because everything here is sound. In the constant thunder-crackle of the pods, they have stopped shouting. The old human voice doesn't carry right in air this thin and loud. So what you hear is their work: the soft *thok* of a bare foot on a runner-path, the creak of a living walkway taking weight and giving it back a half-second later. A low knock on cork-glass to check thickness. A hum, not vocal - nasal, chest-resonant - that vibrates at the frequency the big trunks groan at when the wind hits 20 knots. They sing to the trees on the tree's own channel.

When one does come into view between the trunks, your brain has to recalibrate.

They are very tall and they are not heavy. Four meters if they stood straight, which they never do; they move in a permanent low crouch, long-toed, wide-footed, weight distributed for the bounce. In this gravity, in this altitude, with this little food, heaviness was edited out generations back. Level 2.8 doesn't leave much of the old human chassis intact if you stay long enough.

Water parameter 0.1 makes a religion out of containment. They don't sweat. They don't cry. Their skin is not skin anymore. It's a matte, close-cropped felt the color of the Lattice itself - paper-grey, bone, old lichen - made of hollow insulating hairs that trap a dead layer of air and seal it. If you got close enough you'd see no pores. Their faces are masked by that felt except for eyes - huge, night-adapted, that deep-blue dusk is bright to them - covered in a second, transparent lid that never blinks. They wear clothes even though they don't need to for heat; they wear long, overlapping shawls woven from shed Lattice aerogel, because clothing is an extra vapor barrier, and because leaving your field leaky is rude.

They leak field anyway. You can't live under the violet discharge without learning to carry it. Along the forearms, the backs of hands, the line of the spine where the felt parts, there are raised black combs, keratin and copper, identical in principle to the bell-pods. Cheaper versions. They groom them in the mornings, scraping off accumulated dust and dead insects with tools made of stone.

Because that's what they eat. Or what lets them eat.

The Lattice doesn't give you fruit. It gives you charge and dust. It combs the high wind for nitrates and interplanetary grit and makes it into faint sugars in its glass marrow. The people cannot tap the trunk directly - it would kill a house. So they farm the things that farm the tree.

The wool-tumbleweeds - they have a name for them, *sulk* - are herded. Small, heavy-bodied, silent, rolling on hooked legs through the filament felt, chewing shed aerogel hair and balling it into dense protein knots in their guts. The people pick them like berries, crack their dry husks, eat the soft black curd inside while it still holds a few milliliters of metabolic water. It tastes like ozone and book glue. They never waste the husk - woven, burned for scent.

The mouthless lizards, *parchment-bellies*, are not eaten. They are coaxed. A child will lie for an hour with his bare chest-comb pressed to an old flute in the buttress, letting the tree's standing voltage pour through his body into the lizard on the other side, until the lizard swells and goes docile. Then the child carries it to a new flute that has been growing too fast and lets it drain there. They are electric shepherds. Current as husbandry.

And the kites - god, the kites dominate their sky and their stories. A sheet of living mylar a meter across, no head, just a nerve-knot and a drinking needle, riding static fields between the ancient individuals, never touching stone. When they steal charge from a pod the air rips and they scream, a metallic ionization shriek that echoes across the pan for a mile. The people chart their migration the way you would chart weather. A storm of kites means the sky-field is high, means you don't go out on the runners. A lone kite turning its needle toward you and hovering means you are leaking water and it smells it. The children are taught to stand still then, to make their felt lie flat, to diminuendo their own field until the kite loses interest. If you don't, it will try to drink you, and the burn leaves a beautiful fractal scar that never quite loses its static.

That is their town. Not many. You said density 0.8 and you meant it. Maybe three hundred of them in this one great shallow bowl between four dome mountains that used to be mountains. Each family holds a Tree. Not owns - holds, in the sense of holding a note. Because the Tree is your house and your pantry and your road and your ancestor.

You are born in a hollow coaxed with a growth-key. The needle is not a tool, it's a lineage object. Dark glass, warm to touch, it whispers molecular origami when you press it into the living cork-glass flesh and tell it what kind of emptiness you need. Window where light is useful. A lip where convection is useful. And the Lattice obeys slowly, over months. You grow up watching your bedroom get bigger around you, the walls sealing their white hairs behind you as you sleep. When you die, they do not bury you - there is no soil, no water to waste on rot. They take you to the root-pan, to the central anchor where the Tree drills thirty meters into salt-stung basalt, and they open your suit and weave you into the felt. Your remaining water, your manganese, your nitrate, your tiny charge. The Tree remembers you as a slight thickening in the flute.

Their day is not measured in sun. The sun here is always behind another planet, that permanent dim blue. Evening is when the charge differential gets too big and the bell-pods discharge, all of them at once, a silent tide of Saint Elmo's violet running down ten billion filaments. That is when they stop working. Everyone stands on their bouncing runner-paths or in their buttress doorways and watches the forest that is not a forest turn into a nervous system. The kites flare white in it and shriek, joyfully, and the wool-insects freeze.

That is their prayer. Not words. They all lift their combed forearms slightly to share the load. You can see their silhouettes against the flicker, tall, grey, felted, connected by the living roads, a sparse but unbroken circuit between the ancient individuals. They glow faintly themselves, from the dust on their shawls.

If you stand too long in the open pan between two giants, the pods will turn toward you, because you are warm and you leak. The people do too. If you stand still in one of their towns, after a minute you will feel the runner-path under you adjust its tension, a question. After five, a neighbor you can't see will hum low on the bass note, asking if you are lost. They are not curious the way lowland humans are. They are attuned. In a place that has finished eroding, where nothing sharp is left, where there is not enough water to waste on speech, life becomes what tends. 

They tend.
