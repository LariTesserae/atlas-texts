They live spread out, like the wiregrass does.

That is the first thing you notice when you stay long enough to see a person twice. No one here clumps. Even lovers walk a little apart, it seems at first, until you understand it is courtesy. In a place this loud you need to see the other person's face.

They are not tall. Or rather, you think they are not tall until you stand next to one, and you realize they are — long in the chest, broad in the shoulder — but they carry themselves low. A habit from ducking under troughs your whole life. From keeping your center over wet concrete. Everybody here has the same careful gait, knees a little bent, weight forward, boots planted. Their boots are terrible, beautiful things — felt lined, rubber over steel, then crusted over with a quarter inch of the white-yellow travertine until they weigh five pounds each and have to be cracked off the foot at night with a hammer. They leave white footprints that fill slowly with water.

You would think deaf people would be loud. They aren't.

The children are born hearing. That is the thing that breaks you a little. For five, six years they are screaming and bright-eyed and then they go quiet, not all at once, but like someone turning a radio dial down over a winter. By twelve they have that intent, lipped stare. By thirty most of them can't hear the hawk when it crosses. By fifty they hear only the deep flywheel note in their own bones.

So they never built a culture around sound. They built it around sight and maintenance.

Their hands are their first language. Not a formal sign language they teach in schools — though there is one, old, elaborate, drilled into every child before the wax goes in — but a personal, constant pidgin. The men and women who work the pumping houses have hands that never stop even when they aren't talking; checking a belt, flicking a droplet off a seep wall, wiping mineral from an eye. When they talk to you, they touch you. Two fingers on your forearm means *look at me*. A palm up means *I am going to show you*. They talk while holding tools, while holding a child, while carrying a bucket of shrimp casings. They talk with their whole arms.

Because you cannot shout over the water for sixteen hours. You would bleed your throat. So the city talks with big things.

Every crossing, every aque-duct joint, has a frame for signs. In the morning the sign-men go out — usually older workers whose hearing is gone entirely and who therefore like the high walkways — with buckets of whitewash and tar and repaint the day. WHITE CIRCLE with a black arrow: The east dam in this bowl is close to overtopping, don't go that way. RED BAR across a trough: This wheel is being de-scaled, the water will be hot. A child's lost boot is drawn in crude cartoon. Everything important is drawn big, high contrast, because the ground-cloud condenses on your eyelashes and the air itself is dripping.

At night they use lights. Every apartment, every shop, has a pair of bulbs in a wire cage outside the door, one white, one red, salvaged from God knows what. White on means *I am up, you can come in, I am willing to share quiet if I have it.* Red on means *not today* or *sleeping before early shift* or *sick of you*. After ten at night whole valleys go dark except for this red-white constellation reflecting in the standing water, blinking slowly when someone changes their mind. Young people court by walking the long way around to see if a certain window has gone white.

They work at slowness. That is their religion, although they would not call it that.

No one here makes anything that gets exported. No one can remember what the factories were supposed to fabricate originally. Pumps? Perhaps. Now their job titles are verbs for staying even. De-scalers, with long iron scrapers, go through the troughs on suspended catwalks and knock the travertine off before it closes the channel. It grows back a quarter inch a year with a sound like fingernails. Levellers, with transits made of water-filled tubes, walk the bowls checking that the city is sinking evenly. It is — a millimeter a year, everywhere, the whole high table settling under its own wet weight. Belt-men oil the flywheels that have been turning since before their grandparents' grandparents were assigned here. These are not jobs that end. You do them until your hands know the span of the dam by feel and then you show a younger person. The pride is not in finishing but in not needing to be told.

They look, after a while, like the rock.

Not literally — but the water leaves a mark. Their hair is cut short because otherwise it mats with lime. Their eyelashes are pale. Where the seep sprays off a chute, you can see them open their mouths slightly to taste if the mineral balance has shifted. They develop a permanent faint cough, not from sickness but from the aerosolized sulfur and old oil in the mist. Their skin, especially the forearms, gets a tiny stipple of white, deposits that won't wash off. The old ones are almost marbled. Mothers scrape their children's knees carefully, because if you let a scab crust over with travertine it scars in a raised white welt.

They are unbelievably gentle with small, fragile things.

The only things that grow easily here aside from algae are the eyeless shrimp in the troughs and the children. So the children are raised communally and preciously. You see them in packs, always in the afternoons, when the low sun turns the entire city into glitter from those impossible hovering droplets. They go out with nets and pails and sieves, scraping shrimp from the concrete, but also just watching the seep walls. Their game — the only game everyone knows — is to catch a hovering drop before it decides.

They hold their breath. A bead of water, perfect, trembling a millimeter off the limestone, reflecting the whole orange sky. It hangs there because the air is charged and saturated and patient. The child puts a fingertip underneath, doesn't touch, waits. Sometimes the drop sinks and falls. Sometimes it lifts. When it finally loses nerve and drops onto their skin, they scream with delight, a silent scream you can see in the cold because you can't hear it anyway over the roar.

The adults have their version of the same game. They hunt quiet.

Because the roar — the total, physical, low roar of water moving over concrete for fifteen miles — interferes with itself, it creates standing nodes of cancellation. Pockets of absolute, ringing silence the size of a kitchen table or a small bedroom, drifting a foot or two a year as the stone builds up. The adults mark each one they find with a chalk circle, dated. Inside the circle the sound of the city simply stops. No hum. You can hear your own heart. You can hear the thin air rasping.

And in that silence, they talk.

They drag chairs into it. Two, three at most. They sit knee to knee and speak out loud, in voices atrophied from disuse, cracked and hoarse, about the ordinary things — my boy's tooth, the belt on Wheel 4, whether to paint the sign white again. They say it quietly, as if loud words would break the bubble. When the node moves or collapses — and they do — they leave the chalk ring and the chairs exactly where they are, memorials. The city is patterned with hundreds of empty, perfectly preserved chalk rings around rusted chairs, sitting in deafening noise, waiting for quiet to come back, which it never does in the same place.

That's how they remember. Not by building monuments, but by leaving a place for the world to be quiet in, long enough for a conversation.

And then going back to work, bent-kneed, eyes on the waterline, hand on your forearm to say *look at me, I'm here, we still have to keep this bowl from becoming a lake.*
