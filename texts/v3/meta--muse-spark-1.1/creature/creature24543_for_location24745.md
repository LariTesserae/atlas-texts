They live here because nothing else was ever going to.

Not first the fish. The fish are the newest tenants.

The first ones to live here are the ones that were poured in with it.

The pumps are alive. That is not a metaphor you learn to stop making — it is a fact you learn to live around. Each circulation engine is the size of a hill town, bolted to Elevation Zero with bolts as thick as redwoods. They inhale. They hold the whole sea in their chests for a second and then shove it out again. And the vibration of that — that low, sternum-pitched thrum you feel before you hear — is their territory mark. They have been running without anyone telling them to stop for longer than any fish has been alive. Barnacles have grown over their intake grills until filter plates flutter with them. Anemones the size of barn doors have rooted over their outflow lips, opening and closing in the false tide. Inside them, in the dark thermal-exchange mazes where a man could get lost for days, live the Eel-Mothers.

You hear them before you see them. A chittering spill. The drainage pipes that lace the underside of the floor were made for water, not animals, but eels don't care about intended use. They are black, as long as your arm, then as long as your body, then longer, breeding in the warm side of the heat exchangers. They pour out of an overflow at shift-change — the old shift that no longer comes — in a slick ribbon, thousands at a time, and hunt the open water between pillars. They never touch the floor if they can help it. The floor belongs to the pumps.

Above them, in the open water proper, live the ones who ride.

The Silvers were seeded. Someone, in the planning stage when this place was still a diagram, wrote PROTEIN BUFFER / SELF-SUSTAINING in a margin, and dumped a billion fry into the filling water. They went feral in a perfect place to go feral. There is nothing to stop them. No shore to beach on, no predators that can corner them except those that learned to live *like* a pillar. So now they are the weather inside the weather. A school comes and it doesn't come — it arrives. The water goes white-silver for minutes, then an hour, a wall of flank and flank and eye moving with a precision that looks like engineering. Sound stops inside them. The engine hum cuts out, replaced by the soft roar of a million tails. They turn between two pillars three kilometers away and the whole mass flexes like a muscle. When they turn, the light changes.

And where there is that much meat moving, there are mouths that learned to follow.

The Not-Whales. They are not whales, they do not breathe, but they took the job opening for "whale." They are filterers, thirty, forty meters long, with backs plated in the same orange-white bacterial mat that grows on the pillars, so at first you think a piece of structure is moving. They are slow in the way only truly huge things in truly clear water can look slow — you misjudge them until one passes a pillar and you realize it covered two kilometers while you blinked. Their heads are blunt shovels, scarred white where they have scraped maintenance catwalks trying to scrape the barnacle fields off girders. Their calls are not songs; they are structural pings that set the rebar in the pillars humming in sympathy. They travel alone, usually. Sometimes a calf follows so close it looks like a remora, until you see its own scars.

The ones who cling live on the vertical.

You only see them when you get close enough to a pillar to hold it, and then you realize the pillar was never empty. The concrete is fur.

Every bolt-hole, every seam left by formwork, every half-moon bite mark from the boring machine is occupied. Pale crabs with carapaces the size of satellite dishes, blind, their legs jointed to exactly fit the standard-issue service ladder rung — evolution found the gauge. They farm. They scrape the black-green algae that only grows on the windward edge of a pillar where a current hits hard enough, and they herd it, and they fight over it with slow, hydraulic shoving matches that can last days. Below them, door-sized anemones open whenever a current-river passes, filtering what the crabs miss. Above them, the kelp-things — stunted, angry triffids that found a home in cable trays — let their fronds stream twenty meters into open water to snag shrimp.

The shrimp are everywhere and nowhere — clouds of glass that click. That high tinnitus under everything is them talking to each other, millions of tiny snaps, saying *here, here, here*.

And then — because when you build a place so big and leave it running, you don't just get animals — there are the people who stayed.

They were never supposed to live here. They were supposed to service here. In shifts. Now they are the long-term residents of the service galleries *inside* the pillars. You will find a doorway a hundred meters tall, sealed against pressure that's no longer an issue, and inside, dry. Light from emergency strips that have never gone out. Air that smells like cold concrete and ozone and fish oil. They are tall and thin, or short and broad, depending on which pillar clan you meet — all of them barrel-chested with modified lungs, all of them with eyes that have learned to judge distance in kilometers of clear water. They paint their bodies with bacterial orange so the Not-Whales won't hit them.

They don't swim. They ride, same as everything else. They hook onto the maintenance barges that still run on their old loops, cable to cable, pillar to pillar, grinding along on schedules no one remembers writing. Some of them have learned to ride the current-rivers without a barge at all — they wear winged pressure suits stitched from pump gaskets and kelp fiber and they step off a gantry and fall sideways for ten kilometers, arms out, until the next pillar grows out of the haze.

They don't remember what the Reservoir is for. Some say it was a battery, to hold cool. Some say it was an ark that forgot to load. Some say it was a mold and the world above is the casting that was lifted off and left. What they agree on is this: you don't own a pillar, you tend it. You scrape the bad scale before it spalls. You keep the intake caves clear. You listen for changes in the thrum.

Because beneath everything — pumps and eels and silvers and blind crabs and long-lunged people — there is the last resident. The one that lives everywhere at once.

The building itself.

The concrete is still curing. In a structure this big, it will be curing for a thousand years. It is still shrinking, still settling, still ticking and pinging as it contracts in the warehouse cold. You can put your hand on a buttress and feel it, under the algae and the barnacle grit: that unnervingly cool, smooth, young surface, humming not just with engines and water but with its own slow, mineral life. Hairline cracks form and close. Rebar sweats.

It hasn't learned to be silent yet, because it hasn't finished being born.
