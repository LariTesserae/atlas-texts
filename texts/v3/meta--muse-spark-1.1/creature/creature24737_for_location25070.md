What lives here doesn't live *in* it. It lives *on* it. Clinging, low, and flat against it. Because there is nothing underneath.

**The carpet itself is the oldest resident.**

Not a single plant but a single agreement to be a plant together.

The base is moss — not the soft stuff of forest floors, but a black-green, iron-hard prostrate moss, *Grimmia* and *Andreaea* types, that has never known shade. It grows by microns. Its leaves are so tight and waxy they hold that morning rime-weep directly in their bodies. If you peel it — don't — you find it is not rooted; there is no soil. It has glued itself to the basalt with rhizoids like wire, and underneath, in the millimeter of damp it has made by existing, it has manufactured its own dirt from its own dead. It is an inch thick and maybe a hundred years old.

Above that, stitched into it, are the cushions. *Androsace, Arenaria, Saxifraga* — the true endurers. They don't grow up, they grow in. Each is a hard dome, a tight bun of a thousand tiny rosettes pressed together so tight that the interior of the cushion is ten or fifteen degrees warmer than the air around it. Inside, dead leaves compost, insects shelter, humidity stays. They are greenhouses the size of a dinner plate, with their own weather. Right now, at noon, the pink and white stars are open. Each flower is a parabolic antenna aimed directly at the sun, its ovary tucked deep in the fur of the cushion where it will not freeze. They smell like nothing — there isn't enough air to carry scent. They flower because the light tells them to, not because it is warm.

Painted over all of it are lichens — orange *Xanthoria*, white *Lecanora* — they aren't plants at all. They are a bargain between fungus and algae that has decided this young, angry rock is an okay place to wait out a few centuries. They live on light and breath, nothing else.

That is the first layer. The producer. It covers maybe forty percent of the bowl's palm. The rest is still just black rock learning how to be ground.

**What lives for three hours a day.**

At 11:00, when the black walls have thrown enough heat back down into the trap that the air inside stops hurting and becomes merely thin, the carpet hatches.

The bumblebees — *Bombus superbus*, if you want a name — are here all the time, but you don't see them until now. They overwinter frozen in cavities under the moss, their blood laced with glycerol so it doesn't shard. When the sun hits, they shiver. They have to. They uncouple their flight muscles and vibrate for twenty minutes to get their thorax up to 30 degrees Celsius while everything else is at 2. Then, absurdly huge and loud, they lift. Their fur is longer than any lowland bee's, tawny and black, depolarized light gets trapped in it. They don't buzz so much as throb. You feel it in your molars. They move from star-flower to star-flower with the economy of someone who has calculated the exact cost of every second aloft. One flower, two, rest. One flower, two, rest. If a cloud crosses, they simply drop into the moss and wait, dead again.

Smaller than them — the beads. Flightless darkling beetles, wing cases fused smooth. They have been walking the same square meter of moss for their entire lives. They eat detritus and droppings and each other's shed skins. They are so black they disappear when the sun goes behind the wall.

Above them, a living dust. The midge column. A hundred males holding station in the only warm air in Tibet, dancing without going anywhere. When a female goes up, the column briefly knots.

**What lives here without breathing the way you do.**

The birds. They are almost too heavy to be here and you can tell. 

Twenty or thirty Tibetan snowfinches and Brandt's mountain finches, *Leucosticte*, in a mixed flock. They are spheres. They have minimized every extremity — beak short, tail short, legs feathered to the toes. They are puffed constantly to trap air. Their blood is thicker than yours; their hemoglobin grabs oxygen like a fist. They do not have songs — song is territorial, song is expensive, song needs air to shape. They have that one glass bead call: *pink.* Meaning: here. *pink.* Meaning: food. *pink.* Meaning: hawk.

They are not visitors. Their bones are from here. They nested a month ago in the same deep moss pockets where the bees winter, lining burrows with white feather down and yak hair carried up from 2,000 meters below, one hair at a time. The chicks, now, are five beaks in a wool-lined hole. They don't peep. They gape.

Underneath them — under everything — are the pikas. *Ochotona curzoniae* or something close. If the moss is the body, they are its nervous system. You almost never see a whole one; you see assurance: a tuft of grass vanishing backwards into a cushion, a neat haypile of dried saxifrage stalks no bigger than your fist, stacked and curing in a wind-protected angle between basalt blocks. Ten thousand haypiles across ninety meters. An entire invisible agriculture. They are caching summer because winter here is not a season, it is the default state that summer briefly interrupts. Their ears are small not to keep warm, but to keep from freezing off.

**What lives here by eating the others and then leaving.**

Someone has walked through this pantry and left scat full of white feet and feathers to prove it. Red fox, snow leopard, wolf, maybe all three at different hours. None of them live in the bowl. The bowl is too small and too honest to support a predator full-time. It is a refrigerator they check. They come up over the knife-edge at dusk, trot the rim in silhouette, jump down, work the cushions with a nose, take what is surplus, and are gone before first frost snap at four. Their real home is lower, where there are marmots and blue sheep. Here they are only a rumor that keeps the finches fat and the pikas quick.

**What lives here without ever living here.**

The builders of the windbreaks.

Not a people, but a pattern of people. Herders, pilgrims, climbers, the *drokpa* who lost a yak. They cannot stay. There is not enough air to sleep deeply and not enough fuel to stay warm through what happens at 4:01 pm when the shadow slices the bowl and everything stops with an audible tick. So they do what humans do at the limit of habitability: they make a C of black stones, back to the wind that screams over the rim, and they sit for five hours inside the day. They try to make a fire and fail. They leave butter, they leave a carved spiral, they leave a blue thread under a cairn that the sunbleeds white.

They leave because the place teaches you that to persist you must become low and tight and domed, and we are not good at that.

So they mark it and descend, and the moss grows two microns over their bootprint.

If anything owns this place, it is the cold light itself — that third inversion shimmer at noon that makes little impossible rainbows at ankle height. It is not a thing, but it lives here. It comes every day, lies down on the air, refracts the finches as they pass, and vanishes.
