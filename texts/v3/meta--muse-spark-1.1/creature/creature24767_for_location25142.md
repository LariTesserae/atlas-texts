No one lives here the way you live in a place. No one holds it. What lives here *is* the place.

The first citizen is the tower itself — and the tower is not one thing.

From a distance you think: moss. Up close you understand it is a city in miniature, and that it has citizens inside it who built it without knowing they were building.

It starts as nothing. A spore that by accident doesn't blow out of the bowl, caught in a micro-pit in the gneiss. A film of black-green liverwort with leaves like shingles. It makes a coin of green no bigger than your thumbnail and then it dies in the middle. It always dies in the middle first. The living edge creeps outward a millimeter a year, the center becomes a little peat, a little compost of its own dead leaves. That peat holds fog one minute longer than bare stone does. That's enough.

In that little damp, a second species finds purchase. Cushion moss, the bright viridian one, hard as upholstery foam when you press it. It does not root; it clings. It grows up, not out, because up is where the fog hasn't been caught yet. Alpine azalea follows, not as a separate plant but as a tenant, threading woody stems no thicker than wire through the cushion, its tiny leathery leaves held out over the edge like solar panels. Haircap moss does the structural work — taller, stiffer, with hairs that are built to comb water out of air.

For a hundred years this is just a hummock, an ankle-high bump you could step over. For another three hundred it becomes a dome. The inside is no longer moss. The inside is history. Generations of leaves and microscopic stems that never fully rotted because it is too cold to rot properly, so they fermented instead. Slow, airless, bacterial breakdown that produces heat the way a compost pile does. That's the warmth you feel when you put your hand in.

That warmth is the whole trick. In a place where night is always below freezing and noon is only just above it, a few degrees is a kingdom.

By the time it is waist-high the colony has become vertical because it has to. If it spreads it dries. If it grows up it catches more fog. So it grows into a column, widest at the top to maximize bead-surface, narrow at the base where rot would kill it if it stayed wet. And then the top sags. The center, which is oldest and softest, slumps a little and the whole thing becomes a chimney, a thimble. Rain never comes but fog comes every day, beads on every leaf hair, swells, and runs inward along waxy channels into the warm hollow where it melts, after freezing the night before.

This is when it becomes inhabited.

The second order of citizens never leave the tower. You won't see them unless you put your ear right in the hooting mouth and wait. Mites, amber and so pale they are almost clear, the size of poppy seeds, with legs too slow for how busy they are. Springtails like grains of pepper that jump by snapping a forked tail under their bellies. They live in the millions in the peat walls. They eat the dead. They shit soil. They graze fungal hyphae that bloom white as nerves through the rot. The ticking you hear when you lean inside is their mandibles. It is not incidental sound. It is the sound of the building being built. They are making more interior than the mosses can make alone, lofting the peat, tunneling, aerating, keeping the slow compost from going anoxic and cold. Without them the tower would drown in its own water and die. With them it stays warm and hollow and breathing.

And then, in some but not all of the towers, a third order. The white moths.

They are not pretty. White like old paper, wings frayed, bodies fat with antifreeze glycerol. They hatch in the warm peat, they eat peat, they pupate in peat, they emerge in June-midwinter and fly not out, but up, one vertical foot to the lip of their own chimney, where they sit and shiver and wait for another moth from the same tower to do the same. They have never seen another tower. Their whole world is the interior of one apartment building, its warm fungal smell, its circle of sky. When they die their scales add to the insulation.

That's why the towers have to be apart. If they were a thicket, the moths would mix and the pattern would blur. Here, twenty feet of bare stone between each one is a genetic ocean. Each tower drifts into its own micro-dialect — a little more red here, a little more haircap there, a slightly different hooting note because its hollow is shaped by a thousand years of slightly different mite architecture.

The only things that commute are bigger.

The salamanders live under the lip of the bowl, where the stone finally gives up holding water and lets it go down into the mountain. They are the color of wet gneiss, black with a granular skin that looks like stone until they move. They are impossibly slow. In summer they migrate — fifteen feet in a day — from one drip-pool to the next, belly flashing that sudden orange, lichen-orange, like a warning. They eat the springtails that fall, the moths that misjudge. They carry peat in the creases of their legs from one tower to the next, and with it spores, eggs. They are the mailmen, the only ones who remember the whole city at once.

And the snow-finch. She doesn't live here. Nothing that needs that much food per minute could. She lives down a thousand meters where there are seeds. But she comes up on clear afternoons, a flicker of buff and white against all that dark stone, and lands on the tallest old tower in the middle — the elder, four times your height, whose north side has died and is covered in liverwort like a shawl — and she kicks and tears. She is looking for the moth pupae near the top. She scolds while she does it, because the wind is loud, because this is not her place. Five minutes and she is gone. The tower leans an inch that day from her weight and then sighs back.

The last citizen is the ruin in the center. It had to happen once. A tower that got too tall and too warm and the bacteria got too efficient and the peat got too soft, and one noon crack — that rifle-shot sound of ice, but deeper — it folded inward. Its warm heart opened to the sky.

That is where the soil finally wins. In that atrium of fallen moss, light falls straight down without being hooted or split. The peat is no longer being built, it is being finished. In it that ankle-high willow, old beyond all reason, thick as your wrist, holds on. And the saxifrage, with white flowers that actually smell, faintly of honey and stone dust, because there is no competition for pollinators up here and even a whisper of scent carries for miles in thin air. The saxifrage will not live anywhere else in the bowl. It needs true dirt, which only exists where a city has died.

So this is who lives here. The builders who grow themselves. The tenants who eat death and make heat. The prisoners who never leave their warm house. The commuters in black stone coats. The visitors who need to steal. And in the middle, the dead neighborhood that has finally become a meadow no larger than a table.

If you stay until the afternoon cloud pours over the rim, you will see them all working at once: dripping, ticking, hooting, cracking, breathing out of forty chimneys. From the rim it looks like smoke again, like people are home and cooking. They are.
