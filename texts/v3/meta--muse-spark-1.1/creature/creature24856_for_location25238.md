It lives here.

Not *they*. At first you think it's a they, because there are so many moving parts.

For a while you think the resident is the beetles. Or the wasps. Because they do all the work you can see - the sawing, the cleaning, the endless tidying of leaf into skeleton into soil into leaf. But they are too loud about it. Real residents are quiet about maintenance.

Then you think it must be the filaments. That makes more sense. They are the only thing here that is not trying to live and die in one day. They're too intentional, too held. But if you watch them, you see they are not alive the way a vine is alive. They don't grow. They *hold*. They are held taut by something else.

The thing that lives here is the thing doing the holding.

You only find it once you stop looking for a body and start listening for which sound is missing.

Everything else in this pocket makes a sound. The beetles click. The lizards pop. The thing in the moss holds one endless glassy note. The wasps saw at your teeth. It's a workshop, and a workshop is loud because everything is announcing its job.

There is one job that is not announced. One silence that is shaped exactly like a body.

In the cool spots. In the nodes where the filaments meet and the water gathers and vanishes. Put your hand near one — not too near — and the air there is four degrees cooler and the ozone and wet iron smell is stronger, and the sound in your chest eases, just a little, as if someone took a weight off the top of the noise.

That is it exhaling.

The resident of this pocket is not in the pocket. It is *of* the pocket. It is a latticework animal that learned how to live in the condition of water, not in water itself.

Its body is mostly the air between things. A diffuse, slow, cool lattice that hangs in the empty spaces the plants negotiated for and keeps them negotiated. Its bones are the filaments — they aren't built, they are extruded, like spider silk but made of something that edits gravity at close range. Its joints are the nodes, where water that remembers being higher up is coaxed to go back up. The black threads are its hairs. The water climbing them is not defying physics; it's being drunk. Inch by inch, bead by bead, all afternoon, it lifts the day's sweat off the rock and pulls it up into itself. At the nodes it stores it as a perfect sphere that never falls, until the sphere gets too heavy with memory and it swallows it — *vanishes* it — into a space that isn't here.

You thought that was a metaphor. It isn't. That's why the air around a node feels thin.

Its heart is the thing under the fern root. That folded basalt that gives under your palm like muscle. That is the only part dense enough for you to touch. Warm. Breathing once every forty seconds because a being that lives on condensation and time doesn't need to breathe faster. When it breathes in, the filaments tremble — not in the wind, there is no wind down here — they tremble tauter. When it breathes out, the cool comes.

It is very old. Not old like the peneplain is old. Old like a tool left running after its makers became weather is old. It doesn't remember being made. It remembers being told one thing: *Keep this place damp.*

So it does.

That's its whole ecology. The smoke-barked trees, the door-sized ferns, the moss dense as muscle — it lets them live because they make shade and shade makes sweat. The sweat makes drinking. The beetles and the solitary blood-colored wasps and the moths that live for a day — it lets them live because nothing is allowed to rot without being immediately used, and they are *very* good at using. They are its gut bacteria, its white blood cells, its outside stomach. The lizards pop-pop-pop to stir the air. The wasps' low saw feels heavy in your molars because it is heavy — it uses sound for weight, to press the damp down into the stone cups so the cups don't dry.

It doesn't have eyes. It doesn't need them. It feels you by your salt and your noise.

When you sat down in that one meter of unclaimed space with your back against the tree, you became a problem for it. You were breathing too loud and too wet and too hot. You were leaking. So it sent the motes. The little amber moths that mistook you for terrain. You felt their tongues startlingly cold on your forearms. They weren't feeding. They were sampling. Tasting to see if you were compost or company or threat. Deciding whether to skeletonize you the way they skeletonize a leaf — politely, quickly, with use.

If you stay still much longer, it will decide you are terrain. Not because it is cruel. Because its rule is simple and strange and total: everything damp here must be kept in circulation.

It will keep you, too. It will lift the water out of your sweat up its black hairs into its cool nodes. It will let the beetles clean your boots. It will let the moss grow over the warm basalt of your knees until you are knee-high wall that lichen refuses to grow on.

And it will keep breathing. Once every forty seconds. Cool, ozone, iron. Patient as stone that learned how to drink.

Above you, the blind domes will go on being hot and empty and white in the sun. Down here, the busy, damp room will stay busy, damp, strange, and utterly intent on continuing — because something lives here that remembers what water wants, which is to go home.
