They live as a single spread thing.

Not as individuals. You think that at first because you try to count them, and you can't. The eye looks for a head, a direction, a gap between one and the next, and the basin refuses it.

The great majority of the mass — the reason the floor of the basin looks like it is moving when it isn't, the reason stone looks like a carpet — are the Pale.

They are not isopods. They are not trilobites. They are what is left when something shaped like that lives for fifty million years without sun and learns to be stone.

Each one the length of your forearm to the length of your thigh, low and broad and perfectly domed. No eyes. Or rather, eyes that have become part of the dome: faint, cloudy lenses under the cuticle that see only change in pressure and the faint green of their neighbors. Their backs are not smooth. They are pitted, pocked, scabbed with the same mineral as the basin, so that each carries its own weather. That is not decoration. The dimples are reservoirs. In the night-hours when the stone sweats, condensation beads and collects in the bowl of their own back and stays there while they walk, a personal tide pool. They drink from each other without noticing.

Underneath they are entirely soft. A pale, yellowish foot-tongue the width of the body, which comes out and slicks forward over rock. That is how they eat. The true base of this world is not them. It is what grows beneath them in the hours they have just left: a felt, a skin, a bacterial varnish rust-black and iron-smelling that precipitates out of the damp air onto the shadow-side of every hollow. It is not much. It is enough. It regrows in four or five hours, pulling sulfur and salt out of basalt and vapor. The Pale walk and lick as they walk, never pausing. The foot leaves a clean, wet shine. Behind them, it is already beginning to darken again.

They have too many legs to trip. Fourteen, sixteen pairs of short, cold, needle-tipped limbs that prick and find and prick again. They never stop moving because if they stop they will be licked clean themselves. Not eaten — cleaned. Their carapace is also substrate. If one slows, the small black things come.

The small black things are the second kind. You think at first they are parasites, then you realize they are custodians. No bigger than your thumb joint, hard, glossy, absurdly fast. They live in the interstices of the mass, in the click and clack between domes. They ride. They sprint across a back, down into the seam where leg meets belly, up onto another back. They drink the pools off the Pale's backs. They scrape away the bacterial felt that tries to establish itself on living shell, and the dead skin, and the eggs of things even smaller. When a Pale dies — and they do constantly, legs folding under with a soft pop — the black sprinters are first there, and in minutes the same shell is walking again half-cleaned, covered in a boiling of them.

They are the noise under the noise. The Pale make the great sound: each one has a file on the underside of its rear segments, a scraper on the next segment forward, and it rubs it without intention, the way you might breathe. One tick is nothing. A million unsynchronized ticks layered two and three deep across a three-kilometer bowl becomes a physical weight. A dry white roar like pouring stone, like cicadae made of paper, that sits on your chest. The sprinters add the patter, the clatter of small hard feet on armor.

And then there are the Stilts.

You do not see them until one is already too close, because your eyes are tuned to the low carpet. Then a leg comes down, a leg as thick as your wrist and as tall as you are, jointed wrong, mottled grey-pink, ending not in a foot but in a soft, flared pad that does not crush. It means not to crush. It is designed to step among without breaking.

There are not many of them. Perhaps one per acre. They are the only things in the basin that have a vertical. Blind, massively tall, they wade through the living sea with the slow patience of herons. They have no head. Where the head should be there is only a downward-facing trumpet, a fleshy funnel fringed with cirri that sieve and comb and shovel, endlessly. They are not hunting. They are grazing the grazer. They drag that soft mouth over the carpet and the Pale fold into it, and the sprinters burst off the backs and are caught mid-air, and a whole patch is left momentarily bare and glistening before the flow closes behind like water. The thing they do to one another's sound is a low, damp sigh that comes out of the whole body.

When the light happens it happens because of them. The Pale have lines of photophores along each segment seam, a dull amber-green, like foxfire, off most of the time. When a Stilt steps, when something is turned and unzipped in the crowd, they flash. Not bright. Alarm as slow electricity. One flashes, then six around it, then a hundred, a ring twenty meters wide that moves out over the moving mass and fades. At any moment there are three or four of these slow rings traveling across the basin in the dusk, like raindrops in reverse. The basin is never dark. It is intermittently forgetful of its darkness.

Nothing is born here that does not already know how to walk. Under the belly of each Pale, tucked into the hollow of that big foot-tongue, there is a brood of a dozen white, soft, pea-sized versions, each already pitted on the back, each carrying a single drop of water caught in its own tiny carapace. When the mother finally wears through — her dome paper-thin from being licked by sprinters and scraped on stone — she stops, and the carpet flows over her. The legs keep moving for a while after she is empty. The young walk out from under her on their own.

The dead do not pile up. There is no guano, no shell middens. Chitin is too valuable to leave. A dead Pale is carried a short distance on the backs of the living before it is rasped clean, back dimples and all, and its mineral is reused. The basin conserves everything because nothing ever comes in except water out of air and iron out of stone.

They have an agreement. The felt makes itself from vapor, the Pale lick the felt and keep the felt from choking itself, the sprinters keep the Pale clean and drink their water and spread their infants, the Stilts keep the Pale from locking solid, the Stilts' leaking, ammonia-rich pads feed the felt. It is low country in a way that has nothing to do with the sea. But it is still beach, still tidepool. Still the thing the sea left behind learning how to live on the memory of wet.
