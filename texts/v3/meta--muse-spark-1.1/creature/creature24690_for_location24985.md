What lives here is not a single thing. It's a treaty.

This place was never empty long enough to be colonized. It was *occupied* the moment it opened — because it opened inside things that were already crowded.

The majority shareholder — the citizen — is the slater.

They are not the pill bugs of your garden. Those are terrestrial and apologetic. These are *Ligia*, sea-slater stock, the ones that ran the intertidal rocks and never quite learned to be inland. Here they have learned. In here they are the wall itself.

They live shoulder to shoulder, back to back, their slate-black dorsal plates making a second concrete. If you didn't know to look you'd think the wall was textured. Then the texture breathes. 

They are not still. They can't afford stillness. The room never dries and never floods, so they never have the luxury of quiescence — the thing woodlice do when it's too dry or too wet. They are always on the edge of too much and not enough, so they are always adjusting by a millimeter. A living isopod tessellation, each animal holding exactly as much space as its body when it is slightly hunched. When one has to turn around to graze the biofilm off the fresh concrete, ten others have to lean. The adjustment runs down the wall like wind through wheat. You can watch it travel from the floor to the ceiling seam.

They eat the wall. Literally. The fresh fracture is still high in lime, still sweating calcium, and in the humid air it grows a greenish bacterial skin you can't see until they scrape it clean. Each slater's mouthparts rasp. They eat the concrete's youth. They eat the rust dust that falls from the pipe lagging. They eat each other's shed skins the instant they are shed — you will see a pale, perfect ghost of a slater being pulled apart by its neighbors while its owner stands soft and white and terrified nearby, waiting to harden enough to push back into the crowd.

Between them, in the negative space of their legs, live the periwinkles.

They shouldn't be here. They are sea snails, *Littorina*. Some gull must have dropped them, or they rode in as eggs on a boot. They are reduced here, simplified to a black dot with a trapdoor. They hold their ground. A slater will flow around a periwinkle the way water flows around a boulder. The snail has learned the rhythm of the drips. When the air gets too hot from the steam artery, it pulls its operculum shut and waits out the dry pulse. When a drop lands within an inch, it opens and drinks with its whole body, the foot swelling. It doesn't move more than an inch a week. It doesn't need to. The food comes to it on the legs of slaters, as detritus. When a slater dies — and they die often in this density — the periwinkle is there first, before the beetles.

The beetles are the police. Small, flat, black carabids, flightless because there is nowhere to fly. They have an oil-slick sheen that never looks wet. They cannot live like the slaters, in contact. They have to move. So they run on top of the living wall, on top of the living floor, a constant patrol on top of the city. Their legs are too long for efficiency; they trip constantly, right themselves. You think they are frantic, but they are methodical. They are tasting with their antennae for the soft ones — the freshly molted slater, the hopper with a broken leg, the periwinkle that left its door ajar too long. They drag. They are strong for their size. They will pull a molting slater twice their weight off the wall and into that glove.

The sandhoppers are the memory of the beach. They are *Talitrus* that have gone three generations without seeing sand. They are bigger here, in the dark protein-rich jam, as big as the first joint of your thumb, almost shrimp again, translucent grey with orange gut lines. They don't belong on walls but they do it anyway. They move in sideways bursts, absurd against the steady flow of slaters. They are detritivores made nervous herbivores — they want that fern, more than anyone, but they can't have it. The geckos won't let them near the wet corner.

The geckos are the gods. Not because they are cruel. Because they are scarce and unbothered.

*Mediterranean house geckos, Hemidactylus turcicus.* They came in as eggs in the pallets that built the station in the 70s, nocturnal lizards that found a room that is always night and always day at once. In the rest of the world they are pinkish. Here they have gone the color of the new concrete, that yellow-white fresh break. Their bellies are glass. You can see the dark ball of a snail in one, the three black beetle elytra in another.

They own the verticality. Twenty of them, maybe twenty-three — you can never count because their stillness is perfect. They hold onto the ceiling with those leafed toes right around the hot bulb, right next to the pipe where it's 90 degrees, and they do not blister. They are heat lords. A gecko can be entirely surrounded by slaters and not be touched, because the slaters give them a reverent millimeter. Not from fear — slaters don't feel fear as we do — but from heat and chemical cue.

When a gecko moves, time restarts. It is too fast for this room. It detaches, flicks, lands with a soft *thup* on the opposite wall, tongue out before its feet grip. Where its tongue landed there is a clean circle the size of a quarter, free of slaters, free of everything. The hole fills in within ninety seconds. The slaters shuffle in laterally, closing ranks. You can actually hear it — a faint renewed patter of legs filling a vacuum.

And down in the corner, the green thing.

One fern. *Pteris vittata,* the brake fern that eats heavy metals. It is a runt, a bonsai made by stress. Its fronds should be a foot long; here they are four inches, black-green, curled under, each one dusted with rust felt and with the lime that precipitates out of the air. It lives only because two drip-maps overlap there, making a plate-sized permanent damp that the beetles can't cross without drowning. It is the only thing here that photosynthesizes, and it is bad at it — the bulb is too yellow, the grated daylight is too chopped. So it has gone partly mycotrophic.

Its partner is the mold. A white, flat, merciless mold that is not on the concrete but *in* it. It sends hyphae into the capillary pores of the fresh shear face and dissolves it with acid. It looks soft; it is a miner. Where the fern's roots meet the floor, the mold is thickest, a crust like frost. It is eating the glove too, from the inside out. The glove — that discarded leather work glove fused stiff with old sweat and curing compound — is a high-rise for the mold, a brown damp cave for the beetles, a nursery for the slaters. Everything touches it. It's the oldest thing in here and it will be the first thing to become soil.

That is the truism of this country: everything is becoming floor.

The concrete is still curing. It is still hydrating, a low, exothermic birth that will take thirty more years. It puts out its own heat, just barely, a warmth that the slaters can feel. The rebar inside it is still expanding as it rusts, pushing more aggregate off, making more new edge. The room is getting bigger by millimeters a year, and the life is keeping up with it, filling the new space as fast as it appears.

If you stay, you will see that no animal here looks up at the grating. No animal here knows there is an ocean thirty feet away through solid concrete, or a city beyond that. Their sky is the underside of that pipe, their weather is *plik*. They have made a whole closed system out of the space between two disagreements in concrete. They are not trapped. They are *fitted*. 

This is a type of life you only get in seams: life that doesn't need a world, only a crack in the world that happens to hold steady for a little while.
