Nothing lives here. 

That's the first thing you learn after your ears give up.

You sit on the cold sand and let the roar fill your chest until it stops being sound and starts being pressure, and you think: nothing could live here. It's too cold, it's too loud, it's too dark to grow right. And for a long breath, that feels true. That feels like the point.

Then your eyes finally quit trying to see and start seeing.

What lives here first is green.

Not everywhere — it can't afford to be everywhere. In this latitude, in this 34 degree air that never warms, green has to be careful, has to be miserly and perfect. So it lives in islands. A pillow of moss on the big fallen basalt boulder that came down before anyone counted winters, so deep and so tight you could lose your whole fist in it, bright as bottle glass even in the grey lace-light from the fall. It has been alive longer than you have been alive. Longer than the name of the river above. It grows a millimeter when the summer is kind. When a bead of water hits it, it doesn't drink, it just holds.

Next to it another one, bearded off a lip where the ceiling drips, a hanging beard that never swings because the air never moves, just glistens. And between them on the floor stones, the liverworts — not moss, something older, something from before leaves — spread like wet, black-green hands pressed to the rock. They shine. They are so slow you cannot see them living, only that they have lived.

That is the bulk of it. That is the forest here. An archipelago of soft planets in a sea of cold black sand.

What lives here second is water. Not as a visitor — as a resident.

There is the little thread that walks across the floor, which is alive the way a thought is alive, finding a way around each pebble. And there is the other water, the one behind the back wall. The river up there is low and wide and brown, but here, after it throws itself off the shelf, it becomes a wall, a hammer, a lung. It hits that thin, cracked basalt and it hits it and it hits it and every hit makes the whole pocket hum. The water forces its way through in vibrating seep, in beads that gather on the ceiling and hang a long time before they fall. In winter it lives as ice, knobbed and clear on the underside of the world. When you put your palm to the back wall, the stone purrs. That water has been trying to get into this room for ten thousand years. It never stops.

So the room's true fauna is rare because the room's landlord is loud. A white moth that blundered through the curtain and will die here because it can't find the dark that is somehow brighter than the grey light. A clump of bats wedged into a seam near the top like a dusty knot, breathing once a minute all summer, not so much hibernating as listening with their whole bodies, waiting for the roar to stop, which it never will. Once, a dipper shooting through the water-light like a thrown stone, and then gone.

All of that lives here because it can't live anywhere else, or because it got lost.

And then —

This is the part that makes you stoop even lower, even though the ceiling isn't that low here.

No one lives here. The air is cold enough to take the feeling out of your knees through your pants. The light never holds still enough to mend a tear or read by. You couldn't sleep with that hammer in your teeth.

But someone lives *with* it.

Not in the way an animal lives in a burrow. In the way a church bell lives with a ringer. The room is kept empty on purpose.

You see it in the three cairns near the side wall. Someone carried those stones in. Not the black basalt here — pink granite from upstream, from where the water is still a river and you can stand in it. Each one no higher than your knee, gone furry with that patient moss, except the very top stone where a hand has brushed the moss away. A year ago. Three years ago. Yesterday — you can't tell here, because everything that is touched stays touched for a long time. Nothing rots. A footprint in the sand stays for a season. A handprint on a moss pillow stays for a decade.

And the sticks. Blackthorn, split with stone, not steel — you can see where the grain was worried apart, chewed rather than cut. Driven into the sand between the cairns. Little figures at the top bound with sinew: a bone button, a feather gone to colorless spine, a twist of wool the color of old snow. Offerings, or maps, or just something to do with your hands while the roar filed your thoughts down to nothing.

And the leaning slab propped to make a little dry triangle where there is no dry. Under it, a flat stone with a bowl ground into it by another stone, round and round for longer than a person can stand to be cold. In the bowl, dark water that has been still long enough to have no ripples even though the whole world behind it is shaking, and in the water, three white pebbles placed one by one, not dropped.

That's who lives here.

She — or he, or they, one long chain of them with the same cold hands, the same need to get out of the wind — does not stay. She comes down the worn scoops sideways, like you did, dripping from the sedge. She sits where you are sitting and she breathes that invisible cloud into the 34 degree air. She listens until the deafening stops being river and starts being a hum, until the hum starts sounding like the room itself remembering how to make a sound.

She picks the moss off the top stone. She puts a white pebble in the bowl.

She comes because this is not a living place. It's a listening place. A place too loud for any other animal to claim, so prayer can live here and not get eaten. Grief can live here. A question that doesn't want an answer can live here.

In the shifting lace of the waterfall light, on the perfect green pillows that have lived longer than any story, the absence of her lives here now, waiting for the next one to come and keep it empty again.

For as long as you sit, until your knees go numb and you have to go back up — you live here.
