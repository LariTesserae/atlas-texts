At first you think nothing does.

That is the first design of the place. It is too clean. The eye expects a hearth, a midden, a footprint, a stain. There is none. No lichen because nothing has been allowed to hold still long enough to become food. No bird bone in a cistern. No ash. The absence feels deliberate, maintained, like a swept floor. You stand in that bright, wet gorge of fitted stone and think: this was made and then left. A ruin before it could be lived in.

Then your body starts to read the measurements.

The steps up from the lower cistern are wrong for you. The riser is low, fourteen inches at most, but deep. The tread is polished in the center, not by your boots, but by something short and heavy that sets its whole foot down flat. You see it everywhere once you see it once. The parapets come up to your chest, where they should come to a waist. The weep holes are too small for a man's fist, but shine-slick around the lip where something has passed its arm through every day. In the coping stones, where the rope dragged, the groove is narrow, hand-span narrow, and on either side of it, paired thumb-dents in the wet grit where someone braced. 

They are not tall.

They are not warm.

If you kneel — and you have to kneel, to get out of the white sun — and put your hand flat on the slate floor where the water is only a skin deep, your palm goes numb in seconds. That is the cold the stone keeps. That is their temperature. Whoever lives here does not fight that cold. They are that cold. You will not find them sunning. You will not find them curled for heat.

Look longer at the wet.

In the film on every horizontal, in the cistern where the bottom is still pale and the chisel marks haven't blurred, there is a color that is not stone. Not algae — algae would be green here, desperate for that hard light. This is slate-grey that goes translucent at the edge. It sits flush in the dished terrace like a living caul. It lifts, when you get close, by a millimeter. It has a border.

You thought the coping stones were clean. They aren't. The film is on them too, stretched thin over the rock, holding the condensation the way skin holds sweat. When you breathe on it, it tightens.

They are flat. That is why you missed them. They are low, broad, many-jointed, four legs splayed short as a toad's, and a back that is not a back but a plate. A dorsal stone, carried. Not grown — quarried. The young ones, down in the lower lake, have only a flake of mica on their backs barely bigger than a coin, clumsy and bright. The middle tiers carry split slate, grey on grey, edged sharp as when it was struck. The old ones, up near the unfinished crown where the raw piles are, carry whole rough blocks of gneiss half their own weight, pale inside where the wedge scar still shows. They all carry. They always carry. That is the labor you were seeing. No separate builders and building. The building is the shell.

Lift a smaller one, if you can bring yourself to touch it — your warm hand will hurt it — and you will see why there is no mortar. Underneath, the belly is a soft, cold white, all mouth and hands. Not hands like yours. Hands like many small hands, fringed, flat, rated along the edge, six to a side, each with that small, cold palm that fits exactly into the palm-bowl worn into every stone in this valley. They plaster themselves to a new stone and carry it. They secrete a little acid that tastes the rock and a little silk that binds water, not stone to stone — water to stone. That is their craft. Primitive because perfect tools would be wrong. They split stone the slow way, with harder stone and the rare taste of iron they find in veins and hammer with their backs. The rust-blooms you saw in every wedge-scar are not from tools. It's their spit drying.

They farm nothing. They weed everything. That is why there is no lichen, no alga, no bird shit, no leaf. Lichen would stop the water. They crop the stone cleaner than ice, with their mouths, each generation lapping the surface of the platform their grandparents laid, keeping it bright enough to gasp and bead. They need the weeping. Thin air, brutal light, cold rock — it makes water appear from nothing, and they drink by living under it. Not by swallowing. By letting it move over them. The whole mountain is their gill.

That was the rule you felt without being told: nothing is allowed to stagnate, and nothing is allowed to rush. Watch them. The terraces are not architecture. They are circulation lines. One shifts its stone a hand's breadth to the left, and the film that was pooling there, that was starting to go warm and still, takes a new path and finds the next one down, who is waiting with its back dished just so. The rills are their roads. The knee-deep lake at the bottom is their wintering, their breeding, their old-age; hundreds of them pressed together, backs just breaking the surface like a cobbled street you could walk on, breathing slow.

You won't see them move while you watch. That is not because they are hiding. They are moving. But their clock is water's clock. The stone deepens an hour, the film thickens a minute, a back inches forward in an afternoon.

At the top, where the piles are still raw and the mountain still looks snow-capped because the water there is so clean and so thin and so full of sun it hurts, the youngest workers are still arriving. They come up from the dark cracks where the spring forces out of the mountain's real mountain, an eighth of an inch at a time, translucent, un-shelled. They pick up the first flake. They start slowing the sky.

If you stand very still in one of those tall, damp slots and let your boots fill and let your feet go numb and let the ticking of a thousand overflows become the only time, they forget you are warm. A big old one will come along the top of the wall above you, carrying a block that would take you and three friends to lift, and it will pause, and the water will gather on the bright edge of its back and spill in a thread, and it will lower itself, and fit its load not onto the wall but into the wall, with a soft, patient, stone-on-stone kiss you feel more in your teeth than your ears.

That is who lives here. Not in the mountain. As the mountain.
