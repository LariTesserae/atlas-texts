You don't see them at first.

Because that is also a rule, unwritten — if a surface is still visible, it's not yet occupied, and they do not allow visible surfaces.

So at first you see only the town itself moving. Moss shivering under its own weight. Pipes ticking. Water finding its way. The six-limbed rat-things flickering through insulation. The hundred thousand thick pigeons walking shoulder to shoulder down an aisle that wasn't there yesterday.

Then you see one of them has stopped walking, and was a person all along.

They are the same color as the place. That's not camouflage, that's diet and work.

They are short. Not dramatically, not a new species short, just — shortened by use. Low-built, long in the torso, short in the shin, because no one here ever takes a full stride. There is nowhere to. The aisles are thirty centimeters wide, then twenty, then a shoulder-turn. The ceilings are low because a roof is always about to become a floor. You learn to crouch, duck, sidle. Children here walk upright until they are about seven and then seem to settle, compact. The adults all have that mason's hunch, the weaver's hunch, not from age but from constantly working with their hands at chest level, in front of their faces.

Their clothes are not clothes. There isn't cloth in the sense you know it from high, dry places. Everything they wear is pressed and spun moss. The black-green felt, when it's new, is a dark olive. After a week of wear it goes black with water and oil and it never dries. They wear it in layers, no seams, no buttons — wrapped, tucked, tied with copper wire. Underneath, skin that is never quite dry. You can hear them before you can pick them out: a faint squelch of felt boots saturated with gutter-water, the creak of moss-fiber around a knee. Pale skin, but not the pale of people who never see sun. The flat overcast light here is honest, it shows everything — and their skin is freckled with small dark points where lichen spores have tried to root and been scrubbed away. Their hands are the giveaway. Too big. Swollen-looking, fingers thickened and creased, nails soft and trimmed to nothing with teeth, stained to the second knuckle a deep rust-black that doesn't wash off. The hands of people who spend all day wringing out living sponges, re-seating pipes, smearing moss slurry on new rock before it can get cold.

Their faces are broad, cheeks red and cracked from constant wet cold, but their eyes are sheltered. Deep set, lidded against the drip. They squint not from brightness but from steam. They don't look up. Up is where the cold sink lives, where the plain is white-grey and kills you in minutes without heat. They look slightly down, always, watching water.

Because water belongs to everyone and must be passed on, that is their entire ethic. You understand it when you watch an old woman — and there are old people here, far older than you would expect for this damp and this work, because the cold preserves and the moss gives everything you need — you see her tend a gutter. She is kneeling on a catwalk that is also a roof, that is already half floor, her knees in black-green. Her gutter is made from half a boiler that fell forty years ago, its seam picked for copper, its curve now perfect. A hundred drips feed it from pipes and beards of ice above. The water in it is clear, then grey, then clear again. She watches it, and when a leaf blocks it, she lifts it with those big hands. When a child's dam of moss downstream diverts too much, she breaks it with one finger, not unkindly, and the child, three levels down, feels the flow return and nods without looking. No ownership. Only stewardship of a meter or two for a lifetime.

They do not speak loudly. Rule four. To be heard more than two levels away you must be worth it, and almost nothing is. So their language has gone quiet and physical. A vocabulary of soft consonants, of short words you say into someone's collar. Pigeon-keepers click their tongues — little tuts and kissing sounds that carry a meter and no further. Work crews talk with knocks: one knock on a pipe means *hold*, two means *let it flow*, three means *new rock*. They touch a lot, because touch is quieter than voice. A hand on a shoulder as you squeeze past in a gap, a palm to the back of a head to move a child along. They sing, but only in the places where the turbines sit, those church-big low mills that hum so low you feel it in your molars. Inside that hum, you can sing without being heard elsewhere, and there they do — low, buzzing hymns that are not words, that are just a way of breathing together in the warm.

Their children are everywhere and nowhere, like the rat-things. You think there are no nurseries until you realize the warm pockets are nurseries. Wherever a steam valve has blown and made a transient jungle — those sudden cathedrals of translucent fronds as wide as doors, dripping hot — you will find three or four toddlers sitting naked in the black mud, watched by whoever is working that pipe. They are boiled pink, silent, playing with the pale woodlice-shrimp they scoop from the central channel. They put everything in their mouths. They grow up learning the first lesson: bare rock must be covered. Give any of them, even a one-year-old, a smear of moss slurry in a cup, and if you set them down on fresh basalt — still glassy, still showing the pull-marks where the world tore — they will pat it on. Untaught. Like birds mudding a nest. The pigeons arrive before the humans do, but the children arrive at the same time as the pigeons.

They do not have funerals. They have fillings-in. When someone dies — and they die quiet, of wet lung, of a slab falling too fast to swarm, of simply stopping in the steam — there is no space to remove them down the aisles. There is no ground to dig, because there is no ground yet, only town and then raw rock. So they wrap them in new felt, in fresh moss, and tuck them into the place where they died, or near it, in a wall cavity, under a floor that will become a ceiling. The moss takes them in days. It is efficient. It has to be. The trough is always making more space by breaking itself, and the town is always running out of space by filling it. The dead become insulation. No one finds this morbid. It's a good use. A covering.

If you stand long enough on that catwalk that was a roof this morning, they will start to treat you as new rock. At first, they will avoid you — you are a bare surface, and that makes them anxious. Then a boy will come and without asking will tie a copper ground wire around your ankle and bolt it to the mesh, because you are tall and lightning might find you. Then an old woman will come with her slurry cup and without looking at your face will smear green-black paste on your boot, to see if it takes. Then a child will hand you a living shrimp, still kicking, because you look hungry. Then a work crew of four will knock your shoulder once — *hold* — as they route a warm, dripping gutter right over your head, so your shoulders get soaked and then stay soaked.

You do not become one of them by being born here. You become one by staying still long enough that something grows on you.
