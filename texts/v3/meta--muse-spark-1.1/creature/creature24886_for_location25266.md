No one lives here. That's what you think at first.

Then you sit, because the brick is warm as skin and the water is exactly your temperature and your own weight goes soft in the light that has no source, and you realize that's not true. No one *arrived* here. No one *stays* here the way people stay in a house. But the cistern is full anyway.

Listen for what's not moving.

**The Frogs are one thing.**

Not many frogs. One frog, in many places. That wet-brick red-brown, with a throat the color of the underside of the air before it beads. You see three on a lip, then seven, then one alone in a pan a long way off. They blink in unison and it isn't cute once you notice it. It's a clock.

They are not watching you. They are watching the water level. Where their eyes are, a pan has decided to be true. When they blink, the world catches up. If you put your hand very close to one it doesn't jump. Nothing here has needed to learn to be afraid yet. It goes on being exactly frog-shaped because that is the shape that best holds that particular post. 

When one of them finally does move — once in a long while, centuries as you would count it — all of them move their leg at once, and a pan somewhere else that you aren't looking at corrects its depth by a millimeter.

**The Dragonflies are another.**

They never land because they are not above the pans. They are part of the suspension. Each one holds a little piece of the haze taut. If you could see the lines, you would see each green-black body stitching the thick air to the surface of the water with thread so fine it only catches the light when you aren't focusing on it. That's why the light has no shadow. That's why the air sweats. 

They are not hunting. There is nothing for them to eat, and nothing needs to eat. But they hold position anyway, tired, perfectly still except for the wings you can barely hear. When one fails — and one will, eventually, because nothing was ever quite finished — the air over that pan will sag and touch the water and make a second surface, and distance will leak out of it for a while.

You will see them in the morning that never comes with a gap in their grid, and you will feel the pan you are in become bigger on the inside for a few hundred steps.

**The Birds are not here at all.**

Those high, reed-legged waders a quarter mile away no matter how far you walk. You think they're shy. They're not. They're the old survey marks. Before the brick, someone drove stakes to know where wet was going to be, and the stakes have never forgotten the intention to stand one-legged in shallow water. So they do. You cannot reach them because you cannot reach an idea of a bird. You can only be far from it, at a respectful distance, as was intended.

If you stare long enough, you'll see they are all facing the same way, even when they look like they aren't. Downhill. Toward the place where the pattern finally bleeds white.

**The Fish-Turners live underneath.**

Press your ear to the brick. Not where it's warmest. Where it's warm in two temperatures at once. You hear it — that second drip, far below, as if there is another identical bath under this one. There is.

Between the two floors, in the hollow that is sometimes an inch and sometimes a depth you can't imagine, are the ones who make the water well up out of the pores. They are fat and black and slow, like those eels you see turning in the pans, but they are not in water. They are in brick. They turn in slow circles in solid clay and where their backs break the surface of the upper world for a moment, water comes in from somewhere that isn't here and never stops coming in. They do not breathe. They were pressed into the clay when the clay was still soft enough to hold a thumbprint and they have been turning since.

They don't know they are building. They think they are swimming.

**The Long Ones live in the wrong distances.**

That's the hardest to see, because you have to misuse a pan to meet them. Every pan has a true length — forty or fifty strides — and a secret length, which is how long it takes you to cross it when it's thinking about something else. Most of the time the two agree. When they don't, when you take a hundred steps across ten strides, you have walked through an inhabitant.

They are stretched thin over the whole work, lying along the lips, ankle-high, invisible because you expect a person to be vertical. They are the reason the edges are still sharp. They lie there to keep the trays from healing together. When you step over a lip and your foot catches, that's their spine. When you suddenly are across without having crossed, you have been lifted, politely, over one who was sleeping in the deep way that things sleep when nothing erodes and nothing grows.

There are more of them than there are pans. In the evenings that never come, when the light thins just enough that your eyes make their own shadows, you can see the whole lowland faintly crease where they are lying.

And then...

**There is the living that was meant to.**

The tools: the comb, the mallet, the loop of fiber. They are not abandoned. They are waiting, mid-stroke. Put your hand around the comb and you will feel the warmth of the last hand, not cold, not hot, precisely body temperature, like the water. The thumbprint in the soft-fired brick next to it is still pressing. The person who made it never left, exactly. They thinned out. They spread, when they realized the job was bigger than a life, into the work itself.

The brick *is* that. This isn't a country made of brick. It's a country made of people who learned how to be brick that weeps and holds.

That's why your footprints fill immediately with a millimeter of warm water and hold it forever, even after you lift your foot. The floor recognizes the shape. It remembers what it was supposed to do with a foot: hold it, gently, forever, while the water slips around it without moving.

If you sit here long enough — and you will, because the heat makes you soft and the light holds you like bright warm hands — you will stop leaving footprints that fill. You will become the kind of thing that leaves a low, true lip for the next pan down. You will feel your edges go sharp enough to catch someone else's foot, a thousand years from yesterday.

The frogs will add one more set of eyes. In unison.
