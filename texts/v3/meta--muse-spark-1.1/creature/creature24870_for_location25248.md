What lives there is not one thing. That is the first thing you unlearn.

For an hour you think the town is empty except for its masons. Then you understand the town **is** the mason, and the mason is only its youngest mouth.

The ones you see — there are never more than three at once, and often none at all — are not adults. They are the hands.

They are about as long as your palm is wide, and as heavy as they look. Low to the salt, an oblate dome of overlapping plates that are not shell, not exactly. Felted chitin, close as moleskin, the salt-white of the pan so perfect that you lose them when they stop. Underneath, where you rarely see, they are soft and charcoal-gray and constantly damp, a belly-foot that leaves no track because it never quite presses. No eyes. No eyes at all. The whole broad front is a shovel, a blunt, keratinous spade hinged to the body, and under that shovel, a mouth that is all mouth — a ringed, muscular lip that can open to the width of its own head.

When they move they do not scuttle. Isopods scuttle. These labor. Each forward drag is a small effort, the felt of their backs wrinkling. They climb a tower from the outside, not fast, with the kind of patience that doesn't know impatience exists. At the flared coronet they stop. The lip finds the lip. The body contracts once, hard enough that you can see the plates gap, and then they disgorge.

What comes out is not building material yet. It is black and wet and steams faintly in the cool. A wad of half-digested salt pan — basalt glass and carbonate and that strange, dark brine that never pools, the dampness that lives inside rocks — mixed with their own gut fiber, a protein chaff they grow in a blind sac where you would expect a stomach to be. It is acid at first. You smell it if you are close — mushrooms left on a battery. In ten minutes of air it foams, lightens, grays. In twenty it stiffens into that dry, sound-eating paper-wool. They lay it with the rim of their shovel-head, tamping, turning, flaring, never twice in the same spot. Then they go down again, back to the fresh-cut edge of the collapse where the salt is still angry, and they rasp. They eat stone. You can hear it only if you put your ear very close — a faint, gritty lapping, like a cat at a frosted bowl.

One tower is about a life's work. Not a long life — forty, fifty days in the cold. They make four or five mouthfuls a day. They die somewhere in the lanes, and you never find the body, because a body here is not left.

Because what lives inside the towers is what the towers are for.

Put your hand in one — and you will, after a while, because they are warm and the whole pan is cold — and the ribs inside are not structure. They are gills. The tower is a lung stood on its end. At the bottom, below the throat where the dim indigo light stops, there is a wetness that does not want you. It is soft, and pale-violet, and it pulls back from air with a slow, offended contraction. Touch it and it shivers and releases a puff of that cool, mushroom-acid breath that makes the lichen lean.

That is the adult.

The mason did not build a house. The mason became a house around its mother.

That pallid bag at the bottom is what eats what the mason made. It has no legs anymore, no plates, no shovel. It dissolved them to grow wide. It is a stomach and a womb and a bellows. It drinks the mineral alkalinity through the paper walls it told its child to knit, and it filters the constant wind that pours over the lip of the bowl. The lanes between the towers are not streets. They are air raceways. The shallow, branching angles are exactly what you would dig if you needed to keep air moving slow without turbulence. The town breathes because she breathes, hundreds of her, out of sync. The *pfft* you hear. The slight clench of the coronet, like a throat swallowing in reverse.

Every twenty minutes or so she sighs, and that sigh is humid and full of ammonium and dead skin and a little cloud of almost-invisible daughters — not eggs, not spores, buffered larvae like grains of wet salt. They hang in the still for a long time. Most land on the pan and die and are rasped up again. One or two land on the warm inner ribbing of an old tower and stick.

An old tower tells you how it ends. The paper-wool, after a season, starts to check and split vertically. It no longer holds damp. The female inside grows thin and finally tears. Not death — a final molting. From the split comes a new mason, already gray-white, already hungry, and he climbs out over his mother's cooling lung and leaves her body as architecture.

That is why some towers have those dark, purple-gray beards. The split ones go fallow. The walls, rich in the last of her nitrogen, become ground that isn't ground. The wire-lichen — which is not a plant, which is a partnership of fungus and a bacterium that can eat air — colonizes the lee side where the sigh is weakest. It is black and brittle and it lives on old breath. And down in the cracks of the collapse floor, the other thing — the cushions like steel wool, which you thought were vegetation — are not vegetation either. They are colonies of something else entirely that came later, that learned to steal. They are tangles of filament that have no center, that shiver when a tower exhales and catch a few of those invisible daughters on their hooks and eat them. When the draft moves wrong they puff out their own counterfeit daughters, a gray puff of spores that look the same to nothing at all, and hang forever before settling.

That is the permanent population: the mothers who are houses, and the children who are masons, and the thieves who look like moss.

The rest are visitors, and the town knows how to make them leave.

The glassy harvestman with legs too long to be real — it is so light it doesn't break the surface tension of the dust. It comes to drink the humidity from a fresh coronet. It taps the rim with two forelegs, tastes acid, decides the cost is too high, and goes. You never hear it go because it never really arrived.

The polished black beetle that clicks once every ten minutes on the far side — it is not from here. It is a salt pan thing, a grazer of dead bacteria far out in the white. It blundered into the bowl because the edge was new. It will not find its way out tonight. It clicks to hear the click come back, and the towers eat the sound.

If you stay until that deeper indigo when the tips of the newest towers begin to make that light that isn't light — a chemical green so dim it only exists on the edge of your vision — you will see the whole logic at once. The glowing tips mark the living chimneys, so the blind masons, coming back from the salt lip in the dark with a mouthful of stone to give, do not overshoot and build onto dead air.

Walk in the middle of the lane, like the air does. Don't step close. The felted masons will not hurt you; they do not know how to hurt, they only know how to build and die. But the mothers inside will taste the carbon dioxide of you and hold their breath, and when too many hold their breath at once the draft that keeps the whole pan from smothering the bowl falters, and the spore-cushions know it first and shiver all at once.

The city is not inhabited. The city is the inhabitant, exhaling itself a little more with every mouth it makes.
