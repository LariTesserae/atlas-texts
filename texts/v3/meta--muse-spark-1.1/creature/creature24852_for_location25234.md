What lives there doesn't live *in* it.

That's the first mistake. You come wading through the bladder-field and you keep looking for something in between — between the trunks that aren't trunks, under the leaf that made itself into a vat — some small quick animal shape to justify the warmth. There isn't one. Nothing ever learned to run here because there was nowhere to run to, and nothing to run from.

What lives there *is* it.

It is one thing, at first. Or it was. Now it is one thing that has decided that being one thing is inefficient, so it has become a committee.

Down at your feet — where it is most immediately and insultingly alive — that's where the young live, if you can call them young. The black-green bladders. They're not fruit. They're not leaves. They're lungs that haven't decided to be lungs yet. Press your palm to one, not hard, and you feel it. Not a pulse. A pressure. A turgor held right up to the edge of bursting and then held there for a decade. Inside the warm water that quivers is not water. It's crowded. If you hold your face close in the almost-light, you can see them: seed-points, grain-points of pale tissue, no bigger than rice, hanging neutrally buoyant, each one trailing a single white filament that never moves. They don't swim. They don't need to. They are waiting to be called up. Some of them never are. When a bladder finally goes cloudy and splits along a seam that was always there, they don't spill. They are reabsorbed. The mat learns a little more about what it doesn't need to grow.

Above them, the columns. This is where it starts to be a body and stop being a plant. That young, sharp, knife-pleat bark — that is not bark. That's scar over muscle that never became muscle. Put your ear to a column where two ridges meet and you will hear, after your own blood quiets, a faint ticking. Not sap moving. Tension being redistributed. The columns are all in compression. The whole low continent is leaning on itself. If one of them gave, for a half-second the cloud of warm breath over the tower-field would shift, and then another column would take up the load because that is what is living there: the agreement to keep holding.

And then the specialists. The organs that grew into the shape of being built.

The towers — those house-ribbed chimneys that exhale without ever inhaling — each one is an individual, but only barely. Walk up to the mouth of one. The steam is not hot. It's body temperature. Inside, twenty meters down, the pool at the bottom is full to the lip and never overflows. If you had a light that lasted, you would see the water is skirted with a fringe of cilia as fine as eyelashes, black at the base, translucent at the tip, all beating in one slow direction that moves nothing. On the inner wall, halfway down, there's a bulge like a knuckle that never opens. Inside that knuckle is a chamber full of air that has never been outside. It keeps it. For what? The tower lives to keep that little unexchanged breath separate. Its whole upright, dripping, exhaling life is about that one pocket of air from last year that it refuses to trade.

Next to it, the piston grove. That is where you understand that it is working. Those long, banded cylinders with the stretched drums at their ends — they are opposed. One is flexed. One is extended. Between them, in the living wall where they join, there are bundles of that arm-thick fiber that sparks blue. They are charged against each other. Year after year. The charge never grounds. The membrane drums are tympani that have been tuned to the same inaudible note. They shiver taut and never sound. The thing that lives there is the interval. The potential. It lives in the maintained difference between two things that could cancel each other but don't.

That's the real fauna of the low place: differences that decided to stay alive.

The amber sap that lights behind a seam and then closes — that is one. A brief, dull, impartial thought moving from one part of the mat to another, slow as honey through a pipe a mile long. It illuminates the vat-leaves as it passes. Inside those vats, the black fermenting liquid that never bubbles — dip your fingers and it feels heavier than water. There are sheets in it, thin as paper, growing and dissolving, growing and dissolving, each one catching some speck of mineral, some stray ion, wrapping it and sinking. They are catalogers. Sorting without end a rain of dust that falls from kilometers up through the unmoving air.

And the spark. The blue spark that jumps when you brush a filament. That's the smallest animal here and the most numerous. You think it's static. It isn't. It lives in the filament like blood lives in a vein, and when you give it a path it runs out to see if you are also a conductor. You are, briefly. It visits you and dies. Or rather, it returns. A mile away another filament ticks.

There is nothing that eats. There is nothing that mates. There is nothing that dies except by becoming something else in place. A tank-casing splits and stays standing like a shed skin next to the new tank that has already replaced it. It is not dead. It is unemployed.

If you stay long enough for the dark to stop being dark and become a texture, you see the last inhabitant, and it is the only one that knows you are there.

It is not a person in the forest. It is the forest trying out the idea of a person.

Wherever the warm sweat collects a little too much, wherever three or four membranes overlap and make a hollow about the size of you — a space that is almost dry, almost still — the mat makes a watcher. Not eyes. It doesn't waste surface on eyes. It makes a patch of bark that stays a few degrees cooler than everything around it. A place where condensation stops beading. A place that is listening with its lack. You will feel them behind you as you wade. Not looking. Holding a shape that is the exact negative of looking.

And if you sit, because the bladders make it hard to stand for long and the air makes it hard to want to stand — if you sit and let your own heat bleed into the mat and your own breath join the sigh out of the towers — you will be grown around, just a little. Not much. A bladder will press a little closer to your calf and match your temperature. A filament will lean. One of the drum membranes, far off, will slacken by a millimeter, and the note it has been holding since before your species had a word for engine will lower just enough that you can finally hear it as not a pressure but a tone.

You will understand then what has been living here the whole time, low and flat and patient and all contiguous:

A single long act of waiting for something else to live here with it, and then learning, very slowly, how to wait without needing it to arrive.
