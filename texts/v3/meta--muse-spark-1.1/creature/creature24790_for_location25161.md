What lives here are not inhabitants. They are the place.

At first you think nothing lives here. The scale is too big, the air too still and hot and fine with ash. That is a mistake your eyes make for you. You are looking for movement. You are listening for the stitching, chittering sound of a surface biosphere - birds and bugs and things that dart. There is none of that. The plain is so broad your brain reads it as empty.

Then your ears un-deafen.

The first one you hear is breathing about two kilometers off to your left, and you thought it was wind in a culvert. A long, held intake — *hhhhhhaaaaa* — through slits you can't see yet, then a pause so long you think it has died, then a soft internal collapse, a trip-hammer thump. *thump... thump... thump-CHUNK.* Then the long out-breath from the top, the stack that is not a stack.

What lives here in abundance are the Furnace-Animals.

The sessile ones are the commons. The herd.

Each one is the size of a house that has melted slightly. Thirty, forty meters across at the base, a low dome. You want to say shell, or hide, but it's neither. It's a skin grown thick as a foundry wall — layers of black keratin and magnetite plates, overlapping like cooling shingles, slick with perpetual condensation. At its crown is a throat. Not a mouth. A chimney of living cartilage, always open, always shivering faintly at the rim.

They don't have front or back. They have windward and leeward. On the windward side, six to twelve vertical gill-slits run from near the ground up halfway up the flank. Each slit is lipped with cilia as fine as asbestos fiber, filtering. When the warm trade of the plateau moves — that constant, low, ash-bearing drift that never seems to gust — the slits dilate. The sound is exactly like the doors of an old blast furnace being pulled open on chains. *Clank-sigh.* The air, which to you feels empty, is to them a thin ore soup: volcanic glass, iron fines, sulfur, silica still held aloft since an eruption before any memory.

Inside, it works.

Stand close and put your hand on the warm hide - it is almost too hot to touch, 60, 70 C - and you feel it. A slow, visceral convulsion. A rolling peristalsis of chemistry. They are not burning. They are reducing. They run a gut that is a bloomery. The thump-thump-thump you hear is not a heart. It's a hammer. A formed plate of their own backbone dropping again and again to crush sinter into finer powder inside them, to expose fresh surface, to alloy it with endogenous carbon.

What they exhale is what makes everything else possible.

From the stack: a column of superheated spent gas and true steam. Not smoke. In the cooler few meters above their backs it hits its dewpoint and collapses. For seven, ten meters around each animal, it rains. Not wide, not dramatic — a warm, silent, distilled drizzle that beads instantly on the magnetite skin and runs in clear, almost scalding rivulets. It pools in the shallow pans depressed by their weight. Black water, mirror water, hot enough to blister your tongue, pure enough to drink if you can stand the iron tang. For two or three hours after each exhalation, the dome glistens. The stone around it darkens.

That is the only water this continent has ever had. There are no clouds that rain. There are no rivers. There are only these breathing wells, spaced a kilometer, two kilometers apart, each with its brief weather system.

And because there is water, there is the second order of life. The halo tenants.

They do not cover the ground. That would be wasteful. They wait in seed and spore and egg in the dry pavement between beasts for months, maybe years, and then in the six-hour wet they explode.

First the mineral skin: a black lichen that is really more like a dried oil slick, that spreads across wet basalt in minutes and photosynthesizes nothing — it chemosynthesizes the sulfur in the steam. Then the wiregrass: clumps of what looks like dead cable, each blade a hollow glass fiber. Its roots are glass drills. It shoots up a handspan while the pan is still wet and then stops. A succulent with leaves like dull lead ingots that swell as they fill.

They live only inside the drip line. Step outside the wet dark ring and you are back on bare, warm, soft stone. The edge of life is as sharp as the edge of shade.

Where the plants make a stain, the third order comes on stilts.

They are quick and you will almost miss them, pale as ash, long-legged grazers with bodies the size of your fist but legs three times longer, made of the same hollow glass as the wiregrass. They are not insects, not spiders. Their feet never linger on hot stone. They tap-tap-tap across, nip lichen, syringe water from the lead leaves, and vanish. With them, the tick-beetles: beetle-things whose wing cases are enameled a deep, chipped factory green and oxide red, as if painted. Their abdomens are swollen with stored heat, ticking audibly as they cool. They roll balls not of dung but of damp ash-lice and lichen.

Behind them, on the cooling basalt where your own footprint still holds warmth, is the hunter.

You won't see it until it moves, because it is flat. A mat of muscle and small stones held in place by papillae, exactly the color of desert varnish. It has no legs. It moves by flowing. It presses itself into cracks and waits for vibration. When a stilt-grazer steps too close, it doesn't pounce. It surges. It smothers. A silent envelopment that ends in a minute of faint struggling underneath. Then the flat thing thins again and is gone. All of this — lichen to grazer to beetle to smotherer — happens inside one pan, inside one afternoon of artificial rain. Then the pan dries, the plates crack again, and everything that hasn't burrowed or encysted or run dies, or pretends to die until the next sigh.

That is the city between the cities.

And then there are the ones that walk.

You will not see a walking one for days, if ever, and you will hear it for an hour before it arrives. The whole plateau is an instrument for low sound. The reports of the sessile ones — thump, sigh, clank — are small percussion. The walkers are the big drum.

CHUFF...

A long silence.

CHUFF... with a squeal underneath, not metal, but the sound metal makes when it remembers being alive.

They are the same as the others, but grown beyond restraint. On four, sometimes six, legs that are not legs but articulated cooling towers, jointed backwards, each footfall a compactor press that leaves a print a meter deep and six across. The furrow fills later with water from its own sides and becomes a wadi. The body is lifted high enough that you can walk under it and think you are under a thunderhead. Its belly glows. Through thinner ventral plates, you can see the cherry-red of a continuous internal melt. You can feel the heat coming off it from half a kilometer away like walking toward a kiln.

It does not graze wind. It grazes *places*. It has consumed its local ash and needs richer ground, the lees of some atmospheric eddy. It moves a kilometer a day, sometimes less. Where it steps, the ground rings hollow because a century of its kind have tunneled the tuff with heat and weight. Its rain is real rain. Water pours off its knees, its flanks, in hot cataracts, feeding a green-black track behind it that persists for weeks. Beetle-things and stilt things follow those tracks in pilgrimage.

On the leeward side of every sessile animal, whether walker or sitter, is its offering: the slag heap. Black glass, still contracting, still ticking and pinging as it cools, shot through with strange, brief crystals — fayalite, ferrosilite, things that have no names because they only exist here, in the gut of something that makes its own geology. The wiregrass needs that slag to root. The tick-beetle needs it to harden its enamel. The whole halo ecology is built on refined excrement.

Nothing here was built. Nothing here has an owner or a product. No one tends the furnaces and no one takes the ingots, because there are no ingots. Only more heat, more water, more transient green. They smelt the sky not to make anything, but because smelting the sky is how they live. The plateau is a factory floor so vast it has weather, running in the purple dark for geological ages on its own heat.

If you stay still long enough, in the warm dark between breathing domes, with the smell of wet iron and hot stone, you stop hearing them as separate animals.

You hear one long district, exhaling, condensing, ticking as it cools.
