The first time you look in from the levee road you think it's people.

Two or three of them. Maybe four. In patched coats and rubber boots to the knee, moving slow because there is nowhere to move fast. You can't see their faces under the hoods. They seem to live there, in the sense that their boots are there and their gloves are on the line and they never leave the bowl.

If you watch longer you realize that's wrong.

There are two or three that *stay* — but that's not the population. The staying ones aren't even really people. They're places where staying happens. One is always near the breaker boxes. One is always by the little gantry that runs back and forth on its three meters of curved track. One sleeps in the tin shed and leaves the bare bulb on.

The rest — and the rest is the actual town — doesn't stay. It comes and goes and comes again. That's the business.

Watch what they're building:

For a little while, there's a stack somewhere near the center, a neat, square, black little stack of cut brick — peat that has been cut and pressed into shape. All identical. Cool and damp. The stack gets to about knee high and then, while you watch, it starts to sag. The bottom brick goes soft. The whole thing slumps into the water it came from. It melts.

Then the crane shudders and goes to make another.

That's what the pit does. It makes things out of itself that are almost solid, and then those things dissolve again.

The workers are the same way.

You start to count them and you fail, because there is no way to count them. There's the hundred identical black work gloves strung on the drying lines, for example. Each one is a hand, but is it a person? There's the electric motors, pig-sized, wrapped in wet tarps, that whine under water until they burn out and are replaced by another identical motor. Is that a person? There's the chain-falls hanging from the gallows-timber, swinging because someone brushed past even though no one brushed past — just water passing through.

If you push close together the things that have to exist for work to happen, they start to look like one thing with many elbows.

Here, close together is the law.

To get from one shed to another you have to turn sideways between a transformer that has been hot for twenty years and a stack of new chain still greased from the factory. You have to step over a sluice that is six inches deep and has no visible bottom because the peat water in it is that black. You have to duck under the glove-line. Everything touches. Everything has learned the shape of everything else exactly, because it has to.

That rubbing has dyed them all one color. When you first saw the pit from the levee you thought rust and old wood. Up close you see that nothing is rust and nothing is wood anymore. It's just coated. A thin, identical film of black peat silt over everything — the tin walls, the rubber boots, the concrete-mixer drum that is now a water tank, the few bleached sedge tufts brave enough to grow through a pallet crack. All one dark, wet brown-black. If you took one outside, you could tell it was a glove or a motor or a man, but inside you can't.

That's how they live.

They are not fed, exactly. Water feeds the place. It weeps in through the clay walls, clear, from the peat fields outside, and it wells up from below, black and old. There's always more of it than you need. It lifts things. It carries the loose brick dust from one corner of the bowl to sink it in another. The people — the few permanent ones in patched coats — don't actually haul much themselves. They open a sluice. They start a pump. The pump throws a black fountain into a pond that wasn't there and then some other pond drains between the duckboards and whistles.

Everything is always mid-motion but nothing ever crashes because water doesn't let things crash. It buffers. It hushes. The motors are wrapped to keep them dry and the wrapping makes them quiet. The brick is too wet to clack. The people, living in that bath of tap... tap... tap... of condensation from the tin roofs onto flooded sheet steel, have gone quiet too. Their work is listening for a change in pitch when a cut clogs, or for that harsh suck when a board lifts and the mud underneath decides to take it.

You'd think an animal would live there — it looks like a swamp. But it doesn't. The heron knows. Every few days one comes and stands on the levee — long legs, high up where it can see the whole 300 meters across — and looks down at all that wet, black diligence. It looks for a place to stand where it won't sink through pallets into bottomless mud, where it won't have to share space with a sputtering breaker box. It can't find one. There's no shore inside the pit, only work, and very close work. So it leaves.

What lives there instead is what can live touching.

What lives there is a crowd that is also a single thing. A settlement that is also a stirring. If you had to give it names — and you don't, at first — you'd call some of them weavers, some haulers, some cutters, some guards who just stand to say this corner shall be a pond for a minute. But they trade jobs constantly. A glove becomes a gasket. A pallet becomes a dam becomes a floor again. The pile of cut brick that melted in the south corner yesterday is the pile that is being formed in the north corner today, and it will melt again.

They have been doing this so long that the turbulence has stopped being an event and has become an address. The gullies that bring water down the bowl wall have been in exactly the same braids for a hundred years. The puddle that always forms in the middle, no matter how many times they lay a new steel plate over it, is the same puddle your grandfather would have seen if he stood on this same levee. The disturbance is permanent. The settlement is settled, not because it has finished, but because it has agreed to never finish.

Nothing is ever hauled out over the levee road. In normal work, finished things leave. Here, evaporation can never get ahead of the seep. Cold keeps things from rotting. Wet keeps bringing in new silt, new black, new gloves. Work keeps shuffling it around. So the pit fills, half-sunk, to the brim with its own essential, unfinished things, and stays lit.

At dusk you can see it best. The gray goes flat. The sky has no color left. But in each tin shed the single bulb comes on, because the work never stops, and the light reflects three times in the black water between boards and a fourth time, blurred, in the sweated-out condensation on the inside of the tin. From the levee road the whole dent — that awkward, pressed-thumb dent in the flat, flat peat — looks like a shallow room sunk below grade, crowded, humming low, spilling quietly over with light and water and hands doing near-touching work.
