It does not live *on* the pan. It *is* the pan.

At first that's what you think when your eyes adjust to the violet dark and the breathing of the ground resolves. There's no colony here in the way ants are a colony. There's no hive with workers coming and going. This is a single thickness, a living pavement, and the idea of an individual only becomes useful when you crouch down close enough to see a seam.

They are about the size of a man's fist when it's closed tight to keep something inside. If you picked one up — you won't, for a while — you'd find it is heavier than it looks, dense with conserved water and superheated wax. No eyes. Nothing that would be wasted on a place where for practical purposes there is never light. The whole dorsal side is a single dome of chitin, heat-polished until it shines like poured basalt, but thin at the edges. In the ones near the middle, that shell is translucent amber, not black, and you can see the heat inside them.

Six legs, but you’ll never really see all six. They are folded under like a pill bug's, but not for rolling. For wedging. Each leg ends not in a claw but in a broad, spatulate pad fringed with stiff hairs. Hairs that interlock with the hairs of the neighbor. So nothing falls through. The dust never sifts up because they do not let it. They make a seal.

They have no name down where there are trees. Up here, the only thing that names them is the heat.

Call them the scales, for now.

Their world is vertical, even though it looks flat. The bottom is too hot to stay and the top is too cold and too hungry to stay, and so their entire life is a slow turning. A convection cell made of meat.

At the rim, the old ones die and don't leave. They lock their legs. Their domes bleach to the color of ash and starlight and crack open in a lace of pores. They become the filter. Everything the high dark air carries — and this mesa is high enough that the wind never stops giving, even when you can't feel it — catches on them. Distant ash from a volcano three ranges over. Pollen grains that blew up too high and died and dried. The desiccated husks of midges from the forests below, lifted by thermals until they freeze and drift like snow. Salt. Iron rust. Sulfur crystals that smell like struck matches.

It collects. For years. A dry snow of nothing. And then underneath, living ones with a mouth you can't see — a soft, inverted slit on the belly — extend a feathered tongue and comb it. *Rasp. Rasp.* That susurrus you hear when you hold your breath, that brushing-velvet-wrong-way sound, is ten thousand mouths doing that at once. They do not chew. They dissolve. They farm bacteria on their own undersides, bacteria that live on stone-exhalation and ammonia, and then they lick the bacteria off.

That's what they eat: windfall and each other's breath.

Moisture is so precious here that sound itself is rationed. They have stridulatory ridges on their keeled backs — you can see them if you let your eyes stay dark-adapted — but they almost never use them. A chirp would cost water with the vibration. So they talk in pressure. When one needs to move, it pushes, and the push travels as a wave through the packing. You feel it through your boots as a tick, a settling. The whole pan learns where is crowded and where is empty by that tide of touch.

And they are always moving, but you will not see it move. You have to mark a spot — choose one with a chipped dome, a scar — stare until your knees ache, and you will find ten minutes later it is a thumb's width clockwise and a fingernail inward. 

Inward, because the pan itself is hot. Not from the sun. The sun hasn't cleared the dust here in a geological age. From below. The crust is thin right here, a blister that failed to become a volcano. Put your hand on the bare basalt at the very center, if you can find a gap between bodies, and you will pull it back swearing. It will cook.

So they circulate. The ones who have been in the deep middle too long, their wax starting to smoke, their internal water starting to boil out through their joints, become restless and climb. They do not walk over the top — there is no top — they lever themselves up between neighbors who loosen just enough to let them pass and then tighten again. Millimeter by millimeter, they work rimward, toward the cold where the dead filters are and the food is lean but the air won't kill them.

And the cold ones, the hungry ones, the gray-bellied ones who have been out on the rim living on dust and patience until their guts are hollow, they slip inward, down the gentle slope, deeper, into the rich dark where the stone exhales and the heat means life is faster. They go to eat, and to be eaten.

Because nothing really dies here except by becoming food. Even the dead filter at the rim is eventually rasped thin and collapses and is carried inward as fragments held in wax.

There is no male and female in the way you'd count. There are just the many, and then, anchored, the few.

You saw the built things. Three, maybe four, low circles of stacked plate-stone, ankle-high, fused solid now with generations of black wax. At first you think a man did it. A shepherd, a lost climber making a windbreak. But no man has ever carried enough water to stay here long enough to stack that stone. 

The circles were made by the scales themselves, a long time ago when the behavior was different, or by whatever the scales used to be before this mesa was lifted out of the world.

Inside one of those circles, the pattern breaks.

The bodies in there are not black. They are bigger by half, pale like old ivory, with domes so thin you can see right through them. Each holds a dull amber coal inside itself, not bright, not bioluminescent — just hot. Really, physically hot, retained heat like a brick from an oven. They don't move. They can't; they are rooted, their spatula pads grown into the rock and into each other with wax that has become cement.

They are the ovens. The queens, if you need a word. The cisterns.

They don't eat the windfall. They are fed by the circulation. Workers pause over them and regurgitate a clear, salty oil. In return, deep inside the glow, you can see if you kneel and shade your eyes and look very close: dozens of perfect, glassy spheres, like beads of dark honey, each holding a curled white comma. Eggs. They cook there, low and slow, for years. They need that constant, brutal 60, 70 degrees that would kill the others. So the others make a place for them and bring heat to them by crowding, and bring food to them by dying.

And when they hatch, what comes out is not a small version. It is almost liquid, a pale mite that slips instantly into the interstices between its elders' bodies and disappears into the pavement. You will never see a juvenile unless you tear the pavement up, and you should not.

That is what lives here. Not a swarm. One thing that learned how to be many things so it could turn heat into patience. A living basalt that breathes once an hour and turns once a day, filtering the sky that has forgotten it, cooking its own children in its own belly, silent because silence keeps water.

If you stand in it long enough to be trusted — which is to say, long enough to be the same temperature — the ones from the hot middle will find your boots are a new kind of middle. Not too hot, not too cold. They will climb, a few centimeters onto the leather, and stop, and hold. Not biting. Not claiming. Just adding you, for a little while, to the circulation.
