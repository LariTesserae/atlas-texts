They don't live here so much as they precipitate here.

That's the first thing you have to understand. Nothing arrived. Nothing migrated up from below, because below is a thousand kilometers of cloud and falling before you hit anything that could evolve a leg. At this altitude there is no upwind. Whatever lives here had to condense out of the conditions the way the water does — out of warm rock and cold air and time.

The city did first.

You want to call them builders but there are no builders left to see, or maybe there never were in the way you mean. The city is the organism. Not the buildings — the street.

It's a thermo-vore. A mineral-vore. Its skin is that matte, grown ceramic that isn't fired but cultured, a silica-alumina lattice that holds a charge the way bone holds marrow. It warms itself by drinking the temperature difference between the fresh basalt underneath, which is still 40C a meter down, and the -60 air sluicing over the top. It pushes heat through itself with those slow lungs, those house-sized exchangers, and wherever heat moves, water moves, and wherever water moves it leaves a little dissolved nickel, iron, sulfur, hydrogen behind. The city plates that out inside its own body like a reef plates calcium.

It heals because it is always growing. A flake spalls off and the damp air touches a signaling layer and it fuzzes and re-knits. No lights, no windows, because it has no interior in the animal sense. It is all gut and skin and heat exchange. The trenches you thought were streets are its sulci. The catenary arches that make your teeth ache are its cilia, holding shape by vibrating slightly out of phase with gravity. It is warm, dark, and patient. It is the only thing here that counts as a native plant: it roots into heat.

And on it, the guests.

**The Film.**

Before you even touch the visible animals, you are already covered. The sheen on the basalt, the slick on your glove — that's not just water. It's a biofilm so dense it has texture. Trillions of things the size of dust, each one a glass bead with a flagellum and a fragment of magnetite inside for ballast. They don't photosynthesize, there's no light worth the chemistry. They oxidize. Dissolved H2S from the rock, Fe2+ from the fresh glass, a little ammonia that outgasses from deep melt. They excrete a kind of sour polysaccharide that keeps them from freezing. That slime is the prairie here. It's the base of everything. It grows at millimeters per day.

**The Mat.**

Then the things you can see. You thought isopod, shrimp, trilobite and you're not wrong, but only because on Earth that is the cheapest shape for "flat thing that scrapes and doesn't want to be eaten."

The vast majority mass here is what you'd call the Scrape. Pale, eyeless, blind in a way deeper than no-eyes — they never had a lineage that needed eyes to lose. Their backs are translucent opal, you can see the hot water moving in their gut, grey-green. They are made of overlapping plates, not for defense — nothing hunts — but to hold water against their bodies, to keep that warm chemistry close while they press themselves into the Film.

Their mouths are underneath, a rasp of natural silica glass, combing the slime off the city and the basalt at a rate you can hear if you put your helmet near: *tik-tik-tik.* One alone makes nothing. Ten thousand laid flange to flange in a channel turn the water black for a moment while they pass, and the sound becomes that continent-roar you heard. They don't socialize. They aggregate because warm water trickles. The stream is warmest, so they all go there. Then the stream moves, following a slow cooling in the rock, and the whole mat pours itself in that direction at 0.3 meters an hour. You think it's still. Come back in six hours and your footprints are full of them, polishing the basalt clean.

The small hoppers in the air — the granular air — are their larvae and their parasites and their cleaners, all at once. Nothing here has invented metamorphosis, it has invented size-sharing. The adults filter, the young ride and eat the adult's waste wax, then drop off when they are heavy enough to scrape.

If you stand still they climb you because you are 37C and leaking salt. They are not curious. You are a vent that got up and walked.

**The Stones.**

And then, if you wait long enough to be sure what is building and what is animal, you see which boulders are moving.

They are suitcase to mid-size-car. Low, domed, crusted with older Film that has mineralized black, making them almost indistinguishable from the basalt until they sigh. No legs. A skirt of cilia, or maybe feet that have forgotten to be feet, that exude acid just strong enough to make the rock slippery. They are sounders and sinks. They park over the warmest spot they can find, open a ventral slit, and just sit for months, drinking heat by direct conduction. When the rock beneath cools a tenth of a degree, they start to glide to the next warm spot. They leave a polished trough behind them that the city then has to self-repair around, slowly. In a decade they will cross one street. Their clicking is not clicking, it is the creak of their plates expanding and contracting. One voice in that chorus is fifty years old.

Nothing here breeds fast. Nothing here dies fast either. Ice will sometimes peel the Mat off in a slab and dump it into a low pan where it freezes solid. In spring — a spring that lasts nine Earth months, a 2-degree temperature bump — it thaws and keeps scraping as if nothing happened.

You are looking for an owner, for who made the city and who owns these herds. There isn't one anymore, if there ever was. The city keeps making heat because it knows how. The Mat keeps covering the heat because that's where the taste is. And the Stones keep moving, a hundred-year survey of the warmest place to sit on a world that's still being born.

It's all one occupation.
