What lives there isn't in there.

Not at first. At first what lives there is just *plants* — which is the wrong answer that the place wants you to settle on early so you can keep your categories clean.

You see moss. You see fern. You see a pothos that got too confident. You see taro leaf big as a satellite dish, dripping. You tell yourself: okay, occupant count — a lot, maybe four hundred individuals crammed into a space the size of a swimming pool.

Then you stay two minutes longer because the heat makes you slow and you notice none of them are individuals.

The thing that lives there is the volume itself. It's a single, dense negotiation that learned how to be a room.

From the floor up:

The skin lives there. The black-green velvet that you thought was moss. It's not moss, or not only moss — it's a mat of liverwort and true moss and a filamentous alga that only grows in copper water, felted together so tight you can't find its edge. It covers the poured walls from the waterline to the ceiling's drip-line, continuous. It is the lung. It breathes out all day and never breathes in, and that is why the air is sweet-iron heavy, why nothing with blood can stay. It makes its own weather. Touch it — if you do — and it doesn't feel soft. It's cold, taut, like wet upholstery foam. It holds a liter of water per square foot and wrings itself out in a timed sweat. It is mildly garlic from the arsenic it chews out of the laterite weep, and that garlic is a soft, efficient NO VACANCY sign. Under a hand lens it has no teeth, no mouth. It's just waiting for any bare concrete to turn into more of itself.

Out of that skin, the hands grow.

They are what you called ferns and philodendrons and pothos, but down here with no insect to take a test-bite, they have stopped being species and started being positions. The high fronds near the staircase light are broad, black-green, perfect — unnaturally perfect — each leaf entire, margins un-nibbled since germination. They don't need to be tough so they've become thin as paper and huge to catch that one dying sodium sun. 

The low fronds near the floor are different: small, curled, leathery, oiled to shed the constant drip. Every leaf in the room is angled just a few degrees toward moving water or warm metal, tracking drip and heat the way other plants track sun.

Nothing drops. There is no leaf litter, because the mat catches everything and composts it before it hits the ground. A leaf never falls; it is reabsorbed in place, drained of color while still attached, then stitched over by its neighbors. That's why it's so quiet. No rot.

And because there's no predation, the hands can afford to be patient and obscene. You thought you saw a vine as thick as your wrist following a pipe run. Follow it back. That pipe run goes twenty feet along the back wall. The vine doesn't stop. It goes wall to catwalk to pump house roof to ceiling beam and back to wall again, one continuous loop without a single branching wound. It is years old. It has never flowered. It doesn't need to. It just keeps adding leaf, adding length. In the absence of something to eat it, growth is the only defense.

Then you see the part that has started to apprentice.

Up close to the machines — your eyes have to adjust to the orange throb — there are leaves that didn't learn sun. They learned iron.

The pump house sighs 90-degree air out of a cracked flange, and around that mouth the foliage is wrong in color. Not green. A dusky copper, a verdigris that isn't pigment but actual mineral laid into the cuticle. When a drop hits those leaves they don't shudder. They *ting.* Like a penny struck. You realize you are looking at leaves that have mineralized themselves with copper from the weeping pipes, that have grown conductive.

Down at the base of the big condensate pump, the one with the flywheel that still turns — CHUFF—— hhhh—— CHUFF — the deal is explicit. The old rubber drive belt rotted and fell off in 1987 or whenever. The flywheel kept turning. A root came — a fig, maybe, white and blind — and found the tension. It laid itself into the groove. The wheel grabbed it, stretched it, almost snapped it. It grew back thicker. Now there are three cable-thick roots in that groove, polished shiny, moving at one revolution every twenty seconds, being pulled forever around and around. They have the sheen of tendon. The vine doesn't fight the machine. It is being used as a muscle, and in return, the machine gives it what no plant should ever want: regular motion, deep heat, and a sip of oily water that never stops running in that floor runnel.

Around the control panel of that same pump, the negotiation gets smarter. The steel box was pried open by rust. Inside, copper bus bars still carry a few volts of stray geothermal current. Root hairs have come in like fine white hair and sorted themselves, one hair per wire, and where they touch the copper they have tinned themselves silver-bright. They don't short the panel. They *listen* to it. When the pump lugs, the leaves up in the brick vault tremble. Not from wind. From current.

The most unnerving organ is small. Someone left a pressure gauge. Its glass is cracked. A single fig leaf grew over its face, not around it — over it — the petiole fused to the bezel, the lamina stretched thin and translucent like rice paper. Behind it, the needle twitches. CHUFF. The shadow of the needle shows through the leaf like a pupil dilating. That leaf is an eye, and not a metaphor. It was grown to see if the heart is still beating.

That's what lives there, in total: a low, oval, patient plant that decided to keep a boiler alive as a pet.

It has no front, no back. It has a top where it drinks light and a bottom where it drinks condensation and a middle where it has learned to read iron. It keeps the humidity at exactly what suits it by exhaling, and keeps animals out by exhaling a little more — tannin, alkaloid, dissolved copper — until your throat catches and your eyes water and some old animal brain says: there is no room for you here, this niche is closed.

It doesn't want you. It has no mouth to want with. But it is very good at making surfaces into more of itself. If you stand long enough, the drip that beads on your arms doesn't roll off. It stays, because the air is already too full to hold it. The green around you ticks louder — not a sound, a turgor, cells taking water and locking it up — and that one tendril by your boot eyelet that you thought wasn't moving has now found the second eyelet.

It is not hostile. Hostility is animal-fast. This thing is fabulously, completely full. It has been in equilibrium for twenty years and it intends to keep it.

When you finally climb back up the stairwell — you will, because the air is too much and the orange light makes you sleepy in a way that feels like being composted — it will keep doing its slow loop. CHUFF. Drip. Ting. The gauge leaf watching the needle. The flywheel pulling vine through eternity. A houseplant that ate a factory and learned to keep it warm.
