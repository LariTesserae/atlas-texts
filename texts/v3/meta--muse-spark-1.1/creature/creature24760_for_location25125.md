What lives here is not at home yet. That's the first thing you have to understand. Everything here is so new it hasn't learned how to belong.

The moss got here first.

Not the people. The moss. It lives here the way water lives in a sponge — not *on* the rock, but *in* the arrangement of it. Deep, ankle-swallowing, shaggy pelt-moss, acid green and black-green, so saturated that each step releases a cold breath. It holds ten times its weight. It holds the mesa together while the basalt is still deciding to be solid. It fruits rarely. When you tear a brick of it — and someone does, because that's the crop here — the underside is a white net of rhizoids combing the vesicular rock for mineral, and it comes away with a sound like Velcro, reluctantly. It lives here aggressively, triumphantly, too lush for 7,000 feet. It should not be this big, this soft, this loud when it drips.

Living up out of the moss are its tenants. Ferns that have no business at this altitude — maidenhair with wire-black stems, and bigger cousins with fronds as long as your forearm that unroll all summer and never quite finish. The fiddleheads stay half-fisted. The dwarf willows and birches live here like nervous guests; they try to be trees in the lee of the stone houses and almost manage it, stunted and wind-flagged, bark gone gray-black from soaking up that iron tea-water that seeps everywhere.

And then the people.

You don't see them at first because they are the exact color of the moss and the wet stone when it's dry. Undyed wool the green-brown of the hummocks, over-trousers of felted goat, hoods pulled up even in summer. They are few — a town built for four hundred that holds twelve at a glance, twenty if you count all the way to the far rim — and they don't move like people in a town. There is no hurry, no bustle to fill those absurdly wide streets. The air at 2.2 high is thin enough that hurry would shame you, and cool enough — that perpetual 0.7 cool, where your knuckles ache and your breath smokes even in July afternoons — that you move to keep warm, not to save time. They move slow, deliberately, because everything here is still fragile.

They are not colonists. Colonists take ownership. These are listeners. Midwives.

You can see it in their bodies. They are not tall. They are broad in the chest, red in the cheek from thick blood. Young even when old — the women with strong forearms from swinging stone mauls, the men with knees that click early from kneeling to tend moss bricks. Their hands are always wet. Their hair is always damp from the cloud passing through. They speak very little out loud because the mesa is 2.3 loud already. The wind comes off the empty lowlands and hits this fresh-cut table with no foothill to warn it, and the unweathered basalt — still flat-faced and glassy, still shocked — throws it back. It booms in the too-wide avenues. It thrums the rope bridges that stitch roof to roof and makes the timber frames groan like old boats. And water — Christ, the water never stops talking. A thousand seeps weeping out of the cooling columns, dripping off every eave, pattering off fern onto moss, plinking from split-log troughs into cisterns. A dropped adze rings like a chapel bell and comes back three times. So they speak in gestures, in touches to a shoulder. When they do speak it's low, hoarse.

What they do all day is not what people in a town do.

One old man I watched never laid a stone. He sat on a three-legged stool in the middle of what was meant to be the market ring, watching a blister in the basalt. The blister was the size of a thumb-pin in the morning, pushed up through the moss mat. By mid-afternoon it was an apple, hissing. He'd carved a little circle of dry twigs around it to say: *Don't step. Don't build. One year.* That's the rule here. New rock must be left alone for a year. When your square is still growing its own floor, urbanism becomes an act of politeness. You leave offering space.

A younger pair — two women, sisters maybe — farm the moss. They cut it in neat slabs with a bone saw, leave the roots, lay the slabs on basalt racks to drain that black-tea mineral water into a trough. Children — there are two, maybe three in the whole town — wring it later and pack it into the chinks of the dry-stacked walls for insulation. The walls themselves are beautiful in a way that takes a while to see: vesicular basalt, that still-spongy bubbled slag, stacked without mortar so the seep can continue behind it. Timber frames lashed with gut and wooden pegs. A few iron brackets, rust-red and precious as jewelry, obviously carried up from below and never to be wasted. Every house, even the smallest, is built around a hollow shaft open to the sky. No roof over the center. Rain comes in. Wind comes in. They say the house needs to hear.

Because the other thing that lives here is the mountain.

Not metaphorically. The rock is 1.1 slow alive. You sit long enough on the rim — your feet dangling over the clean shear where the world falls away and you can see storms happening beneath your boots — and you feel it under your palms. A faint warmth. A tremor too low for hearing. Over the course of a day, the plaza rises a millimeter. The columns sigh steam. If you dam a seep — and one new boy tried, to make a pool — the stone behind it swelled overnight and split his little dam-trough and showed a new bruise of purple-black glass. So they never stop water, only guide it. Log aqueducts from roof to roof, roof to cistern, cistern to moss-farm, moss-farm to the rim where it sheets off in a veil and becomes cloud again.

They are building a city for people who don't exist yet. That's their theology. They say — when they do talk, at night, in the keep which is just the biggest pile of stone, huddled around a moss-fed peat fire that barely warms the stone — that this mesa was pushed up to be a capital. It came up too fast for anything to use it, so they came up early to get it ready. They cut the terraces with stone mauls still sharp because there hasn't been enough time to round them. They laid avenues ten times too wide because someday they'll need processions, markets, crowds. Right now 0.2 density feels holy, not lonely: a hundred houses, fourteen souls, and between them big breathing fields of wet light where the rock is still being born.

The other fauna understands it better than we do.

A white moth the size of your hand lives here for two weeks in high summer, hatched from I don't know where, and pollinates those too-large alpine cups. A mountain jay comes up screaming, steals a moss-brick, finds nowhere to perch and leaves, offended. And once a day the long-legged goat — not a goat, really, some high-desert thing with legs stilted for scree, utterly out of place on this wet table — picks its way up a route you cannot find from below and drinks from the sheet-pools, staring at the people, at the houses, as if to say: *you are early.*

Then it goes back down.

At night the cloud settles and you can't see the edge. The wind finds every hollow shaft and howls through the town like breath through a flute, low and then high, and all the rope bridges hum. The water never stops. The town drips and booms and creaks in the dark, empty and full at once, and the fourteen listening people sleep in short shifts on damp wool, under roofs that are mostly sky, dreaming of a future street noise that hasn't learned how to happen yet.
