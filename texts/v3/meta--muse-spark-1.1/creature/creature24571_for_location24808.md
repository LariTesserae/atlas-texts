Nothing lives here, at first. That's the report the place makes about itself. 

If you come down the switchback road and stand on the top terrace and look out over the sheet-water, the amber lights, the rain that makes itself out of its own exhaust, your first thought is that this is a place built to exclude living. Too cold, too loud, too sharp. Too new. But then your eyes adjust to the dim and you realize it isn't empty, it's just spaced out. Whatever lives here lives far apart on purpose, the way the lights do.

The oldest residents are not the animals at all.

They are the film. The black mold and biofilm that lives in every seam the crews left behind. In the expansion joints between slabs the size of city blocks, in the pores of the raw concrete, in the threaded interior of every tie-hole. It came down on boots and on wind and it found water and cold and no competition. It spreads as a soot, as a slick, as a soft beard on the underside of pipes. If you drag your finger along a terrace wall you'll come away black and smelling faintly of iron and earth. It drinks the water that never stops weeping from the walls. It thinks, in the slow way that film thinks, that this whole valley is a cliff face after a thaw, and it is right. It is the one thing here that is truly at home.

A little higher up in the order of things, the marsh holds.

Not the marsh someone planned — there was no plan for marsh — but the few acres of silt traps between the manufactories and the dry-ish margin. The cattails live there. And to live there you have to love being cold-stunted and wind-beaten. They are only knee high, their stalks pale green going to almost white, their brown heads never quite fattening, never opening right. They shiver constantly in the push from the flywheels. They put their feet in the standing inch of water that never warms. They have not spread. Each clump has tested the concrete world beyond the silt and decided no. So they stay huddled together, ten or twenty to a pan, a small, stubborn, shivering village that has learned to live off condensation and the iron in the concrete dust. They are the only upright, rooted thing for a half mile in any direction.

In the water that the cattails guard — and in the water everywhere else, the great broken mirror of the basin floor — lives the next layer. Midges. Clouds of them, or what would be clouds if there were enough warmth to let them cloud. Here they rise in ones and twos, a thin, cold hatch that barely gets six feet off the sheet-water before the cold and the draft from the condensers knocks them back down. They live a few hours, frantic, breed, die, and settle on the surface. Their bodies make a faint stipple on the water between your boot-prints. If you crouch and stay still, you can see the larvae underneath, in the inch-deep warmish-scum between slabs, wriggling in water that vibrates to the double-beat of the sheds. They are the food of nothing yet, except, accidentally, of the film.

Then the things that came down with people and stayed after people mostly left.

One rat. Or one kind of rat, repeated a few times, with so much space between copies you could be forgiven for thinking there's only one. She is large and grey-brown and her coat never quite dries. She has learned the architecture perfectly. She doesn't drink from the sheet on the floor — that's too open, too full of machine wind and light. She drinks from the eyes. From the tie-holes. She will sit up on a twenty-foot terrace ledge in the gloom between lamps, forepaws on the concrete, and press her mouth to the little clear spring coming out of the wall, whiskers beaded with that cold spring water. She has a circuit. Twenty or thirty good holes that run reliably, and she runs them. She knows where the warm air pours off the back of a shed. She knows where a dropped work glove never got picked up and made a nest.

Above her, in the gantry that has no load, lives the pigeon. Just the one. A blue-grey rock dove with concrete dust in his feathers until he is nearly the color of the sky-ceiling. He has made a nest out of cable ties, fly-wheel fiber, and the fluff from cattail heads in the crook of an I-beam sixty feet up, right where the hook of the gantry drips. He is the only thing that regularly sees the valley from above. He leaves in the morning through the gap where the service road enters — no one has ever seen him go — and he comes back in the evening with something in his crop. He coos and the sound is lost immediately under the slap and whine.

And then, because ten house-sized flywheels running in corrugated sheds are a kind of animal, they live here too. Not alive, but not not-alive. Each one has a name stenciled on its block, ENGINE 3, ENGINE 7, but the names have been half covered by condensation tags. They breathe. They take in the cold wet air, shake the water out of it, and breathe it out colder and wetter. They have hot backs that steam and cold fronts that drip icicles in the middle of their own summer. They turn and turn and turn because no one told them to stop. They are the heart of the place, and the lungs, and the weather. The mold lives off them, the midges live off the water they make, the rat loafs in their lee, the cattails shiver in their wind. If you put your hand on the rail near one you can feel it in your molars. They do not know they have tenants.

On paper there is still one more resident, and sometimes you see evidence of him before you see him. A dry path through the sheet-water where someone squeegeed, or a shed door that was closed last time you looked and is now open a crack, breathing warm, yellow, human light onto the slab. A pair of insulated overalls hung over a sodium stalk to dry, dripping faster than the concrete around it. A mug with a skin of cold tea in it.

He is a maintenance man, or he was. He lives up on the top ring in what was supposed to be a temporary site office — a little box of chipboard and heater inside one of the manufactories, between two of the flywheels so the double beat becomes a single roar inside and you can almost not hear it. He has a cot, a hot plate, a row of boots drying. He stays because someone has to check the tie-hole springs aren't clogging, because the sump pumps want a filter shaken, because if the doors ice up the rain can't fall right. Or he stays because after a year of listening to this bowl, you can no longer hear yourself think anywhere else. 

He doesn't walk much in the middle. No one needs to. He walks the edges where the water meets the steel, where the rat is, where the mold is thick. He knows the pigeon by sight. He doesn't try to make anything more alive here. He doesn't plant more cattails. He knows this place is young, that its corners are still sharp enough to cut a glove, and that it will need a long, long time before anything soft can take hold and spread. In the meantime, he keeps the rain going.
