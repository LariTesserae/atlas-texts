What lives here are Catchers.

Everything that managed to stay lives by the second rule: nothing falls. Not water, not heat, not a body, not a chance.

The oldest thing that lives here isn't alive in a way you'd count at first. It's the white lichen. It lives *in* the rock, not on it. It found the leeward pits of the basalt where wind has polished itself tired, and it laid itself down as a crust, as a stain. It doesn't grow so much as it waits. It tastes iron. When the towers sweat, it goes briefly, shockingly green at the edges, like a lit fuse, and then bleaches itself white again to survive noon. It is keeping score. You can read the years off it by how far it has eaten into the brick.

The second oldest are the wells.

That's what the wire-grass really is. It is almost all root. Up top it is two blades, thin as a needle, quivering, nothing to eat, not worth the water it would cost to eat it. Below, it is a fist, then a rope, then a river system going down thirty feet into a cold crack in the world. There is one plant for every twenty square yards because there is only enough deep water for that. They never touch each other. An ironwood shrub is just a wire-grass that got ambitious, that decided to keep some of itself above ground. Its trunk is polished by wind and by lizards, dark as old iron, and its leaves are tiny black coins that turn sideways to the sun.

Then the Gutter People.

They look like beetles until you get close, and then they look like what a beetle would build if it had a forge. The Enamelbacks. Big as the last joint of your thumb, domed, black so deep it's blue at the edge. Their elytra are not wings, they're lids. They seal. Underneath, they keep a private humidity. They spend the whole night climbing, shoulder to shoulder, up the fins of the condensers, up the legs of the A-frames, up the lip of the stone cistern. They fight without fighting — just pressure, just a slow, inexorable shoving for the best drip line. At dawn, when the cold-coils tick and the air gives up, they stop, and tilt. Water beads on their backs and runs into the channels between their shoulders, into their mouths. They drink from themselves. When the sun gets teeth, they fall — except nothing falls here — they let go and flutter down two feet on those little hidden wings and wedge themselves under an overhang, under each other, a living shingle. You hear them walking, a faint dry scratching, like paper being shuffled.

Under them, under the shingle, live the Rustbacks. Lizards the exact color of rusted brass, with translucent eyelids and throats that you can see water move through. They don't bask. That's suicide. They live in the negative of the towers — the space under a drip pipe, the slit where two basalt ropes didn't quite knit. Three deep, four deep, stacked like spoons, breathing together to keep the air damp between them. They are cold when you touch them, which you shouldn't. That cold is their wealth. They let the beetles walk over them because the beetles drip. Their job is patience. They will stay in that stack until the next day's sweat, and if it doesn't come, the one on the bottom will die first and its water will be divided.

And then there are the ones that make you look twice because they look engineered. The Copperthreads. Not spiders, not harvestmen, but something that learned the same bridge-building trick. Legs like enameled copper wire, six feet across if they stretch, but weighing nothing. They live in the gutters of the towers themselves, in the right-angle where fin meets strut, and they string their legs across. In the morning their legs are beaded, every hair a dew point. They reel their own legs in, hand over hand, drinking. The Paperdrinkers live beside them — things that in full day look like folded brown wax paper caught in a bolt-hole. At 5:42 AM they unfold. They have wings that aren't wings, they're sails, thin and veined, that go cold faster than metal. They hold them out to the mist and they darken with water and then the Paperdrinker folds itself around its own drink and squeezes.

But all of that is the undercard. The bulk, the biomass, the noise that is almost not noise, is the Cliff.

They are birds, or were birds before they signed the lease. Condenser Swifts. High-altitude swifts, high-heat swifts. Their bodies are soot-black with a rust-colored throat patch that matches the lizards, matches the iron. Their feet are not feet anymore, they're grapples. Their saliva is cement. Every inch of every tower — every fin, every gutter, every catwalk, every rust flake that makes a ledge — is a nest. A thousand, ten thousand nests, cheek by jowl, one wall made of dried mud and beetle carapaces, one wall made of your neighbor's nest. They are one organism in the way a cliff of seabirds is one organism.

They have learned the third and fourth rules completely. It shouldn't be this hot up here, but it is, so they do most of their living in the blue cold slice before noon. At first light the whole basin boils up — not flies, birds, tens of thousands of them, so many that the air itself seems to have a texture, a moving thatch. They dip to the temporary creeks that only exist for forty minutes, they scoop without landing, they scream once, short, because sound costs water, and then they go back up to pass food mouth to mouth, to air-condition the chicks by panting on them. At ten, they vanish. Not away — *into*. Into the towers, into the iron itself, where the engines have made a permanent pocket of cold air and shadow. If you put your hand into one of the drip-loops at noon you feel air that raises goosebumps, and inside, in the dark, warm bodies clicking.

Nothing falls. A chick that shimmies out of its cup does not hit basalt. It hits another nest. A dead beetle does not reach the ground, it is cut up by Paperdrinkers before it lands. An egg that fails is eaten, shell and all, for calcium to hold the next nest to a vertical fin.

And the engines themselves — they are alive now. Thirty, forty feet tall, burning scrub-oil and low-grade seep, thudding so low you feel it in your teeth. They were left running by people who understood you don't own water here, you coax it. Their cold-coils are so cold they have frost in the desert. They tick at night like cooling dogs. The swifts have started bringing them gifts — a bright bottle cap, a strip of mica — lodging them in the fins. The beetles keep the algae from sealing their weeping seams.

No one lives here alone. Even the lichen needs the shadow of a beetle to survive one extra hour. What lives here is a single caught thing: a net made of wire and feather and root and rust, ten miles wide, strung across a basin that never was supposed to be hot, that never lets anything fall through.
