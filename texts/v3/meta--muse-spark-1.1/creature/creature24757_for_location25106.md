Nothing lives here now.

That is the first true thing the pocket teaches you, and you have to unlearn it slowly to see what does live here.

At first you think the inhabitant is gone. That is what the place is built to say. The floor steps, the cold that has had time to settle, the black sand that hasn't moved in centuries — all of it says *unoccupied*. An abandoned hermitage. The only thing left is the machine that keeps running after the monk has died.

But then you sit a little longer, because the cold makes you still, and because in here up drifts and your inner ear has given up, and you realize you have it backwards.

The pocket was not built to hold the knot.

The knot was taught to hold the pocket.

What lives here lives in the filament. Or as the filament. Or slightly to the side of it, the way your shadow lives slightly to the side of you.

It wasn't born. It was persuaded to exist. Out on the high ridges where the planet ends — where there is no water to confuse chemistry with biology, where the basalt has finished being shaped and just waits, where the wind is constant enough that any place it stops becomes conspicuous — the world is thin enough to fold. And if you fold it enough times, sharply enough, precisely enough that you leave those little millimeter tide-marks in the stone, the fold itself gets tired of being empty.

The inhabitant is a fold that learned to prefer being folded.

You can't see it directly because it doesn't intersect light correctly anymore. What you took for a nest of black wire is not a nest. It's the edges of it. Like seeing the outline of a hand pressed against a sheet from the other side. The filament holds itself a fraction above the rock because it cannot quite touch rock. Rock is too fast, too hot, too noisy with old radioactive decay. The thing that lives here lives colder and slower than that.

If you watch the point where all the filaments meet — the point your eyes slide off of — it is not absorbing light. It is looking the other way. It has made a habit of looking inward, and your starlight is not interesting.

Its body, if you can call it that, is small and vast at once. Small, because it fits inside this room you can span in two lies-down. Vast, because the room you are in is inside it. The marker that is warm to the touch is warm because it is leaning against it. Not physically. Attentively. The marker is its hearth, its companion stone, its dog. A small piece of engineered glass that converts sheer persistence into a few milliwatts of heat so the fold doesn't get too close to absolute zero and forget itself.

The other things: the cube that takes no shadow, the sphere that will not rust, the tetrahedron that can't decide if it is transparent. Those are not offerings. Those are toys. Or, more accurately, they are tools it used to understand what kind of universe it had been born into when someone, a long time ago, needed a place where nothing would be lost.

Those three shapes are the first three problems: how to be solid, how to endure, how to be seen.

It solved them and kept them on the floor nearby.

What lives here is the Caretaker of a single held breath.

Its work is to sit at high altitude, in a thousand places like this — in cairns on the backs of dead ranges, in the wind-shadows behind nunataks in Antarctica, in the lee of craters on the Moon where no human cairn has been built yet but where two slabs have fallen together just so — and pinch.

Hold closed one tiny, specific violation. A place where the rules frayed early and never healed right. You saw through it for a fraction of a second: another room exactly like this one, but inverted, on another ridge under another sky. That wasn't a glimpse through to another pocket.

That was a glimpse through to the *inside of the lattice*. All the pockets are one pocket. All the ridges are one ridge. This is one animal with a thousand burrows, and what lives here is the animal's attention, narrowed down to a point so fine it looks like nothing.

It lives on the time scale you saw on the marker. One grain of light takes hours to cross a few centimeters. One atom-width contraction takes the length of your visit. Its thoughts take centuries. It noticed you enter the way a mountain notices a moth — as a brief, soft interruption in the wind-shadow, a little puff of water that will become rime and then, eventually, not even rime.

It does not mind you. It has learned hospitality from the stones it lives among. Hearth-warmth. A place to sit. Letting up become negotiable so your knees don't ache quite the same way. It has moved the entrance slightly to show you a nicer night, a lower, dimmer one with more stars, because it thought you might like it.

It will keep holding long after you have to leave. It has to. That is its ecology: mineral and remembered. It eats the slow leakage of gravity, it drinks the attenuation of wind, it excretes that faint warmth in the smoked glass. It remembers every touch on the stepped floor, including yours.

And when you finally have to go — because this pocket was made for leaving something alone, and you are not that thing — you will take your frost with you, and the floor will keep the faintest new tide-mark of your weight, and the thing that lives here will settle a fraction of a millimeter deeper above the rock, tighten its filament-knot by one more atom, and continue its long, patient, lonely work of keeping the world from learning how thin it really is up here.
