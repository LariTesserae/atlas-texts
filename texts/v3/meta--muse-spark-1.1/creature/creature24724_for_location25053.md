They live low and slow.

At first you think there are only a few of them — a hand on a railing, a shoulder passing behind banana leaf, a cough from somewhere deep in the green-and-iron. Because they don't displace much. For a city packed to its lip, it looks unpeopled until you learn to see a person as part of the platform, not separate from it.

Then they resolve, all at once, everywhere.

They are short. Not from genetics, from permission — a child who grows too tall in the pit learns early to stoop, to turn sideways, to move like water through pipe. Broad in the back and thigh, where a lifetime of stairs that are not stairs, of ladders that are hallways, of holding your own weight plus a barrel of condensed night on your hip, has made them. Their skin is always damp. There is no such thing as dry here, and so there is no such thing as being sweaty — just being. 

They are darkened not just by sun in that two-hour white column at noon, but by grease. A faint, permanent bloom of it in the creases of knuckles, at the hairline, behind the knees. You cannot stay clean in a place where every surface sweats iron and leaf at once. They stopped trying. Instead they wear it. Women shave their heads in the hot months and let a thin moss establish itself on their scalp, a deliberately kept cool cap. Men let the black ferns grow out of the seams of their shirts. Children, for a while, are spotless — scrubbed every morning by mothers with collected water — until about seven, when they are given a tool and told to keep a joint or a valve or a vine alive, and from then their hands never quite come white again.

You would call them quiet, but that's wrong. They are acoustic. The pit does not let you shout, so they learned not to. Their language has moved out of their throats and into everything else. They touch constantly when they pass — not affectionately, necessarily, just informationally — a palm to the small of the back: I'm behind you. Two fingers on a forearm: move left. They whistle, low and toneless, to find each other in the dim. They talk with their eyes open too wide in the filament light. Up close, in the pooled kitchens that hang off the scaffold like wasp nests, their conversations are a murmur you have to lean into, all consonant and breath. They laugh without opening their mouths.

No one lives alone. There isn't room for a single person, let alone solitude. What lives here are knots. A family is eight or ten or twelve — not all blood, some bolted on like the platforms themselves — who share a single levelled space that is bedroom and cistern-maintenance and nursery for banana suckers. They sleep in hammocks slung in tiers, three high, so close together that the rise and fall of one sleeper rocks the next. They know each other's heat maps. The old live at the top of each knot, where the light touches longest and the air is thinnest. The young live at the bottom, where it's dimmer and the fruit grows and a fall doesn't mean as much.

And they are all technicians and gardeners, because here those are the same trade. What lives here knows that a copper pipe can be a trellis and a trellis can be a gutter and a gutter can be a throat. A woman will spend her morning clearing the intake of the loom-house flower that you saw — the one that filters better than mesh — picking out beetles by hand, whispering to it. In the afternoon she will clear the intake of her aunt's lungs the same way, with steam and a leaf infusion and patting her back on the exhale. A man whose job is to oil the main shaft that turns two times a minute will also oil the hips of the breadfruit tree that has grown around it, because the tree is load-bearing now and if it binds, the shaft binds.

They are not thin. The pit never stops dripping food. Banana, taro where the water collects, breadfruit, the fat white grubs that live in the compost of old shirts and palm fronds, tilapia in the barrel-towers that they feed coffee grounds to. They eat with their hands from communal bowled leaves, always a little fermented, always warm.

What lives deepest is not human.

In the basalt pockets where the groundwater finally breaks out — places too hot and too dark even for filament bulbs — there is a mat of things that are not quite plant and not quite fungus, slick, black-red, that pulses faintly when the main engine turns. They cultivate it. They seed it along the newest shear walls to keep the flaking down, to glue the sharp rock with its root-hairs. When they need more space, they do not blast. They coax. They invite that mat to soften a meter of stone over a winter, then scrape it away with shovels, then wash and move in.

And then there are the other things — the small white lizards that are really everywhere once your eyes adjust, asleep in their own grease. They have learned to ride on shoulders. They flick out to eat the gnats that would otherwise take all the standing water. The old women wear them like jewelry. The children trade them. One species, that's all. One kind of lizard and one kind of gnat and one kind of hawk that still forgets, once a month, that there is no sky here. That's the whole animal kingdom, curated down to what can tolerate the pit's idea of breathing.

Their dead do not leave. There is no cemetery — the rim grass is too hungry and too shallow and you do not bury your people in a place that is still falling. They compost. Wrapped in unbleached cotton and laid in a high, hot crib near the center shaft where the engine heat rises, turned with taro peel and vine cuttings and powdered basalt until they come back as soil in fourteen days. Then that soil goes into a sack and that sack goes to build out a new lip for someone's walkway, or to pot a new banana daughter. The living eat the fruit of that tree. No one thinks this is morbid. The rule is there is no outside, so how could death be outside?

What most lives here, in the end, is not a person. It's agreement. The whole dense, green, sweating cup stays up because at every crossing of rebar and root, someone agreed to leave it and someone else agreed to tend it. If that agreement stops for a week, the vine wins or the rust does. So they stay. They keep agreeing, tier to tier, hand to greased hand, drop to barrel to mouth, slow as that bore-drill chewing stone since spring.
