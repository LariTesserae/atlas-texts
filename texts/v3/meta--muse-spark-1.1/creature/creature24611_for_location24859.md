If you live here, you are low.

Not short. Low.

You don't stand up all the way, even if you can. The ones who were born tall learned quickly to stoop, to crouch, to fold themselves into the seam between a pipe-roof and a highway-floor. Over generations, you stop being born tall at all. Your spine goes wide instead of high. Your feet go wide, splayed, paddled, paked with too many toes to keep you from skating on the inch of water that is always moving over everything. Your center of gravity is somewhere in your knees. When you run, you skitter.

You are wet. You have to be.

Nothing here dries. So nothing here tries to stay dry. The people — human, near-human, only-human-once — wear things that are meant to be soaked and then soaked again. Woven stone-fiber that never rots, sheets of reclaimed membrane that hang off the shoulders like fins and channel water away. Most of them wear very little at all, because cloth is a place for mineral to crust and for things to hide, and in the way you can be nudged aside by the thermal squeeze of a thousand bodies, you are rarely cold.

Their skin, their hide, their casing — it all has that polished look. Shoulders burnished by rain, then by other shoulders. Elbows that shine. Scalps shaved or scaled or crusted with hydrophilic filaments that drink the condensation and pass it, drop by drop, to a fleshy bladder at the nape for later. Eyes with a second lid, clear and fast, that snaps shut when a chute roars or the wind throws spray sideways. Ears that can be closed. Most of them close their ears.

Because you cannot live here and listen the way an outsider listens.

It's too loud. The roar of water in its stone throats, the groan of the city cooling itself, the shriek-and-haggle of the market-reef. So the ones who stay become quiet in their own way. They don't shout — shouting is drowned. They speak in hands. In taps on a shoulder, in elbows, in a kind of rapid, whole-body sign that can be read at a glance even in a crowd where you're cheek-to-cheek with eight other species. Mothers drum instructions directly onto their children's skulls. Lovers press foreheads together and the water between them carries the vibration. When they do use voices, they use the low sounds, the ones you feel in the floor. A chest-voice that travels through bare feet.

They eat the city, because there is nothing else.

That's not a metaphor. There is no plant. No green thing. No chain that starts in a leaf. So everything starts in the city. Under your feet, in the warm dark that is never fully dark, there are vats. Old, old vats, some as big as cathedrals, some as small as your fist, all humming. They turn mineral seep and heat and scraped biofilm into curds, into paste, into sweet protein strings. The rats — not rats, but you call them rats — are part of it. Translucent runners who know the pipe paths, carrying packets of paste in their cheek pouches, trading flicks of scent. The big slab-crabs rake the clear pools with baleen mouths and excrete a fine, edible mud. The insect-clouds that are sometimes insects and sometimes tools dissolve yesterday's wall and sweat out a bead of sugar as waste. And people farm it, milk it, cut it, steal it.

So you learn not to ask where food came from. You just know whose corner makes the best mud-cakes, which vat-mother you should not haggle with.

No one owns space here for long. That's the other rule that got into their bodies.

A man, if you saw him alone — which you never do — might be broad, bowlegged, his back a low arch, his hands huge and flattened from a life of holding himself up while he works on vertical faces. A woman might be a lattice, barely there, all extended limbs and perching toes because she lives in the scaffolding layer and her baby clings to the back of her neck and to the strut above her at the same time. A child here is never alone either; they come in braids, four or five tangled together, learning early that moving as a linked knot gets you through a crowded chute faster than elbowing alone.

Some of them are not born at all. They are assembled. You can see the joint-lines, bright ceramic under skin. They have been repaired so many times with such lovingly mismatched parts that they look like an idea of a person that several different people had at different temperatures. They care about straight lines. They'll be the one on your bridge with a vibrating wire, coaxing the parapet back to vertical while the crowd flows around them, patient but not waiting. For a little while, that corner will be square again.

And then there are the others who aren't anyone's idea of people. The flicker-birds that aren't birds but are everywhere your eye isn't, drinking off ledges until the ledge tips and they are gone in a sheet of noise. The slow builders that live in the deep drains, blind, kneading basalt-concrete with their backs. The swarms, black and glittering, that are the city thinking out loud — if one settles on your arm for a second you feel your hairs rearrange.

None of them think of this place as harsh. They think of it as ample. There is always water. There is always warmth. There is always a crevice that wasn't there yesterday exactly the size of you. You never have to go far to be fed, or touched, or needed.

They sleep not in beds — there's no room — but in stacks, in hammock-nets strung in the steam of the cooling veins, in shallow warm pools where they curl together like spoons in a drawer, breathing water and air at once. They don't dream of mountains. Or sky. Their horizon has always been a wet wall thirty meters away with a shop cut into it, so they dream of hallways opening, of water going somewhere new. 

And while you stand there on your low rise that is a roof that is a street that is a dam, trying to get your breath in the warm press, they are already packing themselves around you. A shoulder that belongs to a man who is also a tub of filament eels. A hand with too many knuckles offering you a cake of mud that steams. A child-braid flowing between your legs without breaking stride. Something overhead laying down a new path, hot ceramic singing as it cools, so that where there was shadow a second ago there is now a market, and it is already full.
