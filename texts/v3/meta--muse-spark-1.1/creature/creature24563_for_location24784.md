They live here. 

Not *on* here. *Here* is what they have become.

At first you think they are dead. That's the lateness in your thoughts from the thin air. Everything in you that knows what an animal is wants movement, wants breath, wants a nervous twitch. They have none of that, so your brain files them as pattern on stone. Texturing. Fur.

Then you crouch down until the cool gets into your knees, and you see one properly.

It is not a moth. Moths want. This does not want.

Call it a Shingleback, if you need a word, but it has not needed a word for longer than your language has been shaping air. It is the size of both your hands overlapped. If you found a small one it would fit in your palm. If you found a big one it would cover your chest. They are all the same, scaled up and down, which tells you they are not growing. Growth is a kind of change, and change left this place before your species learned to strike a flake off a core.

It has no face. The top is a back of slate. Not like slate — true plates of keratin and silica and something else that has left its biology and become mineral, overlapping like shingles on a roof, like scales on a pangolin that forgot it was supposed to be an animal and decided to be geology instead. Each plate is exactly the color of the bone-and-iron bedrock beneath you, but not camouflaged. It isn't hiding. It is *agreeing*. The sun has been falling hard and clean through dry air for a hundred million years and it has baked its color into them.

Underneath, the belly is a disc. A pale, warm, slightly tacky holdfast that seals to the stone. Not suction. Bond. If you run your fingertip — and you should not, but you will — around the edge where plate meets stone, you will feel no seam, just a sudden, faint warmth like a rock that has been sitting in sun. That is its heat. It takes light and makes stone-warmth. It has done this for so long the stone beneath it is a different color, a younger color, because the sun has never touched it.

Six limbs. Folded under so tight you would miss them unless you were looking for the math of it. Jointed, black at the knuckle, hairless. They are not legs anymore. They are memory of legs. Every few centuries one might unfold a millimeter to adjust for a gravity pool, for that place where weight collects like invisible water and makes your molars ache.

Around the entire rim, the fringe. Cilia, white, finer than spider silk, stiff in the still air. They are not moving. They are tuned. The crust on the pans — the salt and the yellow efflorescence that tastes like static — that is what they eat, though eating is too fast a word. They precipitate. They transpire backwards. The air holds less moisture than a sealed jar, but it holds ions. Dust that is not dust. The thin, thin exhalation of the planet itself. The fringe combs it out of nothing. One ion at a time. Century by century. Enough.

There are trillions of them. That is not a number. That is the condition of the Roof. You thought the plain was bedrock. It is not. It is them, edge to edge, gap to gap, in a tiling pattern that if you stared at it for years would resolve as a single, unsolvable equation for covering a sphere with almost-no-overlaps. They do overlap, slightly, at the edges. The younger resting on the elder. The elder resting on the stone. In places, three deep. They have worked out precedence.

They do not speak. Sound here is 0.5/3, which is to say, there is one sound: the holding. If you lie down — and the cool is not killing, so you can — and put your ear close enough that your breath would move a cilia if your breath mattered, you hear it. Or feel it. The low note of all of them being at once. It is not communication. It is their weight in reality. A single chord held since before the wind quit.

They see nothing, or they see everything in the way stone sees: pressure and heat and the long slant of light. If you stare at one long enough its plates will go transparent, not because it moved but because your own optics give up and start showing you the truth underneath: another Roof underneath this one, with its own trillions, with its cairns in different places.

Oh. The cairns.

Because someone built. Three stones leaned. A low ring that was swept once and then never needed sweeping again. A capstone placed with care over five smaller ones. Debit age chert, sharp as yesterday.

That was them, too.

Not these, exactly. An earlier instar, if instar is a word for a thing that decided to stop molting forever. When water was still a rumor, when the lichen was still trying to be a plant and the wire-growths still had sap, they were mobile. Just enough. They could lift a plate. They could unfold a limb. They could pick up a stone because the stone was in the wrong place for the pattern. They could strike a flake because the lichen was good under-edge and needed scraping. Tech 1.3, primitive, but deliberate. That is the only change allowed here now, says the rule of this place: *Nothing changes except by deliberate, tiny, primitive craft.*

They finished crafting. The Roof became perfect. The pattern became complete. So they laid down. Each one found its lee side — behind the friendly dome of a buried skull-boulder, behind the cairn it had built to mark the place where it would stop — and sealed its disc to the bedrock. And became Roof.

They are still there, under the shingles. Not dead. The opposite of dead. Dead is noisy, it rots, it leaks. They leak nothing. They are in a state that old Earth books called estivation, but taken past biology into physics. A single metabolic tick every lifetime of mountains. They are waiting, but not for anything to arrive, because Rule Two: Nothing arrives. They are being densely, as you thought. Their abundance is not hunger. It is sufficiency.

If you walk — and there is nowhere to go, only a soft rim ten kilometers away that is not the edge of the world but the edge of the district, where light folds and shows you your own back — you walk between them. You learn to see the gaps. You learn to put your boots only on stone. They allow it. Not generosity. They just have no mechanism for refusal. You are a temporary mobile phase, like water used to be. You will pass.

The other things that live here live on permission from them.

The black-and-rust lichen in the lee is a tenant farming their shadow. It does not grow, it exists in a circle because the Shingleback's shadow never moves — the sun barely moves — and a circle is the perfect shape for a thing that has learned not to want more.

The gray-green wire with the bead of resin at its tip is rarer. It is not a plant either. It is an antenna. When the gravity pools thicken, when the air gets thick without moisture and your double-shadow shows for a half-step, the bead trembles. The Shinglebacks feel it through the stone.

That is all.

When you leave — and you must, because you still breathe and need water and your thoughts still arrive late and then all at once — your handprint in the dense silt will remain. Next year. A hundred years. Until one of them, after a long deliberation that takes a season of your great-grandchildren's lives, decides that handprint is an incorrectness in the floor and unfolds one ancient joint, and sweeps it flat again.
