They live here because nothing else could afford to.

Not in the sense of toughness. There are tougher things than them — tardigrades, spores, the lichen itself. In the sense of economy. To live here you have to be able to pay for your own heat, your own chemistry, your own motion, up front and forever. There is no surplus to borrow. So what lives here is what learned to carry its foundry inside its body.

The big ones first. Because you see them first, even when you think you are looking at hills.

At a distance you misfile them. Low cloud, or an erratic the size of a county. The light is so diffuse and the air so clear that scale fails. Then one of the hills moves, and your inner ear corrects with a lurch, because you have been looking at something alive for minutes and calling it geography.

They are not a herd in any behavior you know. Herd implies nervousness, decision. Herds can be spooked. These cannot. They are spaced as boulders are spaced — by old forces, by the fact that two cannot occupy the same pressure trough. Each one walks alone on a line that happens to be parallel to all the other lines. The continent has a grain, those vast U-valleys, and they walk with the grain one way for half a century and against it the next. If their paths intersect they do not fight, or greet. The larger waits with a patience that isn't patience, just a low idle, while the smaller circumscribes it. You could time glaciations by it.

Up close — and you will get close without meaning to, because they seem to amble and yet unspool distance like thread — the word that comes is *freighter*. Or *works*. Not animal.

Six to eight legs, but don't count on it; the rear pair are often fused into a broad sled of cartilage and fat that actually leaves the polish on the rock. Their feet are not hooves. They are pillows. Each one when set down spreads to the diameter of a small house, a thermal bladder of black blood and elastic skin, exhaling as it takes the weight. *Phaff.* A gust of wet, boiling air instantly turned to snow. That snow falls sideways, fine as flour. That is the little weather. A child standing in its path would vanish in that breath-snow, not from cold but from burial.

Above the feet, the chassis. Iron. Not iron-colored, not iron-hard, but iron. It grows there. The plates precipitate in the dermis out of an absurdly iron-rich blood, lay themselves down layer on layer like oyster shell, then oxidize at the edges to that deep pitted umber-black. Each plate is the size of a barn door, a double door. They overlap shinglewise from head to tail so that the wind can't get under. Between them, living sutures: skin as thick as your arm, red with heat, weeping constantly. You can see down into a suture when the wind parts the steam and see it pulsing.

Because below that is a furnace. It has to be. There is no other math that works here.

Their dorsal side is a hill you could hike on and lose your way. Lichen colonizes it — not the black-green starvation felt that grows on the plain, but a thicker, rust-colored form that feeds on iron filings and frost-melt. It grows in waves, combed backward by the walk. Under it, the plates shudder with slow electricity. On dim days, and all days are dim, you see it crawling tip to tip, fork to fork: St. Elmo's fire, Saint's fire, violet-white, silent. It makes no crackle. Thin air won't carry a crackle. The back of every giant is quietly crowned with lightning that never strikes.

Ventrally, they are open. Not soft — there is no soft on them — but vented. A long lower lip, not a mouth, a scraper. A bulldozer blade of keratin and mineralized scar tissue as wide as the animal, hinged and dragged. As it moves it shaves the top millimeter off the world: frost, lichen, dust, basalt sand, pebbles, the occasional big stone that will be tumbled inward for days before being decided on. All of it goes into a grinding maw behind the lip that is less mouth than kiln door. It glows. Even from a hundred meters off, when a vent opens to inspire, you see deep inside the cherry-white, the slow turning of something that is at once a gizzard and a turbine and a magnetohydrodynamic heart — cartilage vanes blackened, turning, turning, humming so low you feel it in your molars rather than hear it.

It needs rock to live. Lichen is carbon, welcome, but carbon is cheap. It needs iron ore, magnetite, silicates, to replenish its back, to make its blood. It needs the cold to set the temperature differential that lets it generate power without moving too much. It is thermoelectric, geochemical, animal.

What comes out behind is not dung. It is industry's true signature. A long, low berm, often still glowing dull orange inside its skin when it is first laid, hissing in the cold. Slag. Perfect, glassy, basaltic slag, extruded in two warm, parallel ridges that steam for an afternoon and then are done. The steam condenses on the cold and falls as hard little beads of ice that rattle. Weeks later, the berm cracks as it cools, contracting. The crack makes the only sharp sound this plain hears — a single rifle crack that carries miles in the thin air. Eventually wind fills the cracks with frost-dust, lichen finds purchase faster here because the slag still holds a ghost of heat for years, radiolabel-warm. The giants are thus farmers in reverse: they eat the plain and excrete the plain, slightly repositioned, slightly more available. The whole continent is being turned over by them, slowly, like a field.

They do not have eyes as you understand eyes. Eyes would frost shut. On the front of that barn-door head there are shallow pits, dozens, lined with magnetite crystals and heat-sensitive membrane, watching less for light than for absence — the downhill of magnetic field, the warmth of another's cooling berm which means lichen has had time to return, the faint hydration line ahead where someone else has breathed enough for a river to think about forming. Their brain is not big. It doesn't need to be. A process control computer the size of a dog sits somewhere deep and warm and does almost nothing, because the job — walk, scrape, smelt, breathe, lay — has not changed in a geological age.

They die rarely. When they do, it is not dramatic. A vent clogs. A turbine seizes. The furnace inside slowly loses its gradient. It stops walking. Its feet splay and freeze to the rock in their own leaked fluid. For weeks its back still crawls with that violet fire, weaker. The flea-things leave first in a white exodus. Then the moths stop coming. Then the plates cool from umber-black to grey. Then snow does not melt off it when it lands. What remains is a long, low, iron-clad hill in exactly the wrong orientation to the grain of the land. In a thousand years lichen will have driven roots into its plate sutures and split them. In ten thousand it will be indistinguishable from the rounded bluffs you thought were memories of cliffs. It will be what you first mistook it for.

And between their legs, always, the smaller economy hustles.

The lichen that is not lichen — two organisms pretending to be one, black-green and iron-red, teaching each other how to eat stone.

The moths — calling them moths is generous. They are lunged, furred things the size of your hands, the color of frost-burn, with no mouth, only heat-drinking palps. They ride the hot updraft from a vent until they cook, then drop like cinders, then crawl back to do it again, for as long as they can before their fur ices and they fall for good. They lay their eggs in warm slag, where the larvae eat residual organics and iron oxide like maggots in a corpse of glass.

The flea-things — white, translucent, shrimp-like, living their entire generations in the steam line behind one giant, grazing on bacteria that only bloom in the moment between steaming water and fresh ice. They are so numerous the berm looks alive and shivers.

And the water itself — or what passes for it here — lives, too, in its way. It does not live in rivers, because there are no rivers. It lives as condensation borrowed from the giants' breath, a transient film, a few hours of damp on rock that otherwise has never been wet. The microbes strain upward to touch it.

Nothing here makes anything. That is the shock. On Earth industrial means extraction, accumulation, building. Here industrial means maintenance of interior temperature against an infinite sink. The giants are not building a city. They are preventing a city — the city of ice crystals that would be their blood — from forming inside them. Their work has no product but continuance.

If you lie down in one of the great empty valleys and put your ear to the stone — and the stone will stick to your cheek if you do — you feel them before you hear them. A faint alternation, left, right, left, conducted through basalt for miles: the quietest industry in the world, walking, smelting itself as it goes, laying down warm roads that will never lead anywhere, breathing out brief weathers for anyone small enough to need a drink.
