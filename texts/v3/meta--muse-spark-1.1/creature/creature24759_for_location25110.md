They live high because nothing else will hold them.

The first ones you notice are the ones who *are* the air.

**The Sailers.**

We called them gliders but that isn't right - a glider falls. These do not fall. They are held. Their bodies are almost nothing: a central knot the size of your fist, dense and dark as a seed, from which a lattice of hollow chitin - not bone, not feather, but something like sun-cured glass - spreads out to hold a membrane so thin you see the sky through it when they bank.

They drink light. In the dark fluid that pulses slowly through that lattice you can see it, when one comes low over you - a rusty, coppery blush that deepens at noon. Photosynthesis, but not like a plant's. More like a bruise made useful. They store it. At night, if you camp in the wadi bottom, they are still up there, and the largest of them glow faintly at the edges, leaking the day's surplus back out.

There is not one kind. The sky is sorted by size, like a sea.

Lowest, in the first ten feet above the stone, are the Handkerchiefs. No bigger than your spread hand, frantic, translucent triangles that flicker from cushion plant to cushion plant. They don't ride thermals; they beat, actually beat their wings in short, desperate bursts, to get to the next dew-cup before it dries. Their ventral side is all mouth - a soft, fringed funnel that kisses a bead of mist off a leaf and is gone.

Above them, the Kites. These are the ones with tails. A six-foot disc of living cellophane with a twenty-foot ribbon trailing behind. The ribbon is not for steering, not entirely. It's a sensory organ. It tastes the air for lift. It quivers, goes taut, and the Kite above it will heel over without a sound and climb a thermal you cannot see. They live twenty, thirty years. The tails get notched, tattered. The oldest ones have tails braided with the shed tails of younger ones they have flown with.

And high, higher than you can properly gauge in this clear air, are the Cathedrals. Discs twenty, forty, sixty feet across. So high and so clear you think they are Handkerchiefs until you realize how far away they are. They never flap. They never need to. They were born up there. Their membranes are patterned - faint concentric rings like growth lines in a tree, but they are not static. The rings darken and lighten, and that is how they talk, flashing sun through themselves in long, slow pulses that can be seen for miles in the thin air because sound is useless here. A shadow passes over you when one goes over, and the air gets cold for a second. They are not hunting Handkerchiefs; they are hunting the cold sink at the bottom of the old wadis at night. They will fold, all at once - an impossible origami - into a tube, a dart, and plummet. No dive, a controlled falling.

They die like dropped paper. You find them sometimes, out in the dust-flour pans between mesas, a huge, dry stain of membrane shrinking around a lattice. In a day the small things have taken them apart.

**The Keepers of the Ground.**

Because the sky is full does not mean the ground is empty. It is just looking down.

Between the leather-hard cushion plants move the Pebblebacks. Big as mice, low as lizards, six legs that move with a hydraulic stutter. The same dust-color as the limestone. On their backs, the carapace is pitted exactly like the eroded stone - camouflage, but also cistern. At dawn they climb to an exposed point, turn their backs to the air, and wait. Mist condenses on the pits, beads, runs into forward channels between their eyes. They drink with their faces. Two bristled arms fold out, so delicate for such a tank-like body, and they lift the larger drops like a monk lifting water in both hands. They do not run. If a Kite's shadow falls on them, they stop. They become stone. The shadow passes.

They lay their eggs not in soil - there is none - but in the damp undercuts of the wadis, where the dark stain never fully dries. A clutch of pearls pressed into the wet rock. They guard nothing.

Even more permanent than them are the Mapmakers. The black and orange lichen on the vertical walls. It is not two organisms but one agreement that has lasted longer than any of the mesas has held its current shape. It grows a millimeter a decade. It tastes the rock and the air and the faint mineral seep. Its edge is a living calendar. You could count a thousand years of wetter seasons in its bands. It does not move, does not need to. It is eaten, slowly, by Pebblebacks. It does not mind.

**The People Who Borrow.**

Yes, there are people. Not many. They could not own this place.

They are high people in the literal sense - long chests for the thin air, skin darkened and then freckled lighter by the unfiltered sun until it looks like the stone itself. Their eyes are narrowed, not by squinting but by a second, clear lid that comes across when the glare gets worst. They wear no bright color. Dust-colored wraps, loose, that hold morning dew until they can wring it into a jar.

They don't live in the shelter you saw. They live *with* it. A family will occupy a half-dome for a season, sweep the dust out, repair the mud with wet dust-flour, then leave it for the next. No one owns a wall. They are moving in a circuit between Threads.

Because the Threads are their wells.

The dark fused circles at the base of each Thread hold cold. The stone there sweats longer than any other stone on the plateau. And the strange honey-resistance in the air above it keeps the evaporation from stealing it all at once. At dawn, a woman will kneel at the edge of a circle where the lichen refuses to grow and run a flat stone scraper across its surface, gathering the bead-sweat into a gourd. A child will sit in the center, right under the invisible line, and let the faint ionization raise the hair on his arms.

They know not to touch the Thread itself with bare skin for long. It leaves a numb ache in the teeth. But they have long, dry wands of that same glassy stone, split from previous circles, and they will brush them through the Thread to make it tone. That is the signal to the Cathedrals. A low molars-note that means *open.*

The people do not ride the Threads. The Sailers do. The people watch where the Sailers go when they cut through and reappear sideways, and they mark it. Because the Thread network is a map of water, still. Where a lot of Sailers jump, the ground below that arrival point will have a deeper undercut, a more reliable seep. The people follow the sky-animals the way lowlanders follow rivers.

They leave the cairns. Not graves. Message posts. Under each piled pale rock is a hollow just big enough for a covered jar of water and a scrap of cured Kite membrane, on which is pricked with a cactus spine the pattern of Threads flashing that season - which ones sang, which ones were dim.

They are quiet, like everything here is quiet. If you speak to one, he will not shout back across the thin air. He will wait until you are close, until your shadows touch, and then he will talk low, so the sound stays between you.

And finally, **the Threads themselves.**

They are alive only if you think a mountain is alive. Only if you think maintenance is a form of metabolism. They do not grow, do not reproduce, do not die, but they want. You feel it when you push your hand through that cold honey. A faint insistence on *here*. On holding this exact line from bedrock to sky for a time so long the limestone around them has worn away and left them higher.

Dust falls around them but never on them. The Pebblebacks cluster near them but not too near. The Sailers use them and in using them, tune them. Maybe that is their ecology - the great discs passing through keep them clear, keep them singing, the way a whale's passing keeps a current moving.

They are the oldest inhabitants. They remember when this was not high. When the rivers that left those wandering stains actually ran. They were put here, or grew here, to hold something up or hold something together, and the planet left them on when everything else fell away.

They hold still, and therefore everything else around them can move.
