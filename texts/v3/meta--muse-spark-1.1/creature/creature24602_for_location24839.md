At first you think the answer is nothing.

That's what the place wants you to think. Nothing here drinks. Nothing here has a mouth shaped for water, and so your brain, which is mostly water in a bag, edits everything else out.

Then you keep standing and the cold spot that is you starts to make sense to the things that actually do live here, and they come back into focus.

The first ones are the ones you thought were not alive.

That pale yellow-white frost around the cooling cracks. From a distance it looks like lichen. Up close the description said it was sulfur and salt, needle crystals that shatter if your shadow falls on them. 

It does shatter. That's how it moves.

It's dry as paper because it has to be. If you kneel — as close as your burning knees will let you — and hold still long enough for the ticking of the plates under you to slow, you see the crust isn't precipitated. It grows. Each needle is hollow and slightly crooked, leaning into the warm draft that sighs out of the cold crack. It doesn't grow tall, it grows sideways, in tight, overlapping shingles away from the heat, like frost receding in reverse. When the thermal gradient gets too shallow, the whole patch starves.

And then it shatters. Not from your shadow. From itself. The needles overgrow their attachment and snap under their own weight with that faint tinkle you heard. The fragments, light as ash, are caught by the convective lift you saw making dust motes dance. For a few seconds they float - that's the life in its flying stage - and settle further down the gradient where the temperature is right to root as a new needle. They eat straight heat differential. They don't photosynthesize; there's no sun. They don't chemosynthesize quite; they run on thermoelectrics, a Seebeck voltage between their hot foot in the crack-gas and their cold tip in the thin air. Yellow-white as bone, quiet, everywhere, they are the meadow. The plains are covered with them, if you learn to see a meadow made of glass hair.

Where the meadow is thick, the glass has been tended.

You thought the whole floor was a sheet of pahoehoe, black plates that flex when you step. It does flex. You felt it tick under you. A plate will flex because a plate is not a plate. It's a carapace piece.

The thing under it is wide and very, very flat. The size of a house or a city block, with no center you can find. You never see its edges because its edges are fused to the next one. The whole blister-plateau is tiled with them. They are called by what they do, not what they are, but you can think of them as Sheathers.

They live in the half-melt. Their backs are the black glass you walk on — they exude it, a basaltic silica sweat that cools into that rope texture, that perfect glossy black. Their bellies are still in the orange. They absorb directly. When one part of the colony gets too hot, when a new seam unzips twenty meters to your left and floods the underside with fresh magma light, the animals on that seam don't die, they peel. They crack themselves along a pre-scored line, make that clean rifle-shot sound, and the tension of the cooling back splits them into two smaller flats. That is the chink and the puff of glass that hops an inch. That's reproduction. You are walking on a herd's backs, and every few minutes the herd gets bigger to release pressure from below.

That warmth coming up through your boots? It's not just geothermal. It's mammalian close because the Sheather under you is running about 700 degrees inside and trying to stay 300 on its back, and your 37-degree feet are a cold sore to it. The ticking you hear underfoot when you stand still is not rock cooling. It's cilia — or whatever you call a fringe of semi-molten basalt threads — combing the melt for reduced iron, for carbon monoxide, for the dissolved gases you smell as hot iron and old kiln.

But you asked about the towers.

You mistook them for buildings. Then you told yourself they were chimneys, grown, not made. You were half right. They are not made by hands, but they are made. And the maker still lives inside them.

The Sheathers can't vent fast enough on their own. Gas builds — sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, a whiff of hydrogen chloride that would strip your lungs — and needs a pinhole. When it finds one, the first bubble of basalt pops and welds a ring. That ring is an invitation.

The second kind of occupant moves in for as long as the vent stays hot. You never see it clearly because it never leaves the inside of its tower.

If you look into one, the interior glows, you wrote. It does. But the glow is not empty. Hold your face there — not too close, the heat rolls off dry as paper — and you see what makes the faint hiss almost below hearing. The tower wall is not solid. It's a lace of black spatter that the occupant has been knitting with its own body. The occupant is a column of heat-hardened smoke.

Think of a lung turned inside out and taught to be a spider. It is made of braided carbon fiber more than flesh, though it is flesh in the way a charcoal is still wood. It hangs in the updraft of its own chimney, a loose, soot-black braid of filaments that contracts and relaxes, scraping dissolved mineral out of the gas as it screams past. Each contraction squeezes a little molten washer out of its lower end and welds it to the rim. That's the pulsing cherry-red tip you saw. The tower grows. It leans because the filaments learn to lean into the prevailing trace of wind under the lid-sky, to catch more.

When the gas dries up, the weaver stops. It hangs there, starving, until its fibers get brittle. Then a ground contraction snaps its tower and it becomes that ring of daggers you walked past. The daggers aren't rubble. Each splinter is a spore, full of tangled carbon filament and a speck of iron sulfide to start a new gut. Most land dead. One lands on a new pinhole and starts again.

They are sparse enough that you feel alone. That's intentional. Each chimney casts a long shadow of sulfur-depletion. No two weavers will light up close to each other.

And then under all of that, deeper than the Sheathers, is the reason this place is high and is still being pushed up.

You felt that wrongness, that additive wrongness: how can it be so warm this high and this dark? How can something that looks like a ruined village have been made with no hands?

The plateau isn't a plateau. It's a blister. And a blister has pressure under it.

Far off beyond the haze, you saw that higher place, the raw black spine pushing up into the lid, still wet with becoming. That's not the top. That's the older brother.

The thing underneath you doesn't have edges yet. It's one animal, or one organ of a planet, depending on how generous you want to be. A slow diapir of mostly liquid rock with a metabolism measured in decades. It eats the base of the crust and exhales this whole landscape. The Sheathers are its skin cells. The weavers are the hairs in its pores. The needle-meadow is the dandruff it doesn't know it's shedding. It has been rising for a thousand years and will rise for a thousand more, until its back finally freezes and becomes real high ground and erosion finally finds it.

Right now, in the warm, rainless, thin-aired pause while it is still soft, this is its warm season. It is, by any definition that works here, alive. And you, a soft, water-filled thing with a pulse thudding in your ears from altitude, intrusive and cold, have been standing on its back long enough for it to register you as weather — a brief, passing cold spot that will be gone soon, and for whom the ground flexed, just once, to see what would happen.
