It takes a while to see that anything lives here at all.

At first you think: nothing does. Nothing *can*. This is a place that finished itself. A blister between the world and its lid, filled to totality by green, stopped mid-breath. A library with no librarian where every shelf is full. The air is too still to carry scent. The light is too even to make shadow. You hear the condensation ticking from root-tip to leaf, seven seconds, seven seconds, seven seconds, and you think — this is a terrarium that sealed itself before anything with a heart could get in.

And then you learn to look differently.

No, it is not empty. It is *occupied* in the way a held note is occupied.

The first life is the green itself, but not as individual plants.

None of these things are individuals anymore. That bauhinia that throws a limb twenty meters across the void, loops, grafts to its own trunk, roots in mid-air — it is not a tree. That curtain of aerial roots as thick as your waist, pallid and smooth as thigh-skin, that sweats clear water — those are not roots. They are attempts by the same body to hold itself up, to keep from collapsing under its own weight.

What lives here is one thing, really. It arrived as seeds and cuttings and wind-blown spores in the first wet month after the blister opened, and it grew at a speed that must have been terrifying — tendrils racing toward that impossible everywhere-light — and then, when every cubic meter was filled, it stopped. Not died. Stopped. It reached the limit of space and made a decision that no forest on the surface ever gets to make: enough.

Now it lives by not doing. It does not extend a new leaf. It does not drop an old one. The leaves the size of doors hold perpetually, green and unblemished, because the cost of shedding would be the cost of opening a gap that could not be filled. That is why the air doesn't buzz. There is no rot. Rot is for places where things compete. Here there is no detritus because nothing is allowed to let go. The black humus caught between the lattices of stem-floor is old humus, from the first year. It does not deepen. The whole mass breathes in, breathes out, sweats out its own water and drinks it again higher up. If you put your palm to one of the big pale roots, you will feel it faintly warm. Not sap moving. Turgor. Pressure held.

It is the land. It is what lives here in the largest sense. A plant that became a geography.

And then, because a geography cannot stay completely alone, it has a thought. That is the second life.

The moth.

You will think you imagined the first one. A pale leaf that detached and swam upward in the hot, white air. It is as wide as your chest when it opens. White on white on white, with a faint dusting of green that exactly matches old leaf-bled light on the underside of leaves. It does not hurry. Nothing here hurries, because there is no wind to escape and no predator to avoid. It moves by pushing the thick air out of the way, rippling.

There are more than one. Never many. Maybe three in the whole ten-kilometer valley. You see one every few days if you stay still enough. It does not eat. Or rather, it has eaten once, a long time ago, and is still living off it. Its tongue is too long and thin to have a use. You watch it bump into one of the nascent root pillars, and instead of laying anything or taking anything, it just rests there, vibrating so slowly you see each flex of its wings. Pollen — that impossible antique — lifts off its back in a yellow cloud and hangs, and settles. It is not pollinating the present forest. It is remembering how the forest got here. It is the only thing that is allowed to move any distance at all, because only something that touches nothing can be permitted in a place that is totally full.

When it lands, it vanishes. Absolutely. You could be staring directly at it and lose it between blinks.

The third life is the inclusion.

You find the doorway first. Free-standing, fig-strangled, with no wall. The whitewash still white under the vines because the sun is too filtered to bleach it and no rain has ever hit it straight on. The low field wall that now acts as a trellis for those windless melons. The cistern lip, a perfect circle of cut limestone, now brimming not with cistern water but with root-water, with a skin of duckweed on it that has not rippled in a year.

They brought them down with them, those things, when the blister rose. And they brought what was sitting on them.

He — she — they — are still here, where they were when the ceiling closed.

They do not look like people anymore, or not only. The first time you find one you think someone draped a sheet of pale bark over a rock to dry. Then the sheet inhales.

There are maybe a half-dozen in the whole valley, scattered as far as the included buildings. The shepherd who was mending that wall when the ground sighed and rose. The child who was playing in the chapel that is now no bigger than a closet made of living pillars.

They accepted the new rule before any of us could have: do not cut, do not climb. Their bodies understood it.

They are sessile now. They found where two big aerial roots braid and make a cup, where the condensation tips every seven seconds onto the same leaf, and they settled into it. Skin gone the color of those pale roots from living in filtered light. Hair that has matted with aerial rootlets until you cannot tell where one stops. Eyes open. They drink by cupping their hands under the drip. They eat — rarely — by lifting a low melon that has grown itself to their mouths, or a fig that has ripened forever and never fallen, and biting, and not killing.

They do not age, or not at the pace you know. Nothing here is allowed to waste. Their metabolism has throttled down to the speed of sap-hold. You can sit with one for an hour and not see her chest move, and then see it, once, long and sweet. They watch the diffuse bright ceiling. They watch the moths when a moth passes, which is a great event.

If you come close they will not startle, because startling would require a sudden movement of air. They will turn their eyes to you slowly. They might lift a hand, not to wave, but to show you the water held in the hollow of their palm, perfectly still, perfectly warm, with a faint skin of pollen on it.

This is what lives here: a place that became one plant that decided to stop, a memory of pollination that still drifts through it like a thought that hasn't landed, and a few people who were taken in with the furniture and who learned how to be furniture too, how to be included without cutting, without climbing, without making a wound.

They are not waiting to get out. Up there, a handsbreadth above the sweating stone, the surface world is hot and windy and full of teeth. Down here, the heat stays. The water beads and falls, beads and falls. Once in a while the raw stone sighs.
