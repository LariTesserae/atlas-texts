They are hard to see at first, because they are the same color as what they live inside.

Not camouflage, exactly. They don't hide. There is no predator up here to hide from, unless you count dryness itself. It's a deeper mimicry than that. If you spend your entire evolutionary bargain trying to become the plant that feeds you, you start to look like it.

You see a shiver in the felt wall of the tunnel you have to stoop to move through. You think it's wind combing through the hair-leaves. Then the shiver detaches and resolves into a person, and you realize how wrong your first scale was.

They are small. Not child-small, but compressed. Four and a half feet at the tallest elder, with a barrel chest you can see even under the layers they wear. The air here is too thin to be breathed by any lung that wasn't born to it, so they breathe like bellows. You can hear them before you see them - a faint, dry wheeze on the inhale, a soft click back in the throat where they keep the flap closed to save moisture. Their chests are drum-tight, latticed with rib you can count.

Their skin is the giveaway that they were human once and have stopped negotiating for anything else. It is not skin the way lowland skin is skin. It is cured. Ash-grey and olive-brown, thickened at the knuckles and cheeks and shoulders into a kind of living felt, patched and seamed like the leaf-felt they line their houses with. No sweat glands. Sweat would be suicide. No tears - you will never see one of them cry. Their eyes would not allow it. Eyes like polished jet set too far forward and lidded three times: an outer lid of that same tough skin, a fringe of their own air-comb hairs, long, dusty eyelashes grown into sieves, and then underneath, a translucent inner membrane that wets the eye with oil, not water. 

They are monochrome and then, when the dim amber light catches them, iridescent. They anoint themselves with the spore grease combed from the air-ferns, black-green and oiled. They wear more of the plant over that. Coats cut from the dead felt-leaves, still velvets on the outside, still sparking faintly. They stitch them with bone needle, beetle sinew, so roomy and shaggy that the person inside is only a knot of density in a walking thicket.

Watch one move. An older woman, because here age is legible as added layers. She doesn't walk upright - nobody does, the tunnels won't let them, but more than that, their hips have changed. She stoops and shambles with a hand always touching, always pruning. Her fingers are the most alive part of her. Long, black under the nails from tannin, tips split and hardened into natural shears, eight fingers and two thumbs each ridged like a beetle's leg. She touches a branch and knows whether it grew two millimeters or three this year. She caresses where a lintel needs to be coaxed left, she pinches off dead comb, she pockets the deadfall into a pouch at her waist because nothing is wasted. Her mouth - her mouth is small, almost lipless, puckered from a lifetime of chewing dry pith. The teeth inside are broad molars, all molars, great flat plates for grinding. The children have the same mouth, already worn.

The children are what you hear first, of course, before you can pick out any single body in the rustling mat.

They are born almost white, a shock in all that black-green. A pale, grubby, pink-grey, like the underside of the iron-bruise rock, blind for weeks, hairless until their first air-comb grows in from their scalp, from the backs of their forearms - soft, fine, dark filaments that at dusk give off the same static blue as the trees. The adults hang them in cradles braided out of live stalks, way up near the canopy where the ion wind is strongest. And they scream. They have to. In air this thin and in a town that is also a single dense, sound-blanketing sponge, if you don't scream you are lost. So the children learn loudness before language. They shriek, they rasp little gourds made from pith-casings, they hammer green bronze chimes that never tarnish right. Their job is to be beacons. They run - and they are the only ones who still run, low, stooped, caroming off felt walls - and the whole town knows where its edges are by where the screaming bounces back.

The loudness is not chaos. It is mapping.

The old woman answers a child's scream without stopping her pruning, with a boom that seems impossible from her small chest. The language is almost all vowel and drum, because consonants dry the mouth. A long, resonant AHH — OO — OUU that vibrates the comb-leaves and sends a cloud of glass gnats shivering out of the foliage in a bright, irritated whine. Somewhere on the other side of the bowl, another voice answers, and a great flightless moth, blind and fat as a loaf of dark bread, swells its white wing-drums and joins in, low enough you feel it in your molars.

They don't drink. That is the one thing you cannot get over. You keep looking for the cistern, for the dew-catcher, but there is none. The cisterns they have hold only packed, filtered air - cool pockets that exhale during the warm hours. Their water is the plant's water, is the air's water, is old water, locked. They get it all by eating. The pith of the air-fern when you grind it yields a flour that never feels wet, but it is 30% bound water and salt and sugar, and they gnaw it all day, slow, ruminant. They eat the drum-beetles, too, whole, cracked on bronze anvils - the beetles carry their moisture in a sac under their parchment backs, a bright, bitter liquor. They collect the horn-like molts of the beetles and grind those too. They urinate almost nothing, once a day, a white paste they pack into the roots. They have been closed loops for so long that their sweat has become language.

When one dies, it is not quick and it is not tragic. It is expected, like a branch finally corking over. They become light, lighter than they have been in decades. They shrivel a little more. And then the family does not bury them - there is no ground to bury in - they unbraid them. The house that was pruned for four generations to hold that family opens at the back, where the living wall is thinnest. They open the felt. They lay the body in the mat, where the hair-leaves immediately, within hours, begin to comb at it, sparking faintly, harvesting the last ions. In a week, nothing is left but green-black felt and a new, soft node on the trunk that the children are told not to prune.

At the center, in that scraped perfect molar of rock where the wind pools, are the driest ones. The elders whose air-combs have gone grey and stopped sparking. They sit there on their cured knees with their little stone scrapers and scrape and scrape, keeping the rock bare so the wind has somewhere to gather and breathe. If you stand there with them at the dimmest hour, as the small amber coin of the sun goes thinnest, you will see why they do it. They hold out their arms - wider than you thought possible, because the extra skin of their coats is stretched - and the entire hamlet-sized knot of life around them, ten feet high, three hundred meters across, all that braided, felted, booming, clicking, whining, shouting, hammering, thrashing, living roof at the top of a world that forgot water, shivers and lights up like a field of slow lightning, breathing in with them.
