They live here because nothing can die properly here.

Start with the obvious ones. The ones that own the place.

### The Forest-That-Isn't

They are not trees. Trees imply water, and sap, and a memory of rain. These have no memory of rain.

Call them **Airwrights**, though no one here calls them anything but `the growth`.

An Airwright begins as a speck - a felted seed big as your thumb, light as ash, that floats for weeks in the dry circulation until its static-field catches on a sharp quarry edge. It holds there. It puts out nothing that looks like a root. It puts out a comb.

The first leaves are not leaves. They are sails. Five meters across, ten, twenty - flat, stiff, the thickness of pulp board, both sides covered in a fur of glassy trichomes you can only see when the light hits at an angle. They don't photosynthesize much. Not enough to justify their size. They farm charge. In air this dry, movement makes lightning. Every slow-turning fan, every footstep on stone, every creak of cellulose tightening makes a ghost of electricity. The sails comb it. You can see it if you stand under a canopy leaf in the dark hours when they dim the suns: a faint St. Elmo's blush crawling over the white hairs.

The trunk comes later. It's not wood. It's wound air. Layer after layer of dead leaf-fiber cemented around a hollow core, dry as a paper wasp nest, strong in compression and useless in tension -- until it dies, when it becomes stronger. They don't grow up. They grow toward the next place the sun will be. A gantry sun moves two meters a day on its rails to keep the canopy even. A mature Airwright will learn that. It will throw a limb thirty meters sideways, then corkscrew, then go horizontal for a while, propped on an older sister's dead crown, defying any idea of trunk and branch and root. In here, root and branch are the same word. An aerial root is a branch that got curious about the floor. A branch is a root that smelled light.

And because nothing rots, nobody ever leaves. A forest on the surface is a cycle. This is an accumulation. Under the living thatch is ten, twenty, fifty generations of dead thatch, perfectly preserved. Leaves that fell a hundred years ago are still there, crisp, still holding their shape, light brown as grocery bags. A dead trunk from the first planting season is still standing, hollow, hard as fired clay, its tunnels still round. The forest is its own fossil record growing over itself.

It is the most abundant thing here, and it is not alive the way you think. It is *present*.

### The Dry Herd

Inside accumulation, you get tenants who learned the same trick: don't waste water because you never had any.

**The Moths.** They are everywhere once you stop looking for movement and start looking for dust that decides to move. Palomino, newsprint, salt-white. Each one the size of your open hand. Their wings aren't scaled, they're felted - the same trichomes as the leaves. They are camouflaged not by color but by texture. They live and die on a single sail-leaf. They scrape the living fur with combed forelegs and eat the static, or the dust that holds static, or the microscopic algae that has learned to live off static - no one is sure. When they fly, they don't flap so much as fall with style; you hear them before you see them, a dry *ssst-ssst* like someone turning pages. When one dies, it stays pinned to the leaf where it stopped, wings flat, becoming dust itself in a week.

**The Borers.** Beetles, but beetles the way a tunnel-boring machine is a beetle. Matte black, each as long as a forearm, with a domed back of fused elytra like basalt. They don't eat the living stuff - it's too tough, too charged. They eat the dead stuff, and they don't digest it, they *mine* it. They drive perfect 8-centimeter circles through dead trunks that are harder than concrete, and leave behind a fine, utterly dry frass that the humans sweep up and use for filters. The beetle spends three years making one tunnel, then dies inside it, legs folded. The humans come later and thread electrical cable through the hole. You can see whole apartment walls laced with old beetle runs, each one still occupied by a little black statue at the end.

**The Shepherds.** Above them - always above them, on stilts. They look like harvestmen if harvestmen were made of clear plastic and bad decisions. Knee-high at the joint, body the size of a fist, legs so long and thin they seem to be measuring the air. Their cuticle doesn't shine; it diffuses light like frosted glass. They farm the moths. Not with intelligence, with patience. They walk the underside of the canopy on legs that don't dent the leaves, and tap. Tap tap. The vibration makes the moths lift for a second, and in that second the Shepherd combs a few off with its palps, eating them dry. The moths never evolve to flee, because there is nowhere to flee to. It's a sustainable harvest that has been running for centuries.

**The Listeners.** What replaces mice where there is no water to drink. Hairless, but not pink - the color of old linen. They have pouches on their cheeks, not for food, for air. Their ears are enormous, fringed with the same fur as the leaves, and crusted at the edges with tiny salt crystals. They don't urinate. Ever. They breathe in, they hold it, they condense their own breath on the fringes of their ears and lick it back. They nest in the hollow nodes where a trunk once forked, lining the nest with moth wings. At night, when the suns go to amber standby, you can hear them talking to each other in clicks too dry to carry far. The city's children keep them as quiet, unlike pets. They sit in a pocket and vibrate.

### The People Who Learned to Not Rot

And then them.

You'd recognize them as human if you saw them in bad light on the surface. Up close, you realize they've been edited by dryness.

They are pale in the way people who live under full-spectrum industrial suns get pale - not unhealthy, but the color of things kept in paper. Their skin is thick at the knuckles, split in fine white lines at the lips and nostrils. Nobody's lips aren't cracked. The children have a game of seeing who can go longest without blinking; they all have a little red bead of capillary permanently at the corner of each eye. They don't sweat. Even when they haul - and they haul, great bales of leaf-board on shoulder yokes - their faces just get pink and their breath gets louder. Their kidneys are legendary to surface doctors, who trade for their cloth. They exhale less than you do. They move slower than you expect, to keep from losing water to air.

They dress in what they cut. Layers and layers of carded leaf-cloth, felted and pressed until it is soft as flannel, undyed, the color of bleached bone and dry sage. Every coat is cut large to hold dead air. Every hood has ear-flaps like the Listeners. Under that, their work clothes are covered in fine white dust - moth dust, frass dust, leaf dust - that never washes out because washing would be a sin.

They don't have a government so much as a maintenance schedule. You can tell what a person does by how high they live.

**The Sun Walkers** - lean, sunburned even under the panels, with goggles of smoked glass permanently pushed up on their foreheads. They ride the gantry rails a kilometer up, in baskets, with long hooked poles, directing canopy growth, trimming dead sails with saws that make a screaming creak. When they cut, the smell that comes out isn't sap. It's hot dust and ozone. They are mostly deaf from the buzz of the panels.

**The Carders** - mostly women, mostly old, who run the houses of looms. Looms the size of terraced houses, all wooden tension and counterweight because a spark could be catastrophic in air this dry. The looms don't clack; they sigh. The Carders feed dead leaves into them, and the leaves come out as cable, as cloth, as insulation batts stamped SURPLUS and lifted to the surface. Their hands are softest in the city, from lanolin they render from Listener nests. Their lungs are worst, from the fibers.

**The Tuners** - their children. Because nothing rots, sound never dies the right way. A room lined in living felt will eat a shout. A room lined in dead trunk will hold it for seconds. The Tuners carve. They are the ones who decide when a living room has decided to close itself off, and carve it back open with knives that never rust. They carve the apartment stacks, the schools inside old quarried galleries, the chapels where a single Airwright trunk has grown straight through for fifty meters and they have carved pews into its living sides. A good Tuner can hear where the plant wants to go next.

They are sparse on purpose. The cavern is nine kilometers long and they have built in maybe eight nodes along its length, each a few hundred people, leaving between them cathedrals of empty air where a single Airwright stands alone with its own dedicated sun, creaking. They visit each other on foot or on cable-elevators that run on dead-tree counterweights, moving slowly because speed steals moisture. At night, when the panels dim, they gather in the felt-insulated rooms and listen to the forest build itself - pop, sigh, the rustle of moths - and tell stories about rot, which none of them have ever seen, which to them sounds like wet, obscene magic.

They don't think of themselves as living *in* a forest. They think of themselves as living *on* a forest, the way you live on a large, patient, dry animal that lets you.

And if you stay long enough to let your lips split and your eyes dry and your sweat stop, you start to hear it too: the low, constant, completely unhurried negotiation between stone that was cut yesterday in geological time, plant that never needed water, animal that never drinks, and people who have learned that preservation is its own kind of growth.
