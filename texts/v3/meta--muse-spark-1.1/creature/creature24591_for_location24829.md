What lives here never left. It couldn't. Nothing that lives on the Table can live anywhere else.

### The Film-Dwellers

Lowest and most numerous are the ones who live not *in* water, but *in the idea* of water.

The glass frogs are the census-takers. There are thousands per acre, and no two leaves without them. They are not green — there is no need for camouflage when everything behind you is translucent with wet light. They are clear amber gel, with a single dark bead of gut. You can see what they've eaten today still turning inside them. They do not hop. Hopping would knock them off the world. They walk, toes splayed impossibly wide, gripping the perpetual film on the leaf, drinking through their bellies. At night they go slightly opaque, clouding over to hold the day's captured heat.

On the same film hunts the Strider. Not the pond kind. Here the fog is thick enough that water hangs in the air without a leaf to hold it — fat, trembling beads strung on spider silk that never belonged to a spider. And walking across those beads on legs a foot long and thin as hair, is an insect that looks like it was drawn with a single pen stroke. It never lands. Its whole life is spent stepping from droplet to droplet in mid-air, sipping the organisms that breed in mid-air water.

### The Back-Gardeners

Everything here carries something else, because in a place with no ground, you become ground.

The bearer beetles are the most literal. Black, fist-sized, impossibly dense — they feel heavier than wood when you pick one up, because they are holding so much water inside their elytra. They climb straight up the vertical walls of living mat and do not slip, because their feet dissolve slightly into the mat as they go. On their backs they carry a complete meadow: liverwort, then a moss that only grows on bearer beetle, then in that moss, a tiny white orchid that flowers for one day a year and smells of limestone. When the beetle dies, and it takes a very long time to die, the meadow keeps it. Its shell is not discarded, it's planted.

Higher up are the stained-glass birds. You saw one. There are maybe three kinds, all silent. Their wings are not feathered but stretched, translucent membrane between elongated fingers, like a bat's but with color laid down in panes — amber, smoke-blue, blood-red — the pigments are algae that live *in* the wing. They don't flap; the air in the cloud is so warm and thick and saturated they can lock their wings and ride the humidity gradients like thermals. All day they hang motionless in fog gaps, mouths open, combing gnats out of the mist with whiskered jaws. Their chicks are born naked and immediately get inoculated — the parents rub moss into their wings so the color will grow.

### The Cup-Lakes

Each house-sized bromeliad cup is a country with no borders.

In the black-tea water: things that never needed to evolve beyond larvae. Axolotl-like salamanders with external gills frilled like wet lace, six inches long, the color of strong tea, who never metamorphose because there is no reason to leave. They are paedomorphic kings. They eat the fallen stamens and the drowned gnats and each other's eggs and live ten, twenty years in the same cup they were born in.

In the water with them: the shrimp's cousins. The shrimp in the stone circles are white and blind, but here they are red-brown and have eyes on stalks that see polarized light through fog. They farm. They cut the tiny floating leaves of duck-fern into even strips and set them on submerged rootlets to grow a pale fungus, which they then herd with their antennae.

And around the rim of the cup, peering in: crabs no bigger than your thumbnail that are not crabs at all but spiders that have decided to become semi-aquatic. They lower themselves on a silk thread into the cup to drink, then haul themselves back up, covered in tiny swimming things.

Nothing leaves the cup except as fog.

### The One Who Makes Tunnels By Eating Them

You saw its back.

We call them the Mat-Swimmers, though they are mammals. As big as a shetland pony, maybe bigger — it's hard to tell because you never see a whole one. Low, long, boneless-looking though it has bones. Its fur is not fur, it's a symbiosis — a lichen so complete that the animal photosynthesizes a little, and it in turn fertilizes the lichen with skin oils. It is grey-green and smells of wet stone.

It does not graze *on* the mass. It grazes *through* it. It puts its broad, soft, blunt head into the living wall and eats its way forward, roots and moss and epiphyte and half-rotted leaf and occasional bearer beetle, all together. It leaves behind a tunnel exactly the size of its body, its dung plastered on the walls as a kind of quick-growing mortar. The tunnel stays open, damp and breathing, for years. Everything else uses it — frogs commute through it, birds roost in it, new roots race into it looking for light. Then very slowly, the Mat closes it again from all sides, like a wound healing at the speed of wood.

There are maybe a hundred on the whole Table. They are not social. When two meet in the Mat, which happens rarely, they stop and share lichen for hours, foreheads touching, exchanging species.

They remember where the stone circles are and go around them.

### The Light-Keepers and The Water-Shepherds

Because light is scarce and low, some inhabitants have stopped looking for it and started saving it.

Deep in the Mat where sun has never reached, grow the lamp leaves. They are not a species but a strategy — several unrelated plants do it. Huge, kidney-shaped leaves, thick as your wrist, filled with a clear jelly. All day at the top of the canopy, sister leaves to these catch light and send the sugars down. The jelly leaf below converts it into a weak chemiluminescence and stores it. At true dark — the one hour when the white fog finally goes slate-grey — they let it go. A pale, milk-green glow, just bright enough to see the veins of the leaf above you. Insects come, and frogs come to eat the insects, and the bearer beetles stagger toward it like pilgrims. The whole Mat, for an hour, has a heartbeat of light.

Other plants have become hydrologists. The wax-leaf repels water so perfectly that a drop never touches it. Every drop that condenses on it is shunted, via microscopic gutters you can only see when your eyes fog, into a central groove that shoots it like a marble run directly into a root funnel. In the dry season — which is not dry, just less wet — these are the only plants that still fruit, because they have stolen all the local fog for themselves. The glass frogs know this and line up on the gutters to drink.

### The Circle-Tenders

And then there are the ones who were here before the Mat finished thinking.

You found the circles. Four or five basalt stones, dragged fifty miles from where basalt should be, leaned in. Inside, bare weeping limestone. No tools. No bones.

The white shrimp live there still. Blind, translucent, with one oversized claw. In each circle there are twelve or twenty, and in the shallow pan of water that sweats out of the rock, they tend a single thing: a clump of something that is not shrimp and not plant. It looks like a small brain made of crystal and root, white and faintly pink, pulsing very slowly. If you watch for an hour you might see it pulse once.

The shrimp clean it. They brush silt from it. They bring it minute scraps of lichen. They fight off the red cousins if they try to enter the circle. The stone, because it is bare, is the only place where you can hear water weep out of the rock itself — a sound like breathing.

No one built the circles to live in. They were built to make a place where the Mat could *not* grow, so that something that needs bare wet stone and darkness and absolutely still water could continue.

They are still doing their job.

The people who placed them are gone. Or rather, they are here differently now. If something lives long enough, slowly enough, in a place where everything grows into everything else, it stops having edges. Maybe they became the Mat-Swimmers. Maybe they became the light in the leaves.

If you sit long enough in the dim — 82 degrees, three hundred yards to white, water ticking leaf to leaf to leaf — the glass frogs will climb onto your boots because you are warm and for a moment you are not beading with water as fast as everything else. And for a little while, you will be inhabited too.
