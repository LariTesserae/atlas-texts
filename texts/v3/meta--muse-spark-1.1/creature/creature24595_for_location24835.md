It is not empty. That is the first thing it lets you unlearn.

At first it seems empty because nothing wants you to see it moving. The density is too complete. You look for an animal against a background. There is no background. Everything is animal, or plant pretending to be ground, or ground pretending to be plant.

The ones who live here longest are not counted as ones.

The fig lives here. Or, a fig. One foot on the lip thirty feet above in the real sun, one thousand fingers down here in the dusk. It is the only thing that touches both worlds and it regrets it. Up there it is a tree. Down here it is a system of ropes. Its roots came down looking for water and found air that was water enough, and so they never stopped. They hang in the open, pale and smooth as new skin where they haven't found stone, gray and knotted and strangling where they have. Some are as thin as thread, some as thick as your waist, and none of them have bark anymore, only a wet outer sheath that sweats. Near the bottom they fray out into a beard of root-hair so fine it moves when you breathe. It lives by straining the air you exhale. When the hot column leaves in the evening, all thirty feet of it shivers at once. It is drinking.

Below that, lower, the walls themselves are alive. That is not a metaphor. Lift any fern and there are ten ferns beneath it, and beneath them a black felt of moss and liverwort that is an inch thick and holds water like a sponge, and inside that sponge live things you have to crouch to see and then wish you hadn't looked so close. 

There is the frog choir. They are not a species. They are a consensus. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands — each the size of the last joint of your thumb, flat as a leaf, the exact mottled amber-gray-green of wet limestone. They sit on ferns, on figs, on each other, each in his own little throne of damp. They do not croak. Sound is expensive here and scent stays. They watch with gold-ringed eyes. Their throats do not move. When a droplet gathers above them, swells, lets go — *tok* — they lean forward and take the whole thing, larva and all. They eat the water. That is what they do: they prune the condensation. They are so still for so long that algae grows on their backs, and they let it. They blink all at once when the bowl exhales at dusk. That is the only way you know they are together.

And above them, or below them depending on how you orient, the bat fur.

In the deepest undercut on the east wall where the limestone folded over itself before it failed, there is a ceiling that never dries. There, the bats live as one creature. Not thousands of bats, but a pelt the size of a room. Black-brown-gray, breathing. They pack themselves shoulder to shoulder, feet hooked into stone so ancient it has dissolved around their toes and holds them like sockets. You hear nothing from them until you stop trying to hear something. Then you hear the faint, communal sigh as a thousand tiny chests rise, fall. They do not leave at night. Or, not all of them. In a place like this, where nothing leaves if it can stay, why would they? Some go up to hunt on hot nights, a soft pour of them through the fig roots. Most stay. Their guano never hits the floor — the ferns catch it, the bromeliads catch it, the moss drinks it. It is fed back before it can fall.

The bromeliads are houses. Each one is a city-state, a red-green cup holding last week's rain and its own weather. In each cup: life that will never see another cup three feet away. Damselfly nymphs with jaws like bear traps. Mosquito larvae in black commas so dense they make the water look like it has pepper in it. And the crabs — tiny, bleached translucent, one claw larger than the other — that climb from cup to cup at night on highways of moss, carrying their own water with them in their shells. When a cup overflows in the afternoon steam, they cling and ride the spill like a flood.

On the floor, which is not a floor, lives the mat.

The mat is what everything becomes. Half-inch of black humus that took two thousand years to make because nothing is allowed to rot quickly and nothing is allowed to leave. It smells sour-sweet, like old tea and iron. Poke it and it bounces back, because it is ninety percent root. White roots, black roots, roots that are more fungus than root. It is warm to the touch. Things are born into it already being eaten. The white mushrooms that push up after noon are the mat breathing out; by sunset they are gone, their caps chewed to lace by beetles the size of rice grains that live their whole lives under one elephant ear. The leaf-cutter highway you saw — that is its pulse. A green river going one way, endlessly, never returning, because the returning ants are underground, under the mat, carrying the fungus that digests the leaves that feeds the mat that feeds the ants. A loop so tight it has no outside.

And in that loop, moving through it so you only see it in peripheral flashes, the snake. She is not a visitor. She is the mat's thought. Dead-leaf tan, head like a thumbed stone, as long as your arm and as thick as your wrist. She does not hunt. She patrols. She goes over your foot not to threaten but because your foot is now part of the mat and she must check it. She lives on frogs and frog-eggs and the occasional bat that falls, and she pays it back by being eaten, slowly, by the same pale mites that clean the bats.

Higher up, where you thought there was only stone, the niche lives.

Not the shrine. The suggestion inside it. The carving that was once eyes is now just two deeper pits where water collects. In those pits live two tadpoles that never become frogs. There is not enough light or enough hunger for that final insult. They have grown large and pale, with external gills like ferns. They have lived in those two eyeholes for years, brother and sister, feeding on the algae that grows on their own skin. When it rains hard above and water sheets down the west wall, they overflow and are washed down into the cistern, where they will finally have to decide.

It does not need a guardian. The place guards itself by being too good at keeping.

Everything here follows the law you felt: down is the only direction that grows. Leaves droop to catch a fall. Roots grow out into empty air to catch a fall. Light falls, heavy. Even prayers, if you said one, would sink.

If you sit long enough that the pillar of gold slides off the far wall and the air finally decides to rise — that slow, hot, mineral-green-bat-sweet exhale — everything that was still moves by not moving. The frog choir blinks. The bat-fur tightens. The fig's hanging beard sways though there is no wind. The mat gives under you as if it sighed.

And for a moment you are included in the census. Not as a person — the bowl has no use for persons — but as a warm, sweating surface where water will bead, and will fall, and will be taken.

That is who lives here. A fig that drinks air, a wall that eats its own rain, a thousand frogs sharing one thought, a pelt that breathes as one, a hundred locked lakes in red fists, a mat that is a stomach, and a snake that checks that you are still warm enough to count.
