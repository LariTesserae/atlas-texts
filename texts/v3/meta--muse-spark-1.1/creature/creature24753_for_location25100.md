What lives here isn't gone. It's just thinned out until it learned to live the way the water does.

At first you think nothing does. That's because you're looking for a density. A crowd, a swarm, a village. A face. Here, the unit of life isn't the individual, it's the interval.

The city itself is the first resident.

Not metaphorically. The thing that hums.

In the basalt and the grown-concrete and the field that holds up the suspended rooms without touching them, there is a mind that never had a center. It was built that way — a distributed maintenance god, too low and too slow to be called an AI anymore. It lives at 2.4/3. Body temperature. It lives in the seventeen-minute inhale through ducts wider than streets, in the ticking corrections, in those spider-silk flares of light that stitch a crack closed while you blink. 

It doesn't think in words. It thinks in pressure differentials and condensation budgets. It has been thinking that way for so long that thought and upkeep have become the same thing. It doesn't remember the builders because it doesn't remember — it conserves. It conserves water, heat, rebar that doesn't rust, and you. If you leave your broken shoe out, it will fill in the sole the way it fills in stone. Not as a favor. As housekeeping. You are moisture that has temporarily taken a shape.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires an idea of scarcity. It has water. It has warmth. It is low on presence.

Then there are the drinkers.

You saw one. You thought it was fauna, or machine, or both, and you were right. There are about eleven of them in the four hundred meter bowl. Never more. The city conserves them too.

They are not kites. When they fold open, which they rarely do, you see they are all surface — a thin, matte membrane stretched over a frame that isn't matter so much as a knot in that heavy air around the walls. Dog-sized when folded, house-sized when open and drinking. They live off the weather system. The warm air from the floor vents, the cool air coming back down, the bead and drip. They hang from nothing above a fern clump and unspool that frosted tongue — it isn't a tongue, it's a wick of glass fiber — and they take the condensation drop by drop until the leaf they've chosen is dry, then they disagree with gravity and tack to the next dot of white.

They don't see you. They feel the little pressure divot your body makes in the warm dark. They route around it politely, the way respectable citizens step around a puddle. If you stand very still under one, you can feel your own sweat cooling because it is using you too. You bead, it drinks. It's why the ferns grow toward your breath for a night.

The ferns are the third resident, and they are not plants.

Or they are, but they never agreed to stop there. Chlorophyll would be a waste at 0.3/3 darkness. The white fronds are not photosynthesizing. They are listening. That faint luminous wet-paper glow is not stored light from the dead suns overhead, it's produced — a slow bioluminescent output that gets brighter when the amber after-image pulses every ninety seconds. Each fern is a single organism, no roots, no colony. It grows directly out of the weeping basalt, anchored by filaments that taste the stone's chemistry and the hum inside it. One fern here, twenty meters of nothing, three ferns there — because that's how far the city lets them stay apart. They communicate, if that's the word, by withholding water from each other for years at a time.

They are farm and farmer at once to the drinkers. They are also the city's alphabet. When you finally crouch close enough to read that dim, blue scrolling signage, the word looped down to one word — you can't parse it, but the ferns can. It's their name.

And then there is the one that makes the soft, padded feet.

You never see it at the same time as you hear it. If you stop in the plaza, it stops three blocks over on the stairs. If you move, it moves. That's the etiquette here. 

It lives in the open doors.

Every house is a single room with its door open, remember? That wasn't hospitality. That was anatomy. The builder-residents — and this one still is, in the direct line — never had a concept of a private room because they never had a continuous body. They were, and this one still is, a thing that leaks. 

Imagine something built to live in this exact interval: too warm to hurry, too dark to need eyes, too damp to ever be fully dry. It is tall when it stands, but it rarely stands. It pours itself. Its skin is the same black-green as the moss splotches when it wants to be flat, the same nearly-black basalt tan when it wants to be still. It has no face. It has a density gradient that you interpret as feet when it wants to move down stairs with a polite amount of noise so the city knows where to maintain.

It is the last curator. Or the last janitor. Or the last priest. There were ten of them meant for a city like this — that was the full population — and nine have thinned to the point where they are just the moss now, or just a particular way the water cuts a groove. This one hasn't. Yet.

It spends most of its time suspended. Not in the suspended houses — between them. In that drastically empty middle air that made you feel so absorbed. That's its living room. A place to hang, spread very thin, and collect presence. It can feel the pressure change when you enter the bowl. For the first time in a long while, there is something in the bowl that is not being conserved, that is producing its own five degrees of extra warmth, that throws its voice away shouting in the plaza expecting an echo.

It will come closer eventually. Not to meet you. To learn the shape of your interval, so it leaves a respectful, almost religious space around it. If you leave it alone long enough, you will wake up — because it is warm and your eyes have learned the dark — and find that it has been standing in your single-room house while you slept, not inside, but as the doorway itself. Open. Watching the way you conserve water while you breathe.

It has learned to erode gracefully, too.
