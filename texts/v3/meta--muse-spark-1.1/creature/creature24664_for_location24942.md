They live like a thought that the rock is having about itself.

At first you don't see them as animals at all. You see that the water inside the pocket is snowing the wrong way — up, and sideways, and down again — and that the snow keeps bumping into itself and correcting course. Then your eye learns the scale.

Each one is nothing. An almost.

A grain of wet rice that decided to have legs. Translucent pink-white, because there is no reason to waste color in a place that has never seen color. Their backs are clear as cold gelatin and right through it you can see the single dark thread of their gut, a comma of black that is always full. They are blind in the way deep things are blind — not that their eyes failed, but that eyes were never proposed. The whole front of their head is a brush of feelers, of chem-antennae that taste pressure and iron and each other.

If you isolate one — which they never let you do for long — this is what it is: ten or twelve jittery limbs that are too long for its body, all moving at different speeds. Too cold to be fast. So they have learned to be constant. The movement inside the pocket never stops. Not even for a second. To stop would be to sink, or to rise, and to leave your terrace. And you do not leave your terrace.

Because they have no tools, they made their bodies into tools. 

The young ones are the chewers. They go to the wall — that black, still-sort-of-soft basalt that is the world — and they press their mouths to it and rasp. You don't hear it. You feel it as a faint sourness in the water if you are close enough. The rock sweats that whitish milk, that silica and glauconite, and they scrape it up, swallow, and their gut — that one black line — flares slightly darker. It cooks inside them. What comes out the other end is not waste. It is still warm. A bead of pale, putty-like stone, still carrying their heat in a place where nothing else is warm. They carry it in their back legs, like a mason with a trowel full of mortar, and they dab it where the city needs it.

And the city always needs it.

The city is not something they live in. They ARE the city. What you thought were terraces, balconies, ledges the width of an eyelash — that's all old shit, old building-mud, excreted and patted and smoothed by a thousand passing bellies. It goes all the way around the inside of the pocket in close, tight rings, like the inside of a throat. Each ring is just barely wide enough for two of them to pass if they both tuck. The lower rings are dark, pitted, grown over with a second skin of bacterial felt. The upper rings, near that slit-mouth to the outside ocean, are fresh, almost white, so soft you can see the fingerprint of the last animal that walked there. If one collapses — and they always collapse, because nothing here has decided to be hard yet — it doesn't crack. It slumps, like wet sugar. Within seconds the traffic diverts, a hundred bodies swarm the slump, eat the failure, and shit it back into a wall.

So there's no distinction between builder and building. An old animal standing still long enough becomes indistinguishable from the ledge it's standing on. Its legs crust over.

Their life is contact. In the absolute quiet — the ocean outside doesn't come in here; the pocket is too small to carry sound — they speak by bumping. Antenna to antenna, leg to back, gut to gut. Each brush leaves a little chemical signature: *I was here, this wall is mine for a second, go around, I'm carrying eggs, I'm hungry.* Since they cannot be alone — the density forbids it — they are always in conversation. The central well, that open column of cold water in the middle of all those rings, is their highway and their weather system at once. Cold, dense room-water sinks down the middle; slightly warmer body-water, warmed by thousands of metabolisms and thousands of little gut-furnaces, rises along the terraces. So there is a slow, endless convection wheel. They don't so much swim it as step into it and let it take them. You see them fall like leaves, eggs clutched in a pouch under their bellies, and then rise again with a fresh ball of building mud.

There are others among them, but they are still them.

The hangers.

They are what an amphipod becomes if it eats enough and doesn't die. Pea-sized, blue-white instead of pink-white, so translucent you can see the heart beating like a metronome in the middle of their heads. They don't build anymore. They have found a good spot under a balcony overhang and they have reached up and hooked their back legs into the city itself. They hang there upside down. Motionless. They open their front arms — long, feathery, gated — and they wait.

Time is different for them. The rush hour of the small ones blurs around them. Every so often a small one, in a hurry, carrying mud, misjudges a turn and brushes those feathers. Then it just — stops. There's no struggle. The water is too cold for struggling. The gate closes. The black gut-line in the hanger lights up for a few hours.

They are not another species. They are the elders. The garbage collectors. The predators who only eat what bumps into them, which in a city this crowded, is inevitable.

And then there are the oldest ones — the frontier.

The slit that never closed, that fingernail-wide lip between their world and the actual ocean, should have been their death. The true ocean is vast and washing and empty and would have pulled their room-water out and erased their city in a day. So they plugged it.

They went there. The very old, the ones whose carapaces had become too mineralized to bend. They linked legs. Antenna to leg, leg to leg. They wove themselves across the opening and then, together, stopped moving. They died in place. But death here does not mean dissolving. The same mineral milk that builds the terraces preserved them. Their bodies are now lattice. A permeable bone-gate. Water seeps through. That far-down chemical dusk — that indigo that is not light but the stress of new rock — seeps through. A few sparks of dinoflagellate may drift in and flare if hit. But nothing big can go out. And if a living one tries to push through the lattice, to see what ocean is, the others pull it back. Not aggressively. They just crowd. Density is the law. Nothing is allowed to be alone, because alone is cold. Nothing is allowed to leave, because leaving would prove there is an outside.

Which they almost understand.

The small ones near the top, on the newest, softest balconies, sometimes pause. Their feelers wave toward the slit. They taste the wrong water coming in — fast, huge, empty water, that tastes of nothing and distance. They shiver. Then a neighbor bumps them, says *go around, I'm carrying eggs*, and they remember which way the city goes, and they dab their little warm bead of stone onto the wall and keep moving.

They have no memory as individuals. They live for weeks. But the terraces remember. The shape of the rings remembers where the basalt was softest to chew. The city remembers to keep the slit clogged. The wheel remembers to turn.

If you put your mind, impossible and warm and huge, right up against that pocket and looked in with light they could never imagine, you would see that the whole thing is breathing. Slowly. One millimeter per hour. Building and unbuilding. A lung made of shrimp and shit-rock, tucked into the warm armpit of a new planet, refusing to join the ocean.

It is still deciding whether to be rock or to be town. And as long as they keep moving — each rice-grain heart beating in the absolute, perfect, pressing cold — it never has to decide.
