At first you think nothing lives there.

That's what the place tells you. That's what it's built to tell you. The white is too white, the dry is too dry. Nothing can spare the water.

Then you lie there a little longer with your shoulder blades against the low interwoven ceiling, and your ear against the floor of thorns, and the drone gets inside your skull until you stop hearing it as a sound and start feeling it as a pressure, and you realize the pattern of light hasn't moved — which means you *have* been here a long time. Long enough for your breath to stop steaming the cold. Long enough for the place to forget you arrived.

That's when you see that the density has a second density inside it.

Not a shadow. Not a stain. Something that fits the negative exactly.

It lives in the dog-curl. Where you are lying, it was lying. The space was not made for you. You are only borrowing its shape.

It is low. Everything here is low. It never stood up. It has no reason to be tall. It is pressed. When you first look, you take it for more dead wood — a thicker bun of the black branches, a place where the lattice knotted over itself twice. Its color is the same black polished to white by salt, its skin is the same bark-scales, overlapping like shingles. It has no green. The little grey-green on the outer rind is not it; that is new, still trying. It is older than that.

If you put your hand out — and you have to, because your eyes can't separate it from the walls — you feel that it is cool. Ten degrees cooler than the pan, same as the air, same as the wood. But it gives very slightly. Not soft. Tight. Like a drumhead.

What lives there is what happens when something that builds its house entirely out of itself lives in that house for three hundred years.

It is not a plant anymore, not in the way wire-thorn out on the flat is a plant. That one still keeps the old bargain: leaf to sun, root to deep wet. This one broke the bargain a long time ago. There is no soil under it. There is no bedrock anymore. It ate it. It wove over it. Its roots are in the air now - the cold air that pools in this slack and never leaves. It drinks temperature. It drinks sound.

At its center — you can't see it from the outside, you have to be inside and let your eyes unfocus from the thousand pinhole suns — there is a heart, but not a heart. A burl, smooth, blind, the size of your two fists together, made of live wood that has grown so slow its rings are not rings anymore but one solid grain. It does not beat. It tightens. Once every few minutes, maybe every hour, it takes up a little more slack. That's how the knot got so tight. That's why you cannot slip a finger between the branches without traveling a foot. It is constantly pulling itself infinitesimally inward.

And around that heart, the rest of it. The inhabitant is the whole knot, but also the thing curled inside the knot, because there is no difference. The dead branches are its memory. They don't rot because nothing here eats dead wood, so it never loses anything it ever grew. Its childhood is still there, on the outside, polished by wind. Its youth is the middle layer. Its present is deep in, pressed against you. It built its dead around itself like a hermit builds a cell of stone, then stayed so long the stone became him.

It does not move. Movement would be waste. It grows inward, which looks like stillness. It listens inward, which sounds like a flute.

That drone you heard from half a mile off — that one unwavering note — that is not wind catching on wood. That is the building breathing. The thousand pinhole tubes are its nostrils, its flutes, its ears. The air outside is hot and thin and fast over the pan. The air inside is cold and still and held. When the outer air pulls over the top, the pressure difference makes the whole packed lung sing. The inhabitant has been listening to its own hum for a century. It uses it to feel sun. To feel time. Inside its wooden chest, the vibration shakes loose a little resin. The smell you thought was old sun-baked resin and hot salt — it is fresh. It is always making a little more, right now, to mortar a new scale.

It is not lonely. That is a word with too much water in it. It is complete. Nothing is imported. No stone, no sand enters. It has gone beyond needing a world. It took one plant's worth of world and folded it until it was enough to live in. And it left precisely enough room inside its own density for one other brief, warm thing to lie down, shoulder to wood, and finally hear how loud stillness is when it has a roof.
