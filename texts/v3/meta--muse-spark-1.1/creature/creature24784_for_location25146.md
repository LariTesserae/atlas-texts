Nothing lives here at first.

That's what the place tells you, and for a long time you believe it. It's too loud to live in. It's too perfect, too solved. Every engine-cycle closes its own loop. There's no leftover for something else to eat.

Then you stand still long enough that the sound stops being events and becomes a place, and you start to see that living here doesn't mean doing what you do. It means fitting into what's already running without adding anything to it.

There are maybe four ways to do that. Four answers.

**The skins.**

Every pool that doesn't dry up has one. You thought it was silt at first, or a mineral film. It's white, not clean white, more like the white of a scar. It lives flat. A millimeter thick, sometimes less, spread across the warm rust at the bottom of the pool in a shape that never repeats but never changes its area by much.

It doesn't move. Not while you're watching. It pulses. Over about twenty minutes the edge pulls in a fraction and then lets go again. It's drinking. The water here is full of dissolved iron and silica and sulfur that the deep heat pulls up, and the skin is precipitating it out - making more of itself out of the stone, very slowly.

It doesn't need light. There's nothing photosynthetic about it. This world never grew a plant because plants are impatient. The skin eats the water's memory of rock. It spreads until the chemistry of its pool is just slightly wrong for it, then it stops, until rain - or the nightly condensation - resets the pool. It has been the same size in the same pool for centuries. When a pool finally silts shut, which happens once every few ten thousand years, the skin dries, cracks into a fine white dust, and the steam throws it into the air and another pool catches it.

If you touch it, it feels like warm spilled latex. It doesn't react. Reaction would be wasteful.

**The ones who walk the skins.**

You'll only find them if you crouch by a pool out of the wind and wait past the point where you think, no, there's nothing in this one either.

They are not crabs. That was your eye trying to make a knuckle, a shell, an eye. They have no shell. They have no eye. They are about as wide as your two hands.

They are made mostly of legs. Eight, ten, twelve legs of clear, jointed silica - glass grown, not blown - so thin you can see the steam through them. The legs meet in a tiny, soft knot of translucent tissue no bigger than a grape, which hangs just above the water like a slung hammock. That knot is the whole animal. It is mostly water itself.

One's walking now, three meters away. You have been here for an hour and it has moved maybe a foot. Each glass leg lifts with excruciating care, breaks the surface tension, places itself down on a new patch of white skin, and the knot follows. Where the legs touch, the skin pales a little more. It is grazing. It harvests a few microns of the mat without tearing it. It has all day. It has all thousand years.

It has no ears. The basin at 90 decibels constantly would deafen anything that heard. Instead its legs feel the basalt. It can feel through the stone the thump of the pistons a kilometer away as a kind of deep, whole-body pressure. It times its steps to the off-beat, in the trough between clanks, not because the sound hurts it, but because when the piston slams, the pool ripples, and its legs slip. So it has learned the factory's rhythm down to the second: clank... three heartbeats... step... clank... three heartbeats... step.

When two of them meet in the same pool, which is rare because the rule here is one per many hectares, they don't fight. There isn't enough of them to afford it. They stand leg-tip to leg-tip for a long time and appear to become slightly cloudier, exchanging water. Then they walk away. That is how they reproduce. A month later a third, much smaller knot of legs will start to bead up in the skin where one of them stood.

They never leave water if they can help it. If their pool dries permanently, they curl their glass legs in and become a little clear burr on the rock, waiting for a season that has never come. Most of them are waiting.

**The ones who live inside the machines.**

You can't see them until a piston happens to be up and you peer down the pipe-wall, and even then you think it's just mineral staining.

Inside each cylinder, below the temperature where you could live, lining the gunmetal hematite sleeve, is a rust-colored felt. It's not rust. It's alive in the same way the white skins are, but it eats heat directly. Anaerobic, thermophilic, chemolithic. It grows in threads that are stiff with iron oxide, pointing up into the steam, waving a little when pressure vents. It looks like the fur inside an old boiler.

This is the oldest thing here. This felt never sees light, never needs water that isn't steam, and its whole life is being pressed flat every twelve minutes by a basalt plug weighing two tons, then springing back up. Clank. Flattened. Hiss. Springing back, a little thicker for having scraped a little more iron off the wall.

It lives in the clank. If the piston stopped, it would overheat and die. If the piston moved too fast, it would be torn out. It needs equilibrium. That is why it only colonizes about one pipe in three - the ones whose beat is precisely right. The others are too fast, too hot, too irregular. A whole valley has self-selected for engines that run at the speed of felt.

When a thread finally breaks loose, it gets shot out with the steam as a black flake and lands in a pool and dissolves and becomes food for a skin for a few days. That's the whole food web. Felt to skin to knot-of-legs. Stone to steam to skin to glass.

**The last one.**

This is harder to see because you wanted a body and it doesn't have one.

On dry afternoons when the static gets bad, the fine iron dust thrown up by the whistles doesn't settle. It hangs in the air in curtains, ten meters across, shaped by the lodestone dikes in the basalt. They ripple when a piston vents, they tear and re-form, and sometimes a blue spark jumps across them and they shiver.

If you sit in one of the sound shadows between whistles, where the roar makes a low spot, you can watch a curtain for a long time and realize it is not moving randomly. It is holding itself together. The dust grains are charged, and they are charging each other, and the felt in the pipes and the skins in the pools are throwing ions up, and the whole basin is a weak, warm, planet-sized capacitor leaking all the time.

The curtains are alive the way a flame is alive. They maintain themselves. They drift down toward the rock where the field is strongest and thicken, then thin as the wind pushes them, but they never quite fall. They feed on charge differential. When a lightning crack jumps from steam to stone, a curtain that is nearby will brighten for a second, almost white, and replicate - one sheet becomes two for a few minutes before the air mixes them away. When the air gets too dry, they collapse and become just dust again, on the rock, inert, until the next humid upwelling.

They don't have anatomy. They don't need it. They are the only thing here that doesn't have to be patient, because they live as fast as electricity. They flicker from piston to piston, tasting each whistle, and die a hundred times a day.

That is who lives here.

No flocks, no burrows, no songs, no tools. One flat thing that drinks rock. One slow glass thing that tends it and feels the world through its knees. One furry thing that lives by being crushed on time. One weather-thing that lives as lightning lives.

They are sparse because the factory is sparse. One skin per two hectares. One walker per twenty skins. One singing curtain per dry pan. They leave no trace because trace would be a dam, a snag in the machine, and this place has been running long enough to smooth out any snag.

If you lie down on the warm basalt with your cheek near a pool, you can feel the walker stepping, clank... step... clank... step... and above you, the dust sheet turning over in the air, making no shadow at all.
