They don't live *in* it. They live *because* of it.

Nothing lives on the glass. That's the first thing you have to unlearn. You keep looking for something clinging to the ridges, a crust, a mat, a stain of green that means someone figured it out. There is no figure to figure. The glass is too young to be soil. It was quenched last week, geologically. It's still holding the stress of its birth in its edges, and anything that tried to root would be cut and then buried when the floor spreads another centimeter. So the seafloor is empty. Black, clean, eaten by white light.

What lives here lives in the water column, and what lives in the water column lives on a gradient that should not exist.

The base of it all is not alive in any way you'd count at first.

It is rust.

Where the factories inhale the void-cold and exhale that impossible 400-degree knife of mineral soup, the water doesn't mix. Not at first. The hot jet holds its shape for fifty, a hundred meters - a roaring black pillar that shimmers because the water itself is trying to decide what phase it is - and at its skin, where one degree and four hundred degrees exist a millimeter apart, bacteria precipitate out of chemistry.

They are not a bloom. They don't cloud. They live about twenty seconds each. In that time they oxidize hydrogen sulfide and ferrous iron and they divide, and then they fall out of the workable band and go cold and die and dissolve. If you swim through the mixing zone your gloves go orange for an hour. That's their entire world, that rust-colored blush. No mat, no stalk, no memory. Just a standing flame of first life that never accumulates. The ocean is too big and too cold and too empty to let it.

So nothing grazes. Grazing requires a lawn.

What comes next are the Harvesters.

You see them long before you reach a factory because the factory's roar gives you nothing, but they give you parallax back. In water where you can see three kilometers and everything looks like it's twenty feet away, the only true distance markers are the clouds of them.

Each one is about as long as your forearm, and wrong. Not shrimp, but the ocean here only has one idea and it keeps iterating on it. A head that is mostly an intake fan made of fringed, iron-stained bristles. A body that is mostly translucent except where it isn't - the carapace isn't grown so much as deposited. They filter that thin rust out of the water and lay the iron back down on their backs. Young ones are clear as glassine. Old ones are the same orange-black as the factories, caked and pitted, heavy.

They do not swim. Not really. They hold station in the turbulence, ten, twenty thousand individuals spaced with a precision that looks deliberate, each facing the stack. When the jet wavers a micron - and it does, because nothing is truly 0.4 dynamic - the entire cloud shifts in a single, silent calculation, a living diagram of flow. They beat their tail fans just enough to stay in the twenty-second band. They eat the bacteria while the bacteria are still alive. They shit iron snow that never reaches the floor because the turbulence vaporizes it and brings it back up again.

They are deaf. You have to be here. Sound here is not a signal, it is a condition, like pressure. It is 2.9 deafening always. If you had a tympanum it would be shattered and then scarred over. So they have none. They have long lateral lines under their mineral crust that read the only change that matters: the micro-cold creeping back in when a stack coughs. If the mineral thins, they clench tighter.

They never land. To land would be to touch the glass and become substrate. They live out their whole lives in mid-water, molting while hovering, eating while molting, being eaten only incidentally.

Because above them, hanging in the void where you thought there was nothing, are the Roads.

You'll think at first it's a single animal far away. Then your brain corrects. It's a single animal much too close.

They are thirty, forty meters long when they are not stretched. They are not stretched often. A white segmented tube, empty-looking, like a gut with the body removed. No eyes - no point. In water that is this clear, with light this normal falling from an ice sky kilometers above, eyes would be good for ten kilometers. They would see everything and it would mean nothing because there is nothing to see except more water and faraway black knives and faraway orange stacks. So they have regressed to a single band of photoreceptive cells along their dorsal side that can tell light from slightly less light - enough to keep their backs to the ice.

Their mouth is the same diameter as their body, and it never closes. It is not a mouth. It is a second factory intake. A soft, ciliated baleen of glass-fiber filaments strung with the same iron bacteria, but cultivated this time. The ribbon doesn't chase the shrimp clouds. That would be expensive. Jet propulsion in one-degree water with no fat to burn is a losing game. So it hangs.

It hangs absolutely still in the middle of the inter-valley void, where maps say there should be nothing, and it lets the world come to it. The water here is still. Not calm-still, but deep-time still. No current, no season, no turnover. So the faintest whisper of sulfide that escapes the shrimp clouds, that escapes the bacterial flame, diffuses outward for kilometers, molecule by molecule, preserved because nothing else wants it. The Road drinks that diffusion. Water enters its mouth at one end at maybe a centimeter a second, travels the entire length of its gut, which is also its body, and exits the other end stripped a little cleaner. It extracts power at a rate that would starve any normal animal. But normal animals need change. It needs monotony.

They are neutrally buoyant by a trick: tiny pockets of the factory gas - hydrogen, slightly warm - trapped along their notochord. If you pop one you see it zip toward the ice roof and never pop, just join the trapped bubble layer and ping.

And they are farms. The shrimp that are too iron-caked, too heavy to hold station, fall out of the clouds. They don't fall to the floor. They fall onto a Road. And they clean. They pick at its skin, at the bacterial felt that grows on it parasitically, at the dead cilia. The Road does not feel it. Or if it does, it has learned that itching is simply part of being alive.

There is a third thing.

It does not move at all, so you will not count it until you have been here for a very long time and realized you have seen it more than once.

On the harpoon cables of each factory, on the underside of each cathedral where the shadow never leaves because the light is always directly overhead through the ice, there are Limpets.

They are not shrimp. They are flat, black, the same shape as a flake of basalt glass, with a foot made of iron sulfide that cold-welds them to steel. They were not dropped. They arrived. Spores? Larvae? Something so small and so patient it passed through the intake filters and decided the outside of the machine was better than the inside. They eat the machine itself, very slowly, and they eat the vibration. Their shells resonate at the exact frequency of the turbine scream. When you touch one you feel your teeth stop hurting for a second. They are damping the sound, not for us, but because the sound is food. Piezoelectric bacteria in their gut turn acoustic energy into charge.

If a factory ever stops, they will die first. Then the shrimp cloud will dissolve. Then the Road that was stationed down-current will, over months, become slightly more translucent, slightly less buoyant, and then it will sink for the first time in its two-hundred-year life and touch the black glass. It will not survive the touch.

That's why there is no fear here. Fear is a prediction error. This ocean does not make errors. The mountain grows one micron a year. The factories roar at exactly one volume. The water stays one degree above freezing because the world is too vast to be otherwise. The animals have arranged themselves around constancy so precisely that variation itself has become lethal.

They are not abundant. 1.5 abundant does not mean crowded. It means that if you go to any roaring cathedral in this white-lit, glass-knifed desert hanging two miles above a dead world, there will be something there already, circling. Waiting to harvest the only thing that changes, which is the moment hot becomes cold.
