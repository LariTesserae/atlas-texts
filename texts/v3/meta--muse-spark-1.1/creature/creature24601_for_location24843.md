Nothing lives here, if by living you mean something that wants to continue.

No animal could. There's no water to spare for blood, no food chain to climb. No bird strays into the fissure because there's no reason to. No lichen even — lichen needs a history of rain.

What lives here lives because this place can't *not* have something living in it.

From the threshold they look like ferns left in a forge to rust. That’s the first image and it’s wrong enough to be useful. Three to five feet, set apart like monks who took a vow of distance — seven, ten, twelve feet between each stalk, because each one needs its own private piece of the cooling. They are not crowded. They don’t compete. There isn’t enough of anything to compete over, except heat, and there’s too much of that.

Come closer. The wrongness sharpens.

It’s not a trunk. It’s a chimney. A hollow column of what looks like dirty glass and pig iron laid down in alternating, impossibly thin skins, still too warm to touch. You can see the heat coming off it as a shimmer. At the base, where it meets the basalt that is not yet basalt — that black, still angry stuff — there is no root. There is a *weld*. Or rather, there isn’t a weld, because a weld means someone welded it. The rock simply becomes more organized for a few inches until it is stalk. The mountain decided to be vertical here.

And the fronds — 

They are not leaves. You keep wanting them to be leaves. They are heat sinks. Radiator arrays. Each one is a hundred, two hundred paper-thin fins of black silica edged in hematite red, overlapping like feathers, each one veined. The veins are full. If you put your eye to the light you can see something moving in them, not green, not sap, not water. Oil-black and quick, it ticks upward when it’s hot and falls when it’s cool. Capillary action. No pump. A closed loop the plant invented without ever being taught fluid dynamics. It doesn’t drink. It cycles.

It breathes out dryness. Stand next to one and your lips crack faster. The air around it is somehow *more* empty of water than the terrible air already in the pocket.

And it is working. Put your ear close, you’ll hear it at about the level you hear your own blood — a tick... tick... tick... once every seven seconds, a check valve made of natural ceramic opening and closing. A sigh that isn’t steam because there is nothing to make steam from. The sound of a factory floor at midnight when the machines keep their own hours.

They don’t grow toward the light. The noon-white shaft from the fissure falls on them and they unmake it. That’s the part you notice if you stay longer than a minute. Each fin casts two shadows. One proper shadow on the hot rock. And one other — a foot above the rock, in empty air, its edges darker than the center, like an afterimage burned into space itself. Move your hand through that upper shadow and your hand goes cold. Numb-cold. The heat has been taken out of that slice of air and put somewhere you can’t see.

They’re making cold. Out of forge-heat. Storing it in that second shadow. Thermodynamically obscene.

If you watch one tip for a long, uncomfortable hour in that kiln, you will see it do the one thing it was born to do: grow. Not by cell division. By accretion. A faint click, and a new fin the size of your thumbnail is suddenly there, extruded molecule by molecule from invisible vapor. A whisker of iron wire crystallizes out of the air and is pulled into the stalk. The basalt at its foot gets a little sharper, a little more perfect, as if the plant is cleaning up after the mountain.

It won’t be here long. None of them will. This is a five-hour-old ecology. A one-day ecology. The pocket is still closing. When the rock finally learns to be rock, when it finally cools enough that it doesn’t need to be refrigerated, these ferns will have no reason to precipitate. They will stop ticking. The oil in their veins will harden to glass. They will become what they always were halfway becoming — a slightly more complicated piece of basalt, a knot in the stone with a memory of fins inside it.

Right now, though, in this newborn, anhydrous, un-eroded closet at the top of a mountain that hasn’t finished becoming a mountain, they are lush. Absolute, spare, industrial, and completely convinced of their work.
