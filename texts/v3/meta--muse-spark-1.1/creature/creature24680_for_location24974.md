Nothing lives here at first.

That's what the bowl tells you. The black terraces, the water sheet, the mat of fines that looks like cured concrete. No green. No leaf, no blade, no fuzz of moss to tell you where the water is. An empty sump that the air uses to wring itself out.

Then the bottom moves wrong.

Not with wind. The water has no wind in it, down here below the rim. The air is too heavy to stir. The movement is against the trickles coming down off the basalt steps, a counter-flow, a slow pour of something pale uphill through two inches of black-clear water. And when you crouch — because you have to crouch to see it — the pour resolves.

They live shoulder to shoulder.

The grazers. Isopods, if you need a word that has legs in it, but these have forgotten to be gray. They are the color of milk-glass left in cold water, each as long as the last joint of your thumb, their backs domed and beaded with droplets that never fall because their cuticle is colder than the air, too. They are the ground, here. Thousands. Tens of thousands. An acre of living shingle that eats by walking.

They keep their heads down. Under each frilled belly a hundred white cilia are combing, scraping. The bottom isn't soil, it's skin — a bacterial film, dark maroon, the thickness of old paint, that grows without sun. It eats dissolved iron, it eats the sigh of sulfur off the slag wall, it eats the air itself. The isopods eat it. You can see the pale trails they leave, a day's grazing as a faint, brighter line that fills back in by evening, because the film doubles when you aren't watching.

They don't flee. When your boot edge comes down, the sheet parts and reforms. One or two climb up to test you. Their antennae are short, blunted, crusted with iron. They taste salt, they taste oil from your laces. They decide you are another banker of iron that will eventually flake and be eaten.

Under them, the mat breathes. That's what the little perfect holes are. The bacterial skin traps gas — hydrogen sulfide, a little methane — and it has to belch. It lifts into a blister and pops, leaving a round mouth. The isopods pause at each pop, as if listening.

In the deeper parts, where the quarrymen drilled and the drill went too deep, the water is up to your wrist. There the other ones hang.

Shrimp that never learned to swim right side up. Translucent, you can read the bacterial matte through them. They hang from the underside of the water itself, feet hooked into surface tension, guts a rust-orange thread from all the iron bacteria they've filtered. If you breathe too hard you break the skin and they drop, and then they kick once, angrily, and re-hook somewhere else. They aren't many, but they are constant — one per blast-hole, like a lamp.

And the walkers.

You thought the isopods were alone until one of the white stones stood up and took a step. It's not white stone. It's a salamander only if you expect salamanders in a mine. Four inches long, also blind, also white, because there is no advantage in any other color down here. Its skin is wet in a different way than everything else — it doesn't bead, it sheets. On each side of its head, three external gills spread like diseased ferns, and they are the only pink in the whole bowl. When it surfaces to breathe, and they all surface every minute or so, the pink floods dark.

They walk. They do not swim. They pick their way across the living carpet on too-long toes, and the carpet lets them. Every few steps a head darts — no snap, just a soft *tuk* like a wet finger against a cheek — and an isopod is gone. Not the old ones, scarred and mineral-capped. The young, pale-pale ones. The rest of the carpet takes one synchronized half-step sideways, a ripple, and resumes eating. Nothing panics in a place where food grows out of air.

Up on the terraces, out of the sheet water, the density turns upward.

The snails that are the same temperature as the basalt. Their shells are basalt-colored because basalt dust has grown into them. They move so slowly you only know they have moved because the condensation trail behind them is unbroken. Each one carries a hitchhiker — a smaller, white snail that eats whatever grows on the bigger shell, usually a speck-mite you can only see when it moves. A small city, moving at stone speed. When the big snail dies, its shell stays, and becomes a terrace in miniature, with a damp shadow behind it where midges lay eggs.

The midges. They never rise above your knee because above that the air is too dry. Down here the saturation is 100% and they drink it without landing. A cloud of gray glass that hangs. If you stand inside it, you feel nothing, then a faint tickle in your ears which is not their wings, it's their being there. They light on your hands, your eyelashes, not to bite — they have no mouths worth the name — but to drink the water that has decided you are a cold rock. When they are full, they sink down and press their bellies to the basalt and let whatever eats midge eggs find them.

And on iron, the last one.

You thought the gantry arm was rusted solid. The underside of it is not rust. It's a colony, a quilt of leathery bulbs the size of a child's fists, stitched together by white veins. At rest they are slack and dripping, each drip timed wrong compared to the basalt drips. Then one inflates — you hear it, a faint creak, like leather — and goes taut and glossy, and condensation rushes to it as if called. It pulls water straight out of mist. Then it sighs and deflates, and feeds that water into the rivulet that feeds the veins that feed the rest of the colony. They have been here long enough to have learned stealing. Where it touches steel, the steel is bright, because it eats rust too, slowly.

And keeping time behind all of them, the pump house engine. *Chuff... chuff... chuff...*

It isn't alive. But everything here has organized its rhythm around it, because it is the only thing that almost stops. Between chuffs the isopods pause. The walkers surface. The bulbs inflate. The snails — maybe — consider moving.

The bowl lives because it keeps. Water comes down. It never leaves, but it cycles. Stone weeps rust, rust feeds film, film feeds carpet, carpet feeds walkers, walkers die and add iron back to stone, midges move water from air to stone faster than stone could alone, bulbs steal it back. Nothing green needs to get involved. It's closed enough that ten minutes holding the whole shape in your head feels like holding a single animal.

If you stand long enough, you bead and you drip, and you become part of the condensation step of the cycle for a while. The isopods will come to your boot and start converting you, slowly, back into bowl.
