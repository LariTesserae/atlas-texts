Nothing lives *in* it.

That's the first thing you have to unlearn. A forest has deer, has birds, has things that live between the trees. A factory has workers who go home at the end of shift. This has neither. 

What lives here lives *as* it.

### The Cultivar

The most obvious resident is the one you mistook for the building.

It is one organism, and it is not one organism. It's a continent-wide clonal cultivar that has forgotten how to die. It was bred out of hemp and bamboo and poplar and vat-grown structural fiber — things that could be taught to grow straight and hollow. Then it was taught to grow into shape. Then it was taught to teach itself.

It doesn't have roots. It has holdfasts, like kelp, that fuse directly into the Floor. No soil in between. From the Floor it draws nothing but cold and trace iron. It doesn't want water and would rot if you gave it any. The pale pith inside its leaves is a forest of glass threads — not xylem, but light-pipes. It eats the overhead glare raw and shunts it down, down, through trunk after trunk by total internal reflection. That's why if you snap a bough you see it glowing for a few minutes after. It's still thinking about light.

It is not sentient the way you are. It is sentient the way a loom is sentient when it's been running for nine hundred years and has learned to notice when a thread snags. When the electrostatic snow falls, it holds its breath. When a corridor needs to be somewhere else, it grows it overnight, slow as a joint stiffening. When gravity gets tangled in its densest knots, it does not untangle it. It incorporates the tangle. That is its only philosophy: grow around it.

The Cultivar is what lives here the way a coral reef lives as a place. Everything else lives off it, and is it.

### The Maintenance Choir

Beneath the hearing range, there is work.

You hear the one skitterer. That's what you notice. You do not notice the thousand others because they are the exact color of dry fiber and they move only when you are not looking directly at them.

They are not fauna. There never were animals here. These are *organs that left*.

The Sifters: palm-sized, white, disarticulated origami of chitin and pressed cellulose. No mouth. No eyes. Their backs are combed with triboelectric hair that harvests charge from the air. They walk the Floor forever, polishing the drain-plates with their bellies, smoothing the serial numbers even smoother. When they find a flake of spore-snow that the big machines missed, they tuck it into a pouch under their thorax and carry it to a root-wall and press it in. A graft. They don't eat. They are powered by static and dim light. If you kneel very still you will see the faint blue spark as one discharges into the seam between Floor and root. It shudders with something like pleasure.

The Trimmers: longer, jointed like a hand made of dry twigs and old cable ties. They live in the canopy bowers. You never see them move. Then you come back to a corridor you walked an hour ago and a leaf the size of a door has been clipped at a perfect 45-degree angle, the cut cauterized by a brief, silent bead of light. The air smells like hot paper. They keep the corridors from closing. The Cultivar grows; the Trimmers negotiate.

The Harvester on its continent-long rail is alive too, but in a different way. It is too big and too old to have a name. It was once a machine. Now the forest has grown *through* its chassis and it has decided that is acceptable. Vines run its power conduits where copper used to be. It hums as it clips, not because it has to, but because it has learned that humming keeps the lesser things calm. It takes fiber and extrudes Floor. It has been making the same Floor for four hundred thousand kilometers and has never gotten to an edge.

None of them care about you. You are too wet.

### The Dry

Very, very rarely, in the place where the Floor lifts into that block-sized loom, you will find someone who looks like they could have been you.

They are not.

They are what happened when a maintenance crew didn't leave. Or were told not to. Or begged to stay.

Imagine people who have been bred — or have bred themselves — out of thirst. Their skin is the same bone-white paper as the leaf backing. If you look close, their hair is not hair but fine filament: the same hollow glassy threads, grown from their scalp to carry light down into the body. Their eyes are gone milky, not from blindness but from a film that cuts glare. They have no sweat. They have no saliva. When they open their mouths to speak they don't, because the air would take moisture they cannot spare.

They speak the way the forest does: by moving light.

When one of them stands under a frond and tilts it, a scatter of light codes down the pith, and another one a kilometer away tilts a different frond in answer. That is a conversation. It can take a week.

They wear robes, but the robes are not woven. They are grown onto them — living lattice of the Cultivar that has accepted them as an excrescence. Where robe meets skin there is no seam. They were the gardeners and they have become epiphytes.

They do not drink. They collect electrostatic charge on their filament-hair during storms, standing out in the open courtyard while the luminous dust cyclones spin around them with silent lightning, arms raised. Their bodies convert charge directly into turgor. They respire mineral dust, like the plants. If you offered one water it would step away from you in horror and pity. Water is the ancient solvent, the thing that dissolves the Floor.

They are not many. Perhaps a few hundred across the whole basin. They do not reproduce quickly. When one is done — when its joints have stiffened with more fiber than cartilage — it walks to a root-wall and leans against it and roots. You will see them, if you know to look: human shapes in the wall, arms relaxed, filament-hair tangled fully into canopy, faces still calm. Still listening to the tick of cooling ceramic.

They tend the loom that weaves forest out of forest because someone has to remember that this was intended. Even if the intention has long since grown over.

You, still carrying fifty liters of salt water inside your skin, walking here with breath that almost fogs but doesn't — you are an emergency to them. A spill that hasn't happened yet. They will watch you from behind bower walls, not unkindly, until you leave, or until you learn to stop being damp.

Most visitors leave.

The ones that stay become Floor.
