What lives here did not arrive.

It condensed.

At first you think it is empty. That's because what lives here has mastered the art of not being seen, which is the art of not moving. You stand at the rounded lip of the bowl with fog collecting on your eyelashes and you think: moss, black water, stone. You think: vegetable, mineral. An uninhabited room.

Then your eyes adjust to the wet-slate light and the room adjusts to being seen.

It breathes.

Not in, out. Not a chest. The whole mat of it - moss mattress and liverwort skin and sedge tangle - rises and falls a fraction because there are bodies under it, between it, through it. It is not that animals live *on* the moss. The moss is roof.

Under every leaf that is small enough to be a roof, there is a tenant.

They are all small because only small things can live where there is no waste. Nothing is burnt here, nothing is chased. To live large is to spend. So they have pared themselves down to the cheapest possible shapes. The beetles are black as basalt polish, their backs beaded with water that never falls, and they are so numerous they touch. Not crawling over each other. Resting shoulder to shoulder under the saxifrage, antennae folded neatly back like hands in a lap. The white moths - you thought they were fungus on the hanging beards at first - there are hundreds of them on a single curtain of moss, each one exactly the same distance from the next, wings shut, not asleep, just discontinued.

The salamanders are the elders. That is not sentiment, it is length. They are the color of wet rock because they have been wet rock for so long. You lift - if you can bring yourself to intrude that much - a slick stone at the edge of the black tarn and there will be ten, twelve of them beneath it, layered like spoons, gills flowering in the cold, eyes open and filming over with condensation. They don't scatter. One will turn its head toward your warmth at the speed of lichen growing. In one forelimb, curled against its speckled chest, a flake of quartz held point-out. Not clenched. Held the way you hold a word you aren't ready to say.

That is what lives here: holders.

The frogs no bigger than your thumbnail are sunk to their chins in the living mat, throat-sacs still, watching. The midges - you first took them for ash in the air - hold station, each in its own pocket of damp air, a galaxy that decided to stop expanding. If you wave your hand through them, they part and re-form around the disturbance and then re-find their exact places, without buzzing. No one buzzes here. Buzzing costs.

And in the tarn, which is less water than a black mirror that forgives too much, the newts float, suspended, noses just breaking the skin. You can see mica glittering on the bottom: the caddis cases, each one built from perfect little plates laid like shingles, too right-angled to be accident, too purposeless to be architecture. One will drift up a millimeter and then stop, as if remembering the price.

Everything here has a tool and does not use it.

That is the tell. The beetle with the leaf, cut too clean. The twig stripped to a point and then set down, still damp with teeth marks. The salamander with its little knife. None of them are building. The parameter for built space is nearly zero - there are no nests to defend, no dams to hold, no homes beyond the body and the leaf directly over the body. Intelligence came here once, the way fog comes, settled into everything a little, and instead of rising into towers, it thinned and spread until each thing had a thimbleful - just enough to know how to pick up a stone, just enough to know it doesn't need to.

So what lives here is not a colony. A colony wants. A hive wants. What lives here has finished wanting.

It is one creature distributed across many compliant skins. If you call it a mind, it is a mind that had a single thought - hold, stay, keep - and succeeded so completely that thinking became unnecessary. The beetle does not need to talk to the salamander. The moth does not need to signal the frog. They have already negotiated every border by becoming the border. They are packed so tightly that competition has softened into contiguity. Predator and prey both holding very still in the same moss pocket, both breathing fog, both with a small sharp tool they have politely agreed not to use on each other.

If you stay long enough, you feel them notice you not as a threat but as a draft. You are too warm, too fast, too loud with wanting-to-see. Your blood is noisy. The newt's eye tracks you because your movement is a kind of forgetting. You have not yet learned what they have learned, here in the mountain's cupped palm where no sun comes and no water ever leaves:

How to be alive without spending aliveness.

How to hold a tool for a thousand years without striking.

How to live so close together that you become a surface.

The mountain made this place to keep one thought from blowing away. And the thought stayed, and grew fur, and chitin, and gills, and small wet hands.
