No one lives *on* it.

That is the first wrong idea your eyes give you and the first one the moor corrects, if you stay long enough to put weight down and feel the mat take you.

Nothing lives on top of this place because there is no top. There is only thickness.

What lives here lives *in* the thickness, and more precisely, it *is* the thickness.

---

At the scale of your hand, if you part the wool-grass and the black heather that has grown through itself, what lives here is a negotiation.

The top is not green. The very top, where the flat white light hits it honest, is bleached and dead - a thatch of last year's tips, sunburned pale, holding a centimeter of trapped air that is always a degree warmer than the air above it. Living just under that thatch, where the light is already green and diffuse, is the working leaf.

One species would not do. No single plant could hold this wind. So they decided — not decided, arrived at — to be one plant together.

There is a moss that does not make individual cushions. It makes a continent. From the lip of the plateau to the sky-colored far ranges, it is one clonal mat laced together by rhizoids fine as nerves. If you took one dinner-plate liverwort leaf and pulled, you would pull up a meter of mat with it, because it is sewn in. The black-green trailing stems of the bog myrtle run through the moss like rebar. The dwarf willow does not grow up; it throws its branches sideways through the moss and puts roots down from them, so that a willow you think is a small shrub is, twenty feet over, the same individual. The heather does the same. They have all borrowed each other's structure.

This is the resident. The bulk of it. The thing that won. A horizontal forest that chose to be meadow to survive the wind, and then chose to be one body to survive the weight of itself. It is heavy. It squeezes its own lower third constantly, and that lower third is always dying.

Below that, where no light reaches, what lives here are the refiners.

The moss does not rot, not properly. The water is too cold, too acid. So it pickles. The leaves that die stay leaves for ten years, for a hundred, turning dark, turning flat, being compressed by new growth above until the air is squeezed out of them with a sigh you can hear if you lay your head down. This is where the real industry you heard about happens.

Midges do not live here so much as the air lives midge. At any given moment, about a third of the atmosphere ten feet off the mat is not atmosphere but gnat, and another third is spiderweb fine enough to catch them. The small fat birds the color of dead grass do not nest; they just run through the mat, constantly, eating, because if they stop the mat grows over them. Their footprints fill with dark water in seconds.

Under that, through that, where the mat transitions from plant to fuel, are the miners of this sunken town: the voles.

Not like lowland voles. These are potato-shaped, short-legged, almost blind, their fur saturated with peat oils until it is waterproof. They do not dig burrows under the mat; they dig burrows *through* the mat, chewing and pushing through living rhizoid and dead peat in soft, raised highways you can see from above as a slightly yellower line. Their tunnels are warm from their bodies and always flooded to the ankle in brown water. They farm there. They carry fragments of a white fungus that only grows on peat walls and inoculate the tunnel sides, then come back and eat the fungus and the loosened peat together. Their tunnels breathe when a water throat chuffs nearby, compressing and rebounding. When a methane flame pops, blue and quiet, a few inches above the moss somewhere else, they stop and listen with their whole bodies.

The amphibians live even deeper, where even voles don't go unless forced.

They are long and pale as uncooked dough, with external gills like ferns that they never lose because they never have to come up. They live their whole lives in the water film, in that sheet of dark water that is always there between the bottom of the peat - forty feet down, where it is almost coal - and the blood-warm basalt. They are not tadpoles and not adults. They just are. You never see them, but if you put your ear to the mat you hear sometimes a very faint click as one moves a pebble in a vent pipe, keeping its throat clear. Not because it wants to keep the factory running. Because that is where the warmest water is.

Above those, but still below you, live the actual factory workers.

They are not animals. The terraces of rust-orange and yellow-white travertine that you thought looked like mills are occupied brick by brick. Each millimeter of the terrace wall is a plate of iron bacteria, stacked like chainmail, rust-colored, laying themselves down and dying and letting the next layer grow over them. They take the dissolved iron the throats breathe up and make it solid, so the throat doesn't clog. Without them the whole hydraulic system would seal itself in a year.

In the pools they sort, the colors you saw — teal and blood-red — are not chemical. They are alive. Consortia of algae that have learned to live at exactly 31 degrees, or exactly 46, or in water so full of sulfur it would strip the skin off you. They divide the spectrum between them. In the coolest vat, blue-green algae. In the hottest, a red coat so thick it looks like velvet. They refine the water by living in it.

And beneath them all, what lives here is the thing that contains all the others.

The plateau itself. The weight, the press, the chuff.

It is not conscious the way a quick animal is conscious. It is conscious the way a bog is conscious — by regulating, by holding, by not letting anything get too far out of range. If a vent pumps too hard and scorches the mat, the mat grows faster there with a different, more heat-tolerant liverwort that insulates it. If peat builds too high and dries, the mat's own weight cracks it and water finds the crack. If too many midges hatch, the threads of a fungus thickened by midge droppings tighten over the pool where they breed.

It knows how many breaths per hour. It knows, over a century, what the average is.

When you lie down and the moss gives under you, and the water beads and vanishes, and you hear that low, complex, subsonic activity — the sighs and ticks and pops and hushes — what you are hearing is not many creatures near each other.

It is one creature with many organs, working.
