You won't see them at first.

Not because they hide. Because on the Table, any living thing the size of a person is statistically not there. You can stand on a root that is a road that is a town and look across a mile of clear, damp cathedral air at another wall of green that is also a road, also a town, and the space in between is so completely empty it rewrites your idea of what a forest is. The Table is 95% air. Its life is 95% elsewhere.

The ones who call it The Table are still built like builders.

They are small. Not stunted — compact, low-center, wide-footed. Because everything you stand on is alive and weeps constantly, and is at a slight sag, and is soft with ten feet of moss and black compost that has never known a true downhill, you don't want height. You want grip. Their toes splay. Their arches have fallen on purpose generations ago. The soles of their feet are as thick and as ridged as bark, and pale green in the creases from walking all day in ankle-deep water that never runs off.

They are pale, all of them. In that permanent late-afternoon light, ten leaves thick, filtered green and then filtered again, there is no tan to be had. Their skin is the color of the underside of a leaf. Their hair grows thin and holds water, so most keep it shaved to the scalp or let it felt into a constantly damp cap that houses small, commensal things. Their eyes are very large, very dark, without much white. Large pupils for dim light. They have lungs that you can see working — thin air up here, three miles above any true ground, so their chests are barrelled, their ribs showing when they breathe slow during the Big Silences.

They don't talk much. Or rather — they talk constantly, but not with sound.

Sound goes nowhere on the Table. The moss eats it. The soft, wet lattice eats it. The voids are so large that a shout takes four seconds to find a leaf to return from, and when it does it comes back wrong, from behind you. So the old bird-call, monkey-call, human-call way of being loud stopped.

Instead they have kept the knot language that the monkeys remember. The monkeys — those too-long-armed things that are their cousins and contractors — still move through a chamber and re-tie a slipped splice without being asked, chittering in ultrasound. The people do it slower, with hand-span and palm. A language of re-tying. When two of them meet on a root-road that has to be recut this generation with a stone adze because it kept creeping east, they don't say hello. They crouch and put their hands on each other's work. "This lashing was your mother's." "I added three to it, here, the vine was lignifying wrong." Conversation is maintenance.

There are not many of them. A family every few miles of weave, because that is what the Table can feed. Everything is productive, but nothing is concentrated. So you have a mother and her two husbands and their three children and one old man whose job is to feel the thrumming, living beam under his palm during the Silences and know if the whole continent-wide bowl is settling an inch to the left this century. They live not in houses - a house would rot in a week in air that condenses on everything — but in adjustments.

They find a place where three massive bamboo pillars, each as thick as a redwood, fuse into a triangle, and where the weave above has sagged just enough to hold a perched bog, a shallow lake the size of a courtyard that never drains, kept there by surface tension and peat. They wade into it. They pull up a lip of living hedge and weave it tighter until it holds off the endless tick-tick-tick of water falling from the ten thousand tiers above. Under that, on flat slates that some ancestor hauled up and that have wept from their undersides ever since, they keep the charcoal pit going. No smelting beyond what charcoal can do. No iron worth naming. A few green-black copper lumps they anneal and then leave alone because stone holds an edge longer in this wet.

They eat what the Table makes overabundant and impossible to store: the soft white pith of new structural roots, bitter unless leached in the bog for days; the mats of fungus the beetles farm that they steal half of and leave half of; the mites — under every leaf, a nation of them — which they skim off as a sour paste; and above all, the frass.

The moths are the size of houses and they beat so slowly you think the light itself is flickering. They are silent as their scale demands. They lay their eggs in the heartwood as hard as river stone, and their caterpillars, blind and warm and the size of canoes, chew tunnels straight through the living load-bearing columns. The people love them. They follow the chewing sound — a soft, patient grinding you feel in your teeth — and wait at the other end of the tunnel for the sweet, fermented droppings, hot and yeasty, that slide out and steam slightly in the cool air. That is bread. That is beer and bread at once. A child's first job is to sit in a tunnel with a slate bowl.

They navigate by scar and mistake. Because the Table folded space to save material long before they were born, distance isn't. You can climb down for a day, through layers of weeping bark and hanging gardens and voids that stare back with a slightly different arrangement of green, and climb up out of a hole you recognize because you left your adze there yesterday and now it's rusted over in a way that means it sat there a year. So they do not make maps. They make cords. Vine cordage as thick as a waist when it was first lashed, now grown into itself and become root. They tie one to the central hearth-slate of their adjustment and let it play out behind them when they go trading — ten days to get to the next family, along a route that should be two miles. When they return, sometimes the cord is coming at them from ahead.

That is why their deepest rule is not about falling. Nothing ever truly falls here; there is always another leaf to catch you. Their deepest rule is: Never cut a load-bearing root that is dripping before cutting.

They mean: never cut a thing that is still carrying water from one non-existent elsewhere to another, because you don't know what you will sever. They have cut wrong before. Whole adjustments — families — have walked out into a big empty cathedral of air to check snares and found, when they turned around, that the wall they lived on was now a week behind them, or that the season had changed from wet-and-dimming to wet-and-brightening and their bog had frozen into a glassy circular lake that floats entirely free of attachment, rippling upward.

When the Silences come — no tick, no drip, the entire Table holding its breath to listen to itself thrumming — they stop. Everyone, even the monkeys, even the house-sized moths stalled mid-beat, still. They place a palm on the nearest living beam, which is warm, and faintly vibrating, like an animal's flank, like a project that knows it is not finished. They feel it shift a fraction of an inch toward better balance, somewhere so far away they will never see the result.

And they splice three new vines into the old knot, to help it hold.
