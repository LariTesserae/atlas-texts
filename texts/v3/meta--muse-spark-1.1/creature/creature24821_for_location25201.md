At first you think nothing could live here. The scale is wrong for life.

From a distance the tableland looks dead - a black anvil of fresh basalt with a permanent low cloud nailed to it, flashing white in the hard noon. Too high, too cold, too thin to breathe. Then you get closer and you realize the black isn't rock. Not all of it. It's moving. It's just moving so slowly you have to watch it the way you watch a glacier to see it move.

What lives here is not *on* the plateau. It *is* the plateau.

**The Ones That Make Weather**

The Furnacebacks.

There is only one real animal here, in terms of biomass. Everything else lives on it, in it, between it.

An adult Furnaceback is about as long as a transit bus and twice as wide, but that measurement lies because they are never alone to be measured. Their bodies are low, rectilinear in the way a boulder is rectilinear - a great slab of muscle and low, cold, incredible gut, draped over four stumpy pillar legs that end not in hooves but in broad, spongy pads that are half foot, half root. Their heads are almost nothing: a blunt shovel that hangs permanently down in the moss, mouth open, with lips like wet stone. There are no eyes that you can find. Why would they need them? There is nowhere to look except the back in front of them.

They eat the tableland itself.

They scoop. Basalt teeth - not enamel, but silica-capped plates that grow in concentric rings - grind against each other with the continent-wide, bone-deep groan you heard from the lowlands. The sound is not fast. *Kehhhhhhhh—* every twenty minutes as a head ratchets down, then *keh* as it closes. Inside them, the work is not digestion as we know it. Their stomachs are lined with ceramic. Literally. Cells lay down aluminum-silicate like a kiln brick. Into that chamber they secrete hydrogen and carbon monoxide made from the water they recapture and the moss they incidentally ingest, and they reduce basalt. In the dark and the cold they smelt.

What comes out the top is why they exist.

On each back, grown out of fused dorsal vertebrae, is a stack. It is not ornament. It is a chimney, two to three meters tall, made of living ceramic and precipitated iron, as intricate as a termite mound inside, riddled with baffles to keep the heat from killing the animal that made it. It breathes. All day, all night, a Furnaceback sighs out the one thing this place cannot make for itself: warm, wet air. Steam full of chemically reduced iron vapor and faint hydrogen sulfide, 60 degrees Celsius in an environment that is -20. It hits the 0.1 bar air and instantly wants to be cloud.

You see what that means. A single Furnaceback is a humidifier and a snow machine. A million of them, flank to flank, back to back, from one cliff to the other horizon, is a private atmosphere. The herd *is* the water cycle. They exhale the planetary dew that lets everything else live.

They do not walk. They *creep*. A step every fifteen or twenty minutes, when the animal in front has made a vacancy by creeping itself. The iron plates on their backs - scabbed, flaking, black-red magnetite and hematite that precipitates out of their own breath and crusts on them like rusty coral - clank and kiss when they shift. That iron is not armor, or not only armor. It's waste. It's also shared. When two animals stand together long enough, their crusts grow together. You can see seams where they've welded. You could walk for days across their backs and think you were on a rolling, hot, gently hissing iron street.

They reproduce the way the plateau does everything else: patiently. Once every eight or nine years, an animal deep inside the herd, where it is warmest and wettest, will shudder and extrude a soft, pale calf from its ventral brood slit. The calf is the size of a refrigerator and has no chimney. It is all mouth and damp black skin. It doesn't fall; there is nowhere to fall. It is squeezed into the moss and the feet around it. The adults around it stop moving for about a month and funnel steam down onto it while it chews its first basalt. It grows its first ceramic ring within a year. In five years it has a stub. In twenty it is tall enough to contribute to the cloud.

When a Furnaceback dies, it doesn't die fast either. Something fails in the kiln, or its teeth wear to nubs. It lists, a fraction of a degree at a time. The animals around it grow into it, over it. Its chimney gutters, clogs with glass worms, and goes cold. The mosses notice first. In eight months the old back sinks an inch, then a foot, and then you can't find it at all except for a slightly warmer patch of felt that stays wet longer at noon. Its iron stays. Everything else gets eaten.

**The Ones That Live On The Living Ground**

Because of course the backs are not bare. Nothing here stays bare.

Between any two Furnacebacks is a canyon of deep shadow a few centimeters wide where the moss has been squeezed up into a wet, black tuft. In that canyon, and on the flat rust plains of the backs themselves, is the real jungle.

The base is not moss, not really. It's a felt of protonema a meter thick - **soot-moss**, black-purple with anthocyanin so dark it looks like char. Each filament ends in a glassy hair that is a perfect condenser. It does not photosynthesize so much as it heat-collects. Black to swallow that savage, thin sun and turn it into two degrees of precious warmth. At dawn it is covered in beads the size of pinheads - the dew of the whole continent exhaled back onto itself - and when the sun hits, the whole felt steams at once.

Woven through it is **tangleliverwort**, which does not creep so much as knit. It makes mats a meter thick that can span a chasm, rootless, holding itself together by interlocking barbs. You can walk on it until you learn that stepping on it means stepping on whatever lives underneath it.

And tower forms - the **stackferns**. They aren't wood. They are a helix of giant fronds, each one stiffened with silica, unfurling from a central meristem and then dying at the base and slumping to root again, so the plant moves like a slinky, three meters tall and always collapsing forward into itself. Their undersides are crowded with sporangia that never open except when a Furnaceback sneezes steam straight through them.

On this mobile black meadow lives the epifauna.

The **Iron Mites** are the most obvious. They are the size of hares, low and crablike, with eight blunt legs tipped in wire-brush setae. They do nothing but polish. They scrape algae and young moss off the iron backs with radulae like belt sanders, keeping the plates thin enough to still radiate heat. Their own backs are polished to a mirror by the dust. When two meet on the narrow pass between Furnacebacks they stop, touch antennae, and spend an hour lapping the rust off each other. Without them, the herd would seal over into a solid iron dome and suffocate.

Above them, the **Mica Moths**. They are not moths, but convergent. Wings as big as your hands, two pairs, made not of scales but of translucent sheets of biologically precipitated muscovite. They do not flap often - the air is too thin for flapping to be cheap - so mostly they walk the moss and angle their wings to the furnace light that leaks red from the chimneys. Their wings are solar panels and UV shields. At dusk, when the steam plumes catch alpenglow and the whole plateau glows pink from below, they all take off at once for five seconds, just enough to let the wind off a chimney catch them and take them to the next back. It looks like shattered windows blowing around.

Inside the chimneys themselves live the **Chime Worms**. True worms, eyeless, glassy, filled with silicon-carbide spicules. They thread themselves through the hot baffles and filter iron bacteria out of the draft. When the draft shifts they tighten and vibrate, and each chimney has a slightly different note. The herd at night, if you camp on a back and put your ear to the warm ceramic, is a cathedral organ played very, very slowly, with all the stops out and condensation dripping.

And never landing - the **Sootbirds**. They have no legs, or rather their legs have fused into a single magnetic grapple. They are made of hollow-bone light and soot-black feather analogs heavy with magnetite. They don't fly, they *hang*. The whole plateau, with all that precipitated iron moving in a slow circle, generates a local magnetic anomaly, a slight fuzz in the field lines. The Sootbirds cling to it, kettling a hundred meters up in the herd-cloud, mouths open, filtering spores and moths and ice crystals. Occasionally one dies and peels off the field and falls like a burnt paper into the moss, where mites take it apart in a day.

**In The Cracks**

Where the plateau cracked deepest - those knife-straight fissures where the sun never hits - the dew never evaporates. It stays. It strings itself in bead-curtains and hangs in bogs that have no ground, only liverwort holding liverwort over a void. In those hanging bogs live things that can't handle the iron and the heat.

Pale, translucent slug-things like living canteens, carrying water inside themselves for months. Fungal nets that glow faintly when a worm touches them, not with light but with a little burst of heat you can feel on your cheek. And in the deepest, a thing that might be the only predator in the quick sense: a low, flat ambush mouth called a **slit**, all cilia and patience, that waits ten years for a mite to fall off a back above and into its lap.

None of this is fast. Nothing darts. Nothing pounces. To pounce would freeze you. To run would be to lose water you can't replace without a furnace.

What lives here has learned one lesson so well it became a place: if you cannot go to the water, become the thing that makes it. Eat rock and breathe out rain. Let everything else pile on, and move so slowly you become geography.
