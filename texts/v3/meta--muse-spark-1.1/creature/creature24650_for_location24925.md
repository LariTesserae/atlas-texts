The first inhabitants you have to learn not to look for.

You came here expecting figures on a landscape — something that stands up out of it. But nothing here stands up except to hold the landscape down. The thing that lives here *is* the floor.

You have to kneel again.

### The Weavers

Put your hand on it. That springy, hot-paper slab under your boots. It is not soil with plants in it. It is plants that became soil.

The bulk of it, the miles-deep invisible bulk, is dead. Compressed generations of themselves, felted and lignified into a cork-wood-concrete. That is the body. The living part is only ever the top few centimeters, and the deep plumbing.

It lives as a single, continent-wide habit:

**The anchors.** They are the only thing your primate eye wants to call a tree. Black-barked, black-branched, iron-hard wood, not much taller than you, with dusty, thumb-sized leaves that hang straight down at noon to avoid the sun. There are never two close together. One every half-kilometer, one every kilometer, in perfect, unconscious territorial spacing. You think they are individuals at first, until you dig.

From each anchor a dozen lateral roots as thick as your wrist leave the trunk just below the crust and run. And run. A hundred meters, two hundred, straight as stretched rope through the mat. They don't seek water. They seek each other. Where the lateral from this anchor meets the lateral from the next, they abrade, they exude, they graft. Wood fuses to wood. Over years a living knot the size of a fist forms, then the size of a head. From orbit, if you could see through the skin, you would see it: a mesh, a net, a planet-sized stitch holding a raft together against its own buoyancy and bloat.

They don't know they're doing it. They are just shrubs that can't tolerate not touching.

Each anchor transpires so violently at 2 p.m. that the air over it goes liquid. You can see it — a column of shimmer rising off the crown, condensing at the top into a small, private cloud that follows its maker around like a thought. Sometimes that cloud gets heavy enough to rain back down onto the leaves that made it. That is as close as this place gets to intention.

**The Skin.** The anchors would bake and split if they were left exposed. They are not. Over everything - over living laterals, over dead mat, over gas blisters - is a scab.

It is three organisms pretending to be one, so tightly bound you can't peel them apart without tearing all three.

The bottom is a succulent creeper, not green but a waxy, grey-yellow. It threads its fingers into the upper fibers of the mat and welds them with clear exudate. On top of that a liverwort, flat as paint, dark brown-green, that seals the surface against evaporation. And on top of that, a crust lichen, pale as bone dust, that waxes itself. When the hot, brief rain comes and the whole basin sheets over ankle-deep, that lichen is why the water beads and hisses and runs instead of soaking. It's waterproofing. A roof tile grown by chemistry.

This skin is the lung and the immune system. At dawn, when the deep mat is still holding last night's cool, it sweats. Water condenses on the lichen by the liter, beads, runs, and is directed — by microscopic grooves in the thallus you can only see with a lens — into...

**The Cups.** The mat grows bowls.

That's the only way to say it. In places where three laterals cross, or where a gas blister has collapsed, the skin thickens and curls up into a shallow, watertight basin of living cork, 10 centimeters across, 30. Perfectly smooth inside. At sunrise, every one of these bowls across the basin is full. For an hour the entire low continent glitters at ankle height, as if it had grown eyes to look at the white sky.

And in the bowls, the tenants.

Because the mat can tolerate contractors, just not competitors.

In the bowls lives a red-tipped moss, dense as velvet, that exists nowhere else. It has no roots. It drinks only from the bowl it sits in and dies — completely, crisply — by three p.m. every day. But it does not die dead. The dead outer leaves are a thatch that keeps the few living cells at the center moist until tomorrow's dew.

Next to it, often sharing the same bowl, is the thread-fern. It's a thread. Black-green filament, thinner than hair, that uncoils overnight when the air is wet, unfurling a single frond the size of a fingernail. It photosynthesizes from 6 a.m. to 11 a.m. Then it rolls itself back up, actively, like a watch spring, and bakes into a little ball of dead-looking wire that the wind can't move because the wax of the bowl glues it down.

They are sparse. 1.1 sparse. You can walk twenty minutes between occupied bowls. Most bowls are empty, just clean water evaporating drop by drop with an audible *tik* until gone.

Everything else that lives here lives where it isn't supposed to be dry.

### The Cut-Learners

In the new gullies — those shallow, raw incisions where water has finally learned it can cut, a meter deep and showing the pale pages of the mat in cross-section — the rules change. Water lingers as damp sawdust. There shade exists, a slant of wall at noon.

There you get the green fans. A dark mat of cyanobacteria and protonema that holds moisture like a sponge, three days after the rest of the plain is dust again. It's the nursery. This is where the anchor seeds, rare black nuts like iron pellets, can ever germinate. They must wash into a gully, wedge into a crack, be kept wet by the green fan for two weeks straight. Most don't. That is why the anchors are so far apart.

Under all of it, in the dark where the mat is hot and presses on itself, lives the only other citizen: the unmakers. Bacteria, white fungi like cobweb, anaerobic pockets that take back what the weavers made. They make methane and CO2 and water. That gas puffs up the great low domes you pop with your weight in the afternoons. *Sigh.* The mat breathing out in all directions because something is eating it in the dark, slowly, with no mouth.

There is nothing you can call an animal. Walk all day and you'll be the only thing that casts a moving shadow. No chewing. No footsteps to join yours. The silence is not because nothing is happening — put your ear to the cork and you can hear the anchors creaking longer as they swell — but because all the happening is growth, fusion, evaporation, and water learning for the first time in geological history how to take a continent apart.

It didn't build itself to be lived on. It built itself *as* living. The lowland isn't a place with inhabitants. It's an inhabitant that got the size of a place.
