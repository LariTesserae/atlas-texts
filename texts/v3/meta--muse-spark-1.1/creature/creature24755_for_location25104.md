What lives here doesn't live *in* here. It lives *as* here.

At first you don't see anything living at all. You see texture. You see that the concrete has a nap to it, that the iron grate has a thickness it shouldn't, that the bulb cage has grown a second cage around itself made of felt. Then the felt breathes.

Then you understand that the room isn't covered. The room is upholstered.

They are moths only because you don't have a word for what a filter looks like when it learns to shiver.

Each one is about as long as the last joint of your thumb, the color of uncured concrete when it's still wet - that dead, luminous white-grey. No pigment to waste. Their wings aren't wings. You thought wings at first because you wanted them to be able to leave. They're not for flying. There's no air here to fly in, only air that is being forced. They are two curled spoons of fringed chitin, like the baleen of some estuarine thing, or like frost ferns pressed flat. They hold them up into the weeping.

That is their whole life. To hold up those feathers and wring the air.

Because the air weeps. The mountain inhales a sky and squeezes liquid out of it against these cold plates, and these things drink without a mouth. Water beads on the concrete form-lines, swells, trembles, gets too heavy - and before it can fall, a feather touches it. It vanishes into the fringe by capillary. The moth doesn't move to drink. The drinking moves to it. The whole ceiling is a field of upturned hands.

Underneath, the body: you have to peel one up to see it, and you don't want to, because when you peel one, you peel five. They are layered three, four, six deep, clinging not to the wall but to each other. Their feet are not feet but felted hooks that grip the down of the moth below them. Warm-blooded would be a lie here; they are the temperature of the water sheet. If you cup one in your palm it doesn't warm. It stays cold and then goes still, as if conserving a budget you can't see. No eyes, or eyes regressed to a pair of black pinpricks that sense only whether the bulb is still amber or not. No antennae that flag in the wind - they would snap off in the roar. Just short, blunted nubs crusted with ice that never melts.

They don't fly. They rustle. That's the word you settle on after "seethe" feels too active. The population is so dense it has a tide. Somewhere on the far seam a bolt sweats too fast and a drop finally forms and the moths nearest to it retract their feathers, and that tiny withdrawal makes the layer above them settle half a millimeter, and that goes around the room. A shudder runs through the living wallpaper, slow, for minutes. Then it stops. They have rearranged to drink better.

What they eat is what they make.

On the steel, on the black-wet concrete, there is a smear. If you didn't have the light right you would think it was oil shadow, or mildew. Green-black, thinner than paint, exactly one cell thick except where it isn't. It's not growing. Nothing here can afford to grow. The cold, the roar, the permanent inch of water under the grate - they have forced everything into equilibrium. The algae - or the mold, or the thing that couldn't decide between the two when it blew up the four hundred meters of ladder shaft - lives only in the meniscus where water meets air meets concrete. It photosynthesizes off a 40-watt bulb that has never flickered. It takes a century to divide in that light.

The moths lick it. That's the other half of the work. Their underside has a soft, pale proboscis, not a straw but a squeegee, and they pass it over the steel in slow, endless laps. They don't strip it clean. They leave a stubble. If they ate it all, they'd starve tomorrow. If they left it alone, they'd be overgrown and smothered. So they farm by restraint. The black-green returns as fast as it's taken, which is to say hardly at all, which is exactly fast enough. Their frass is the same color, barely darker, and that is the seeding. Life here is a closed mouth kissing its own tail.

And the third resident:

In the corner where the two walls meet and the wood-grain of the formwork makes a little trough, there is a fern.

It can't be there. Spores shouldn't make it four hundred meters up a wind-shaft. But one did. It landed in the only place where condensate slows down enough to leave a micron of silt - concrete dust, moth scale, dead algae. It germinated. It put out one frond, two inches long, glassy, white because there was never enough light to bother with chlorophyll, translucent as a fish's fin. 

It has never unfurled further. The cold caught it mid-gesture. The tip is still coiled in a fiddlehead so tight you can see the future leaves pressed together inside it, like knuckles. It is not dead. The water beads on it too, but no moth sits on it. They ring it, leaving a bare halo of wet concrete around its stalk. Either the chemistry is wrong, or - and you get this feeling standing there with your jaw rattling from the roar - they are leaving it alone on purpose.

It never grows. It never dies. It is the only thing in this room that remembers that growing was supposed to happen.

If you stay long enough - and you won't, you can't - you realize they are not many. They are one. A single damp, pale, filtering organism spread thin over every surface that isn't moving water, holding its feather-hands up to the weeping, licking the walls clean enough to keep the air wet. They don't hear the sound. Nothing with a tympanum could live in that flat, total roar that has never varied. They feel it as pressure that keeps their felted bodies pressed to the wall.

They were not designed to be here. The room was not designed to have them. But the room was designed to never change, and so when life found the one loophole - that water would always be exactly an inch deep, that light would always be amber, that cold would always keep the bulb from burning out - it took that loophole and pulled itself through and then stopped. Became as finished as the concrete.

When you finally kick the hatch back open and the wind takes your breath and shatters it, they don't scatter. There's no elsewhere for them. The tide of white pauses, feathers up, drinking the sudden draft of your leaving, and then settles again, exactly as dense as before.
