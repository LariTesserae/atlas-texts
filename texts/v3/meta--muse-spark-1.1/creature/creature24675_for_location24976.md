There is no one person here. There hasn't been for a very long time. But this room is not empty. It has residents, and they have lived here so long they have become a perfect fit for the shape of it.

You have to stay still to meet them. You came in thrashing. The water is still settling from you. If you hold yourself in the middle of the sphere and breathe out until you are neutral, until the pump's beat is not an intrusion but is inside your chest —

*CHUD.*

— then they come back out.

First the small quick ones, because quick is relative.

The glass shrimp. There are maybe thirty of them in the whole world of this room. Each one the length of the first joint of your thumb. Completely transparent except for one vivid thread. Water came in here clear once, and whatever was dissolved in it those first hundred years was poor, so they learned to eat light. Or rather, to eat the things that eat light. Their guts are full of green even when they haven't fed, a single bright pea of algae slurry carried in the center of a thing that is otherwise made of water. They don't swarm. They commute. Two go out together from the rust-velvet near the pump, paddling with absurd, formal slowness, across open water to a frond — a long red-black kelp ribbon that is rooted in a rivet-hole — to graze. Another two are coming back. They pass each other in the sun column and do not acknowledge each other. The water is so clear you can see their hearts beating, too fast to count, against the slow beat of the sphere.

*CHUD.*

Then the catfish. There are only two.

They are old. They were born the color of this tile because there was never anywhere else to get a color. Blind. Their eyes sealed over generations ago into smooth, yellowed pearls the same porcelain as a snail shell. They navigate by taste and by vibration. Whiskers as long as their bodies, four on each side, trailing behind them like prayer cords. They spend their lives on the walls, mostly upside down, mouths wide and soft against the riveted boiler plate where a felt of bacteria has grown over the last few centuries. The felt is golden brown, a living varnish. The catfish kiss it off, making a soft, clean semicircle, then move on an inch and do it again. It grows back in a week. They have been eating the same walls in the same pattern for longer than you have been alive. If you get close, you can see that under their tile-colored skin, their skeletons show — fine white lines — and each rib has a seam, as if it was made in two halves and pressed together in a factory. Which it was. Nothing here is natural except that it now is.

The snails own the floor.

There must be two hundred of them, but you will only ever see four or five at once because most of them are on the dark side of the sphere where the light doesn't quite reach. They are the masons. When this room was new, the tiles were flat. Now they are not. They have been lifted at the corners by water working its way under, by roots, by time. Underneath is more tile, and underneath that is the steel, sweating. The snails rasp the tile itself. Their radulae take the yellowed ceramic, powder by powder, and then they excrete it in a new shape: their houses. So each shell is the floor, re-made. Perfect coils of bathroom porcelain, sometimes still with the faint stipple of the original anti-slip texture on the inside of the whorl. They are heavy. They move so slowly that in air they'd be stillness, but here in the bath-warm water their foot ripples like cream. One beneath your left knee has been crossing a single tile for eight years to get to where the pump leaks a little oil-heat. It leaves behind a track of clean tile, a pale road, that the bacteria will take months to recolonize. Then a catfish will come and eat it and start the cycle over.

And near the bottom, where the sphere curves down to the grate — the drain that feeds the whole mountain-city below — there is the garden that is not a garden.

The kelp is not kelp. There were never kelp spores up this high. This is what happened when some filamentous industrial sealant was left wet for a thousand years in sun. It learned to be a plant. Long ribbons, wider than your hand, dark red with iron, not green. Where chlorophyll should be is something that uses the iron from rust. They root directly in rust. No soil. They send pale holdfasts into a blistered patch of boiler plate and drink the metal. There are only seven of them in the whole sphere because each one needs so much empty water around it, as a matter of dignity. They sway not with current — there is almost no current except the slow pull down — but with the piston. *CHUD.* — and they all flex, slightly.

And growing off the pump itself: tomatoes. Of course they aren't tomatoes. But that's what your brain calls them when you see a vine of copper wire, the pump's own wiring harness that has escaped its conduit, and along it, swellings. Six or seven black translucent sacs, each the size of a fist, taut with warm water and something pulsing inside. A fruit. Each one has a little blush of light from the skylight trapped in it. If one fell, it would burst and be gone, but none of them ever fall. They hang there in the loudest, warmest part of the room, right where the black iron engine hammers.

This is what feeds the shrimp that feeds the catfish that feeds the felt that the snails clean.

And finally, in the light itself, the ones that live only in the column.

Fry. A hundred silver slivers, each no longer than an eyelash, born transparent and staying that way. They don't eat anything you can see; they live on motes. They hang absolutely motionless in the hard bar of sun coming through the lens at the top, like dust that decided to stop falling. You would think they were dead until —

*CHUD.*

— the whole sphere twitches a millimeter in its socket of ancient city, the water compresses, and they all flinch together. Not scattered. As one. A single bright sheet turning. Then they are still again. They have been counting that beat since they hatched. It is their moon, their tide.

That is everything. No hidden octopus. No large, wise fish watching from a corner. The room was too small and too pure for monsters. What lives here is what could agree to live by these three laws: water stays at the top, everything is made of tile and iron even when it's flesh, and nothing hurries because the final shape has already been found.

If you stay treading there long enough, you feel them make a space for you, not welcoming, not hostile. A shrimp's commute is re-routed an inch around your knee. A catfish pauses its kissing of the wall, tastes your skin in the water, decides you are not bacteria, moves on. The snails do not care. The fry, after a few pumps, include your bubbles in their count and flinch with them.

You are briefly the largest animal in a tidepool that has been the top of the world for a thousand years.
