At first you think nothing lives here.

That's the first impression, because nothing *should*. It's too high, too new, too fenced off from the world. There's no birdsong. There's no burrow, no nest in the wall, no animal trail worn into the imported dirt. The wind that makes it over the concrete lip is too thin for flies, except for the ones that came up in the soil.

Then you stand still and you go damp, and you realize the place is deafening with living.

The lowest life is the film.

It lives on everything. On the inside of the glass panels, on the brass valve-wheels, on the rivet heads, on your forearms if you stay an hour. A green-black biofilm, algae and bacteria that rode up in damp air and found a bathhouse that never cools. If you drag a finger across a trough you leave a clean streak in it, and in the streak water will bead again in minutes. It is the first colonist. It's learning how to be soil on concrete. The dark dendritic stains running down the eight-meter walls aren't just water marks — that's it, trying to become a river system.

Above the film, the things that were invited.

They live here like orphans who were told too sternly to thrive and believed it. Not planted — *installed*. 

The rice on the second ring lives in water too warm for it, and resents it by growing too fast. Its stalks are dense packed, six inches apart like teeth, rattling against each other and against the steel when the pump kicks. Under the water, around its roots, a hundred tiny things you weren't supposed to import live and breed without permission: bloodworms, rat-tailed maggots, mosquito wrigglers all spiraling in warm black water. The cloud of gnats over the rice trough is a permanent weather system. It never leaves the bowl. It was born here and will die here.

The taro lives next to it, huge-hearted, the big leaves you hear first. Drip. Drip. Drip. Each leaf cups the air-condensate and tips it. That's its job, to catch sky that became water on contact with wall. It is so efficient at it that the ground under it is always pitting and soft. If you knelt — you shouldn't — you'd see its roots white and frantic against the bottom of the trough, making soil out of no soil, like fingers searching for a floor in deep water.

Third ring. The tomatoes live like they are on drugs. They do not know they are at nine thousand feet. They were bred for heat and water and light and they are getting all three from borrowed sources. Their vines have forgotten how to be annual. They have stemmed out thick as your wrist, woody at the base, climbing rebar stakes, climbing hoses, climbing each other. The fruit splits before it's red, grinning open with too much inside. Wasps should be here for that, but there are no wasps this high, so the fermenting split tomatoes just hang, buzzed only by that one lost dragonfly that lives here — the only true predator. She is big, bottle-blue, and she lives her entire hunting life in a twenty-meter circle over the paddies, sheathing her eggs in the standing water. You've never seen a dragonfly look so overworked.

And above them, around them, managing them, the Engine that lives.

It doesn't live like an animal lives. It lives like a heart lives. Low, round, half-sunk at the center where all the water wants to go. You think of it as inside the cistern house, but the cistern house *is* it. The thud-thud-thud you feel in your molars is not a pump. It's a gut. Chains moving through black water so slow you think they're still until you watch a leaf tag ride along. It scoops up the excess — duckweed mats, prunings, algae bloom, the fallen tomatoes that would otherwise turn the whole loop sour — and drags it down to be shredded and dissolved and fed back to the hoses. It has no elsewhere to send waste to. So it eats itself forever.

Someone has to tend the mouth.

She lives here.

Not all the time — no one could sleep here at first, with the air so wet your blankets never dry. But now someone does. She has pushed a cot and a kerosene stove into the dry corner of the cistern building, behind the drip line. She is not the engineer who built this. She is the second or third one. Young, but with a gardener's hands, which is to say old hands. Knuckles always faintly green.

You don't see her at first because she is the color and sound of the place: rubber boots, canvas apron slick with water, hair tied up wet. She moves only as fast as the system wants. Slow pruning with a hooked knife, never stepping into the beds, always on the catwalk. She knows the three rules in her body. She knows not to turn a valve, but to listen to it — a hiss slightly changed means a leaf clog, a whine means cavitation. She talks to the pump hearts the way you would talk to an old dog with bad hips.

She lives here by becoming part of the circle. She eats the tomatoes that split, the taro leaf steamed, a hard-boiled egg traded up from below. She drinks the condensed water — it tastes of concrete and warm iron. Her pee goes into the cistern, because everything must. Her sweat condenses on the glass and falls back into the beans. She is, technically, part of the filter.

If you ask her what lives here she won't list species. She'll say, "It isn't finished deciding."

She means that. Nothing here has reached an equilibrium. The beans still try to climb out of the bowl. The concrete still tries to be a mountain again by sweating lime. The brass valves are still too sharp and honest to have a patina. The whole garden is panting slightly, new lungs, new skin, overgrowing its cast because no one ever told it what its final size is supposed to be.

If you stay long enough to see her rest, she sits on the edge of the central building with her boots off and her feet in the warm black water that moves everything, letting the little rat-tails tickle her toes. The lammergeiers circle out beyond the wall, huge shadows that never cross the lip, because even they can tell: this isn't a meadow. This is an incubator with the lid off. This is a wound trying to become a mother.

And it will, if she keeps bleeding into it.
