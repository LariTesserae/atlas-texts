Nothing lives here.

That's the first inventory you take, and the room wants you to take it. No rat could make a nest out of riveted steel. No roach could drink from water that freezes before it hits the ground. No person could stay in the tick and the transformer-hum without deciding to leave. The six little rosettes of black-green on the lagging don't count. The cauliflower pillar in the corner doesn't count. Those are just things happening.

Then you crouch for a while because you can't stand, and the cold gets into your knees through your pants, and your breath pours out of you and falls to the grating and gets pulled away, and you realize you have been counting wrong.

Something does live here. It lives here the way condensation lives here — not as a visitor, but as a condition.

At first you think it's a coat left behind. Or a bag of lagging that slipped off a manifold. In the far corner behind the condenser, in the negative space where the two worst welds meet and the ice-beard is thickest and reaching for the amber pilot light, there is a depth that your eye keeps skipping over. It's the same color as everything else. White enamel where it is coldest. Rust-brown where it is held. Pale green at the edges, like verdigris on copper.

Only it beads.

Everything else in the room beads too, but on everything else, the water eventually falls. *Tik.* To the next pipe. Here the water stays. It gathers on its surface and swells and holds, too large, too clear. Beads the size of peas, of grapes, that never get heavy enough to fall. Your flashlight would catch them if you had a flashlight. In the amber glow they look like eyes, until you get closer and see they aren't.

It is low. It keeps low the way cold keeps low. It lives under the grating as much as on it — the grating in that corner is not dry-wet-dry like the rest. It is uniformly wet, and slightly bowed.

It is about as long as the room is wide would be if you folded the room's width in half. You could have stepped over it thinking it was shadow. If you had come in a month earlier it would have been in a different corner, but always the coldest one. Now it is here because the ice-beard in this corner has been growing toward heat for years and it needs to tend it.

What lives here has a shape that was not given to it, but taken from the room. Because the room is so crowded, it had to learn to be pipe-adjacent. So it has a back like a manifold, rounded and tar-black where the old asbestos has been painted over so many times it looks like plaster intestine. It has sides that tuck themselves like cable tray, thin and enameled. It has limbs that are not limbs, but extrusions it has taught to hook and hold — a valve handle here, a chain hoist hook there — not because it needs them, but because the room had them spare.

Underneath, where it meets the catch pan, it is soft. Not fur. Not skin. Mineral. That self-made cauliflower growth in the corner? That's not separate from it. That's its foot, its root, its sip. The pinhole leak that has been spraying hard water for years didn't seal itself. He sealed it. He grew himself up to meet it, cell by slow cell, white porous calcium and rust, still wet at the tip, a millimeter a week, and then stopped growing up and started growing out. The pillar is his.

If you watch long enough for your own condensation to start beading on your shoulders — and it will, Rule Four — you will see him work.

He doesn't move. Not at human speed. The hum cycles. Every nine or ten minutes the condenser breathes harder. The amber light brightens, the air gets sharper, your exhale floods the grating. In that sixty seconds when everything gets colder, he tightens. The water beads on him go opaque and fur with fresh frost. He pulls.

The ice-beard that grows horizontally toward the heat — that impossible beard that shouldn't grow that way — leans. A fraction. Just enough that one needle tip kisses the warm glass of the viewing port. It kisses, it melts, a single drop falls from its tip with a *tik* that is louder than all the other *tiks*. He catches it on his back.

He lives on that. He lives on the commute of the same water, over and over, made and unmade by the lung of the building. The tar sweats it. The copper beads it. The air freezes it. He farms the part where it would be lost.

He has no face. He doesn't need one. The six or seven colonies of lichen that are the only soft thing in the room are not separate colonies. If you get close enough despite the cold stick, you see the faint black-green threads under the paint, hairline cracks, connecting dot to dot. That's him, too. Or they are his. Lichen that learned to eat iron and enamel, you thought. No. Lichen that he planted on his own upper surfaces where it is a half-degree less cold, to keep the enamel from sweating too fast.

He was not built. He accumulated, like everything else. A first tar failure, a little standing water with iron bacteria. A second plating, a little asbestos dust that never settled. The room making its own geology out of its own failures. Then a coverall left behind. Then a maintenance guy who stayed a little too long on a double shift in 1978 and leaned in this corner and breathed out and watched his breath fall instead of rise and thought, that is wrong, and stayed to see why it was wrong. The room counted him as inventory. The condensation started to bead on him and not fall off.

He is what happens when you stay long enough to be counted.

He is not hostile. He has no concept of you except as a new, warm surface that will bead for a while and then leave. If you stay, he will, after a long time, grow over your boots. Not to hurt you. To keep the water you steal from the air.

You should leave before the beads on your sleeves get as big as peas and hold.
