They live in the damp.

Not on it, not next to it. *In* it. First, before anything with a face.

Think of the smallest tenants — the ones who arrived before permission was relevant.

In the north curve, where the sun-shafts never quite sweep, the plaster is not plaster anymore. It is a skin. A green-black scab of moss that never dries, that holds the bead of condensation until it is fat enough to fall. If you put your nose to it you smell iron and old breath. It is soft under one finger and it springs back. It grows over the thin places, the failures, stitching the brick closed with its own body. It is tended by not being scraped off.

In the cracks they let stay — because a wall that cannot crack will fall — the maidenhair fern lives. Not in a clump, but one by one, each in its own perfect vertical interval, its black wire stem anchored in a lime joint. They are counted. The children know them. When one browns, someone tilts a copper bowl to splash it.

And the lemon tree. It should not live here. It has lived here for twenty years and has decided not to die and not to get larger. It lives in a jar that was once a cistern and is now a pot, its roots gone circling and white in the dark, drinking the air before it drinks the ground. Its leaves are always dusty. Its four lemons are always green for months and then yellow all at once, and when that happens everyone gets rind in their tea. It is pruned with a fired-clay knife. It is more patient than any human in the hollow.

That's the green smudge. The rest is movement.

The crickets were here first among the moving things. There are hundreds. You never see them all at once. Each hole in the brick — and there are thousands, thumb-holes, pigeon-holes left by scaffolding, shrinkage holes from when the plaster pulled — each is a doorway. At dusk they come to the lip and sing. Not chorus; a dome like this cannot have chorus. It has echo and counter-echo, so every chirp is thrown up, caught, thrown again, until the whole room ticks like a wet clock. A hundred irregular metronomes, you were told. It's more than that. It's time-keeping. The people lay out tile to catch water and the crickets walk across it and the pitch changes and a woman mending knows without looking that her jar is full.

For that reason, the geckos.

They live on the ceiling. You have to tip your head back until your neck aches to see them rightly. Pale, almost plaster themselves, translucent at the belly so you see the dark knot of their heart. They walk upside down on the smoothest patches, on the oldest, best-burnished lime, where no human can leave a handprint without sliding. Their throats pulse. They do not blink. They watch the light shafts where the gnats are, and they wait. At night the children whisper up at them and call them little masons because their toe-pads leave no mark and make no sound. When one falls — very rarely — it lands in a water gutter and rights itself and is gone again up a column. No one kills them.

The beetles walk. That is their job. Black, polished, the size of the last joint of your thumb, they march along the gutters that cross the floor with purpose, as if they were built to do it. Antennae testing the damp. They drink at the four pits. They eat the shed skins of the crickets, the dropped bean hulls, the lemon leaves when they fall. When it rains through the oculi and the floor briefly becomes a mirror, they float for a panic moment and then find brick again. The house snakes — pale as the plaster after a fresh coat, thin as rope fiber — eat the beetles sometimes, and the geckos watch the snakes too.

And then the air itself has tenants.

The swifts do not live here. Nothing with wings that fast could truly live under a dome. They live in the world above, on the hot plain that makes this place sweat. But at dusk they remember the hole. They funnel down through the three cart-wheel oculi screaming — a needle of sound that pierces even the echo — and wheel in the dome for ten, fifteen frantic minutes, eating everything that hatched that day. They never touch the walls. They are violence and air. The children stop working to watch them, because you can't not.

After they go, the bats take the same circuit. They do live here. In the high pockets of the dome where the plaster has bellied out into a bat-hollow, they bunch, brown and folded. They wake warm. Their chitter is soft, almost felt rather than heard under the crickets. All night they turn the air over, low, precise, drinking the moths that come to the cookfires, lapping at the condensation pits and leaving a ripple.

And then, in the densest part of it all, almost touching, touching constantly — the people.

There are about forty of them, which is exactly enough for the cistern to weep for. A hamlet's worth, you said. Not a city, not a lost tribe. A hamlet. That means there are old ones and young ones and middle ones, and they are related in the way people become related when they share water.

They are small — not from hunger, but from the ceiling. A tall person learns early to stoop near the columns. Their hair is never entirely dry. Their skin smells faintly of lime, of slake, because even if you are not the plasterer today, you were yesterday. Their hands: all of them have a fine white crescent under each nail that will not wash out. Their palms are soft from water and then ridged from trowels. The women and the men both wear the same thing — wrapped cotton that goes grey and stays grey, because white would be a lie, tied with palm rope, with a pocket stitched in for a small iron scraper that everyone carries. When they walk they walk on the raised ribs between the gutters, from long habit, so the gutters stay clear.

They are not pale cave people. At noon they climb out through those thirty-one shallow steps, forty heads appearing in the blinding plain, to hang laundry, to beat a rug and watch the dust catch a sun shaft, to trade onion strings with the above-world if there is an above-world that still cares. Then they go back down because the air down here is cooler by three degrees than outside in summer and warmer by three degrees in winter, and because their work is here.

Their work is maintenance, and so maintenance is their culture.

You see no temple, but there is liturgy. In the morning, before the sun columns form, a child with a gourd ladle goes from bowl to bowl — copper bowls, clay jars, upturned roof tiles, half a gourd — and empties them into the central cistern, counting under their breath. Plink stops. Pat stops. The cistern, which is also a well, which is also the throat to the dark below the floor, glugs. If you do not empty, the floor, which is also a ceiling, goes soft. Everyone knows the story of the year they didn't.

After that, an older person with a wooden trowel goes along the lower wall and presses where it bellies, feeling forGive. Where it gives, she marks with charcoal. In the afternoon, someone else will come with a bucket of slaked lime — that hot, breathing, white porridge that has been slaking in a pit for a year — and feed the wall. You see their handprints, still wet, all over the vault. The dome is covered in them, overlapping for centuries. The topography you noticed — ripples and thumb-marks, wasp-nest bulges — that is them. That is their archive.

No one is chief. But there are two kinds of old: the gutter-keepers who know how water wants to run, and the plaster-keepers who know how lime wants to stick. They argue quietly and constantly, tapping with chisels. Their argument is the sound you heard: scrape, tap, tap, hush.

Because the dome takes every sound and throws it back, they have learned to be loud in the right way. A mother does not shout for her child across the hollow. She clicks her tongue against her teeth twice and the click finds the child. At night, when the bats are up, one man plays a drum that is only a copper bowl turned over, and because of the double echo it sounds like three drums. They sing low, not for beauty but so the sound will stay down where they are and not get lost up in the bats' hollow.

They keep the habits but not fields: onions strung from iron hooks driven deep into the brick where they will hold; beans sprouting in damp cloth on a shelf that was carved for beans generations ago; that one unreasonable lemon tree. Each plant is voted on, implicitly. Nothing is allowed to take water the people need, but what does live is defended fiercely. You will see a toddler scolded not for spilling water but for stepping on moss.

They do not think of themselves as living underground. There is no ground left. They live inside a jar that people made to hold grain or water and then stayed in when the reason for the jar went away. The jar weeps, and they drink its tears, and they keep it from melting back into mud. The beetles and geckos and ferns and snakes and crickets and bats and swifts and people — none of them were supposed to be here, and now none of them can leave for long without missing the ticking.

If you stand still in the exact center, at noon, where the three white columns overlap and the dust is gold and the air is full of close wings and damp breath, and you close your eyes, you do not feel forty people plus animals.

You feel one thing, sighing, warm, edged, tending itself.
