At first you think nothing lives here.

Not really lives. Persists, maybe. Visits. The way a fly visits a lightbulb because the lightbulb is the only warm thing for miles.

And then you stay still long enough for the chuff-chuff-chuff to stop being a sound and become a pressure, and you see that of course everything lives here. What else would it do? This is the only place for miles that has what everything wants — permanent water and permanent heat that never has to be negotiated for.

The humans first, because they are the least adapted and therefore the most obvious.

There are three of them. There are always three of them, though not always the same three. The roster turns over every eight or nine months when someone gets a transfer or a better posting or just gets tired of wiping the handrail before they grab it. One older woman who does the logbook, one younger man who does the painting-over-rust, one middle-aged man who does both because he has been here longest. They live in the back of the office in the space that was meant to be a break room — cot, kettle, a fridge that sweats more water than it makes cold, a calendar they keep up to date not because they need to know the date but because making a mark is a way to say *I was still here today*.

They have become condensation creatures. Their skin is never fully dry. Their shirts cling. Their hair never quite frizzes or flattens, it just stays damp. They move like people who work around machines that never stop — no sudden gestures, no reaching across a belt. Economy. They shout for the first week and then learn to speak low and close to the ear, under the sound. They have pale, soft knuckles from always being wet and forearms burned dark from the one strip of direct sun that cuts under the cloud between 11:40 and 12:10. They don't go out past the chain-link gate. Beyond it, in the real swamp, things are too changeable. Here the logbook says every day:

PRESSURE STEADY / FLOW STEADY / TEMP 41C

and that is a kind of mercy.

What actually owns the place lives on the walls.

On every interior surface of the big hall where the motors the size of chest freezers turn and turn, there are geckos. Not a few. Hundreds. Flat, translucent, the exact color of wet concrete. They arrived — nobody knows how, maybe on a pallet — and realized that a room that is 41C forever and drips clean water and is full of pillars of midges is not a room at all, it is a perfect vertical swamp that never has a dry season. They don't even bother to hide. They ride the pump cages, throats pulsing entirely out of sync with the pistons, eyes filmed. Their feet never make a sound you could separate from the plink-plink-plink of pipe to puddle. At night — which is not cooler, because the cloud holds it all in — they come down and drink off the handrails you were told to wipe.

Below them, in the weeping joints where the new concrete meets the black rock and never quite sealed, live the toads. Dark as the rock, size of plums, shiny. Colonies of them shoulder to shoulder. They have learned to sit still with their mouths open and let the condensation bead and run right in. They never croak. It's too loud to croak. When you walk past they look up with the patient expression of something that has solved its problem completely and is just waiting for you to solve yours.

Above everybody, the insects that make the air solid. It isn't a swarm. Swarms move. This is architecture. Vertical columns of mosquitoes and midges hanging over each tea-colored pan, each open sump, each place where hot oil meets hot water. They don't rise or fall. They just hang, humming at exactly the frequency of the failing seal that no one fixes anymore. And through them, endlessly, without hurry, the dragonflies. Burnt-orange, bottle-glass-blue, as long as your thumb. They are the only thing in the bowl that is allowed to be brisk. They hawk and hover and mate in midair and never leave because there is nowhere better to be if you are a dragonfly that loves heat and still water.

The cats on the tar roof are the town elders. Four or five of them, all related, all half-deaf from the chuffing that carries through the sawtooth roof. They sleep in a heap in the one place the cloud thins enough to warm the tar extra. Their fur is thin and always slightly damp, their ears flat. They hunt the rats whose highways run under the soft pallets — rats that are sleek and almost hairless from the steam, that know the pump timing so well they cross behind a belt in the half-second it goes slack. The cats don't always catch them. They don't need to. The geckos drop tails. The office has kibble. It's a poor hunting that is more ritual than work.

Even lower down, where you stop thinking of life as animals at all, the real citizenry is spreading. Black-green algae that can drink sulfur and tolerate heat coats the bark of that one stunted casuarina and the north side of the water tower and every drip line. A rust-colored bacterial film lives in the pans and makes the water that color. A black mold like velvet grows on the painted-over windows of the office from the inside out, because the inside is wetter. In the hot oil itself there are things living that you don't want to think about.

And — if you stay long enough to feel the pressure on your molars fade — the machines themselves. Not alive. But not not alive. They breathe steam, they sweat water, they keep their own temperature within a line drawn in permanent marker eight years ago. The boiler that has no purpose idles and vents and idles and vents and the caretakers pat it on the way past the way you pat a big old dog that is too hot to move.

None of them are trying to leave. That is the contract.

Keep to the high, dry-ish islands of your own kind: the geckos to the walls, the toads to the weeps, the cats to the roof, the humans to the narrow paths between the motors where the concrete apron is highest. Don't turn anything off. Wipe the handrail, because something that got there before you — small, wet, and perfectly content — was already living on it.
