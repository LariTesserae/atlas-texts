What lives here doesn't live *in* the lowlands. It lives *on* them, *between* them, *because of* them, and only for a little while. Nothing here has figured out how to stay.

First, what lives here is a skin.

Before anything has legs or leaves, there is the biofilm that lives on the basalt itself. A slick, almost invisible varnish of cyanobacteria and lithotrophic yeasts that you only see when your headlamp catches it wrong and the black rock throws back a greenish sheen. It is sour. It excretes acid not out of malice but out of hunger, and it is eating the knives. The sharpness your hands felt — that glassy lip of cooling basalt — is being rounded one molecule at a time by that living oil. It softens the edge enough for the next thing to hold.

So second: what lives here is a root that isn't a root.

There are no true trees with deep taproots because there is nowhere deep to go. So the plants cheat. The dominant things are big-leafed condensers — I'm going to call them sweating sails. Each leaf is a hydrophobic bowl on top and hydrophilic velvet underneath. During the hot day that you never saw under this canopy, the air loads up, soup-thick, 35 degrees Celsius and holding. At night the rock, which keeps a little cold in its heart from how fast it was born, bleeds heat. The leaf does the trick: cold air kisses the velvet, water beads, runs along calculated veins to the tip and falls. One leaf can make a quarter cup a night.

The plant itself is not majestic. It is desperate and fast. Pale, soft trunk like a mushroom stretched into a tree, no bark, just wet fiber that bruises black when you touch it. It shoots up ten feet, throws out three sails the size of ponchos, and then another sail will grow right through it, parasitizing it while it's still alive. They are all epiphytes on each other now. Vines like live extension cords, figs that start without soil and never find it. Air-ferns the size of bears, hanging, not attached to anything but caught in the tangle, their roots just open mouths drinking fog.

And on the underside of every one of those sails lives the light.

Bacterial colonies so thick they look like paint. Yellow-green, the color of a fading bruise, cold to the touch. When vibration hits — a beetle landing, a drop falling, your breath — they flare. That's the dusk you saw. Not moon. Not sun. A thousand leaves blinking at each other when touched. They live off sugar the leaf sweats for them, on purpose. A domestication so loose it's almost accidental.

Third: what lives in the water that never fell.

Every cupped hollow in the basalt where two cooling plates contracted away from each other is a cistern. Tea-cup, skull-cup, bathtub. Some hold an inch of water so clear and warm you want to drink it. Don't. Each one is already full.

At the bottom, a brown slurry of detritus, fallen bacteria, dissolved rock flour. In it, mosquito-like midges, but stockier, with no need to bite — their larvae filter the slurry. Clouds of them rise and fall in columns, tuned to the humidity just above their pool. Above them, the damselflies — dragon's great-granddaughters — four clear wings that buzz so fast they become a seam in the air. Their larvae live in the pool, their adults hunt in the air over it, they never need to leave a three-foot radius.

On the rim, the lizards. Not graceful geckos. These are fat, half-transparent, the color of uncooked sausage, with feet so broad and callused they can run across knife basalt without cutting. Their skin is permeable; they *drink* through their belly by sitting in the pool. You see one puff up and the bioluminescence of the water inside it shows through its throat. It flicks a tongue as wide as its own head to strip midge columns out of the air.

And then, on the lizard, inevitably, something else.

Fourth: what lives here is a throat.

The boomers. You heard them. Amphibian, but with no real need to go back to big water because the little waters are everywhere. They look like toads that have been inflated slightly too much and then glazed. Their backs are pebbled with water-storage bladders. During the day they wedge themselves into a wet crevice and seal it with mucus. At night they waddle out, climb to the lowest stable leaf, inflate a sac under their chin until it is translucent — you can see the blood vessels in it like a lantern — and let out that deep, resonant, almost painful *BONK* that you feel in your sternum.

It is not a mating call, not only. It's territorial sonar. The whole bowl is so loud with insects that without a low-frequency boom that physically shakes water off leaves, they can't find each other. When one booms, its bacterial throat flashes. Then another answers, and another, unsynchronized so the night never has a beat. Their tadpoles are not in rivers; they are in those skull-cups, three or four to a pool, cannibalizing each other until one strong-legged toadlet jumps out.

Above all that, the filing.

The insects are the true owners. The biomass that makes this impossible jungle possible with 0.8 water. Beetles the size of your fist with mandibles like bolt cutters that don't cut leaves — they cut basalt. They scrape the bacterial skin and yeast off the rock and ingest the dust, excreting a fine, gritty, almost-soil in damp piles behind them. That is how soil starts here. In beetle shit. Cicadas with wings like panes of mica who do nothing but sit on a vine and scream with a ribbed abdomen until their body heat makes them steam in the cool air. Centipedes as long as your arm, flat, banded red-black, who have learned to swim between islands of leaf.

Everything is both eaten and eating. You see a damselfly eat a midge, a lizard eat a damselfly, a centipede eat a lizard, a fungus eat the centipede from the inside before it's even dead. The shelf fungi — orange, wet, quivering like lungs — are not decomposers politely waiting. They send out white threads that touch a leaf in mid-air and *tell* it to let go. The leaf drops, is caught by more threads, gone in eight hours.

And finally, because density that high always forces it:

The ones who use tools because they cannot build.

They are not at the top. There is no top here. But they are the most like you in that they carry memory between pools.

Troops of perhaps thirty, moving constantly. Imagine if a marmoset and a raccoon and a very tired child had to share a body. About two feet long, weightless-looking, covered in sparse, damp hair that never dries, plastered down. Hands — genuinely hands, thumbs opposed — but with nails thickened into little horn shovels. Faces pushed in, noses wet, eyes absurdly large and amber, always reflecting the leaf-light. They do not hoot. They chitter and click and wheeze low, to be heard under the cicada saw.

They never stop. They can't afford to. Turbulent — that is the exact word. They bounce. A lead female will smell a rotting log, and the whole knot of them flows there, churning the litter. They travel with three objects, always: a freshly knapped basalt flake, still sharp enough to split a hair, held like a scissor between index and middle finger; a hard vine twig stripped clean; a black thorn wedged behind the ear for later.

They work fast. Flake to pry bark that has only existed for three days, twig to fish for grubs the size of your thumb that are white and blind and make a sound like a wet squeak when pulled. Thorn to pin and skewer. They dip everything in the teacup pools, washing off yeast. They fight constantly, slapping each others' hands away, stealing grubs. They watch you without fear because fear requires a predator that focuses. Here, predators get distracted mid-lunge by another meal. One of them will lock eyes with you, hold up its flake as if showing you how it's done, and then — as you saw — a jewel beetle with headlights for eyes will slam into its spine and it will roll, shrieking, both of them down into a crevice, into the green, gone, already replaced by two more coming from above.

They have no nests, no burrows, no caches. The jungle would eat them within hours. So they sleep in shifts, clinging to vertical stems, one hand always holding the flake. Their children cling to their bellies and already hold smaller flakes. In a year, if this bowl still exists and hasn't collapsed or cooled or been buried by its own rot, they will not be permitted to stay, either. The forest will get too tall, the pools too shaded and acidic, the basalt too dull. Their own success will erase the conditions that made them.

That's what lives here: an entire world running on borrowed water. A condensation engine made of leaves that makes its own rain, beetles that make its own soil, frogs that make their own light, and little frantic hands that make use of the knives the earth left behind, all of it so loud and so crowded and so fast that nothing has time to die properly. It just becomes something else before morning.
