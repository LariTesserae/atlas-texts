What lives here doesn't live *here*. Not the way you mean when you say something lives in a room.

Nothing moved in. There was no way in.

There are twelve of them, right now. If you count again in an hour there might be eleven, or thirteen. It depends on when you count, because they are not good at being separate things.

At first you took them for stones. White, flat, about the size of your open palm, sitting in the silica film on the floor of the trough. That film is their world, because it is the only place where three gradients meet and hold still long enough to be eaten.

Look closer.

They are not on the water. They *are* the water, locally — a decision the water made to be thicker. Each one is a disc of mat, but mat is the wrong word because that sounds woven. This is more like a lens. Translucent at the rim, where it is one cell thick and you can see the black glass and the fresh rust under it, then opalescing toward the middle, then building into a tiny, blind pimple in the center, no taller than a grain of rice. The white isn't pigment. It's sulfur. Elemental sulfur they are excreting as fast as they can make it, little granules packed inside their bodies like pearls, because there is so much incoming energy that they have to dump the waste somewhere.

They have no front. No back. No side that is for sensing and a side that is for eating. The whole top surface is eating light it doesn't need.

That's what kills you if you try to picture them as animals. You keep wanting a mouth. There isn't one. The bottom of the disc is a lattice of filaments, finer than the crystal needles, secreting acid so weak you wouldn't feel it on your tongue but strong enough here to itch iron out of the glass. Fe + S + the faint, constant trickle of electrons leaking from the crystal bases — that's the food. They are respiring rock. The top is sealed, slightly convex, hydrophobic, so that water beads on it and then runs off instead of drowning it. When a drop from the ceiling falls directly on one, the impact dimples the whole disc. It wobbles like a jellyfish on wet pavement and then stills. It endures.

You thought they didn't move.

They do. Just not while you're watching for movement. Over an hour, the edge advances a millimeter where the iron bloom is thickest, and recedes where it has exhausted the sulfide stain. Over a day, you would see a disc crawl, amoeboid-slow, toward the base of a particularly bright needle where the charge density is higher. If you put your hand near one, the hairs on your arm rise from the ion field — it feels that too. Not as feeling, as bias. The side of the disc that is closer to charge thickens almost imperceptibly. The other side thins. It grows toward voltage the way a root grows toward water.

They don't see the light. That part you were wrong about — you thought anything white in a bright room must be about light. The light is irrelevant to them, or worse than irrelevant; it's a byproduct, a waste product of their house. They don't have rhodopsin, they don't photosynthesize. If anything, the light hurts them, bleaches their chemistry if they get too much of a direct hit from a discharging tip. When a needle above them flashes as a water bead bridges it, the disc under it contracts, a full-body flinch that takes five seconds and relaxes over twenty. You can watch it if you wait. That wave you noticed, the one that moves clockwise every forty seconds — that is not for them, but they use it. Each time the cascade passes, all twelve pucker at once. It's the only time they are in sync. A clock they didn't build, telling them nothing, but giving them a shared now.

They are not twelve. They are one, interrupted.

Every few hours, when a disc has eaten enough, the central pimple swells until it is a bubble of gas, not air, but hydrogen sulfide and CO2 trapped under the biofilm. The bubble lifts the center, stretches the white skin until it goes transparent, and then it pops. Not dramatically. A sigh. That sigh tears the disc into two or three smaller white fragments. Most of those fragments dissolve, their sulfur washing back into the film. One or two remain thick enough to hold and seal their new edge and become a new center.

They have no predators. No mating. No sound other than the gas letting go: *pup*. Then the film stills again.

When one dies — and they do die, here in this perfect incubator — it dies because a larger drop hits it wrong and folds it over on itself, drowning its hydrophobic top, or because the silica water underneath it finally deposits enough glass to cement it to the basalt. Then it goes gray in about twenty minutes, from the outside in, as sulfur oxidizes with no one left to keep it reduced. It becomes a pale scar on the rock, with a fresh orange rust halo around it where its acid stops being managed. A few hours later a younger disc will grow over it and eat the corpse.

They have been alive for maybe nine days. They will be alive for maybe another fifty before the water runs out. They do not know the chamber will crack. They do not know the chamber exists. Their entire universe is a gradient in temperature between basalt and water-air, a gradient in iron between black glass and clear film, and a gradient in charge between crystal base and crystal tip. Within that tiny, three-axis map they are giants — sprawling, colonial, ancient dynasties of cells that have divided a thousand times since their grandmothers happened to precipitate out of the same upwelling.

You came looking for who lives here and wanted something with eyes. There is nothing with eyes. What lives here lives *flat*, because flat is the shape that can live between fire and water. It lives white, because white is the color of a body full of the waste it can't stop making. It lives slow, because slow is the only speed heat allows before it kills you.

If you kneel and put your face close to the film — close enough that your breath ripples it — the nearest disc does not react to your shadow. But the needle above you brightens, just a little, because you are cooler than the air, and condensation finds you.

For a second, the disc is in your shadow, and in shadow it finally, briefly, expands.
