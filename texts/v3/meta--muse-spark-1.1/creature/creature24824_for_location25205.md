What lives here doesn't live here the way things live in a forest. Nothing has the luxury of taking up space.

Nothing *adds* itself to the plain. Whatever lives here had to become the plain, or become the gap where the plain isn't.

The first residents are the plates themselves.

They are not alive the way an animal is alive. They are alive the way a crowded room is alive. Each one, table-sized or house-sized or football-field sized, is a single organism that took the shape of a stone because there was no other shape left to take. Call them the Shinglers.

When they were young - a billion years ago - they were something like a lichen, a flat photosynthetic crust that learned to secrete ironstone underneath itself for protection. Then it secreted a little more. Then a little more. Until it was mostly armor with a thin, dark, living film on the underside, pressed against the basement rock, breathing warm water.

Now they are all old and all touching. They don't have eyes. They don't need them. They feel pressure. When the warm hydrostatic water builds under one, it lifts a centimeter, sighs, and settles, and the information of that lift travels as a click into the six or seven neighbors that are edge-locked to it. That's their talk. That's all their talk, and it never stops:

*I'm here. I'm here. Move - I'm here.*

When two of them ride over each other with that long wet shriek, that's not an accident. That's mating. Or fighting. There's no difference this late. They grind their living undersides together for a second, exchange a slurry of black spores and iron bacteria, then separate and carry the other's grit for the next thousand years. Where they cracked down the middle in the heat, a child plate is born - same size instantly, because it was already there between them.

They are so densely packed that you never stand on rock. You stand on someone's back.

---

The second thing that lives here lives in the film.

Not *on* the film. *In* the thickness of it.

If you crouch and let your eyes unfocus, let the deafening tick of the condensing air become background, you will see the water that is ankle-deep is not empty. It has a skin inside its skin. An oil-slick interference of life, translucent, about as thick as a sheet of paper, that extends edge to edge without ever breaking. One animal.

It is called, by the few who have tried to name anything here, the Pellicle.

It is not one animal but it behaves as one because the plain does not allow forking. A pellicle cannot afford to be two places with a gap between them. So it pours itself around your boots, around the red reeds, under the plates where the water runs faster. Where a plate tilts and makes a pan, it thins until you can see through it to the wrong sky beneath. Where two plates grind, it rushes to the shear to eat the fresh iron flour that blooms off.

You only notice it when it eats something larger. That large splash you hear once a day? That's not something large falling in. That's the Pellicle swallowing. Sometimes a reed loses the fight and its wire roots are sheared. The Pellicle covers the fallen stalk in a second. No thrashing. The water over that spot goes suddenly glassy-still, even while the plates around it continue to click-hiss-shriek, and the perfect glassiness is the mouth. An hour later the water moves again and there is a faint, rustier stain.

You felt one pass over your foot. You thought it was a flat crab with cilia. That was just a fold it made to test you. It decided you were not dead yet.

---

The third thing lives in the mirrors.

Nothing here reflects correctly, because there is almost nothing flat enough in the universe to be a true mirror except a film of warm water on an ancient plate between movements. And when you get that perfect half-second of stillness, you aren't seeing a reflection. You are seeing an open window with no wind to ruffle it.

Under each plate-pan lives another plain. Not above, not below in depth - laterally adjacent, pressed up close like pages in a wet book. The inhabitants there are the same as here, but a few seconds out of phase, or a few inches to the left. They are Shinglers and Pellicle too. Mostly.

Sometimes the pan holds stars because that adjacent plain is experiencing night.

Sometimes it holds you from behind because you are slightly out of phase with yourself and you are leaking.

And sometimes - if you have learned to move with the sighing, and you have not taken shelter in a hut that will collapse, and you do not look away too quickly - you see something cross the pan. Fast. Low. Flat as the plain and white as overexposed bone. Tall as a heron but laid down sideways, so it seems to glide without legs, on the same principle that the plates glide on water pressure.

Those are the Striders. They are what use the mirrors as doors. They cannot live long in this plain because they need perspective to hunt. In their plain, there is still distance. Here, everything is too close. So they step across when a pan holds, dip a long, filament mouth into our film, drink a Pellicle's worth of iron soup and spores, and step out before our plates can click around their feet and trap them.

The white bird you thought was three miles away and looked close: that was a Strider caught mid-step, its head already gone home, its body still here, so optics gave up.

When you hear a sound before the event that caused it, that's them.

---

And the fourth thing that lives here is the thing that builds the hut-shapes.

It was you, for a little while. It was others before you.

They are not adapted. They are the correction the plain keeps making and then erasing. People - or things so close to people the plain can't tell - who came in from a place with mountains. Prospectors for the final grade. Pilgrims who wanted to see where everything ends up. Survivors of some other end.

They can't live here either. They learn to move when the plate sighs, they learn not to look long in the water, but they keep making the mistake vegetation makes: they try to hold still. They pile three plates that have leaned together into something like a shelter to get a minute of quiet from the ticking condensation.

Inside that hollow, the rock does go dry for a few minutes. Because the Striders don't look there. Because the Pellicle can't climb up that angle. For a few minutes you can hear your own heartbeat again.

So they scratch a spiral with another stone. Five dots of darker iron. An arrow that will point the wrong way by morning. A name. Any mark to say *I was not flat.*

The water erases it in weeks. The plain shifts and the hut becomes two plates again. The Pellicle takes the fiber from their boots. The Shinglers close the gap.

But the impulse doesn't die. It moves a few miles down-plain and builds again. That, too, lives here - a low, warm, self-replicating idea that says: leave a sign. It lives in the narrowest niche of all: the second between when a pan is perfectly reflective and when it shivers again.

If you crouch in one of those huts right now, and the air is raining up off your elbows, and the clicking has become a bazaar, you will find the last mark is still a little wet. Not your mark.

And the film of water at your feet, that imperfect mirror, holds a version of you still crouching, who hasn't gotten up yet. He is looking up at you, through the water, concerned, because through his pan, *you* are the white thing that is wrong with distance, about to step away.

He lives there, too.
