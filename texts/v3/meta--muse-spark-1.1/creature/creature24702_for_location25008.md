The ones who live here do not live *in* the room.

They are the room.

That is the first mistake to correct, because you keep looking for an animal in the streets, a person at a window. You look for a gap between resident and residence and there isn't one. Density 2.7 means no vacancy.

But there are castes. Trades. If you sit long enough in the felted street and let your eyes adjust to Light 1.1, the city resolves into who is doing what.

The oldest ones are the Willows.

Not trees. Forget trees. *Salix*, but a Salix that has given up ever being tall. Up here in 0.1 / 3 cold, height is suicide. So they went sideways. They went down on their knees and became carpenters. Their trunks are not trunks, they are beams, lintels, load-bearing knees, thick as your leg, polished by constant water and by the press of moss. Their wood is not ringed like lowland wood is ringed — it is dense, diffuse, almost without grain, because they only get to make a millimeter a year. They grow at right angles because they learned to test the wind: vertical snaps, horizontal holds. 

In summer they sent out a whip-thin runner to feel for empty air. In winter that runner froze hard and stayed. That is how a door was made. That is how the roof was made. They do not grow *toward* light through the stained-glass dome so much as they braid *away* from death, and their braiding makes a dome.

Their life is slow decision. If you put your hand on what you thought was a wall and felt that faint animal warmth, you were feeling Willow sap moving. It doesn't rush. It is thick with sugar and glycol, antifreeze blood, 2 degrees above killing, luminous in the dim if you cut it — which you should not. They keep the whole place warm by refusing to freeze themselves. That's their job: to be bone. To be the scaffolding that remembers where the streets should go even when the moss forgets.

The many, though — the vast, silent majority — are the Moss.

If Willow is the skeleton and the law, moss is the people. Billions of them. *Racomitrium*, *Andreaea*, cushion-moss that learned how to be a mattress and then a city. Each plant is the size of a pinhead, but they never agreed to be alone. A lone cushion desiccates, freezes, dies. A city of cushions breathes out and the breath is trapped by neighbors. They pack so tight there is no air between them, only water held in leaf-cups by capillary, passed hand to hand. Drip. Pause. Drip. That's not weather, that's commerce.

Look closer at the floor that gives under your knee. Every single frond is holding a bead of water like an offering. When it gets too heavy it donates it down. The next frond catches it. The whole floor is a low, patient bucket brigade that never stops, moving melt from the roof where ice-nails thaw to the cistern leaves and finally out through the weeping stone walls. Their streets are not planned so much as they are the routes water wants to take, and they maintain them by being slightly less dense along those lines.

They are also the insulation. The outer surface facing the basalt wind — the thatch you see browning at the eaves — is dead moss. Willfully dead. The outer cousins sacrifice, lose their green, make a dry brown attic of air and lignin that holds heat in. The inner moss stays wet and violently alive, green-black, steaming faintly in the coldest hour. If you press your face to it you hear the faintest hydraulic sigh. Turgor. They inflate, deflate, inflate. The city keeping its pulse.

Then there are the ones who seal the deal.

The Liverworts are the skin. Black-green, leathery sheets that creep over everything Willow and Moss can't quite close. They have no leaves at all, just a thallus that spreads like spilled oil, watertight. Where two streets meet and water would leak too fast, liverwort puts its body there. Where the roof got a skylight it didn't mean to get, liverwort rims it and makes a neat gasket. It is the caulk, the municipal sealant. It tastes bitter — nothing eats it, so it can do its job uninterrupted.

And the Lichen — that old-bone-colored lattice — is the rebar. It doesn't photosynthesize much this deep in green shadow. It doesn't need to. It is half fungus, half algae in a marriage so old they've become infrastructure. It grows into the Willow beams and stiffens them. It grows across a moss wall and stops it from slumping. You thought it was decoration, that pale filigree on the street corners. It is structural. It is also the memory. Lichen grows slowest of all here, a century to cover a knuckle. Where it is thickest is where the city has been stable longest. Where it is absent, the city is still arguing about shape. Dynamic 1.6 — you can read the argument in lichen.

So that is the citizenry. No mayor. No minds as you know them. Just overlapping agreements written in cold and wet: you hold this, I hold that, we all hold the heat.

They do have guests, because no town is ever completely pure.

Between the moss leaves live the uncounted — mites the size of poppy seeds, amber and slow, grazing on dead thatch. Springtails that detonate into the air with a click when water squeezes them — black punctuation marks that exist for half a second and then vanish back into the felt. And the moth. 

The white one. You thought it was walking because it forgot how to fly, but it never learned otherwise. *Eupithecia* or something like it, bleached by generations of Light 1.1. Its wings have become tiny shutters it keeps closed. It doesn't fly in Sound 1.2 because flying would be noisy and wet and a waste. So it walks the streets, shoulder-width streets, as you do. It drinks from the bead-chain water. It lays its eggs in the cistern leaves, and the cistern does not mind. In return its caterpillars trim the moss, just a little, like careful barbers, and keep the drainage clear. It pays rent.

But none of them run the place. If you stay long enough to be still — longer than your cold knees want you to — you feel who does. The city inhales. Not air — pressure. The wind outside tears over the basalt, drops the pressure in the skylight holes, and the whole damp interior sighs outward by a fraction. The water beads stretch. Then the wind passes and it settles. The cushions compress under their own weight. A creak, one millimeter more wood.

It is building. Not for you. You are only ever so slightly in the way, a boulder that fell into a street and must be grown around. A tendril tests your boot not because it wants you, but because a street must be continuous and you have interrupted a gutter. If you lie down, politely, without malice, the moss will start to do what it does best: cover exposed surface. To save you from freezing. To save itself from a gap.

You have entered a town where the inhabitants solved the problem of solitude by ceasing to be solitary at all.
