They live here anyway.

That's the first thing you have to understand. At first, from a lower catwalk, you don't see anyone. You see laundry left out to catch the 4pm cloud and you think it must have blown off a line days ago. You see amber windows lit at noon and you think they're on timers. The Sill is so loud and so empty — all that screaming wind harp through the tension cables, all that sheet-metal scream from the nesting flocks, all that grinding rock underneath — that you assume it's abandoned.

Then a door bangs. A figure in a dark waxed coat that never dries crosses a hundred-meter span of open grate, head down, carrying two buckets. Then another. And another. The city is full of people moving between its knots, brief as ants in a very large field of rivets.

They are not hill people. Not mountaineers.

They are maintenance people. Urban people. City people who happen to work at the altitude where weather is made.

You can tell them by the sound of their boots. Everyone wears rubber. Thick, soft-soled, black rubber slickers and boots that squeak on the wet grating. Their coats are too heavy for 8 degrees and too light for winter, because it is always 8 degrees, always damp. Nothing ever quite dries on them. Their cuffs are dark with water. Their hair curls wrong from the constant condensation. Up close, they smell like hot iron and green.

The skin of their hands is the giveaway. Not cracked from cold like alpine hands. Gone sort of soft at the edges, pruned permanently. Nail beds stained orange from the rust-vines. The older ones have a faint, dark stippling on their forearms — lichen spores that have taken a liking. They don't scrub it off anymore. They call it freckling.

They don't live long in house-shoes. They live in stairwells.

The fundamental job here is loosening. That's what makes them strange.

In any other city, you live to keep things tight, plumb, sealed against the world. Here, the world is still pushing up through the floor at three millimeters a month. If you built tight, you'd shear. So the Sillfolk are Tuners.

There are the Tolerance Crews, who go out on the quietest nights with wrenches the size of a man laid flat — long hollow iron bars with two men on each end — and they climb the flexible foundations of the stamping mills and the hab-blocks and loosen the bolts one turn, two turns, listening to the rock groan through their molars. They mark it with chalk. They keep logs like you would for a child: Lift 3, North Seam, +4mm this month, developing gravity drift. They speak to the basalt like it can hear them. "Alright, plenty of room, go on then."

There are the Boiler-Keepers, mostly old women, who live in the belly of the hab-stacks and keep the heat coming just high enough that the mattress-thick moss on the undersides doesn't freeze off and kill the longwings. They have a deal with the longwings. The birds — not birds — scream like tearing metal all morning, but they keep the insects down. The Boiler-Keepers leave scraps of fat on the girders for them.

There are the Fogwrights. Teenagers, usually. For one hour at 4pm, when the cloud comes through the city sideways, the entire Sill becomes a waterclock. Everything that was a handrail becomes a gutter. The Fogwrights sprint. They have the whole map in their heads — which cistern-chapel is cracked this week, which rust-garden needs flushing, which barrel to tip before it overtops and ices a catwalk. They are soaked through every day at 4:07 and stay soaked until bed. They have the best lungs. They can shout over the roar of water and be heard three pylons over.

And there are the Future-wrights. The bridge-builders.

You will meet a man on a crew pouring an exquisite stone arch, its keystone still warm, spanning solid, unbroken granite. He will not look at you as crazy. He will offer you a cup of condensation collected this morning — the polite thing to do — and explain that there will be a gorge here. Next spring, or in twenty years. He saw it during the dusk-see-through, when the lamps buzzed and the finished mountain showed for a second. Old and green and river-cut. He is building his own workplace ahead of time, so he won't be late. In the Sill, superstition is just civil engineering with a longer timescale. They leave offerings of fresh water at those thin places, not for ghosts, but for the people who will live there later. They call them the Downstream Neighbors.

Because of the see-throughs, and because of the indecisive gravity on the eastern edge — where a dropped wrench hovers for half a breath like it's reconsidering, where water beads and climbs three seconds up before falling — the children here play differently. They play "plumb." They tie a weight to a string and watch which way it leans today. They play on the big poured staircases that bolt into empty air and end in a hard wall of new rock, pretending they lead up into the future city. Their parents let them, because they are not pretending.

Their bodies have learned the trick of this place: to be dense where it exists and to respect the emptiness. They cram eight to a hab-block kitchen, shoulder to shoulder, loud, steaming, trading wet socks, mending rubber — a knotted, sweaty, quarrelsome, urban intensity — and then walk twenty minutes alone in howling wind to piss. They are good at being alone and good at being crowded, which is a rare city skill.

They are not the only residents.

They will tell you, quite fairly, that they are outnumbered.

The underside of every catwalk they walk is already occupied by that hanging meadow — door-sized fern fronds uncurling one millimeter a day, black-green moss humming with water, glasswort growing straight out of a weld seam, rust-vines with orange mouths. Nothing was planted. The city sweats and the city grows.

In that green lives the second town. Condensation beetles, fat and black as fists, shouldering perfect spheres of fog-water bigger than themselves along the grates to cap their burrows in the concrete — workers will stop and wait for them to pass, out of politeness. Six-legged hoppers, dog-big, their backs thatched with living moss, shuffling across the gantries in slow herds, scraping lichen off each other's shoulders with flat teeth. And above them all, the longwings — a thousand-thousand membranes and ragged feathers crammed into every girder-hole, shitting white on the iron, screaming at the fog when it lifts, cooking faintly when they get too close to the sodium lamps.

And higher still, between the sparse pylons where there is nothing but a thousand meters of cold air and cloud, the Bellies. That's what the Fogwrights call them. The heavy, slow, buoyant grazers that shouldn't be able to hover in this thin, damp air, that do anyway because the rule about buoyancy hasn't finished curing here. They ring. Literally. Low, Aeolian tones as air moves through their hollow spines. You hear them before you see them. Fog-shapes, translucent, trailing bacterial veils that harvest spores. The children know better than to throw stones at them. The old women watch them to know when the air pressure will drop.

If you stay long enough to stop hearing the fast loud — the stamping mills, the diesel thud, the cable harp — you hear the slow loud underneath, and then you understand who the first inhabitant is.

The Sill itself. The knuckle of basalt and unweathered granite, sweating, grinding its molars. It is not a backdrop. It is a roommate who is getting bigger every night. Patient. embryonic. They leave room for it. They loosen bolts for it. They build bridges over the gorges it hasn't made yet. Sometimes at dusk when the see-through happens, you can see it finished — weathered, softened, with a real river — and you realize that what lives here right now is not the city at all, but a scaffolding built around a promise, and the people who have agreed to be its midwives.

They are damp. They are rust-freckled. Their boots squeak. They listen to rock grow in their teeth.
