At first, you think nothing lives here. Not really.

Things *exist* here. Things pass through, things have been carried in by the river that runs through one room, things have settled. But live? The shrimp don't live here, they just hold station. The cusk don't live here, they browse. The mussels live on the room, not in it. They don't know there is a room. They think they've found a good, vertical reef that hums.

That is what you think for your first few minutes, while the cold gets into your jaw and the pump goes *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK.*

Then you notice the room has habits.

The cloud of shrimp in the middle of the room - it is not quite in the middle. There is a vertical cylinder of empty water inside the cloud, a shape where no shrimp ever drifts. They part around it and re-form behind it, the way river water parts around a piling.

The crabs, on their slow pick across the grating, all make the same detour. There is a place on the north catwalk where the railing has been polished. Not rust-pitted like everywhere else, but worn bright, a hand's width, again and again. The barnacles won't grow there.

And that pulpit you thought was tipped sideways, with the nubs of levers. It isn't tipped. It is standing, and something is standing on it.

You have to wait for the lamps to swing in the current. They do, a little, on their thick glass stalks. When the left one sways forward its cone slides over the far corner and for a second you can see all the way to the back wall, to the four circular turbine mounts that are now fish nests.

There is a fifth mount. Not on the floor. On the pulpit.

What lives here was built here.

He is the same manufacture as the room. Riveted iron plates, soft at the corners now with rust-bark. A bell shape and legs where legs should be. A heavy atmospheric suit, the old kind that is a single room worn around a man, with bolts the size of your fist around the collar and portholes in the helm for seeing. It must have been yellow once. Now it is the color of the walls. Paved in mussel. Whiskered with white anemones that open and close with his breath.

He is not a man anymore, not really. If there ever was a man in there, the cold water that fills the world absolutely has preserved him at the edge of preservation for so long that he has become something else. A man-pickle. A soft thing in hard armor that learned to drink the cold.

But he still keeps the work.

He is taller than you when he stands up to his full height on the pulpit, which is about to happen, because he has felt you. Not seen you. The helm's glass is blind, all four portholes grown over from the inside with a thin brown film. He feels change in pressure. He feels the water in the room move differently when there is a new volume of warm meat displacing it.

He stands.

He does it slowly, never fast, because fast is not allowed in a place where everything has become brittle and everything has become patient. The joints of his suit scream, not a metaphor - a high, metallic squeal that cuts through the low *thrum... thrum...* of the pump and sends the shrimp cloud shattering. His knees pop. His helm tilts. And water inside the helm - because water gets in, of course it gets in - that water sloshes.

This is what his day is:

He steps down off the pulpit, one booted foot at a time. THUD. THUD. The grating shivers. Crabs freeze.

He makes his round.

He goes first to the east intake, where the grate is mostly choked with growth. He lays his mittened hands, which end in three blunt iron fingers, on it and pulls. He has been pulling for fifty, eighty, a hundred years. The grate never clears. He pulls anyway. The current strengthens for a moment and a storm of motes and shrimp parts comes through.

Then to the west outflow, the dark square mouth where the grating is gone. He crouches there and looks in. You know there is nothing in there to see but deeper black, but he looks a long time. His eel lives in there. It comes out sometimes when he is there, as thick as your arm, quick as oil, and rubs along his leg, not out of affection but out of shared use of heat. He lifts a hand and lets it go past.

Then to the pump behind the wall. You cannot see the pump itself, only where the wall thrums the hardest. He puts his whole domed forehead against that wall and listens to the *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK... thrum...* He times it. If it goes THUNK too many times, he opens a small door in his own chest - a toolbox rib - and takes out a hammer that has become a smooth knob and hits the wall once, gently, like you would burp a baby.

That is the work. Water gets in, but work continues anyway.

He does not eat. Not the way you think. The suit feeds him, or the cold does, or the room does. The algae that grows only on the lights is also growing inside his helm, in the thin water around his face. The shrimp sometimes wander into a one-way funnel he never closed. He absently brings a hand up to a valve at his throat and drinks a little of the room.

He does not need light. He has his own. The two cage lamps that still burn amber behind cloudy glass as thick as your hand - those are his eyes. They are on a switch inside his helm. When he throws it, the room has light. When he doesn't, it doesn't. He has left them on for a long time because visitors are rare and he likes to see the shadows fish make.

If you stay still, he will forget you again. The shrimp will come back and fill in the man-shaped hole in the middle of their cloud. He will go back to his pulpit and stand there, not sitting, not resting, just standing with his mitten-hands on the nubs of levers that have been smoothed away by water and time and his own touching, watching gauges whose needles dissolved decades ago.

He lives here because no one told the place to stop having a person in it. He was built here, in this cathedral, bolted together in this air that is now water. He is older than anyone who would know how to turn him off.
