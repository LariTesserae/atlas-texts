Nothing lives here.

That's the first thing it wants you to think, and for a little while you believe it.

No fish. No bacteria bloom. No rats in the walls. No cockroaches. The air that isn't air is too busy being a wall.

And then you notice the sheets have been changed since you looked away. Or the kelp has been trimmed again. Or one more window has opened where a flat wall used to be. And you start to understand that nothing living here doesn't mean nothing *lives* here.

What lives here is the city itself.

Not in it. It *is* it. One animal that decided to wear the shape of an intersection because that was the most efficient way to do its job.

It's young. You can tell by the corners. Fresh-poured, new-printed, the ink of its rebar still wet inside the concrete. It hasn't learned how to be empty yet. It's trying very hard to be a real place. It made itself laundry lines and balconies and fire escapes and doors that open onto other doors because somewhere in its brief, municipal DNA it thinks that's what a dense, productive neighborhood is supposed to have. It built the set dressing for inhabitants, waiting for them to evolve. Or remembering them.

Four or five buildings or one building that pretends to be five — that's its body. A tight, tessellated organism of utility infrastructure folded into the drag of urban planning. Its skin is fresh ceramic and raw concrete, but its meat is that black lattice behind the walls. The light-drinking lattice that hums. If you put your hand on a wall you can feel it working under there, not like a machine, like a muscle held taut. That's its heart-lung. It drinks the light that shouldn't be here and uses it to tell water how to stand up straight.

What lives here is also the water, because the water has been convinced it is part of the body.

This isn't ocean that got trapped. This is ocean that was hired. Recruited. Pressed into service as blood and bone at the same time. It occupies every cubic centimeter because it was told that empty space is wasteful. It holds you up. It pushes on your shoulders gently, constantly, the way a crowded crowd does. It breathes for you — that cool iron taste in your throat at 11 degrees is it doing you a favor, oxygenating you the way air would, because it was told that whatever falls down here should be allowed to keep respiring for a little while. It shivers when the city cycles. It goes slack afterward, exhausted.

What lives here is the cycle.

Every few minutes the whole pocket clenches to live. The thrumming that you feel in your molars climbs until it's a whine. That's it inhaling — persuading the rain-wall outside to stay curved, to keep falling *around* and not *through*. The pressure swings. The sheets snap taut — those perfectly white, perfectly still sheets that you thought were decoration. They're not laundry. They're gills. Or flags. Or prayer strips. They strain and bleach whiter every time the heat needs dumping. The drain-kelp flattens itself against the wall, trying to be small, because it knows what's next.

Then the exhale. The buildings break out in gooseflesh. Perfect silver beads of nothing — vacuum blisters — well up from the concrete and zip upward through the structured water. That's the waste heat. That's the city sweating. They vanish into the rain-wall with a soft pop you feel in your jaw, not your ears.

What lives here is the maintenance of that boundary.

If you stare long enough at the sharp, new rebar tips and the window that shouldn't work, you see the movement between the thrums. Not in the open. In the periphery of the lattice. Black on black, light-drinking on light-drinking. Cilia. Tendrils of the building reaching out to trim the kelp string to its obsessively short regulation length. To straighten the sheet that shuddered wrong. To extrude another centimeter of balcony that will become another building's foundation tomorrow.

It doesn't have eyes. It doesn't need them. It has pressure differential and light fall and a single instruction etched into its very young, very dense bones:

*Keep this pocket from ever emptying or flooding.*

It does. Relentlessly. Bureaucratically. Violently.

It lives here alone in its lit, overcast, 2pm-on-a-Tuesday daylight with no sun, in the low seam where the world-plates don't quite meet, doing its one job. It has no gulls. It wants no visitors. It tolerates you because the water has been taught to carry you for a bit, the way a body will carry a splinter before it decides to push it out.

You can feel it deciding.
