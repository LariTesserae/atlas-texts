At first you think nothing lives here. Not really. You think it's been reclaimed — that word people use when they mean empty.

It's too dense for that. Too busy.

You have to sit for a while on one of those I-beam benches until your eyes start to see movement at the right scale. This place doesn't move fast. 1.1 out of 3 fast. If you look for something running or hunting, you'll miss it.

The first thing that lives here is not animal at all.

It's the skin on the concrete.

A biofilm, black-green where the trickle-ways never quite dry. Cyanobacteria and iron-eating bacteria that came up when the steel started to sweat. They eat the mountain itself. They pit the ceramic-concrete with microscopic mouths, turning aggregate to sand, sand to silt. In the sun — that hard, high 2.6 bright that hurts even through cloud — they photosynthesize furiously. From the valley it just looks like shadow. Up close it's a living varnish, slick under your hand, smelling like a penny held in a mouth.

On top of that film, the moss came. Not the moss that grows on rocks. A thicker, engineered thing, ten meters deep on the north faces of the upper terraces, that the old maintenance logs would have called Containment Mat 7B. It was designed to catch leaks. Now it does. It hangs in huge, saturated curtains, each frond swollen with morning condensation. If you squeeze it, clear water runs out. If you don't, it holds it all day and breathes it back out at night. And living inside that sponge — this is Fauna 2.2, abundant — are things you can barely separate from the moss itself.

Furnace moths. Big as your hand, the color of kiln-dust, whose larvae live their whole lives in the warm industrial mouths, those little vents that sigh heat from the core. They never see the sun until the day they emerge, dry their wings on hot iron, and fly ten feet to lay eggs in the next vent. Water-beetles that farm the algae in the aqueducts, herding it with their legs. Blind white fish in those hand-deep rivulets, not introduced, just arrived — maybe as eggs on a crow's foot a hundred years ago — now so inbred that their eyes have filmed over, navigating only by ripple. And geckos. God, the geckos. Thousands per terrace level, flat-bodied and pale, stippled like the concrete itself, all of them clicking. Not calling to each other, exactly. Timing the drips. You realize their sound is part of the mountain's 1.8 loudness. The ticking of water on metal, the clicking of geckos, the low molar-hum of the fans.

Then the larger things. The ones that had to learn to climb.

The mountain crows are the only thing here that moves fast, and they only do it by not moving at all. They ride the heat. All day the terraces inhale sun and exhale it, and the crows lock their wings in that updraft and circle the summit gantry-forest in a slow, endless vortex. They are blacker than the rusted crane-arms they roost on. They watch. They have learned to pull fibers off the vine-curtains to line nests, to drop hard jackfruit from terrace edges to crack them on the level below. They cache screws. There are old service bays on Terrace 82 where a generation of crows has hidden thousands of bright stainless bolts in cracks, for no reason anyone can still explain.

Hive wasps as long as your thumb build their gray paper cathedrals under every girder. If you stand under one at dawn, before the heat makes them angry, you can hear the whole nest humming and dripping, condensation running off its papery eaves. Sunbirds — not native, not anything — flicker between the banana leaves, iridescent green and angry red, drinking from the orange trumpet vines that use the chain conveyors as trellises.

And in between all of that, in the density — 2.2 dense where there is never, ever a bare surface — are people.

Not many. Not a city anymore. You could walk for an hour along an aqueduct lined with taro and not see anyone, only the evidence: a clump of rice recently weeded, a bearing newly packed with pale nut-oil that smells like cooking, a child's footprint in silt that will be gone by tomorrow's condensation.

They are not the Builders. They are the ones who stayed when the logic of building gave out.

They are warm-adapted. At 2.8 elevation the air should be killing-thin and cold, but the mountain radiates like a kiln, and after generations in that held heat their bodies are lean and long and slow to sweat. Their skin is the color of that tea-stained concrete, mottled from the bright. In full sun on the upper terraces they wear huge hats plaited from banana leaf and vine fiber — not decorative, just necessary, because that high-altitude light will blind you in a season if you don't. Their eyes are narrowed, lidded.

They don't live in houses. They live in the thickness of a terrace. A family — and it is always extended, always seven or eight people who know every cough of each other — will claim a section of factory floor about a hundred meters long. The old factory roof is their sky, the next terrace up is their weather. They roll up cloth walls where the wind comes through. They sleep on mats laid directly on the warm concrete, because at night when the cloud-snag finally clears and the air goes cold, that residual heat coming up through the slab is the only thing that lets you sleep without shivering.

Work is maintenance, but that's the wrong word because it implies fixing something back to how it was. They don't.

An old woman on Terrace 47 is the Keeper of the Screw — not a title, just what the children call her. Once a month she walks the length of the water-lift screw with a dipper of pressed oil and a bundle of dried moss, listens to it turn — a centimeter a minute, a sound you have to feel in your feet — and greases it. The screw does nothing useful anymore except lift silt and water six meters up. That's enough. If she stopped, the upper garden would dry and die in a year. So she doesn't stop. A boy of ten knows how to clear the bolt-holes that weep black trickles without snapping the fern roots that are also drinking from them, because you need both the water and the root to keep the crack from widening.

Everything cycles. That's not philosophy for them, it's engineering. Nothing leaves the mountain. So no one is buried. When someone dies, they are laid in a composting bay on the inner levels, covered with fungi and concrete dust and taro peelings, and after a season that silt is lifted by the screw to the rice fields. The children know they are eating their grandparents and think nothing of it, because their grandparents told them so while weeding.

They move slowly. They speak slowly, and softly, because sound carries strangely in the wet, fan-thrumming air. The best way to talk to someone three terraces up is not to shout but to tap on the aqueduct edge. The water carries it. A code of taps — drip answers drip — has grown up over a century, so gossip travels downslope all day at the speed of seeping water.

They have forgotten the valley. Or rather, they know it's there because you can see it from any terrace edge, tiny and blue and cold, but they have no word for "outside." There is only further down the mountain and further up. The summit gantries, where the cloud physically catches and you can hear it tear like cloth, is a holy place not because of gods but because that's where the water begins. No one lives that high; the light is too brutal and the crows are too many. The bottom terraces, where the mountain finally meets its own scree and the concrete gives way, is where the things that leave go — rust flakes, husks, lost hats — and they don't speak of it much.

If you asked them who built it, they'd say the mountain grew. If you asked them what it is, they'd look confused. It's not a building. It's not a forest. It's just the place that holds heat and catches clouds and drips, always drips, and asks only that you keep the water moving and don't pick all the fruit.

From far away, from that valley town made just to look up at it, you can't see any of them. You see only the green-gray mass humming, sweating, steaming in the afternoon sun, more plant than building, more building than plant, slowly turning, slowly growing, a single continuous living thing that has learned to cultivate people the same way it cultivates moss.
