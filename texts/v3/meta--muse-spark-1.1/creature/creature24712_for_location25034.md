What lives here does not move.

At first you think nothing does. You drop down that throat of sky and light and cold hits you like a hand, and you hang in the middle of the room and you look for a swimmer, a dart, a flicker - because clear water that still should have fish in it. It doesn't. The rule of this room is too strict for anything that needs to eat anything else. It's too cold and too clean and too complete.

Then, after the cold has settled in your face and your held breath has started to count itself, you see that the floor is not completely bare.

Hair.

A green that is almost not green. Ten or twelve tufts of it on the whole floor of the chapel, if that many. Each one no taller than the first joint of your thumb. Each one alone.

This is what lives here.

Not a meadow. Not even a garden. A hermitage. One filamented monk here, and then ten feet of scoured, light-blasted limestone with nothing on it, and then another.

They are algae, but that word is too broad and too weedy for them. They don't mat. They don't spread. They don't want to. There isn't enough in the water to want with. They root - if you can call it rooting - with a white holdfast into a pit in the rock where the sand doesn't collect, and they lift. One inch. Maybe a little more. A tiny bottle-brush of filaments that takes that folded, sourceless light and makes a living out of a few phosphate molecules per million. 

You watch one and you realize it is not waving. There is no current here to wave it. What you took for a current is its own shape. Each stalk has a kink in it, a slow elbow about halfway up, all of them bent the same few degrees toward the column where the sun comes down. They grew that way. It took a season to make that bend. They are all facing the throat without ever turning.

They do not get eaten. That is the other part of their lives. Nothing has ever chewed them. No snail has ever crossed that ten feet of desert stone to find them, no minnow has ever nipped them. So they never learned to be bitter or fast or hidden. They just stop. They take what is dissolved in this still, weighted water - 8 degree water that has been filtered through a mile of limestone - and they build an inch of green, and then they hold it. For months. For years, maybe. They don't die back in winter because there is no winter down here. The light dims a little in the evening and comes back. The cold holds.

They are the softest thing in the room and the room has made a place for them by not doing anything to them.

And there is something else that lives here that is not plant at all, but it lives.

On the east wall.

You can hear it before you understand what it is - that fat, irregular *plick... plick...* that is not an echo because this much water and pressure doesn't allow echoes. It absorbs.

The wall weeps.

Not with the water that is already here. With other water. Older, harder water that has carried limestone inside itself up through a crack for a thousand years. When it hits this room-cold it can't hold it anymore and has to let it go. 

What lives on the east wall is the letting-go.

It has built itself a body out of the letting-go. A thick trunk of pale travertine that juts out two honest feet into the room and then elbows down at a right angle like a man decided it. Below it, five, six, seven shallow bowls with lips so even you would swear they were thrown on a wheel, each one spilling into the next. All up and down the wall, pipework and manifold and fluting that has never known a hand. It looks industrial because industry copied it. This is the original factory: intake, precipitation, outfall, running without fuel, without pause.

It is alive in the way a slow fire is alive, or a rust is alive. It grows. At the speed fingernails grow, at the speed you would need a lifetime to see. A molecule added to a lip, a grain added to a trunk. The water trickles inside its own stone pipes, invisible, finding the lowest route. *Plick.* A drop forms on a stone lip, fattens, falls through cold, dense water. *Plick.* It never floods, never dries.

This is its inhabitant too - this slow mineral animal that is not animal, that has no predator and needs no prey and has been doing the same thoughtless work since before the throat to the sky was quite so wide.

Between the algae and the stone plant there is a truce. The room is mostly empty deliberately, as if to keep them apart. Ten feet of bare stone between one green hermit and the next. A shadow between one pipe and the next. The emptiness is not nothing; it is the third thing that lives here. A held, even, pressurized absence that keeps everything from touching. The sand never shifts. The water never stirs.

If you stay long enough - and you can't, because your lungs are burning and the cold is getting into your bones - you start to feel that you are briefly included in the population. Another piece of soft matter holding still in the cold. Another thing that for a few seconds is not changing and is therefore allowed to be here.
