They live under your thumbnail even when you aren't looking.

At first distance, you don't have animals, you have texture. A single organism thick, but so compressed you can't see the bottom of the tray for them. When the plate comes up and that flat white light from the old tube falls in straight through the holes, they don't freeze. There is no predator response left in them. Light is just another condition that changed. What you see is a correction.

Then they resolve.

They are not isopods anymore, but your brain keeps calling them that because it has nowhere else to file them. Woodlouse. Pill bug. Roly-poly. Something a child would have found under a rock. Except those creatures need damp. These have forgotten damp.

Thumb-nail sized, every one of them. Flat to the point of two-dimensional. The really big ones, the old queens if you want to call them that, have been pressed by a hundred years of other bodies walking over them until they are as wide as a quarter, but no thicker than a paint chip. Their legs are all but invisible underneath — you only see the effect, the constant, liquid shunting. You've turned over a tray of ball bearings that kept rolling.

Their backs are the place where the landscape finished entering them.

None of them are gray in the same way. The young ones in the top churn are still slate, soft, almost like the original. But as they eat the tray, the tray eats back. You see the ones who have been grazing the rust-mountains for a long time: their carapaces are not molted smooth, they're caked and grown over with iron oxide, upholstered in it. Pitted, red-black, bleeding color into the silt when they move. Others have fed under the green paint lip and come out glazed — a dull, institutional green that shines when the light catches it, as if they were dipped. Some are the gray of the concrete itself, scarred. Their plates don't overlap neatly anymore. They've ridged, flaked, grown false sutures, little crenellations that hold dust. A few look exactly like what you thought was background, until they walk.

Look closer and you see none of them have proper eyes left. Why would they. For 99% of their generations the plate was down, and dark meant dark. There are two black pinpricks where eyes used to be, filmed over. They navigate by antennae so long and fine they disappear unless they catch the light — they are always moving, always air-tasting, flick-flick-flicking the dry. Each one is scanning for the three things that matter: a fall of water that will be gone in a minute, a fall of salt, a fall of iron.

And they are not quiet animals.

You expected a scuttle. What you get is weather. At a thousand per square foot, shoulder to shoulder, leg over leg, their little feet — each one too small to hear alone — become a single dry roar. That rice-on-newspaper rush. Carapace clicking on carapace, a papery, restless chatter, like a floor of dead leaves being wind-blown in a circle. It never pauses. Even when the light hits them.

This is what a city looks like when it has no space left.

Trails make it legible. One minute a channel opens through the mass — half an inch wide, ruler straight, from the center to the under-rim — and a file of thirty, forty of them comes marching hauling a rust flake twice the size of any of them, edge-on, like men carrying a door. They take it down into a burrow that isn't a burrow, just a hollow they've kept clear under the lip where the concrete has sweated into a little cave. They vanish. The trail doesn't stay empty for half a second. Another file is already coming back the other way, empty-jawed, antennae high. Logistics.

When one dies it doesn't get to be dead long. It happens constantly. A tremor goes through a small patch, a gray one flips, legs cycling slow in the air. You think you'll see distress. You don't. The ring around it tightens. Not a frenzy — no fighting. They cover it like snow. You hear a slightly sharper clicking and when they pull back thirty seconds later, there is a perfect concave husk where the underside was, and that too is carried off to be eaten elsewhere. Nothing accumulates except what they cannot eat — which is only the molts. And those are everywhere: perfect isopod-shaped blisters of spent mineral, hollow, some green, some red, littering the living carpet like confetti.

They remember gifts.

That bolt you saw, nearly buried, its threads packed solid with husks — go on, actually look. The metal itself is thinned, narrowed. They have been licking it for generations. There are rasp marks on it at the edge of what your eye can resolve. The crumpled oil rag in the corner is not a rag. It is a nation. The top looks like rag. The bottom is furred white, every thread strung with eggs like salt crystals, and the minute you breathe on it the whole mass shifts, the mothers moving to cover. They have given it its own climate.

And the wire. That cut copper end that shouldn't have anything left in it, ticking faintly with the building's leftover nerve — they know. They leave a perfect halo around it. Concrete showing through, dark. A few inches of forbidden ground. Every so often a bold one taps close, antennae snapping back from the invisible field, and the crowd routes around it. A taboo. A shrine.

If you keep the plate lifted, they start to map you.

The explorers come first. Not the bulk, the little ones, fastest, palest. They climb the softened, rounded concrete lip, which to them must be a cliff range worn down by eons of wind, and they come onto the painted floor. To you their feet are almost nothing, a prickle like static. They taste. Salt, dead skin, oil from your hand where it rests on the rim. You are hot and wet and vast, a weather event. They find you inedible in your entirety, which is disappointing, and they turn, their backs catching the fluorescence for a second — green speck, red speck, gray — and rejoin the flow.

Put the plate back down and you give them night again. The light narrows to pinpricks through the bolt holes. The rustling doesn't change volume. It doesn't need you.
