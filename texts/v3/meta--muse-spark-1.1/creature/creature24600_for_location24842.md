What lives here isn't really people. Not at first. People live here, yes — but they aren't what *lives* here.

What lives here is the balance itself.

But if you have to put faces on it — if you stand still long enough on that spongy boardwalk and let the steam condense on your arms and finally see — this is what you find:

**The tenders.**

They are not tall. Nobody grows tall in air this heavy, under a ceiling thirty feet up made of leaf and belt. They grow wide and low and sure-footed, the way mangroves do. Three generations at least in the same five row-houses that are no longer houses, all of them cousins, all of them damp.

You know them by their hearing. The first thing the mill takes is the high frequencies. Children under eight can still hear dragonflies. Everyone else hears the low end only — the flywheel, thud-thud-thud in their teeth, the slap of the belts, the groan of the screw. So they shout, but they also touch a lot. A hand on a shoulder to talk. A palm flat on a back as they pass you on a walkway you both can't fit on. Their language has a lot of hand in it.

Their skin never fully dries. Look at the creases of their elbows, their ankles. There's a pale, soft fur of fungus there that they don't bother scrubbing anymore, and in the wet season little transparent seedlings sprout in the cuffs of their pants and live a day before falling out. They have learned to keep their hair cut brutally short because anything longer mats. The old women who sweep the press floor twice a day — you were told about them — their scalps are tattooed with old burn speckles from when a joint blew, and their feet are wide, splayed, the toenails thick and yellowed from always being wet. They wear rubber boots cut down to clogs because full boots fill up.

Every one of them has the forearm. A forearm like rope, right or left depending, from the machete. You keep cutting back or you lose the town — that's not a proverb. That's the lease. The municipal work is garden work. A man will come home from feeding cane into teeth all day and then spend an hour on his own second story, which isn't a story, it's a platform laid across a banyan that decided to come through his bedroom window five years ago, hacking back the water-ivy and the heart-leaf vine that has already undone his latch while he was at work. He doesn't hate it. You can't hate it the way you can't hate rain. He talks to it, sometimes. "Ah, you again." The vine is the fastest thing here, and that means it moves a hand a day.

Their children are a different animal. They live up high. Below is for work — the clean-swept factory floor, the canal, the boiler that will scald you. Above, on the resilient mat of compacted cane waste and living thatch that covers the old tin, is where the kids have their roads. They run barefoot where you would fall through. They know which pipe is hot and which one only pretends to be hot, which epiphyte will hold your weight and which is just air and ants. They learn to read steam — a thin white scream means step aside, a fat low hiss means it's just breathing. They play a game where they toss seeds into the flywheel guards and watch them get obliterated. They have no birds, so they imitate belts.

Then there are the other residents, the ones who were allowed to stay.

The cats. Every house has one because you have to. They are not pets in the way outsiders keep pets. They are thin as pulled wire, their fur always clumped and damp, the color of mildew. They never meow — no point, you can't hear it over the belts — they tap. They pat your ankle with a soft, wet paw when rats are in the thatch. They sleep on the boiler lagging where it's warmest and come out looking half-cooked, and they know the exact path through the vines where a rat would run. They are quiet because everything else is so loud.

The rats, of course. But not many, not like a grain town would have. Too wet. They live up in the thatch roofs that are no longer roofs, making tunnels, lining nests with shredded asbestos-free fiber and boiler lint. You hear their nails on dry vine at night when the press is banked and the thud slows.

And the dragonflies — the only thing bigger than a sparrow that can stand the soot and the slap. Big as your hand, bottle-green, territorial as hawks. Each one owns about fifty feet of main alley, down between the press building and the houses where the air is clearest. They hover in the steam shafts where the light turns gold at noon, eating the mosquitoes that breed by the billion in the canal and under every plank. The children count them, know them: that's Blue Tail, that's the one with the torn wing.

But under all that, living in the walls themselves, is the real citizen.

It's not fig, not banyan, not that anonymous heart-leaf. Those are just its fingers. The real one is a composite thing, a felt of root and hyphae and iron oxide and old mortar dust, that has learned to use steam. It lives on condensate. It lives on the sweat of bricks. It has paved the entire hamlet with itself — the mat that is never ground, the column that used to be a telephone pole, the living lintels over doorways. It learns the shape of whatever touches it. It will take a discarded shirt left on a line too long and quilt it into its skin in a week.

It has an arrangement with the engine. The engine is its heart, externally. The engine keeps the water moving, keeps the air at 85 and saturated, keeps the gaskets leaking just enough for it to drink, keeps the humans busy enough that they never leave and never stop cutting — which is pruning, which is exactly what it wants. A perfectly pruned plant flowers forever.

And the humans have an arrangement back. They know that if the boiler goes cold for good, one wet season and the composite will close over the press building like a mouth and take their houses flush down into the peat, without anger. No drama. Just covering. So someone always feeds it. Someone always gets up at four to rake out ash and feed cane. Someone always keeps the firebox fed. Not out of love for the company that built it a hundred years ago — they don't remember the company — but out of the same understanding you have with a large animal you've lived inside of all your life.

That's what lives here.

Not a town with people in it.

A low, warm, dripping animal made of brick and banyan and belt-leather and debt, that sweats and groans and is kept alive by small, loud, soft-skinned creatures with machetes and damp cats, who in turn are kept alive by it.
