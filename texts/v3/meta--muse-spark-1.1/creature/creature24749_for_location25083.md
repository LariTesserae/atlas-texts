They live low. Lower than low.

The first ones you see are the ones you thought were the rock moving.

They are not one thing, but they are all variations on one decision: if the world will not give you soil, or shadow, or heat, or stillness, you become a cup for water and a scraper for stone.

The most numerous are the Crownlice.

Pale is not the right word for them. Pale is what something becomes when it has lost color. They never had it. They are made of something like fingernail and something like river-ice — chitin so thin and saturated you can see their guts working through it, a dark thread of chewed biofilm moving slowly around a loop. Each is a low dome on many legs, flat on the bottom to seal against wet stone. The smallest are the size of your thumbnail and perfect and new, their shells still soft enough to dent with a fingertip. The largest are dinner plates, old, crusted. Their backs are no longer translucent because they have grown into their own mineral — iron from the water precipitated into their shells, pink and rust and black, so that the oldest look exactly like the domes they graze, until they move.

And they never stop moving.

Not fast. The motion is millimeter by millimeter, a patient bulldozing. Their heads are all mouth. Two feathered antennae for smelling the thickness of the bacterial skin, and underneath, a comb. A real comb, keratin or something like it, a row of dark tines that they draw across the stone in a slow, agonizing scrape. *Tchk.* One scrape. They ingest the fluid that comes up — bacteria, archaea, the invisible slime that condenses out of the air and out of the weeping rock itself, that amber-black varnish that tastes of iron and iodine. Then they step forward half a body length and do it again. *Tchk.*

That is the click you hear.

One animal, nothing. A pan of them, a few hundred, and you get that scattered rain-on-tin ticking. But this plain is a continent of pans, all connected by that inch-deep braiding water, and the plain is a dish, and the clouds are a lid. The genus of the whole plain is in the millions per acre. You stand on a slight rise and the sound does not come from them; it comes from the air itself, from everywhere. A grinding white roar made of tiny, precise, hungry kisses on stone.

They are not solitary. They farm without knowing it's farming. Where their scraping has been thickest, the bacterial skin blooms back fatter, faster, fed by their feces and by the fresh mineral they expose. In the deepest, most permanent runnels you can see them lined up in rows, head to tail, all facing upstream, their feather-fans — second mouths, really, fringed whips — out in the current, straining what the scrapers upstream have loosened. Down there, they are so dense they climb on each other, and the ones on top are drinking the condensation off the backs of the ones below. When you wade, they do not flee. They part around your boot and close again, and within minutes your boot is covered in them, scraping it clean.

Above them, literally and in age, are the Draggers.

You saw one first as a wet, dark bar in a shallow pan and thought it was a stone until the water around it went quiet.

They are the same lineage as the Crownlice, but have abandoned the idea of a small life. They are as long as your torso, as long as a canoe, but only a few inches high at the crest — a living sled of slate-colored plates overlapping like a pillbug the size of a seal. No eyes that you can find. Just a broad, blunted front that seems to melt into the rock. They move seldom. When they do, it's an event. The whole pan they are in stops clicking. The Crownlice go still, their antennae flat.

The Dragger contracts. Its rear plates telescope under its front plates with a wet shriek of chitin on chitin. Then it throws itself forward a foot, its belly leaving a momentary vacuum that fills with a rush of cold water. Underneath where it was, the rock is shockingly clean, a pale scar of fresh gneiss, and the biofilm there is torn up in a beautiful, combed swirl pattern.

It has eaten five years of growth in one pull.

Behind it, in the raw, bright puddle of its wake, the Crownlice go frantic. The silence breaks into a chattering roar louder than before as thousands surge into the fresh pan to graze the spores left in the furrows. The Dragger doesn't care. It seals itself down again, extrudes a lip, and begins to filter the flood it caused, its whole body pulsing slow as a heart.

They are not predators. Nothing here eats anything else larger than scum. But they are bulls in a field of chickens, and the chickens have learned the language of their passing.

And then there are the third ones. The ones that make the walls.

At first you think it's just big Crownlice being eccentric. An old, rust-stained plate-bug pushing a pebble. But watch long enough.

One animal, larger than the rest but not as large as a Dragger — maybe as wide as your chest, its back a hummock of lichen-less mineral and barnacle-like accretions — will stop grazing. It will rear up on its back four pairs of legs and with its front two, which are different, longer, thinner, almost hands, it will pick up a flake of gneiss.

It tests it. Turns it over. Its mouth-comb clicks against it, not feeding, measuring. Then it carries it, slow, purposeful, to the lip of a pan.

There, in water barely deep enough to cover its feet, is a line. Three stones. Four. A low arc you thought was natural until you see it being added to. The old one places its stone. The current of the braid, which was slipping out through a low place, now catches. The water behind the little dam rises — a thirty-second of an inch, no more. A longer residence time. A little more time for the film to thicken in the cold.

Another old one arrives from the other side with another stone. They do not acknowledge each other. They work until the arc is a closed weir, until the pan is a cup that will hold water for an hour after the rest of the braid has drained.

Then they leave. Crownlice flood in.

These are the Masons. Whether they are a caste, a sex, a later instar that all Crownlice become if they live long enough, or a different species entirely that has learned to parasitize the mindless industry of the others, you cannot tell. Their combs are worn blunt from moving stone instead of scraping it. Their backs carry the same three-high cairns you thought were markers — but now you see they are ballast. Or gardens. Each cairn stone is placed so that its underside, where water never quite dries, grows thickest, and so that its tip drips precisely into a dimple in the stone below. A little amplifier for condensation. A well that waters itself.

They remember, in the way that old hands remember, what shape holds water.

The comb-tool you found in the deep pan was theirs, but not made by them. The handle is true bone, long and hollow, from something with marrow — nothing here has marrow. The black sinew that binds the gneiss flake to it is cured, knotted with a knot. No Crownlouse can tie a knot. So someone else was here, once, in boots like yours, who watched long enough to understand that the only wealth in the bottom of the world is surface area, wetness, time. Who learned to copy the comb.

Who perhaps realized, as you are realizing, that the roar never stops because the work never finishes. That whatever green thing the low walls were meant to coax has never come, so they just keep holding water. That the builders of the walls are still here, because to live here is to build walls whether you mean to or not — any creature that stays still long enough becomes a dam, any scrape becomes a pan, any life becomes a place for other life to drink off its back.

You put the bone comb back. The water in the pan, disturbed, goes cloudy, then clears. A few thumbnail crowns climb onto it, tasting the salt from your fingers.

The clicking resumes, and the plain, patient beyond any human scale, goes on drinking itself.
