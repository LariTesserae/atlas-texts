You think at first nothing does.

That's the trick of it. The room is so loud and so empty that your brain files it as uninhabited out of self-defense. It's too loud for anything to live in, you think. And then, because it is so empty, you see the four kinds of green all at once and think, ah, *that's* what lives here. The moss. The fern. The philodendron that has no right to be that big.

It takes longer than you expect to see her because she is the same temperature as the room.

She is in the sump. Or she is the sump.

When you drop down the ladder your eyes go to the far wall, to the height, to the clouded glass block, to the catwalk that ends in mid-air. You don't look down at the water right away because the water is not interesting — it's a shallow, clear, warm sheet moving in a concrete gutter that goes around the room like a moat. You step over it. You stand on the dry-ish high spot in the middle where the slabs overlap and have been worn into a little dome.

Then you notice that one stretch of the channel, where the philodendron has rooted, is deeper than the rest. The concrete there has sagged, or been worn. And in that deeper place is something that is not concrete.

She is the color of concrete when it is wet and then gone a little pale as it dries. Not gray, exactly. More like the color under your fingernails if you press them. She is about as long as you are tall if she stretched out, which she never quite does. She lives folded.

If you stay long enough for the roar to flatten — ten minutes, twenty — you start to pick apart the low end of it, and you realize part of that low end is her breathing. Not loud. It's just that she is large and the room is a drum.

She used to be something else. A maintenance man? A girl who worked the low floor and found the door that doesn't close? The room doesn't keep that. The water has taken the sharpness off that story the same way it took the corners off the slab. What is left is a final shape.

Her head is mostly in the water, or mostly out of it. It doesn't matter. She has no hair — hair would rot here — but she has a crown of that maidenhair fern growing directly out of her scalp, out of the seam where her skull meets whatever her skull has become. The black stems are her stems. She doesn't brush them away. When a bead of condensation falls from thirty feet up and hits one of her leaves, she shivers and the whole channel ripples.

Her arms are too long and have become round. This is what happens when you spend decades with your elbows resting on the same concrete lip to listen to the building. The rebar shows bright where hands have steadied themselves; her forearms show bright in the same way. Polished. Her fingers have widened at the tips and gone soft and flat, like the pads of a newt, or like moss itself. She uses them to tend.

Because she tends. That's why she lives here and nothing else does.

You watch and you realize the impossible gloss on that one philodendron, those dinner-plate leaves curled around the pipe bracket, is because she holds them under the drip when it strays off. The pipe that is as thick as your torso sweats and drips tok-tok-tok. She reaches, slow as condensation forming, and moves a leaf half an inch. The moss on the north wall is so thick and even because she pats it. She eats what needs to be eaten. A pinch of algae off the floor slab, a fallen fern frond that has gone yellow. She sifts silt.

Mostly she keeps the level.

The rule of the room is that the sump never gets fuller and never gets emptier. That's not automatic. Not anymore. The float valve is rusted open and the pump that you can never see would run dry if something didn't sit in that deeper place and displace exactly as much as needs displacing. She sits. She stands, once in a while, unfolding to a height that makes sense of that too-tall room, and steps — one, two steps — to the spillway where the channel drops two inches and hisses white, and clears a plug of leaf-matter with her broad hand. Then she folds back down.

She is deaf, in the way you become deaf here. Not damaged — adapted. The roar is her weather. She doesn't hear you climb down. She feels the vibration in the ladder, in the slab. If you put your hand in the warm water — and you will, because eventually the heat makes you want to — she will go still and then, very slowly, turn her face a fraction toward you out of politeness. Her eyes are like the glass block window: clouded, full of ordinary daylight, full of motes.

She doesn't want company. She doesn't mind it either. She is patient like built things become patient when water works on them long enough. You are another kind of condensation. You will bead, you will fall, you will join the sump for a while and then be pushed on.

If you stay till your own forearms are beaded and dripping, you will see her drink. She doesn't scoop. She just leans forward until the warm wall-weep runs down the crown of fern and over her face, into her mouth, which is small and round and lipless now, a neat O, like a pipe opening.

She lives here because this is the only room in the whole Mill that finally became what it was trying to be: a single, closed, warm, wet, perfectly balanced mouth that waters its own garden forever.
