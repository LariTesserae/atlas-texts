It doesn't live *in* the bowl. It *is* the bowl.

Not one thing. A circuit.

Start with the ones rooted, because they are the ones that make the place be a place.

**The Combers.**

You thought they were plants. That's almost right. They're not individuals the way an oak is an individual. Each is a colony that decided to be a tree for a while.

From the pumice rises a leg, not wood — a braid of a hundred black cables, each cable a previous frond that got old and turned inward, laid itself down and hardened into waxy hair-rope. No rings. If you cut it, which you shouldn't, it doesn't show age; it shows texture, like cutting a dreadlock, dry and sweet and faintly smoking. The living part is only the top six feet.

The crown is the animal. Ten feet across, a ceaselessly moving head of maybe two hundred fronds. Each frond is a harp. Along its spine, thousands of filaments finer than human hair, each one hollow, each one tuned — short and stiff at the base for the high shriek, long and supple at the tip for the low thrums you feel in your molars. They are black, then rust, then a translucent amber at the very ends where the wax is still being extruded. They never stop moving, because the wind never stops moving. When a vortex hits right, the whole crown leans into it and *bows*, and the note it makes is so low it isn't sound so much as pressure on your chest.

They are thirsty in a way that has nothing to do with water. Every filament is electret — it holds a charge the way amber holds a charge. As the dry air rips past, the field builds. You see it late in the violet light as a faint St. Elmo's crawling, a lace of violet fire from filament to filament. That's when it's feeding.

It is an electrostatic predator for things that aren't alive. The air up here is never empty. It is full of what the wind brings off the lowlands and off the space between: salt glass, pumice flour, spores from below that died on the way up, a thin, invisible fog of uncondensed water that never had the cold or the courage to become rain. The Combers want the water without ever making it liquid. Their filaments go rigid, fully erect, and the field spikes — you can hear it go from roar to hiss — and for a few seconds they are the most attractive thing in the bowl. Dust snaps to them. Moisture rips. On the filament tips, a brutal little chemistry happens, driven by the charge: atmospheric H2O split on catalytic salts they sweat. No drop forms. Instead the filament makes wax, hot and hydrophobic, right there. A waterless photosynthesis made of lightning and hunger.

That's why it smells like hot beeswax and iron.

When a Comber has had enough, it is heavy. The wax clogs its filaments, the dust mats them. Its crown droops, dull and fat. It needs to be cleaned.

That's what the others are for.

**The Kites.**

At first you don't count them, because they are the same scuffed violet as the sky and because they never fly straight. Then one comes close and you understand they are everywhere.

Call them Shredders. They aren't bats. They aren't rays. They are what happens when evolution decides that bone should be used for whistling. Six to ten foot wingspans, but the wing is not skin — it's a membrane grown over with the same electrostatically active felt as the Combers, only theirs is on the bottom. Their bodies are tiny, tucked up tight: a keel of hollow, flute-perforated bone, no gut to speak of, a head that is mostly a mouth and a pair of electrostatic eyes — simple black bristle-pits that see charge the way you see light.

They don't flap. There's too much free energy to waste on flapping. They are thrown. They climb the western rim where the wind pours over like a silent river, let it pick them up, then they surf the shatter. Inside the bowl the wind is a broken thing, twenty small tornadoes eating each other. The Kites live in the shear. They stall, they tumble, they snap from one eddy to another with a crack you can hear, screaming the whole time — not in pain, in echolocative excitement, a thin tinny *kii-kii-kii* that bounces off the Comber crowns.

And then they strike.

A Kite will ride an updraft up and over a fat, drooping Comber and then fold and drag. It lets its belly bristles kiss the Comber's streamers. The air between them is suddenly not air, it's a dielectric about to fail.

*CRACK.*

The discharge is white-violet and audible from the other side of the caldera. For an instant the whole crown is lit from inside, its filaments standing out like nerves. The Kite convulses midair — all that stored charge dumps into its bristles — and it shrieks, louder, orgasmic, tumbling ass-over-end. It is full now. Along its belly the captured salts and organics and that catalyzed, never-liquid water-wax mix are atomized and absorbed into a kind of dry crop that ferments without water, a slow hot battery.

The Comber shudders, suddenly light, its filaments clean and clumped and then springing apart again, singing a clearer note. Some filaments break off in the discharge. Some tiny things that lived inside are knocked loose.

Because there are smaller things.

**The Hummers.**

You have to sit in the warm stillness at the foot of a Comber and let your eyes unfocus to see them. In the crown, when it's fat and quiet between gusts, there's motion that isn't wind. Things beetle-thick, thumb-long, wings beating so fast they blur violet too. They are the Comber's lice and its lovers. They chew the old wax, they lay eggs in the cable-trunk, they scrape salt. Their wings are resonant, tuned to the same frequency as the frond they live on, so the frond doesn't reject them. When the Kite discharges, they explode outward in a puff, and half of them get eaten mid-air. The Kite's mouth blooms open like a funnel, not biting, inhaling charged dust.

Everything eats the discharge. Even the ground.

**The Ground.**

That soft mattress under your boots isn't pumice anymore. Step lightly and it sighs. It's shed. It's a meter deep in places: mats of spent filament felt, amber wax combs gone brittle, the paper-light skeletons of Kites who misjudged a shear and cartwheeled into the ground and couldn't get enough wind under them again. All of it milled by dry wind into a powder that still holds a ghost charge. When you scuff it, it lights faintly. It smells of cinnamon when disturbed because the old wax is full of cinnamaldehyde, a preservative. Nothing rots here the wet way. Things just get drier and more aromatic.

If you dig down six inches, it's hot. Not warm — hot. The bacterial life is there, but it's a dry life, a slow rust. Colonies of black salt-eaters linking filament to filament with coppery threads, running the bowl's leftover gradients down to nothing.

And then —

**The People.**

They weren't many. Maybe a family at a time. Maybe one.

They figured out the rule faster than you did: life needs flux, not water. So they built for flux.

Their walls on the north side are not houses. They're wind-shadows. Low rings of stacked pumice, shoulder-high, open to that bruised sky, placed exactly where the big eddy curls back on itself. Inside, the air is still enough to think. They put their hearths there — not fire pits. Shallow basalt bowls full of black glass slag, where they focused charge. You can see it if you sit at dusk: the Kite-cable strung between the posts begins to hum, a low angry bee-note, and the tip of it bleeds violet into the slag, and the slag gets soft without ever getting hot in a way you understand. They were making glass and tools out of nothing but air and lightning.

Their tools are still there. Long needles of Comber spine, shaved down with other spines, fire-hardened in the electric hearth to a cherry-black that never dulls. Wax-picks. Combs. The loom — two basalt teeth set in the ground with a crossbar, where they wound streamer silk. They were making cloth that should not exist. If you touch the cable wound there now, your hair lifts all over your body and your teeth buzz. It is still holding a storm from forty years ago.

They kept tallies carved because they had to know when to be inside or outside. Notches for the days the western wind ran true and the Kites swarmed so thick the sky was torn paper — too dangerous to be under the crowns. Notches for the rare still nights when the Combers sang in one long chord and the water-fat air made your skin prickle and every piece of metal you owned tried to leave.

One of them liked the edge. The ladder over the eastern rim is made of Comber cable braided with human hair to keep it from charging, pegged with basalt pitons. The bottom eight feet have been sawed off by the wind itself; the fibers are frayed into a cloud. Whoever climbed it last either went down to the blue below and never came back, or climbed up to watch the Kites arrive, or just wanted to hang in that place where you jump and you stay up a half-second too long because the updraft and the charge and the light-bent air all lie to gravity at once.

They are gone now. But the bowl remembers the shape of people. In the quiet at foot level, under the roaring organ forest, in the warm stillness that smells of hot wax and ozone, the places where they sat are still warm in a different way than the rock. Put your hand on the low wall. The powder that is old filament and old bone and old cloth sparks faintly under your palm.

Above you a Kite misjudges and shrieks, dumps light into a crown, and the crown roars back, louder, and throws its seed — not seed, a puff of charged glass fluff that hangs, and hangs, and then, all at once, darts sideways as if alive, toward the next vortex, toward the next mouth, toward the next gradient.

The whole small country is screaming its one idea.

*Give me a difference and I will live on it.*
