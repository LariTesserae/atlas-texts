At first you think nothing lives there.

That is the trick of it. That's how well it's been kept.

You step down the three stones and you see the frogs and you think, well, frogs live here. And sparrows. And beetles. Tenants. But this room wasn't built for tenants. A roof of woven sapling and stone isn't raised to give sparrows a place to tuck bast. A floor isn't fitted slate over slate so beetles can skate.

The stools tell you someone else sits here. 

There are three of them, each a single forked log cut low, the crotch worn through bark and sapwood until it's polished like a river stone. Not worn by a person shifting in impatience. Worn by long, wet weight settled and settled and settled again, in the same place, for years. The shine is at knee height for a tall man if the tall man were sitting with his legs folded under him. The stools are set not facing each other, but facing inward, toward the sag.

The pans tell you someone else drinks here.

They were left stacked once, but now they are spread, as if placed apart on purpose in the shallow film. Each holds clear water, and a caul of pollen, and a clutch. Not tadpoles. Frogspawn, but different. Larger. The beads are the size of peas, clear as the condensation that makes them, with a black pinprick inside each. The water in the pans never spills and never fills higher, no matter how much the walls weep. It keeps its level.

She lives in the sag.

In the center where the slabs have bowed under the weight of centuries, the film is deepest — not half an inch but two, enough to cover your ankle if you waded through it. That is where the stone has been cupped out, not by mauls, by a drip that comes from below air. And in that cup sits the water so still it looks like a separate piece of glass laid into the floor.

She sits in it, and she is the same color as the wet slate when you first look. You will stare straight at her for a minute before your eye finds an edge.

She is not big. A loaf of dark bread, a big knitting bag, a large old bullfrog. If she stretched her hind legs she would be as long as one of the stools is tall. She is broader than she should be, flat to the ground, and her skin is not frog skin. It's stone-skin mimicked so perfectly you expect lichen on her, and there is — a sparse green-black crust growing on her back like it grows on the slabs, because she does not move often enough to discourage it. Her throat is the color of rawhide. Her eyes are set so wide they almost belong to the floor, black, with a horizontal film that blinks from below, slow.

She has been there long enough that the room has shaped itself around her breath.

When she inhales — every two or three minutes — the chorus stops. The *kunk kunk kunk* of the little slate-glass frogs cuts out all at once, as if a conductor has dropped a hand. In that silence you can hear the basket of the walls creak in. You can hear the hundreds of drips lose courage. And the condensation that has been beading on the alder lattice lets go of the wood and hangs.

That's what you saw from the corner of your eye. Little perfect spheres, twenty, fifty at a time, trembling a hand's breadth below the weave, not falling. Floating. She is holding them. She tethers them with the surface of herself, which is not skin but tension. The water in the room is hers. It is continuous with her. The film on the floor, the weep in the stone, the beads on the wall, the suspended globes in the air — they are one extended body, and the frog in the middle is the knot that pulls it in.

Then she lets go.

She exhales — you will finally see your own breath fog when she does, because the air goes colder — and the spheres rain down straight, plinking onto her back, onto the lichen, onto the cupped stone. The smaller frogs, her children, her brothers, her choir, all inflate at once and sing louder for a moment, as the water is renewed. The sparrows rustle. Midges rise in a cloud that was entirely still a second ago.

She doesn't look at you. She never needs to. You are warm, so you are already a disturbance in the water. Condensation is beading on your forearms, on your eyelashes. If you sit absolutely still on the third stool — the one that isn't occupied by her two sisters, who are nearly invisible under the pollen-skins of the pans at the far edge — the frogs will forget you. The beetles will start to use your boots as islands.

And then you will feel her sit heavier, though she has not moved. The rope coil by the wall, swollen and unused, will twitch as water runs sideways along it. The wooden mallet will darken another shade as it takes on water. The room itself will settle a fraction deeper, as it has learned to do every day for three hundred years, to keep its lip exactly level with the marsh outside.

She is not a goddess. Gods want offerings. She wants nothing plugged and nothing cleaned. She wants the grout to stay cracked so the rush can have its ugly clump. She wants the ceramic pans left exactly where they were left, so she can lay in them. She wants the three stools left where they are, because sometimes, once or twice a year when the fen is lowest and the air outside is almost dry, the old humans who know the knot-names of the rawhide still come down the three stone steps with soft feet and sit.

They do not speak. They sit with her in the cool and listen to the magnified ticking of a hundred intervals, until their own breath marks the air, until their own forearms bead, until they too are hanging for a second before falling.

That is who lives here. The one who keeps a little piece of wet, low, loud, living emptiness in place, by being it.
