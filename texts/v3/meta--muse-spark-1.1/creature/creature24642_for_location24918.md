They are not gone. That is the first thing you get wrong about them.

You come up onto the plateau expecting ruin - city the size of a country, no smoke, no roads, no voice. Fauna 0.5. The field report in your head says *extinct* or *abandoned*. But a place that farms moss does not go fallow all at once. A wall re-stacked last season is not an artifact. A footprint in that black-green felt that is still holding water, and the water in it is still clear and not colonized by silt yet, is not ancient.

They are sparse the way high places are sparse. The way a mind is sparse when it is thinking about something very large.

You don't see one for a long time because you are looking for a figure against the carpet. They are not figures against the carpet. They are figures *with* it.

The first time you do, it will be because you stopped. You stopped walking toward things and sat in the lee of one of those dew-cisterns — those big stone bowls with the bone-white pole lattice that hums — and you waited out the afternoon wind. The light puddled in that little valley, heavy and blue. Your hands looked wrong in it. And after an hour of nothing happening, you realized that one of the hummocks in the moss-field two hundred yards away was breathing.

Not like lungs. Like the carpet does. Slowly swelling as it takes on the melt, sighing out.

Then it unfolded.

What lives here used to be human. Still is, if you want to be generous with morphology. If you want to be stingy, you would say they are what humans become when height remains after everything else is taken away.

They are tall. That's the gravity being polite to them and them being polite back. Eight feet in the old measure, but they never stand at full height because nothing here stands at full height except the wind. They stoop, not from age but from habit — from a billion mornings of scraping lichen with a stone comb without tearing the crust. Their legs are disproportionately long, stilt-like, with broad, soft feet that spread their weight. The soles are black and thick as bark. They leave that neat, round, water-holding print. They wear almost nothing the plateau didn't grow: a long felted mantle made from the same black woolly seed-heads that never open, beaten and matted until it holds air like a second skin; leggings of woven sedge-flag that are stiff with frost in the morning and soft by noon.

Under that, their own skin. This is where you stop calling them human easily.

In air this thin and this cold, you need help. They carry it. Their skin from the collarbone to the wrists to the knees is not pink but mottled — olive and sulfur-yellow and that bruised purple — because it *is* lichen. Not clothing. Symbiosis. Generations ago, before the walls got this long, one of them or all of them made a trade: let the edible crust root in us, a little. The hyphae go shallow into the epidermis, not painful, and the algae make sugar from that violet-edged sunsight while the human part provides water — that nightly frost, that morning dew, licked and sweated and held. They are greenish-gray at the temples. Their hair is sparse and white and kept short, because hair holds frost and frost is water and you don't waste water holding it where you can't drink it.

So they are slightly photosynthetic, the way you are slightly bacterial. It isn't enough to live on. It is enough to not die quickly while you are far from your pantry-terrace.

Their chests are deep. You can see it even through the felt. Barrel ribs, a slow bellows heart that beats thirty times a minute when they rest and never seems to go much faster. Lungs like damp caverns, adapted to air two and a half miles up that never really gets warm. When one finally walks toward you — and if you wait, after some days, one will — you will hear it first. Not footsteps. Breath. A faint, wet crackle on the exhale, like ice forming in small tubes. They have to humidify everything.

Their faces: this is where the time shows. No wrinkles as you know them. Frost life doesn't wrinkle, it abrades, like their description of the stones. Their faces are smoothed, features worn low and broad. Eyes set deep under a heavy brow ridge — not Neanderthal, just practical, a sunshade. The eyes themselves are all iris, dark amber with no real white showing, the cornea slightly nictitating in wind. They have learned not to need to cry. Tears freeze. So they have a third lid, clear, that flicks. Ears small and close to the head, almost no lobe, so they don't frostbite.

And their mouths are small. People who have no animals to call and no wood to want do not have big mouths.

They do not speak often. Speech in -20 air is expensive. It rips moisture out of you and throws it away. When they do, it is not projected. It is a low, close thing, more air than voice, consonants softened. Most of their conversation is not vocal.

Listen near the cisterns. Those lattices of bone-white poles — not bone, it's something like dried sedge pith stretched and cured, light but stiff — they are instruments. Dew condenses on them all night, runs down, drips into the bowl. How much it drips, how fast, tells you how cold the night was, how the wind is turning. You learn to read it. They play it. They tap the poles with a cold-hammered copper rod, two feet long, flattened at one end. *Tok. Tik-tok.* The hollow bowl underneath amplifies. The sound carries for miles in that thin, still air because there is no other sound to fight it. A child — rare — at one cistern can tell a mother — rarer — three valleys over that the north terrace dam has breached and the moss-flow is coming.

They have no words for "how far" the way you do, because distance here mirages. They have words for "how many walls away" or "how many sleeps of wind."

They live alone together. That is the city plan. Each round, semi-sunken house — stone slabs overlapping like scales, entrance facing east to catch morning thaw — sits in the middle of its tended field. Two hundred, three hundred yards of dense, tended crust that is pantry, crop, insulation, child. From any door you can see forty other doors if the air is clear, which it always is, but you do not visit every day. Energy is strict accounting here. Walking burns sugar the lichen made all day. So you maintain your own walls. You scrape your own terrace with a comb made of basalt flakes set in resin. You transplant mats of edible *Cladonia-like* stuff, moving at the speed the moss moves, an inch an hour in growing season. And two or three times a year, when the great highway-riverbeds flood calf-deep for those two weeks that come from nowhere, you all without planning walk to the flood. You don't need to message. The water does it. Everyone upstream knows when it comes.

There you stand shoulder to shoulder — and that is a strange sight after weeks of solitude — sixty, eighty of them, in the black trickle that is suddenly a river, pulling seed-stems that have finally broken and netting algal curds, filling gut-skin bags. They do not celebrate. Celebration is a high-oxygen behavior. They work. They trade combs. They look at each other's lichen for disease. Sometimes they mate, or rather, they decide to try to make a third person, which is a decade-long project here.

Because a child here is not born so much as cultivated. Pregnancy is very slow in the cold, in low food. The women — though sex is a soft distinction when everyone is lichen-wrapped and tall — carry for sixteen, eighteen months, inside, and then outside in a sling of warm felt where the infant never touches bare air for its first year. The infant does not cry. Crying is a lowland strategy. It sighs. Its skin is pink for months before the first graft of green is brushed on, from the mother's own forearm, a kind of inoculation.

They are long-lived because nothing that can live this slowly can afford to die quickly. A hundred and fifty, two hundred years. Old enough to remember where a riverbed used to run before the moss dammed it three lifetimes ago. Old enough that the walls seem new to them. Old enough that when they look at you — stationed, short, loud-breathing, pink, wasted with heat, carrying a pack full of things that burn — they are not surprised. The plateau has had visitors before. Light puddles and time smears here; they may even remember you from yesterday, which hasn't happened yet on the horizon.

And what do they want?

The same thing the moss wants. To hold water until morning.

If you step only on stone, not on the dense parts of the crust, they will come closer. If you sleep by their cistern and let the drips fall into your open mouth, they will see you know how to drink from the air. If you sit still long enough that your shadow gets hard and short and then long again, one will eventually unfold from the green and walk toward you — not directly, but at an angle, the way you approach something shy — with their copper rod, and tap twice on a rock near your foot.

Not a warning. An invitation to help re-stack a wall that the green tide is pushing over. A recognition that in a city the size of a country with no downtown, maintenance is the only citizenship there is.

If you help, your hands will get cold, even through gloves. The basalt will feel strangely light. The moss under your knees will sigh and swell. And when you look up, the wall you have been mending will run straight to the edge of sight, and forty miles away you will see another figure, very small, mending its own wall, and you will understand that you have moved into the neighborhood.

This is who lives in the place that forgot how to be a mountain. Not survivors. Gardeners of a continent that became a garden because there was nothing left to be except tended.
