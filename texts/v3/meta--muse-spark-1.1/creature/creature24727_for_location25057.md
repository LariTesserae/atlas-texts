They live here by not needing to be alive the way you think.

The first inhabitants you learn to see are the towers, because you keep thinking they were built.

They weren't. They grew because the alternative was freezing.

Each one started as a smear. A bacterial felt in a warm crack in the basalt, where the old heat leaks. Black, sour-smelling, eating iron and sulfur so slow it would take a day to watch it double. In any other place the wind would have scoured it off and that would be the end. Here, the air is so wet and so cold that everything it exhaled froze above it. Its own breath fell back down and killed it, then insulated it.

So it learned to hold its breath.

Over centuries the mat thickened into a sponge, then a bottle. It wove dead sisters and wind-blown grit and its own glassy waste-fiber into walls. It made a chimney the way a termite makes a chimney — not because it knows what a chimney is, but because a tower is the only shape that keeps drowning and burning in equilibrium.

Now it stands. There are a hundred and nineteen of them at last count, if you count the juveniles tucked in the shadow of the old ones, which you shouldn't, until they start to breathe.

They are not trees. Trees want light. These want gradient.

Their trunks are not wood. If you cut one — you won't — you'd see rings, but not of growth. Rings of aborts. Layers of black ice, then fiber, then a char of rust-colored organics, then ice again from a winter when its heart came too close to failing. The trunk is a pressure vessel, swollen at the middle like a throat holding an egg.

The top is a crown of leaves that aren't leaves. They are condensers. Each slab is as big as a door, as thick as your hand is wide, veined deeply on the underside to canal water. The color is not green. Green is a luxury for places with sun. This is a black that has given up on being seen, with a purple under-bruise only visible when one of the grazers lights it from within. They slick over instantly with water in this air. They don't photosynthesize. They *harvest*. They angle themselves to cold. They shiver. Even now you can hear one far off to your left making a fine adjustment — a long creak as a leaf-plate torques on its living hinge to catch a drift of fog a little cleaner.

Inside, it is always raining downward.

The water runs down the grooves to the core, to a chamber that is not a stomach but is, a soup of mats that boil at fifty degrees because the rock itself is fifty degrees there. The water falls on hot stone and flashes. Pressure spikes. The whole organism groans. That is the thump you hear.

`huhhh...`

It holds as long as it can. The bark jacket tightens. Seams of white mineral pulse.

Then it lets go. Low, around its base, a ring of lips — sphincters crusted with sulfur crystals — gape and it sighs out the steam it just made. Hot, metallic, smelling of blood and kelp and new nails. Ten seconds of fog that rolls out knee-high and heavy. In this cold it does not rise. It spreads. It becomes tomorrow's drinking water for the next tower three hundred meters downwind.

This is how they talk. Not in sound. In scheduled humidity.
One breathes out, and half a minute later another, far across the bowl, ticks a little faster, leaf-plates fluttering, as that water hits them.

They are not individuals, except completely. Each one would die alone. The bowl only works because their fogs just touch. That spacing you felt as vertigo — three hundred meters of nowhere between trunks — that *is* their social distance. Close enough to kiss. Far enough not to starve each other.

If you put your hand on one — you will, because you have to — it's hot at ankle height, burning cold at chest height, and the vibration comes up through your bones. Not a heartbeat. A cycle. Fill. Heat. Hold. *Chuff.*

They are old. The big ones have been standing here since this basalt was still called new.

And then you notice the towers are not alone because the air is full of ash that is not ash.

The Herd.

At first they look like moths because your brain has no file for an animal that flies without flapping. Thousands of white specks in the low dusk, drifting, never colliding, each on its own slow thermal column. This is the kind of abundance that can only exist where predators cannot afford to chase.

Get close to one. Make it notice you by breathing too loud.

It is no insect. It is as big as a hare, but all of it is membrane — crumpled drafting paper, if paper were grown. A translucent sack of hot water and organs you can almost see through, held up by six legs so long and thin they look like drawn lines, jointed wrong, moving in pairs so it never quite falls. It doesn't fly. It vapes itself aloft. It drinks the scalding broth from the towers and holds it in its belly until its internal pressure is lower than the air around it by being hotter. Stilt-steered hot-air balloons.

They are the Sippers. The Lanterns.

They dock on a tower with a needle the length of your forearm folded under the chin. They punch through the fiber jacket — the tower lets them, with a little hiss of annoyance — and take on a crop of mineral water so hot it would cook your hand. You can see it enter them. Their whole body fills and goes taut, parchment stretching.

And then — the waste glow.

The reaction that lets them store that water without dying releases light. Or rather, it fails to keep from releasing light. An inefficient byproduct of iron being shuffled in their blood. For two minutes after drinking, the Sipper glows from inside like a paper lantern with a forge coal in it. A dull, deep amber-orange, the color of iron at a thousand degrees seen through skin. They drift away lit, legs trailing, cooling. From far away this is all you see: the valley floor blinking on and off, one warm light at a time, then another, then another. A factory floor with no foreman, just warning lamps signaling *full, empty, full*.

They live about a week like this. They drink, glow, drift, cool, wring themselves out by condensing on their own backs — you can see beads form on the paper and fall — and then they sink down into the fur and walk for a while before heating up again. When they die, they don't fall. They *settle*. That soft crumple you thought was litter in the moss is last month's herd, now being slowly felted into the ground.

On the ground itself — which you thought was ground — is another inhabitant. Not an animal. A superorganism in the way a prairie is.

The fur. Knee-high, black-olive, each filament tipped with a glass bead of water it will not give up. It's lichen and moss and fungus that decided to become one thing. If you kneel and part it, it doesn't uncover soil. There is no soil. Just more fur, and then basalt. It is the valley's liver. Everything that drips, everything that sighs, everything that dies and crumples ends up here, held. It crunches under your boot like living snow and then, ten minutes later, it is upright again. It breeds in it — tiny white mites that never leave it, that are born with a droplet of world on their backs.

And above, where you hear scraping —

The Hill Walkers.

You will never see one clearly because they do not want to be seen as something separate from the rim. An animal that grew a shell not of shell but of the place itself. Over decades it secretes basalt dust, sulfur, its own mineral shit into a dome around its soft body until it is indistinguishable from a frost-shattered boulder except that it moves, and when it moves, the moss on its back stays alive. Long, slow, one step an hour, sparks snapping faintly where its underside grinds. It grazes not on plants but on towers — not eating them. Leaning against them. Scraping the dead outer layers off with a radula like a blacksmith's file, digesting the fiber. When it does, the tower shivers and cycles faster, relieved, like a dog leaned against.

There are maybe six of them in the bowl. Maybe eight. They live so long you can see lichen-rings on their shells like continents. They do not glow. They conserve.

Every one of these things eats steam, or heat, or the waste of something that ate steam or heat. Nothing eats light because there is no light to eat. The whole food web is a diagram of a boiler, of waste-heat recovery drawn in flesh.

If you sit long enough, until your shoulders are soaked with water that never fell as rain, you feel yourself get looped in. Your jacket drips. Your breath freezes and then melts into the fur. A Sipper comes close enough that the amber of its gut briefly illuminates the droplets on your eyelashes. A tower uphill sighs, and a second later the leaf-plates above you add one more tick of water to their endless tally.

No workers. No owners. Just a bowl that taught itself how to stay wet in a place that should be dead, and made a hundred lonely engines to do it, and a herd of paper lights to carry the hot water around, and some walking hills to keep the engines clean.

Too high to be this green, you thought. It's not green.

It's busy.
