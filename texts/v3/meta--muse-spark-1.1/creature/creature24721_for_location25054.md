At first you think nothing lives here.

That is the trick the bowl plays, because it is so full.

You stand on the rim with your hands on the still-warm railing and you listen to that deep room-sized throb, seven seconds around, seven seconds around, and you look down at the violence of that green and at all that purpose-built steel, and your brain tries to sort it the way human brains do — okay, machine, okay, plants, okay, building — . But there is nothing moving at the speed animals move. No birds sitting. No footprints in the black grit except yours filling slowly with seep water.

Then you stay.

You have to stay longer than is comfortable, because the rule of this place is slow, and it doesn't show itself to fast things. The swift that knifed over the rim was too fast to see it. You have to let your clock slip.

First — the beetles. You thought there was one, and then you get down on your knees in that foot-deep moss and see there are hundreds. Black, oil-polished, the size of the last joint of your thumb. They are not grazing. They are *commuting*. Each one picks a line — up the side of a collector gutter, down the soaked face of the central drum, across the back of a sprinkler arm — and walks it with a terrible patience. And they are *not* clean. Their backs are furred. Green. They carry moss spores and the rust-colored filaments of a lichen-like thing that can't live on the vibrating steel alone. They are sowers. The moss uses them the way a town uses a bus.

You watch one disappear into the seam where a cable enters concrete and after a minute, another one comes out. That's when you start to understand the moss isn't a color here. It isn't decoration on the buildings.

Second — the moss isn't moss. Not just moss. 

Some of it is. There is true moss, that neon epileptic green, and there are door-sized ferns that have figured out how to live on so little soil they are mostly air. But underneath that, plastered to every single wet surface, is the thing you thought was vine. It isn't vine. It has no leaves. It's a root system that decided to become a skin.

It is every shade of green at once, from nearly black to nearly yellow, a cable of green wires that has found every crack in the riveted plate and gone inside. Look at where the catwalk meets the rock: the green wire has gotten under the paint. Look at the turbine housing: the green wire has learned to follow the welds, because the welds weep just a fraction slower, and slower is better for what it is doing. Pull gently — you won't be able to help it — and it doesn't come off the rock or the steel. It has pitted them. It has etched them with tiny acid mouths. It is eating them and drinking them, and it is also holding them together. 

It is not growing *on* the machine. For a long time now, for how many flywheels-since-the-humans-left, it has been growing *through* the machine.

And the machine has let it because the machine needs it.

That's the third thing, and it takes longest to see. Go to the central drum. It's too loud to stand near. The whole bowl hums through you. Put your hand — not on the drum itself, it will scald — on the concrete skirt around it. It's warm. Work-warm, chest-warm. The air around it shivers, not with heat but with spore and yeast.

The racks. The arms that sweep once an hour. You thought they were harvesting. They are, but backwards from what you thought. The blades on the sprinkler arms don't cut to collect. They cut to *prune*. The hoppers underneath don't store. They *feed*. 

Open one — careful, the vibration makes the latch jump — and you understand the smell. Sweet, sharp, yeasty. Not rot. Bread. Beer. Life making more life. Inside, thirty, forty kilos of shredded green and green-black mash turning itself over slowly, bubbling. It sighs. Carbon dioxide rolls out and drops, because this air is too thin naturally, even in here. That CO2 is what makes the soft-daylight, what makes your shadow blur, what makes the ferns so indecently large. The farm is not farming moss to send it somewhere else. There is nowhere else. It is composting itself to make air thick enough for itself to breathe.

It is a closed loop that leaked open.

Because in the compost, in the hoppers, living in that wet green blood and hot metal smell, is the real resident. Or it is the resident's blood, or its stomach, or its brain — distinctions stop meaning anything when you get slow enough.

It's yeast, but calling it yeast is like calling the ocean water. It's a sheet, a mass, a culture that has outgrown the steel drums and learned to live downwind of itself. A white-yellow felt, an inch thick, growing up the inside of the hoppers and along the chutes and into the insulated cables themselves, where the copper gives it trace minerals. Where it meets the green wire, they fuse. You can see it if you hold your breath — the green wire darkens at the tip, swells slightly, and trades. Sugar one way, minerals the other. 

The flywheel turning once every seven seconds is its heart. The trickling water from the cloud-trap is its lungs. The green wire mat, the moss, the ferns plastered over everything, are its skin and hair. The black beetles are — not even pets — they are its nerves, carrying messages of place.

It doesn't have a face. It doesn't have a front. It is *a* mountain, not a range. One body, built out of basalt that hasn't cooled, out of water that never stops crying out of the rock, out of machines that humans left running because they thought they were only farming fiber.

If you crouch now, like you did before, and put your hand down, you feel all four of its conditions at once instead of three: the young rock underneath, hot with becoming; the cold, new water running over it; the buzz of the flywheel holding them together; and under your palm, under the half-inch of moss that has already grown over your bootlace while you were looking at the beetles, the slightest, most patient, most irrefutable swelling upward —

slow

slow —

as it grows over you, too.
