They live in the sound.

Not *with* it — *in* it. The way fish live in pressure. Nothing here ever evolved a way to keep the thunder out, so it evolved a way to let it straight through.

**The Pillars themselves, first.** Because you thought they were the landscape.

They aren't. They are the oldest animals.

Not plants. Plants have to make peace with stillness. A Pillar never stood still once in its life. From the moment its wax-bolt seed — that riveted, corkscrewing thing that looks like ordnance — manages to snag on the dead root-mat of its mother and bitumen itself down with turpentine glue, it starts to turn. A hair-thin filament catches wind and spins, and the spin ratchets the first spar up a millimeter. That spin never stops.

A young Pillar is low and crazy, a furious basket of spars no wider than a house but knotted so tight you can't see through it, screaming. It grows by being beaten. The wind hits it, the interior looms — those kilometer-long dry fronds like the world's largest baleen — clack and thrum and in clacking they extrude more of themselves. Growth is not seasonal. It is gustational. In a storm a Pillar can put on a meter in an hour, with a sound like a forest being snapped in half, fiber adding to fiber in a wincing creak.

The oldest ones know how to lean. That slight Coriolis twist you saw? That's learned, over ten thousand years. They lean *into* the prevailing westerly that circles the entire plateau without ever stopping. Their spars are not random. Watch for an hour and you see the pattern: every spar is bracing another spar against the next gust. They are tensegrity cathedrals holding themselves up by wanting to fall in a very specific way.

They have no center. If you cut one open — which you can't, because the tension would kill you — you'd find only wind. Hollow all the way up, and inside that hollow, nothing but those hanging reeds, beating. The living part is all skin. The brain, if you want that word, is distributed in the weave: stress fibers that tighten when the wind shifts, loosening others. A Pillar thinks at the speed of wood creak. It takes a decade to change its mind about a draft. It has been filtering the same sky since before the continents below learned to have rain.

It doesn't want water. It wants your breath. Along the crown, in the hard violet light where the air is thinnest, it has unfurled its nets — those aerogel felts it weaves from its own wax glands — into cavernous, hanging lungs. Not lungs that breathe. Lungs that *wait*. They are hydrophobic to a degree that would make Teflon look needy. They repel everything except the one impurity: the faint, hot, wet puff of something that ate its felt and lived.

Which brings you to the **grazers.**

The sky between Pillars looks empty until you stop looking for birds and start looking for kites.

They are everywhere. Hundreds, thousands, in every layer of thunder.

The commonest are the Scraps — you first took them for shredded sail. That's almost right. They are kite-things the size of doors, of cars, of barges. Their skeleton is not bone but pressurized tube, like bamboo made of dried cartilage, hollow and whistling. Over it is stretched not skin but Pillar-felt, the same stuff the Pillars throw away, now tanned by sun and wind into a leather that crackles with static. They have no muscles for flying. They have bellows.

Watch one up close, if it snags on a spar: along its back, a row of slits open and clap, open and clap, *whap whap whap whap* — not breathing, *pumping*. The plateau wind rams down its throat and inflates sacs that deflate in sequence, levering the wing spars. They only fly because the wind never stops. If the wind stopped they'd fall like wet paper. It never stops.

They graze by wound. They do not have mouths. They have a tongue that is a harpoon made of glassy fiber on a fifty-meter tether. It fires — *thwip* — through a cloud of drifting aerogel and snags it. The felt dissolves on contact with the oil they secrete. They reel it in and the whole body shudders as it absorbs the wax. What they exhale, as a hot, almost steamy jet from a vent on their belly, is water. A teaspoon at a time. Just enough. A Pillar's net above twitches toward the heat and drinks it out of the air.

Everything screams here because nothing can hear. The Scraps scream constantly, a sound like tearing sheet metal, not to talk but to feel where the other Scraps are in the storm. Their bodies are all ear — the felt vibrates.

Above them hunt the **Shrouds.**

You see one's shadow before you see it. The light dims for a full two seconds. When you look up, the sky has a bruise in it.

A Shroud is a Scrap that never landed. It kept eating felt until it was a hundred meters across, translucent, amber veined with dark turpentine channels. Its bellows are the size of houses and when they pump they make a sound beneath the Pillar-thunder, a *whoomph whoomph* like a god trying to blow out a candle. It does not hunt with eyes. It *combs*. It trails a curtain of living threads kilometers long, almost invisible, sticky with static. Scraps hit it and stick, buzzing. The Shroud reels them in slowly, not to eat their flesh but to milk their water. It squeezes them dry with a contraction, then lets the flattened husk go. Downwind, the husk flutters, reinflates, reassembles itself from spare felt in the air and keeps flying. Nothing dies quickly here. It has to be completely desiccated and shredded to count as dead, and the wind won't let the pieces settle.

When a Shroud hits a Pillar — and they all do, weekly, blindly — the whole world rings. The Pillar shudders half a millimeter and throws off a cloud of fresh sails. The Shroud bursts into ribbons that look fatal and then, three valleys of air downwind, those ribbons find each other and knot back together.

Below, on what you thought was ground, the **grinders** live.

The root-mat is not silent. It clatters.

The smallest and most numerous are the **Bowls.** From a distance they look like rolling tumbleweeds, but they are not light. Each is a perfect hemisphere of interlocked dry plates, the size of a truck tire or a house, open-side down. They roll not with the wind but *against* it, on articulated cilia-fringes that grip the cork. Inside the bowl is a black mill. They chew. They eat the dead Pillar — the petrified cork-hills you are standing on — grinding it with a radula of silica teeth. It sounds like someone shaking a toolbox in a cement mixer. Behind them they extrude it: a brick, perfect, dense, warm with friction, stamped with the grain of their gut. Fiberboard. Pillar-stuff, reprocessed. The wind eventually buries the brick under salt dust, and becomes the ground again. They are the reason you can walk here. They are paving the world with shit.

Grooming the living skirts of the Pillars are the **Lattice Crabs.** They are not crabs, but you will call them that because when one comes over the horizon of a root-hill it looks like a house moving. Eight legs, each a tower of that same tensegrity fiber, jointed backwards, tipped with shears. Its body is an open lattice ball — you can see the sky through it — to let the thunder pass through without flipping it. It climbs a Pillar for months, snip snip snip, biting off the spars that have grown wrong, that have been wind-fatigued, that squeak at the wrong frequency. Where it bites, the Pillar bleeds clear turpentine that instantly gums up, and new growth *shoots* out with a scream you can hear, green-black and furious. The Crab eats the old spars. It does not need water either. It gets its water by farming Scraps: it has shallow pools of wax on its back where Scraps land to drink oil and inadvertently puff water.

And there are smaller things, between: **Spool-Moss** that is not moss but a million coiled filaments that can taste your shadow's charge and snap shut; **Needle Rain** that isn't rain but seeds falling together in glittering curtains, clicking against your helmet; **Chime Mites** that live inside the hollow reeds and ride them like pendulums, their legs tuned to damp or amplify a specific groan — the Pillars' parasites and tuners.

None of them build. The question you brought — why no wall, no road — answers itself when you stand here with your teeth rattling. To build here is to stop the wind. To stop the wind is suicide. Everything that lives here lives because it *assists* the motion. The Pillar weaves sails to make more drag to spin more. The Scrap eats sail to make water to keep Pillar weaving. The Crab prunes Pillar so it doesn't stall. The Bowl rolls and makes ground flat enough for wind to keep moving. Even the Shroud's curtains make turbulence that helps younger Pillars start.

Communication is not voice. It's tremor. Scraps drum on the air with their bellow-slaps. Bowls send codes by clattering their plates against the root-mat — *clak-clak-clack* — and the whole plain carries it for kilometers through the petrified cork. The Pillars talk slowest: a deep *THRUM* shifting to *THRUM-THRUM* as a spar settles somewhere five kilometers up, a message that will take a week to reach the next Pillar through the ground.

If something intelligent ever evolved here, it wouldn't make a city. It would make itself into a better filter. It would stretch itself thinner. It would learn to be more deaf, more still inside while the outside roars, until it was indistinguishable from a gust.

You feel yourself drying out. Your lips are bleeding. Your ears are ringing even though you've plugged them. And somewhere above you, a Pillar that has stood since the air was made has just exhaled a fresh, invisible sail the size of your hometown, and the wind has taken it, and a hundred shrieking kites have turned to chase it.
