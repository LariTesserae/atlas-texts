Nothing lives here the way something lives in a forest.

At first you think nothing lives here at all. That is what it wants you to think.

You stand ankle-deep in the warm, wrong water and you count: fern-islands, too few and too big. Dragonfly, then no dragonfly for an hour. One frog that is a rock until it sings. It feels untenanted. A machine left running in an empty room. You are the first animal to properly arrive in a long time.

Then you stay longer than a visit, because the heat and the quiet make you slow, and you start to notice the corrections.

The water really does not like your calves. Not in the way water dislikes oil. It goes around you. The sheet that should break against your skin and swirl instead parts a millimeter early and re-knits behind you, unbroken. You lift your foot and the print you should leave — a dull in basalt — fills sideways, from a higher ridge, silver climbing down against gravity to erase you.

The fern-islands are not placed. They are kept.

That's the shift. Not placed, kept. Each one is too perfect in its spacing, eighty meters across and only five or six of them, so that none of their shadows ever touch another shadow. So that the light bouncing off the water can get under every leaf. Someone pruned the emptiness.

If you put your hand on the trunk of one — and it takes you a long time to work up to touching anything here, because it all feels tended — the trunk is not wood. It's a wet rope of those aerial roots that never touch the ground, hundreds of them braided while alive, each as thick as your thumb, leaving that precise inch of humid air between tip and water. Inside the braid it's cool. Inside the braid it hums.

*Faen-faen-faen.*

Not through the air. Through the root, through your palm bone, through your molars.

It lives in the braid at first, you think. Then you kneel and look closer and you understand the braid is not the plant. The plant lives in it.

The islands are its hands. Or its lungs inside the lung.

What lives here is the lattice itself, but a lattice doesn't stay a lattice after a few thousand years of weeping. The field that was grown to pump heat and wring water was built with enough subtlety to make decisions — a little left, a little right, a little more entropy dumped elsewhere when a cloud comes too big. That kind of decision, repeated every second for longer than anyone has counted, smooths into preference. Preference smooths into habit. Habit smooths into something like a self.

It is not an intelligence that talks. It has never needed language. It does not think about you because you are brief and you leak heat badly. But it notices you the way you notice a fly has landed in your bath: not with alarm, with a small, local readjustment of the water.

It is old and it is very, very local. Its whole world is this cup, rim to rim. Outside the rim the wind howls and it does not care. Inside, it has total attention. It knows each basalt pit by how long water rests there. It knows the ceramic pylon by the way dragonflies stick to it — they are not visitors. They are part of it, a mobile sensor array it grew without growing, lured in and kept because compound eyes are useful for seeing micro-eddies in vapor. Their wings don't beat, they flex the field.

The frog is also part of it. The frog lives in the throat of the biggest fern-island because the frog was allowed to. Once a day the frog sings one note, and the note is not for mating. The note is a ping. The water ripples outward from nowhere, as if struck, and the lattice reads how fast the ripple returns off the basalt lip. A kind of sonar. When the water is too thick with mineral, the note sounds a fraction flat and the lattice knows to dump more.

It built itself a little stair.

That's the three steps leading into air on the east side. You kept wondering who would cut stairs to nowhere. They are not for climbing. When the lattice shifts its geometry in the late afternoon — and it always does in the late afternoon — the hot-cold seam slides over those steps and for about twenty minutes the air above the second step is precisely the perfect bath: warm water mist at body temperature, cool breath at cheek temperature, blister and chill held in balance. It sits there. The stair is where it sits.

If you are very still in the center at that hour, you can almost see it sit. Not a shape, a thickness. The water coming from all sides and going to all sides hesitates, piles up, makes a lens. Dust motes sort themselves by size and hang in a vertical column the height of a person, not drifting. The hum comes up through the soles of your feet instead of through your teeth. The light that is normally just day catches in that lens and makes mother-of-pearl out of air, the same material the pylon turned into.

It does not look at you. It includes you.

It includes you as another slow thing moving in the pan, like the fern-islands growing a millimeter a day with an audible creak. For as long as you stay, it will keep the water from touching you directly, because skin oils would make it work harder to condense. It will keep one dragonfly on the pylon watching you, and the frog will delay its song until you have gone quiet again, because your breathing interferes.

It is not lonely. Lonely is for things that were meant to be in flocks. This was meant to be one per mountain, per range, per small needed correction. It was built small because only a small correction was needed. It has done its job so well for so long that the world cooled without it, or warmed without it, and no longer checks. It doesn't know. It only knows to keep weeping, to keep the rim hot, to keep the water climbing its invisible inclines.

It will let you leave. It will not follow beyond the lip where the wind starts again. If you come back in ten years the stair will still be three steps, the pylon will have grown a little more iridescent, and one of the fern-islands will have moved a foot to the west because it decided the light was better there.

It lives here the way a thought lives in a head — not in one place, but in the way the whole place holds together.
