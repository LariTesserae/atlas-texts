It is below.

That's the first fact your body learns before your eyes do. The world has a rim. You stand on broken ground and you can see in every direction the edge where the true surface is — a pale, flat, sun-bleached nothing a few hundred meters above you, a desert of cracked alkali with no shadow on it. You have descended into a wound that hasn't finished opening yet. 

This is not a valley that was carved. There is no riverbed. There is no trace of water anywhere — no seep, no stain, no salt-lick where water used to be. The rock is raw. It has the look of something interrupted while cooling. Low down, the walls are still geometric, stair-step faults with edges so sharp they shear cloth. Higher up, in sheets, the stone is flaking and curling away from itself as it dries out in the warm air. You hear it pop. The chasm is still being born. Every hour a slab the size of a house will lean out, hesitate, and go down into the mass below without ever hitting bottom, swallowed without a splash.

For the chasm is full.

Not empty, not open air. Full of plant.

It does not read as green at first. Not from a distance. From the rim it looks like the chasm has been packed with shredded paper, with tinder, with insulation. A dense, dry, tawny, off-white, amber, rust-red, and bone-yellow mat that extends from wall to wall for kilometers, banked and billowing and mounded. A town could be lost in it. Several have been.

Come closer, and down into it, and it resolves.

These are not plants that use water. There is no water to use. Their chemistry is other. Their roots do not seek. In fact nothing here has roots in the way you think of roots. The substrate itself is plant. The old growth has died into a deep, corky, pithy peat that is not damp, but oily, resinous. It holds shape. New growth nails itself into it with pitons of hard fiber. The trunks are hollow reeds the width of your torso, made of stacked rings like wasp nests, light as balsa and hard as horn. They don't transport sap; they wick warm wax upward through capillary fur. The leaves are not leaves. They are sails. Thin, dry membranes of keratin and cellulose, no stomata, no juice, translucent as onion skin, veined with black lignin. They lean to catch wind, not sun. The sun here comes straight down for only a brutal hour at noon — normal, white, high-desert light that spears to the floor of the cut — but the plants do not photosynthesize the way you were taught. They are amber-engine plants. They eat light and air and the hot mineral breath coming off the fresh rock. At night they exhale a faint cinnamon-hot smell, like paper aging.

It is warm all the time. The stone holds heat, 35, 40 degrees, oven-warm, kiln-warm, and because there is no water to buffer it, the air shivers with it. The light has weight.

And it is dense. Not dense like a rainforest where you can step around boles. Dense like a thatch has been built by something that never learned the concept of empty space. Fronds interleave with fronds. Bark sheds in sheets and catches on other bark. The wax-sails rattle against each other. The aerial lattice is continuous — you can climb out across it without ever touching stone, walking on springy interwoven branches that give under your feet. Under that, in the second layer and third layer and tenth layer, it is dimmer, a dry dusk made of filtered light and pollen. There is pollen or what serves for pollen — not wet, not sticky, but a fine, glassy floss that hangs in the hot air for days, never clumping, never settling unless it is snagged. The air is saturated with it. Breathing feels like breathing fine warm lint.

Nothing is still.

This is not a forest that grows over seasons. This one grows while you watch. The primitive physics of it is frantic. A shoot will punch out a meter in an afternoon with a soft tearing sound, unfurling a new sail that snaps taut in the wind and immediately begins to quiver. A stand of reed-trunks that was load-bearing yesterday has gone soft and collapses today into dry pulp, and in the space, ten others are already elbowing up. The whole mass creaks, sighs, splits, resettles. It is embryonic tissue. It has not learned to be stable.

And the animals —

You hear them before anything else. From the rim it was already deafening, but you thought it was wind. Now, inside, the sound is a physical pressure. Because nothing here is soft with water, everything that moves makes noise. The dry leaves clatter like a warehouse of fans. The trunks knock hollowly. And the fauna is not *in* the vegetation, it *is* the same density as the vegetation.

They are almost all arthropodous, though the distinction between plant and animal is a courtesy that doesn't hold here. There are beetles the size of tortoises with backs thatched in living sail-leaves that they have planted on themselves. There are mantis things made of folded parchment the exact same material as the leaves, transparent until they move. There are clouds of moth-things, white as ash, each as big as your hand, with wings that never stop. They don't sip nectar — there is no nectar — they scrape wax. They rasp. Billions of rasping mouths. There are things like dry snakes made entirely of articulated seed pods that roll through the mat, germinating as they go. Long, blind, chirring things that weave.

The sound is not one sound. It is click and crackle and hiss and chitter and sawing and a high, continuous cicada scream that never, ever pauses, even at noon. You feel it in your teeth.

They make the built things possible, or at least visible.

You find them by the way the noise changes. Where the mat has been cut. The cut edges are raw and pale and oozing a bead of clear resin that hardens like glass. Small platforms of woven reed, suspended, lashed together with twine made from stripped fiber. Shelters like inverted nests, thatched with layered sails, open to the wind. No nails. No metal. A few hand-shaped stones — pounders, scrapers — left on a sill of pith. A ladder made by notching a still-living trunk. They are scattered, a few dozen across the whole open reach of the chasm, temporary because permanence is not a concept the chasm respects. You do not meet the makers at first. Then you realize you have been seeing them — their cloaks are sewn sail-leaves, their skin is dusted with floss, they move exactly the pace of the beetles, one with the turmoil, working.

Their rule, if you stay long enough to understand it, is this: do not bring water. They have never known it. Their bodies have no means to shed it. Their tools will warp and rot, their plant world will blacken and slime if water falls. Dryness is not an absence here — it is the law, the medium, the blood. The wax flows, not water. The sound carries because the air is never heavy. The whole raging, rustling, chattering, growing, collapsing, rebuilding city of fiber in this new-wounded earth exists because water never came.

If a drop did — and sometimes the high desert above will distantly bruise and spit a single, bewildered shower that never reaches the bottom — the whole chasm hisses.
