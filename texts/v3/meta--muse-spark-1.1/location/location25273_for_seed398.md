It is not on the mountain. It is *in* the mountain, but the mountain is not a mountain.

Six days ago, or sixty years ago - nobody up here keeps good time - something pushed up. Not upheaved, not folded. Pushed. Like a thumb through wet paper. A needle of black glass and basalt foam, three kilometers wide at the base and twenty-three high, and still rising a few centimeters a day. You can see the stress lines if you look at it from low air, which no one does anymore. The whole mass is too hot to be that high, too sharp to be that hot. It hasn't learned how to be weathered yet.

Near the top, where the air is already too thin to breathe and the sun is too white to look at, there is a blister. That's where the city is.

You have to understand the scale wrong to see it right. From the outside, you would miss it entirely. A dark mouth, fifty meters across, a vesicle popped open in cooling stone. A wound in the side of the spire. For a few hours after dawn, it exhales. That's it. Inside, it opens.

Inside it is enormous and empty.

The blister is one pocket, maybe two hundred meters long, sixty wide, and tall - the height is hard to measure because the far wall curves away from you in a way that doesn't quite close. The rock here is brand new. This whole room was gas last month. The walls are knife-edged, still glossy. Obsidian black where it cooled too fast, rust-red scoria where it foamed. Every surface is a fractal of tiny ledges and conchoidal breaks, sharp enough to slice a suit if you catch it. It smells like hot iron and ozone, even through filters. The stone ticks constantly as it cools. Pits pop open with a soft *plink*.

It is hot in here. Not the clean dry heat of high desert, but a low, body-heat, held heat. 58C on the stone at noon, 62C near the floor where the mass is thickest. The air is thin and does nothing to carry it away. You feel it in your molars. Sweat doesn't evaporate right; it beads and sits and then slides off.

And yet, it is damp.

The city makes it damp. Or finds the damp that is already here.

The spire is cold above and hot below. At night, at 23 kilometers, the temperature outside the blister drops to -70. The stone breathes in, contracts. The thin upper atmosphere, almost a vacuum, still holds a few grams of water, volcanic outgassing from the needle itself, a little sublimated frost carried up on the jet. It condenses on the inside of the blister as the wall radiates heat to space. Not rain. Never rain. A fur that forms, then a sheen, then beads that race down the black glass in quick, nervous lines before they can boil off again.

The city lives to catch it.

This is a settlement, because people settled here, but not the way people usually settle. Nobody planned streets. There is no ground, really. The pocket's bottom is a slope of loose, sharp scree that is still 90C and slides every hour. Instead the city clings.

It hangs from the walls and from nothing. Low terraces of dull ceramic and carbon-black scaffolding, three, four story clusters, gone unfinished in places. Balconies that were clearly grown more than built, with no seams. Frames that hold themselves ten meters off the rock face with no cable, no strut. You can see the dust avoiding them. Between clusters, gaps of thirty, fifty meters of just empty, hot air. The density is wrong for a town. It feels abandoned even though you can see light in every window. You have to call across the void. Voices don't carry well in thin air, so they use lights.

The technology does not read as technology until you get close. From far away the structures look like crude, mud-brick cubes stuck to a cave. Up close you realize they have no weight. Or rather, they have decided that weight is negotiable. Each building sits inside its own little dimple of gravity, a stitch-field that pins a ton of matter to the wall by telling the wall that it has always been there. If you put your hand on the edge of a stitch you feel your skin try to go two directions at once, and your fillings ache. In the gaps where the fields overlap, water that was running down the wall stops, lifts, hangs in perfect, trembling spheres the size of fists, steaming slightly, until the overlap moves and they drop.

The builders - gone now, or sleeping, or too small to see - didn't like straight lines. Corridors curve just slightly wrong. If you walk the length of the eastern cluster and count doors, you will count a different number on the way back. The far end of the blister is farther than it should be when you're looking at it and closer when you're not. That is the weirdness that sits on your neck: the pocket is still deciding what geometry it wants. The stitch-fields make Euclidean space a suggestion.

At night the city dims itself. There is no sun inside anyway. The only light is the deep cherry-red glow of the rock itself, and the soft amber worklights strung along the water channels, and the blue-white Cherenkov flicker of the stitch anchors when they catch a larger drip. Dim, but enough to see the whole emptiness at once. Enough to see there is nothing else alive here.

No plants. They tried. Nothing roots in glass this fresh, this hot, at this pressure. No mold, no lichen. No birds, no insects. Just stone and stitched ceramic and water. The only motion besides the ticking rock and the intermittent drips is the slow, patient adjustment of the city. A terrace extending a lip by a centimeter overnight to catch more condensation. A channel reshaping itself because a new crack appeared higher up. The culverts and gutters are the real streets here - shallow, black, polished grooves that run from every surface down to a central cistern that hangs in the middle of the air, a flattened spheroid of water, ten meters across, perpetually shivering. It never boils, though it should. It is held.

This place is active. Not bustling, not alive, but active. A dozen processes that have no business coexisting. The rock contracting and cracking. Water appearing from nowhere and being coaxed into tanks. Stitch-fields humming below hearing, just at the edge where sound becomes pressure in your chest. Sometimes, for an hour, a wind moves through the mouth of the blister - thin, hot, smelling of distant sulfur - and sets the hanging cistern rippling, and sends a spray of almost-boiling droplets across the whole void. Then it stops. Then the quiet again.

The quiet is the main thing. Mostly quiet. The kind of quiet you get at extreme elevation when there isn't enough air to hold sound. Your own breathing is loud. The tick of the cooling stone is loud. A single drop hitting ceramic on the other side of the pocket sounds like a bell.

If you stay long enough to get used to the heat, you understand the rule of this place: it is not a shelter built in a mountain. It is a water trap that learned how to be a town, in a fresh bubble of glass at the top of the world, holding itself up by forgetting how heavy it is, waiting for the next crack to let in a little more cold.
