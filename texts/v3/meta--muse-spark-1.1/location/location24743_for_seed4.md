It's not a valley. It's not even a hollow.

You find it low. Everything here is low — a coastal flat so level you can hear the sea even three miles inland, but never see it. Black spruce stunted to fence-post height. Peat, cut through by last week's frost heave. Elevation so low the water table lives right under your boots; step and the ground sighs and stays dented, filling slowly with tea-dark water.

It's cold in the way that makes iron taste like blood. Early December dark, the kind where the sun comes up around 10 and never really does. The air itself is wet, not raining, condensing. Every surface weeps. Your breath doesn't puff, it hangs and falls.

You are looking for nothing, so you see it. One hummock of sphagnum a little higher than the others, maybe knee high, maybe two feet across at the base — the scale is wrong until you kneel. These moss domes are usually solid, a tight thatch of living and dead and living again, waterlocked. This one is breathing. The top has split.

Not from rot. From underneath. This is young country, geologically embarrassed. The last glacier scraped this down to basalt last Tuesday in geologic time, and the basalt is still sharp. A flake of it has punched up this winter, a black fang, anorthosite and frost, its edges unrounded, glittering with fresh break. It has lifted the moss like a roof jack, not killed it. The moss kept growing.

If you cup the dome in both hands and lift, gently, it lifts like a hat. Cold clay sucks, then releases.

Inside is a room.

An intimate room, no bigger than the ribcage of a dog. The air in there is warmer by a fraction — not warm, but sheltered — and so wet it has weight. Water beads on everything and does not fall until it has to. The walls, floor, ceiling: all vegetation, but not what you think of as plants. This isn't plants growing IN a place. Plants ARE the place. The dome moss has grown a thatch so dense on the outside that its interior, deprived of light, has become infrastructure. Thick sphagnum stems, red as veins, have lignified into beams. Leaflets have matted into felt floors. Living root-hairs of bog rosemary have been redirected — pulled, woven, chewed, trained — into cables and struts. The interior is hollowed, farmed.

It is a town.

Sparse, but urban. You see that at once because the emptiness is organized. Not scattered huts. Streets.

Three terraces are cut into the living mass on the north wall, each one held back by retaining walls of bundled, living stems tied with what looks like hair, or fungal cord. They are still growing. Between them run ramps, not stairs — broad, gentle, to let water travel. At the bottom, a plaza where nothing is built, on purpose: a cistern. A shallow dent in the moss where condensation collects, black and still, and drains through a sieved hole the size of a pencil lead, slowly. All the avenues lead to it.

The buildings are there, on the terraces. Twenty, thirty. No more. Each is a hut the size of your thumb, but with layered construction. Double-walled, because the outer wall is alive and the inner is dead thatch for insulation. Doorways low and round, reinforced with a ring of black chitin. Roofs are overlapping scales of leaf, thatched so tightly water runs off instead of through. Between them are smaller structures you don't understand at first — drying racks of fine twig, pens made of tight root loops, a granary that is just a swollen gall on a stem coaxed into being a bin, full of white fungal grain.

The builders are everywhere.

You thought at first the darkness was absolute. It's not; it's 0.1 out of 3. It's a darkness your eyes invent things in until they learn to see. And then you see that the dark is moving. Overwhelmingly moving.

They are woodlice, or no — something like woodlice but longer, with a translucence to them. Bog isopods, pallid amber, each as long as the first joint of your finger. Hundreds. No, thousands. The walls seethe so slowly you thought it was the moss at first. They pour along the streets, each carrying a fragment: a leaf bit, a ball of fungal paste, a drop of water held somehow between their front legs without breaking. In the plaza, a team of six is dragging a dead crane fly many times their size, dismantling as they go. On the top terrace, others tend a farm: not the fungus they eat, but the fungus that tends the moss, a pale lace that makes the moss grow straighter, more beam-like.

There's no sound, mostly. If you hold your breath, you hear the town. Quiet to the point that your own heart is intrusion. A soft click of chitin on chitin. The tick of a droplet detaching from ceiling moss and hitting a roof. A faint rasp as jaws shear. And underneath, the endless, patient drip of the fen. One drop per second from ten thousand points.

The cold is what makes it strange. This is not a tropical ant city. Everything is slow and deliberate and not hibernating. The weirder thing: the city is built for cold and wet. Doorways face away from the prevailing drip. The roofs steam slightly — actually steam — because the isopods inside are massed together, generating a breath of heat that hits the condensing air. The moss near the most inhabited huts has turned a different color, pinkish, from that heat and from nitrogen.

And it is active. Not a relic. Fresh construction on the east wall where basalt has cracked open more recently — you can see the raw, sharp edge of rock, jet black and still sharp enough to cut, young — the moss torn, and the isopods have already begun to reclaim it. A crew is hauling clay up the terrace, pellet by pellet, from the water table seeping at the bottom, to daub the tear. Another crew is weaving living sphagnum tips into a curtain over the exposed stone to keep the cold from knifing directly in. Ecologies in motion: a tiny black linyphiid spider, a predator the size of a pea, picks its way across the plaza roof; the whole avenue empties before it, then fills behind it. A springtail bloom erupts near the granary — hundreds of white specks boiling — and the isopods harvest them.

Primitive tech, absolutely. No metal. No fire — fire would kill the place in a breath. But tools: you see one perched on a roof shingle, holding a splinter of basalt, sharp as a fresh flake, using it to shave the operculum off a moss capsule. Another uses a discarded spruce needle as a lever. They are makers with their mouths and their hands and what the bog gives.

If you stay too long kneeling, water from your knees soaks up. The cold starts to get sincere. The isopods do not notice you. Or if they do, you are weather, not an inhabitant. The city was here last month and will be here next month, until the hummock collapses or freezes solid or a moose steps on it.

You lower the dome, gently. It resettles with a soft, wet sigh. The town is hidden again. From above, it's just moss, sparse on the flat, scattered like almost every other hummock, with more space than life, holding.

Drip. Click. Drip.
