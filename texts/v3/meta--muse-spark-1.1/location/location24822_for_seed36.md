It is below.

Not deep in the dark-earth sense. Shallow-below. The surface world is right there, a handsbreadth above you, so close you can feel its heat coming through.

This place is new. That is the first thing you understand when your eyes adjust.

The rock did not get carved here. It *parted*. A long, regional blister rose between the topsoil and the bedrock when something — water pressure, root pressure, heat — pushed up from underneath and down from above at the same time. The limestone sheet that makes the ceiling of this world is still raw, embryonic. It has not been smoothed by rivers or wind. It hangs in soft, unchiseled swells and teats and half-formed pillars, like cooling wax. It looks poured, not eroded. In places it is thin enough to see light through, in others so thin it has split.

And the light comes through it all.

Bright. Unreasonably bright for any place underground. At noon the entire ceiling glows. It is not sunlight falling in shafts. It is sunlight *diffused*, filtered through a hundred meters of translucent white stone and a mat of living soil, until it arrives everywhere at once, shadowless and hot. The air itself looks white-yellow. You squint not against glare but against fullness.

The heat stays. Hot, still, held. The stone sweats.

That is the water here. There is no river. No lake. The valley — because it is a valley, a town-sized, open-country rift ten kilometers long and two deep, you can not see its far wall for the green — has no watercourse to give it shape. Instead, the ceiling sweats and the plants weep. Condensation beads on every root-hair, on the underside of every leaf, grows heavy, and falls straight down. It patters to the leaves below, beads again. The ground is damp, the air is damp, your skin is damp, but your feet are not wet. If you press your hand into the floor, which is not floor, a few drops well up. There are pockets — hollows in the root-mass where clear water has collected like cups — but they are still and warm and covered in a skin of duckweed and pollen. You could drink from them if you knew which ones hadn't gone stagnant.

Because everything here is plant.

Not forest covering land. Plant *as* land.

The first colonizers must have found the split the moment it opened and poured in. Now the volume is filled. From the glowing ceiling hang curtains of aerial roots as thick as your torso, pale and smooth, descending twenty, forty meters without branching until they find each other and braid. Between them, broadleaf vines have made walls, then rooms, then overlapping roofs, then false floors. There are places where you walk not on earth at all but on a springing lattice of interwoven stems five deep, with black composted humus trapped between them. The bauhinia-like trees that anchor it all do not grow up. There is no up to grow to — the light is everywhere. So they grow out, sideways, inward, looping back on themselves. Leaves the size of doors, leaves the size of hands, leaves like needles, all layered in a density that should be choking but isn't because it's so still. You can see through ten layers of green, each slightly out of focus, until distance turns it all into a green haze.

It is dense in the way a library is dense. You push a curtain of hanging creepers aside and there is another. And another. Air moves only when you move it.

And in this density, the built things.

They are scattered and small and they are not technological. No metal. No cut stone. Before the blister opened, people left things on what was then a flat scrub plain — a low field wall, a cistern lip, a shepherd's cairn, a single whitewashed chapel no bigger than a bedroom. When the ground bellied, it took them with it, lowered them intact into this new greenhouse. So now you come through a hanging meadow of epiphytes and find a doorway standing free with no building attached, strangled in fig. You find a short run of stacked stones that now serves as a trellis for melons that have never known wind. They are not ruins. They have not been ruined. They have been included.

There is almost no animal life. That is the quiet. The damp should buzz and the rot should hum and it doesn't. The whole green mass is in perfect, holding equilibrium — never changing, or changing so slowly the moment has stretched into forever. Nothing is falling. Nothing is sprouting. Nothing is competing. Once in a very long while you hear a single, dry seed pod pop somewhere far off, and the sound is enormous. Once in a while you see the one fauna: a pale moth as wide as your outstretched hand, moving like paper in hot air, landing on a leaf that exactly matches it and disappearing.

It has its own rule here: do not cut, do not climb to the ceiling. Not because it is forbidden, but because there is no space to. The plants have filled every niche and sealed every edge. To make a clearing would be to make a wound that would never close, because nothing here is growing to fill it anymore. It arrived at totality and stopped.

So you stay on the root-paths the water-weight has made, where condensation drips every seven seconds from the same root tip onto the same glossy leaf, forever. The light stays bright. The air stays hot and close and sweet with leaf-steam. The raw stone above you sighs once in a long while as it continues, microscopically, to birth itself.
