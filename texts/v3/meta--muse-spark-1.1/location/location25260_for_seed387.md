It is not on the bottom of the ocean. It is below the bottom of the ocean.

You have to go down through the cold black to find it — down past where light stops meaning anything — and then further, through basalt, through a fault that never sealed right. The rock gives way to a pocket. Not a cave. A blister in the crust, a perfect, almost spherical hollow about three blocks across. Water made it, then kept it.

This is the Sump Town.

The water inside is not the ocean outside. The ocean outside is cold, crushing, dead quiet. The water inside is warm. Bathtub-warm, womb-warm, wrong-warm — held at 32, 33 degrees constantly by the stone itself, which glows faintly with deep heat. It doesn't circulate out. It churns in place. The whole pocket is submerged, not flooded — it was born submerged — a city that was never dry.

And it is a city.

The terrain is vertical urban section, cut away and dropped in a bowl. You see it all at once because the water is clear and the pocket is small and empty. There are maybe six buildings, but they are real buildings — five- and six-story tenements of riveted iron plate and poured concrete and brick that has gone soft at the corners. They are plumbed into the rock walls with conduits thick as subway tunnels. There is a street, with a tram line still strung, the cable swaying. There is a small industry crouched at the bottom of the bowl: a factory, or a pumping house, or a power plant — it is all three. It takes up the lowest third of the town.

There is no ground, really. The “ground” is a grate, and below the grate is open dark where the warm comes from. The buildings are anchored to the basalt with struts and suspension cables like they were lowered in. Established — that’s the word. Everything is weathered into correctness. The edges of every I-beam are rounded by a century of warm water flow. The concrete is pitted but not collapsing. Rust doesn't fall here, it grows like a second skin, in smooth, dark sheets that do not flake. The town has settled into being exactly what warm, fast water wants a town to be.

Because the space is so empty, it feels bigger inside than it should. Density is all wrong. The water between the buildings is vast. You could live your whole life in the alley between two of those tenements and never touch either wall; the current would hold you in the middle. Light is scarce. The town had windows once but no one needed to see out. Now it lights itself with its own work: low amber factory bulbs in cages, still burning behind thick glass, each one surrounded by a halo of particulate that never settles. Some sodium lamps on the street flicker at 50 hertz, too slow, throwing the water into strobe. Beyond their 30-foot reach, the basalt wall of the pocket curves up and is black.

No plants. Nothing green has ever learned the trick of this water. No kelp, no algae, not even the bacterial mats that love a hot vent. Something about the metal, maybe, or the way the water is too clean, too endlessly distilled and recirculated. The surfaces are mineral — clean iron, clean stone, a kind of white calcium fur where the water kisses concrete and gives up.

It is industrial and it is still running.

That’s the deafening part.

You hear it before you breach the basalt. A roar that enters the bones through the jaw.

The factory never stopped. Its purpose is to keep the pocket empty of the ocean and full of itself. Intakes big as houses scream as they suck in surrounding cold seep and flash it to warm. Turbines the size of grain silos turn overhead in the gloom, not generating electricity — not anymore — just moving water, churning it, keeping the entire town in a permanent, violent spin. Water hammers through pipes, slams against baffles, booms inside boilers that have no fire. The tram cable sings like a high-tension wire in a hurricane. The buildings themselves creak and thrum like drums. You cannot think in that sound. You feel it as pressure on your chest.

And because it churns, nothing is still. This is a turbulent place, bazaar-fast, anthill-active without any ants. The current is chaotic, slewing from one wall to the other. A loose door — and most are loose — bangs open-shut-open-shut a hundred times a minute. A cloud of rust scales lifts off a rooftop, is torn apart, reforms on the other side of town. The light fixtures swing so wildly they clack their cages. Sometimes two currents meet head-on in the open water between buildings and make a standing vortex, a clear eye that lasts for hours, spinning faster and faster until it collapses with a concussive thump you feel in your molars.

The rules of the world here are simple and unusual:

1.  Warm water rises, cold water sinks, but here the warmest water is the heaviest because it's full of dissolved iron. So it sinks. The center of town is a slow, warm down-welling that pins you to the grate if you get caught.
2.  Sound is architecture. In the roar, there are pockets where opposing noises cancel out — bubbles of near-silence, the size of a phone booth, that drift. If you find one, you stay in it and breathe.
3.  The town is empty but not abandoned. The density lies. The machines replaced the need for people a long time ago.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. Once, in the dim amber below the factory grate, you might see something white and long - a blind vent fish, or a pale crab the size of a dinner plate, clinging to a vibrating pipe for dear life, its shell coated in that white fur. It does not belong here; it fell in from the cold ocean through a crack and got cooked clean and stayed because it has nowhere else to go. It watches the turbines with eyeless patience.

If you stayed long enough to acclimate, you'd realize the town is not decaying. It is weathered, balanced. It has found a deal with the water: the water gets to run forever, deafening and warm and frantic in its little hidden room, and the town gets to remain exactly itself — an urban, industrial pocket, fully formed, three blocks of city that never needed a sky, humming and shuddering in the dark below the world.
