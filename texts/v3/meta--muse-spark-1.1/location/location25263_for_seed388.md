It is not a city. It is the size of a city block that decided to become a city anyway.

A perfect, almost circular sink in the lowlands, maybe three hundred meters across, rim to rim. The land around it is flat and unremarkable, but here it simply gave way a long time ago — not a violent collapse, an old sigh. The limestone softened and settled and the water found it. So you arrive at a lip of wet grass and weathered rebar and look down, and there it is: a room.

The scale is wrong in the way cathedrals are wrong. It is small enough to hold in one glance, but too dense and too tall to take in at once. Everything is down there, layered and pressed together until there is no negative space left.

The bottom is not land. It's water with opinions about being land. A black, warm, shallow marsh that never drains because the water table is right there, right under your foot if you had a foot to put down. It weeps. That's the right word. The walls of the sink weep constantly. The old industrial terracing — three, four, five levels of concrete bays, rusted gantries, brick engine houses built right into the rock — all of it weeps from a thousand cracks and joints and cold sweats. Condensation forms on the undersides of every pipe and falls again. You hear it before you see it.

This place was built to move water. That was its purpose and it never stopped.

There are pumps the size of houses, boilers green with verdigris and moss, flywheels ten feet across turning so slowly you think they have stopped, then you blink and the same spoke is somewhere else. Belt drives still run, slack and shining with water and algal film, connecting everything to everything else. Electrical switchboards stand open to the damp air, their ceramic fuses furred with copper crystals, buzzing with a low, patient 60-cycle hum that lives in your molars. It's loud here — not sharp-loud, but saturated-loud. 

The sound is:

The drip. Uncountable drips from a thousand heights hitting a thousand surfaces — tin roofs, broad leaves, open water, iron.

The sigh of steam where it escapes a valve no bigger than your thumb and vanishes into the dim.

The hum of the dynamos.

The groan of metal that has been wet for a hundred years and will be wet for a hundred more.

There is almost no light. The sink is deep enough that the sun, even when it is warm — and it is warm, wet-lung warm, laundry-boiler warm, a constant 80 degrees of saturated air — never reaches straight down. What light there is is filtered through steam and through a ceiling of vegetation that grew over the rim and kept going, a thatch of philodendron and strangler fig and hanging curtain ferns that meet in the middle and leave only perforations. Sun comes down in solid columns through those holes, full of swimming particulate, and strikes the mist and breaks. Everything else is lit by worklight: caged amber bulbs on rusted cages, still burning on a circuit no one tends, and the bluish-green foxfire of bacterial bloom in the water.

It is lush past the point of gardening. Past orderly. Vegetation does not grow *in* this town; it is a structural partner.

Moss makes gaskets. A species of black-green fern that likes heat and iron grows out of every bolt-hole, its roots digging into concrete and splitting it with a patience that matches the flywheels. Bromeliads the size of bathtubs sit in the elbows of pipes and hold reservoirs in their cups, each one a complete, animal-free pond. There are no insects. No birds call. No rats in the walls. Nothing eats the plants, nothing pollinates them that you can see. That is the strange quiet under the loud: a jungle with no buzz, no chirp, no splash of a frog. The leaves are perfect, untorn. The water is clear enough to see down through duckweed to submerged staircases that still go somewhere, and there is nothing swimming. Even the rot is wrong — things decay by softening and slumping, by rust, not by being consumed. The copper leached from all that wire, the oil film that has never broken down, something in the air from the old works — it made this a plant-only place. The machines kept the humans away after a while. The plants didn't mind.

And because nothing eats, everything stays. Density becomes its own rule.

On any single vertical, from water to rim, you might have: water crowded with water-lettuce, the rusted hull of a screw-pump, a brick arch grown solid with maidenhair, a laundry line of cables still hung with ceramic insulators like beads, a balcony of an engineer's house with its windows all gone and its floor made of solid moss eight inches thick, a second house built on its roof out of corrugated steel that has softened and folded like paper, a fig root as thick as a man, and then the iron lattice of the original crane that lifted all of this down here in the first place. Everything touching. Everything holding something else up.

The erosion here is finished. This isn't collapsing; it has found the shape it wants to be. The concrete has weathered to look like limestone. The limestone has weathered to look like concrete. Edges are rounded. Channels are carved exactly as wide as the water that uses them. The slow slump of the walls happens on the scale of seasons — a walkway an inch lower after the spring rains, a new spring opening in the billiard room of Factory 3. The machines respect that slowness. They do not hammer. They push. Water moves from the lower sump to the upper cisterns to the distribution headers to the weeping walls to the sump again. A closed devotional loop.

If you were to climb down — and there are stairs, though they are more like creek beds now — you would find the rules posted without being written:

1. Everything sweats.
2. Nothing is idle, but nothing hurries.
3. Light is rationed.
4. No animals. The town tried that once, it didn't work. Now the doors close on their own when something warm tries to enter.
5. The water keeps everything.

Near the bottom, in what must have been the central shop, the floor is a solid mat of roots and pipe and basalt-fine silt, and it quivers slightly under the turning of something far below. All the pressure gauges down here agree: somewhere around 30 PSI, steady. The needles vibrate. It has been steady for a long, long time.

And in that dim, loud, green, iron, wet, warm room that thinks it is a city, the water continues to condense on the cold iron and fall back down as rain, a private weather system inside a hole in the flat world, too small for maps, too dense to fail.
