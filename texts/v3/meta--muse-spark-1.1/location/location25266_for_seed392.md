This whole lowland is a work.

Not a town built *on* land. There is no land under it. From the far salt-haze to the far salt-haze, everything is made.

You are standing on a floor. The floor is brick — soft-fired, lowland clay, still holding the thumb-press of whoever pressed it. It's pink-orange when dry and deepens to blood-color where it's wet. It goes on for a valley's length. It is not flat. It was laid by hand in broad, shallow pans, each pan a hand's breadth lower than the last, each with a lip no higher than your ankle, each forty or fifty strides across. A country-sized cascade of trays.

It is young. You can tell by the edges. The lips of the pans have not yet worn round. The corners are still sharp enough to catch your foot. The mortar is still soft in places, unfinished. Somewhere someone stopped work just yesterday, a thousand years ago. The channels between pans, cut with a stick-tool, still have the striations of fingers.

And it is hot and bright.

The light does not come from a sun. It comes from everywhere above, white and even and total, like looking up into the inside of a kiln before the firing starts. It has no source and therefore makes almost no shadow. Everything is lit from itself. The air under that light is thick. It sweats. Water beads on the underside of the air, gathers, and falls, not as rain but as a constant slow condensation. The bricks weep.

Each pan holds water.

Not deep — two inches, three inches in the deeper ones, water so clear and warm you can barely feel it until you step in it. The water does not flow. Or rather, it is always flowing, in a way that never changes. At the high edge of a pan the water wells up out of the brick itself, out through the pores, and slips, glass-slow, to the lower lip, where it slips over into the next pan down. An infinite gentle overflow that never lowers the upper pan or raises the lower. A circuit with no loss. The weeping rocks never dry.

Where there is water and brick and heat, there is green.

Vegetation here is not planted. It arrives. It colonizes the made-world with impossible lushness, but sparsely, as if respecting the architecture. In one pan, a single banyan-like tree has taken root directly in brick. No soil. Its roots have melted themselves into the brickwork, prying one brick up by half an inch, and they drink straight from the seep. Its canopy is twenty feet across, a dense, glossy roof of leaves that drips and drips. Three pans over, empty. Then a yard of rice, but not rice — something like rice, stalks too thick, heads too heavy, growing without ever having been sown, standing in perfect rows though no hand placed them. Then a tangle of elephant-ear taro with leaves as big as doors. Then a long bare stretch of wet shining brick where nothing grows, where the light doubles.

Everything is sparse enough to see the shape of the whole. You can stand on one lip and see a kilometer in any direction across the open trays, to the dimming where the light-thick haze eats distance. The horizon is not a line of earth, but the place where the brick and water pattern finally becomes too bright to distinguish.

It is loud with life, quietly.

The fauna does not seem to need ground. Clouds of dragonflies, each as long as your finger, iridescent green-black, hover over each pan and never seem to land, their wings making no hum. Small frogs the color of wet brick sit at the lips and blink in unison. You can look away for an hour and look back and they are still blinking at the same moment. In the water, fat black fish eels turn in slow circles and do not grow, do not eat. High wading birds with legs like reed-straws stand one-legged in pans a quarter mile distant — you are never close to them, no matter how far you walk toward them. The whole valley buzzes at the threshold of hearing and then stops, leaving only the faint tick of water over brick.

The rules here are simple, and wrong:

1. Nothing erodes, though water always runs.
2. Nothing grows, though everything is lush. That tree you saw has always been that same size. The rice heads have always been bent, heavy, unharvested. If you break a leaf, the two halves stay exactly as they were when separated, green and turgid, forever.
3. Nothing was ever quite finished, and nothing is falling apart.
4. The built world underneath is hollow in some places. If you press your ear to the warm brick, sometimes you hear a second drip, far below, as if there is another identical land-bath underneath this one, also overflowing.
5. Distance is not reliable. Some pans are bigger on the inside than the outside. You can walk across a ten-stride pan for a hundred steps and still not reach the other lip, and then you're suddenly across, without having crossed.

There are tools here, left scattered. A wooden comb for smoothing mortar. A stone mallet. A loop of woven fiber. All primitive. All abandoned mid-stroke. No bodies. No footprints except your own, which fill immediately with a millimeter of warm water and then hold that water forever, even after you lift your foot.

If you sit down, which you will because the heat makes you soft and sleepy, the brick is warm as skin. The water around your ankles is precisely body temperature. The light presses on you gently from all sides, like bright warm hands. The dragonflies hold position. The frogs blink. The water spills over the lip in front of you at the speed of glass, forever, without ever moving.
