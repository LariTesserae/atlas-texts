It is low. Not low like a valley between hills — low like a place that has barely decided to be land at all.

You are standing only a few meters above sea level, but you cannot see the sea. The world here is flat in its larger gesture and savagely vertical in its details. A plain that extends past what you can make out in the dim air, three, four, maybe ten kilometers in every direction until it dissolves into cold mist. Town-sized. Valley-sized without being a valley.

The rock is young. That is the first wrong thing your hands understand before your eyes do. It has no manners yet.

It is black basalt, or something like it — a fresh crust, still with a glassy skin in places. It cooled fast, maybe under water, and when it contracted it shattered into a dense forest of columns and blades and broken teeth. Not a field of boulders. A field of presences. Every square meter has something in it: a knee-high needle, a waist-high flute, a pillar as thick as your body leaning into the next. They are packed so tightly you have to turn sideways to move between them. Near-touching. Layered. The ground is not ground, it is the broken tops of more columns underneath, and under those, more. There is no soil. There is nowhere for soil to collect because there are no slopes gentle enough to hold it for long.

And it weeps.

There is water everywhere, but not as sea, not as lake, not as rain. The air itself is wet. Cool enough that your breath makes a short cloud and then is taken. Cool enough that the stone never warms. The sky is a low, uniform overcast, pearl-gray, light without source — late dusk that has lasted all day. Dim light that doesn't cast shadows, only makes edges softer. And that air condenses on everything. The stone sweats continuously. Beads form on every sharp edge, run down every vertical face, gather and fall. 

Each pillar has a thousand small pocks and vesicles and at the bottom of each one a droplet swells and lets go. *Tick. Tak. Tok.* The rock is porous in a way basalt shouldn't be. If you put your hand on it you feel it seeping into your palm, cold as well water. If you listen close to a larger column you can hear it inside — a faint fizzing, water moving through micro-tubes under pressure from below. This whole plain is only barely above the water table. The water is right there, a meter down, pushing up. In the lowest hollows between the densest clusters, it pools in perfect, black, still mirrors that are only centimeters deep and reflect the dark pillars twice as tall. You step and you are in an inch of water that has no mud in it because there is nothing to make mud from.

No plant life. That is the second wrong thing and it takes longer to notice because your brain keeps trying to put lichen where there is only wet black gloss, keeps trying to put moss in the cracks where there would always be moss. There is nothing. No green, no brown, no yellow. Not even a film. The rock is too new, the water too mineral-poor, the light too dim, or something stricter than that is in force. The world is mineral-only. The water that falls from the stones is clear and tastes of nothing but stone and cold. Where it dries — which it almost never does — it leaves a lace of white salt, a few crystals thick, that the next sweating dissolves again. White on black, then gone.

That is why it is so loud.

There is no softening. No leaf, no humus, no grass to soak sound. The plain is all hard, wet, close surfaces — an acoustic maze of dense glassy flutes. And every drop hits stone. Not earth. Stone. A drop of water on basalt makes a sharp, clean clack, not a splash. Multiply that by millions. At any given second, in a hundred-meter radius around you, ten thousand droplets are falling, a few centimeters or a few meters. It never stops because the condensation never stops. It is not rain — the sky is not raining — but it sounds exactly like a hard rain in a city of stone gutters. A constant, clattering, chinking, ringing applause of water on rock. In wider spaces where the pillars are a little taller, wind moves — a slow, cool draft from the unseen sea — and then the pillars themselves sing. They are hollow in places; water has blown through. The wind catches the edges and hums, low and resonant, a chord that comes and goes so slowly you think you imagined it until it returns.

You have to raise your voice to be heard over it.

The process is slow. Nothing here is in a hurry. The pillars do not topple. The water does not cut them yet; they are too young to have developed streams. The whole plain breathes on a cycle you can only see if you stayed a year: in the morning, more fog, more beading; in the afternoon, a little less; in winter, the pools freeze into perfect black glass with the pillars locked in it; in summer, the water table drops a hand's width and more stone is bared. Patient. Minimal flux.

There are things built here, but you can go a long time without seeing one, and when you do you are not sure it was built.

In a small clearing where the columns happen to have failed and made a little amphitheater barely wider than your outstretched arms, someone has stacked three flat shards into a low windbreak, just high enough to sit behind. The stones are of the same rock, but the stacking is deliberate. Lichen would have marked age elsewhere; here there is no such clock. It could have been last week or a hundred years ago. Further on, another — a ring of sharper teeth pressed points-inward, a nest shape, empty. And further, a taller, precarious cairn of five stones balanced where no wind could have left them. They are rare, scattered, pointless except as a statement: *I stopped here.* 

Nothing technological about them. No metal, no cut mark, no fire-black. No plastic, no cord. They are just this same weeping mineral put briefly in a different order.

Because something lives here, rarely.

You will hear it before you see it. A change in the constant clack — a larger splash. In one of the black pools, moving with absurd slowness so as not to touch too many cold edges, is a pale thing the size of your hand. Not a crab, not a shrimp, more like both misremembered. Translucent white, almost clear, with long, feeler-like legs that test each column before committing. It drinks the condensed water, or grazes something invisible in it — a bacterial film your eyes cannot see because this place is not supposed to have plant life, but must have something even simpler. It does not look at you. It has no eyes you can find. When you step too near, it stops for minutes and then continues, because hurry is not a concept that has arrived here yet. There might be one of them per acre. You will not see two at once.

The strangeness is not in any one thing, but in how the place holds together despite its contradictions. It is dense and open at once. It is wet and loud like a rainforest, and dead as a fresh lava field. It is a beach that uplifted yesterday, but as cold and dim as a place far inland in late winter. The rules here are simple and strict:

1. Water comes up, not down.
2. Nothing softens hard.
3. Everything that happens happens very slowly, but once it starts, it never stops making noise.

If you stay long enough, your coat soaks through, not from rain but from standing still. Your shoulders start to hear the ringing as music. You start to see the plain not as chaos but as columns all leaning slightly away from a center you haven't found yet — as if something enormous and slow breathed out under this fresh crust and froze, mid-exhale, into all these near-touching teeth, still weeping from the effort.
