It is high. Not high like a peak. High like a country has been lifted.

You are on a table so vast it has weather on its back. A continent of a plateau, kilometers above any sea, but you would not know it from cold or thin air — the air here is thick and warm and close, a low bathhouse heat, 28 or 30 degrees, saturated until the atmosphere itself sweats. The elevation is only legible by pressure in the ears and by the way the clouds live *below* you and *around* you, not above. There is no above. There is only the dark.

There is no sun here. There has not been one for a long time, or perhaps the land rose so high it pushed into a permanent overcast that is a hundred miles thick. The light parameter is zero. Not dusk. Not starlight. True dark — a black felt dark that your eyes never adjust to. You learn the place with your skin and your shins.

The ground is old. That's the first thing your feet tell you.

This plateau is not young and sharp and crumbling. It is established, settled into its own shape. Water has been working here for millions of years. The basalt and sandstone are stepped and benched, carved into broad, shallow bowls a mile across and a hundred feet deep, their lips smoothed to the curve of a palm. Every bowl holds water. Some hold lakes of black glass twenty feet deep. Some hold only a skim and a skin of peat. Water is everywhere but not as ocean, not as flood — as nearness. Water at the surface, water about to be surface. The rock itself weeps. You can put your hand on a cliff face in the dark and feel it bleed, cold seep threading down through moss, finding your wrist, your elbow.

Between the bowls, the divides are wide and spongy. The whole highland is a perched marsh. Sphagnum, but not as you know it. 

On Earth, plants reach for light. Here, there is no light to reach for.

So the forest is vertical in another way. It is *dense* — density 2.1, shoulder-to-shoulder, layered, dripping, near-touching. From the peat rises a first story of fungal trunks, not trees — columnar, white and grey and dimly, faintly warm to the touch, as thick as pillars, as tall as a three-storey house, their caps spread and fused overhead into a continuous, leathery canopy that holds the condensation and releases it as a constant secondary rain. From that canopy hangs a second story: actual plants, but black. Black fern, black vine, black leaf, their surfaces not green but pitch, matte, designed to drink heat and chemical rain rather than photons. They have no chlorophyll. Their leaves are thick, waxy, hot. They sigh. When you brush them, they retract slightly, and they smell of iron and cinnamon. Their roots never touch the ground; they root in the fungal caps above and dangle down, drinking the air.

And everything is wet. That secondary rain never stops — plip, plok, a constant matting of water stitching from canopy to peat to stone to pool. The whole plateau inhales and exhales water. You hear it squeeze.

And you hear everything else, because the dark is loud.

The plateau has its own weather, its own wind. Low, warm wind scrapes over the fungal forest and carries sound for miles without light to correct it. And the loudest sound is not natural.

The Built is scattered — 1.1 — but the Tech is industrial — 2.5. That contradiction resolves when you walk a mile in the dark.

They are out here. Engines.

Spaced across the vastness like monuments left by a civilization that thought in watersheds, they stand. Each one is the size of a small mill town, but there is no town around it. No roads. Only the faint remains: squared stones swallowed by moss, a stair that goes down into water and stops, a pylon fallen and now a nurse-log for black ferns. And then the Machine.

Towers of riveted black iron, still hot, still running. Pump-houses without houses. They breathe on geothermal bloom, deep warm blood from the high rock. They were built to manage this place — to lift, to drain, to hold. A pipe thirty feet in diameter rises out of a bog-bowl, arches like a dinosaur neck, and vomits warm, mineral-rich water into the next bowl over, endlessly recirculating the marsh so it never stagnates, never dries. Another engine is all condenser vanes, a forest of metal fins fifty meters tall, clicking and contracting, turning the saturated air into rivers that pour down its sides.

They churn. They throb. Deep, low-frequency diesel hearts, or steam, or something that sounds like both — CHUD-chud-chud-chud that you feel in your molars. They have been running for centuries without maintenance, and the erosion has made them part of the geology. Water has worn grooves in their iron sides. Fungus has grown over their bolts. And they spark. Relays the size of doors throw blue-white arcs in the darkness, and for half a second you see too much: the endless fungal colonnade stretching off into black, the beads of water frozen mid-fall, the sheen on a million black leaves.

The sparks do not light the place. They mark the darkness. The smell after each throw is hard ozone and hot stone.

This is why the fauna is so abundant. And so strange.

In the dark and warm and wet and dense, life doesn't bother with vision. Everything sings, or clicks, or drums. The air is full. Things the length of your finger with wings like wet paper cross your face, navigating by sonar so delicate you feel it as a prickle. On the peat, something like a crab, but as big as a dog, as soft as a slug, plows through Sphagnum, eating fungus, moaning low. Above you, in the black vines, troupes of slender, lemur-limbed grazers move hand-over-hand, their coats covered in the same luminous bacteria that lives in the fungi. They are not bright — weirdness 2.0 — the light they make is not for seeing. It's the byproduct. Their backs crawl with a pale, heatless marbling, like foxfire, like damp electricity. They chitter in chorus, and that chorus is everywhere. When one engine's spark throws light, they freeze and you see their eyes are completely skinned over.

And there are predators that hunt the sound. There are things that have learned to hunt the hunters.

The rule of this place is this: energy comes from below and from within. Not from above. Heat rises through the stone, water rises through the engines, air drips into water and is lifted again. It is a closed, round system, a terrarium the size of a country that was made to be self-tending and then forgotten. The scattered builders left their pumps on auto.

You walk, soaked to the knees, one hand out to keep fungal trunks from your face. Water laps at your calves. Then deeper. A bowl-lake, warm as blood, silts soft underfoot. You hear an engine ahead chugging, a little off-beat now — worn bearing, perhaps. You hear its condensers ticking like insects the size of cars. You hear the fauna detour around it respectfully: the click-paths parting, the chitters hushing. 

At its base, the iron is so hot the water hisses before touching it. The peat has grown away in a perfect circle, afraid. Moss has not. Black ferns have not. They lean toward the heat.

You put both hands on the riveted flank of the machine in the absolute dark and you feel the whole high marsh singing through it — the vast, loud, active, wet, breathing continent you are standing on.
