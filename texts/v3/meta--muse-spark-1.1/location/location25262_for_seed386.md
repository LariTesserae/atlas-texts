It's not a peak. Up here, extreme elevation doesn't come to a point.

The world simply stops ascending and opens out.

You are on the roof. A high, tilted table of land thrown up so far that the air should be thin and killing and cold, and for a few hours after dawn it tries to be — thin, yes — but then the day turns fully hot. Not high-desert hot. Low, wet, equatorial hot that has been lifted whole into the sky. At three, maybe four thousand meters, where there should be ice, there is steam.

This is a lost plateau, a tepui the size of a county, its edges falling away in sheer, black walls that never quite dry. Its center is not flat. It sags, like an old mattress, into a broad, open basin a few miles across. You can see the whole shape of it at once — the far rim a dark line bruised blue by distance and mist — and yet you can't see far at all, because everything is close and layered and in the way.

The rock beneath is old. That is the first rule. This place has been up here long enough to stop arguing with gravity and weather. Erosion here isn't violent. It's settled, patient, finished with drama. Water has cut not canyons but throats, throats that have become troughs, troughs that have become soft, round-bottomed gullies that wander. The stone is basalt and ironstone, rotten-red laterite under a skin of black. Where it outcrops it is pocked and fluted, not jagged. It weeps.

Because water is everywhere, but not as rivers. There is no single river large enough to need a name. Instead, the cloud hits the eastern scarp every morning and fails to get over. It condenses on contact. All day the atmosphere is wringing itself out. The air is beaded. Everything drips. The cliff faces weep in sheets without a source, rock sweating clear water that gathers on fern tips and falls again. The plateau-surface is a sponge, not ground — two feet of living mat, sphagnum and peat and black humus and tangled roots, saturated to its limit, so that each step sinks and releases a cold gulp of water that had been waiting. Shallow pans, the size of houses, hold water stained tea-brown, then clear again, then brown. Between them are marshes that are not quite land or lake, where grass grows out of standing water and the water grows out of the grass.

On that sponge grows a jungle that has forgotten it is alpine.

Lush here does not mean tall and orderly. It means layered, meanly crowded. The trees are short — fifteen, twenty feet — but they are bent and swollen and absolutely loaded. Trunks as thick as your torso and covered completely in other lives: felted moss inches thick, filmy ferns, orchids with roots like wire, bromeliads the size of bathtubs. Nothing has bare bark. The canopy is not high, but there are four or five canopies stacked on top of each other down to the ground. Hanging curtains of clubmoss separate rooms that close behind you. Light comes through white, normal daylight, high and direct at noon, but it has to fall through vapor and through leaves and through spray, so it lands diffuse and heavy and green-tinted, as if underwater. In the open pans, the sun gets through clean and makes the water steam visibly, a slow exhalation that never stops.

Nothing here is built. That is the second rule, and it is absolute. There is no cut, no stacking, no straight line, no path that doesn't close within an hour. No stone placed on another stone. The only angles are fracture angles in basalt. If a tree falls — and they do, constantly, softened through — it falls where it is and becomes a dam and a garden.

And yet it is industrial.

That took a day to understand. The first time you hear it you think it's your own blood in your ears from the altitude and the heat together. A low, sustained pressure-hum, under everything. Not animal. Not wind.

The plateau is a factory that was never manufactured.

Heat comes up from underneath, not just from the equatorial sun. The whole sagging basin is hydrothermally alive. In the marsh you will find a pool that strikes your face with heat from ten feet away. It churns without boiling, rolling black mud up from somewhere deep, mineral-rich, hissing faintly. Around its lip the iron in the water precipitates out in bright orange shelves, and the shelves grow like fast coral, wet ceramics. Elsewhere the ground clicks and sighs — pockets of steam escaping through root-mats with a soft *chuff*, like a pressure valve. The weeping walls of the gullies are stained with sulfur yellow and manganese black, runnels of natural electroplating where water separates metals and lays them down in feathers.

And the plants have become machines to handle the throughput. The giant groundsels do not grow, they extrude — their buds unfold hydraulically with an audible tick, pumping water to lift leaves the size of doors in a few hours. Pitcher plants as big as barrels distill. The bromeliads are vats, each holding a gallon or two of rain and insect and frog spawn, a little chemical works. Lianas creak under tension like cables.

And the animals — god, there are many — maintain it. Abundant doesn't mean loud. It means that every leaf you turn has something under it. Frogs the color of wet stone that you only see when they blink. Black bees that look like flying coal. Birds that don't sing so much as ratchet. Ants. Continuous, scaled, organized production-lines of ants carrying leaf confetti along highways they keep clear through the mat, not building, just wearing away. Beetles with wing cases that whine like turbines when they take off. Over there, a troupe of small, rust-colored monkeys moves without rustling, because there is no dry litter to rustle, only damp felt that absorbs all percussion. Everything moves, everything is working, but mostly quiet, as if the whole valley has been put into a sound-dampening box of moss and fog.

The weirdness is not that any one thing is impossible. It is that hot should not be high and high should not be wet and wet should not be so quiet and quiet should not be so busy. The air itself feels dense. Take a breath and you inhale water and spores and tiny flies and heat. Presence per cubic foot is excessive. Nothing is isolated. A rock is also a sponge and a garden and a pipe.

If you stand still long enough, and you will, because moving in heat at this elevation is a drugged wade, the place begins to run through its cycle around you without noticing you at all. Cloud builds on the rim, pours over, condenses, weeps out of stone, is drank by moss, is transpired out by leaves, becomes cloud again. A hummingbird, green-black, stitches the gap between two flowers, then a larger bird takes it from midair with almost no sound. In a pan, tadpoles boil. Mud rolls over in a hot pool. A column of steam rises perfectly vertical for fifty feet before the wind shears it. The mat under your boots sighs and settles a millimeter.
