It is not a valley. It is not a mountain. It is a place *inside* a mountain, so high up that there is no mountain left above it.

Near the summit ridge, where the air is too thin to carry snow and the rock is too young to have learned to be mountain-shaped yet — a black, fresh hack of basalt and slag-glass still sharp enough to cut gloves — the wind has pried open a blister. Not a cave. A blister.

From the outside it looks like nothing. A thumb-sized pit in the lee of a pressure ridge, weeping. If you didn't put your face two inches from it, you would miss it. But you put your face there, because at 6,000 meters everything weeps and nothing should.

The hole is perhaps as wide as your two cupped hands. Maybe a little wider. The rim is razor basalt, just cooled. Still warm enough that the rime that tries to form on it dies instantly into droplets. The rock around it sweats even when the air is desert-dry. Water is not supposed to be up here, not liquid. But this high basalt is still exhaling from its birth.

Inside, it is bigger than it should be.

Not huge. That's not the weirdness. It's not a cathedral in a crack. It's still intimate, still a room you could cup. But the inside surface curves away from the entrance at an angle your eye refuses to keep. It's like looking into a burr hole in the world where the depth is inverted. The back wall is somehow farther than the front rim is wide. You have to blink to see it right and when you do, it's wrong again.

Light gets in normally — high alpine noon, flat and white and hard — and then once past the lip, it gets thicker. It hangs. Dust motes in the outside air move fast and nervous. Dust motes inside drift down as if through honey. Normal light, but slowed.

The pocket itself is pristine and violent. The walls are not weathered. They are newborn. Black glass, conchoidal fractures still glossy, needle-sharp crenellations that have never been touched by wind. Under the glass, a duller, hotter mother-rock radiates heat. This whole hidden room is a lungful of hot breath in a frozen body. Outside is killing cold. In here, it is bathhouse hot, a wet, mineral heat that beads instantly on your eyebrows and your upper lip. The air is close and saturated, steaming.

Because the rock is weeping. Every tiny cusp and dagger of the fresh glass has a droplet on its tip. They don't fall. They fatten and then get sucked back into the rock, then well up again somewhere else. In a few places they run *up* the wall, joining, braiding, disappearing into a pore. The water is clear but leaves a rust-white film where it has been. You realize the rock isn't wet because water got in; water is getting out. The whole mountain's deep water table is surfacing here, at the very top, impossible.

There is nothing green. Not a lichen, not an alga, not a wind-blown seed. The heat and the young chemistry have sterilized the glass. This is a mineral world. Black, red-black, the white of salt crust, the amber of water beads. It should be dead.

It is not dead. It is packed.

The entire inner surface — every inch of that wrongly-deep pocket — is covered in a living carpet, two or three deep, bodies pressed to bodies, slick and steaming. They are small. Each is about as long as the first joint of your thumb. Pale, near-translucent, like shrimp left in a hot spring. No eyes. Their skin is wet, not with the rock's water but with their own mucus, and it makes a faint glassy sheen over the whole interior so that at first you think the rock itself is moving.

And then you see that it *is* moving, because they are. Constantly. Slowly, silently.

They are... you would call them crickets if crickets were made of uncooked noodle and had six spidery, too-long fingers instead of legs. Or baby newts. They have no faces you can read. Just a soft mouth underneath and a crown of feathery gills that pulse in the wet heat. Overwhelming in number — there must be hundreds inside a space no larger than a melon. They overlap, crawl over each other, cling to the ceiling that is not a ceiling. The density is such that there is no visible rock left except where a droplet is forming.

And they are busy.

That is the part that takes a moment longer. Because among all that silent, writhing, mass, there is a glitter that is not water.

Each, or almost each, holds something. A flake. A shard of the black glass, newly knapped. Pieces no bigger than a grain of rice, but with an edge that catches the thick light and throws it back white. The walls are sharp already, but they have been working the worst nubs down, striking with the points of their own hard, mineralized fingertips — you can hear it if you hold your breath: *tik. tik.. tik.* So quiet you thought it was the rock cooling. It isn't. It's tool-use. Primitive, compulsive tool-use. They scrape at the salt crust that forms where the water emerges. They cut free the scum of dissolved sulfur and iron that blooms on the glass. They lift it to their underside-mouths. They feed.

No nests. No walls. No built thing at all. They do not build. They only chip, and scrape, and eat, and exhale steam, and lay, in the wettest crevices, clusters of clear eggs like more water droplets that never fall. The birth rate must be furious. The death rate too — under the living layer, there is a thin, greasy paste of dissolved bodies, being scraped up and eaten by the living.

The ecology is complete and closed. Water up from deep rock. Minerals dissolved in it. Animals that mine the minerals with obsidian knives they make themselves from their own home's walls. Heat from the still-cooling mountain. No sun, no plant, no outside. A pocket furnace swamp at the top of the world.

And the physics are tired here.

If you tip your head, you can see your own eye reflected in the back wall, although you are outside. If you breathe out, your warm breath doesn't fog inside; instead, the creatures pause, all hundred of them at once, their feather-gills flaring, as if tasting a weather system. If you stay too long, the sweat on your face will start to run towards the pocket, uphill, away from your chin.

It doesn't want you. It isn't hostile. It is just deeply, quietly, occupied with itself — an active, silent, boiling handful of wet, hot, sharp-edness that managed to happen where nothing should happen, high up where the world is thin enough to tear.

Put your hand over the entrance again and it disappears. Just wet rock. Just wind.
