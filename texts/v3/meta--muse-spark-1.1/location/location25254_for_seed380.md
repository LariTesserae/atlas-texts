It is not a cave. Not really. It's too deliberate for a cave.

You go down.

The surface up there is flat, dryish field and chainlink, but the ground has subsided — a long, soft oval collapse, maybe three hundred meters across and forty deep, like a thumbprint pressed into clay a very long time ago. An old limestone working that decided to finish becoming what it was always going to be. The sides didn't shatter; they settled. They weathered. They are not cliffs now but slopes of layered things: first the overburden, then the pale stone running with dark water, then brick, then rust.

The town is down there. The whole town.

It fits in a single glance, which is the first strange thing about it, because it is unmistakably a town and not a building or a camp. A small industrial borough that got lowered intact. Three streets, not quite parallel, following the curve of the sink. Two-story brick works with arched windows blown out and replaced with corrugated sheet. A water tower that never needed to be tall because the walls are higher. A powerhouse. A row of terrace houses with their back gardens turned into canal. Gantries and covered conveyors on iron legs that step from roof to roof for no reason you can see now, their belts long gone, their frames left as streets in the air.

There is a lot of space between everything. That's the second strange thing. For an urban place it is empty in a way cities never are. You can see all the way across between buildings to the other side of the bowl. No crowding. Each structure sits with a deliberate amount of nothing around it, as if placed for drying. As if something needed airflow.

Everything is wet. Not flooded, exactly — although the lowest street never dries — but weeping.

Water comes out of the rock on all sides, all the time. Not in falls. In sweat. The limestone exhales. It beads, it gathers, it threads down in black shine, it drips from every rivet and girder and windowsill. The air itself is a cold, condensing breath. You wipe a pipe and in a minute it's pearled again. The bricks are two shades darker at the bottom than the top. The mortar grows white whiskers of calcite. The ground is not ground but a saturated felt of cinders and crushed stone with channels cut by habit. Puddles link to puddles link to a shallow, slow, tea-colored marsh at the very bottom that was once a yard and is now a reedless mere, perhaps a foot deep, filled with the reflection of iron.

And that erosion. Nothing is sharp anymore. The town has been here long enough to have become part of the weathering pattern. Brick corners are round as elbows. The iron stairs have had their diamond plate worn smooth in the middle. The water tower's paint has alligatored and then smoothed again under a skin of green-black biofilm. The power lines sag in catenaries described by a hundred years of their own weight. Even the rust feels like a geology, layered, stable. This place is not falling apart. It arrived.

Light comes from above but doesn't reach properly. The mouth of the sink is smaller than the belly, undercut, so the sky is a cool, narrowed oval, always overcast down here even when it isn't up there. A few amber sodiums still burn on the gantry legs — they've been burning for decades, maybe, on a trickle circuit — and they make long, trembling cones in the damp air without really lighting anything. On the south wall where the collapse left a notch, a shaft of pale day falls straight down and hits one wall of one building and stops. In that shaft, there is moss. In that shaft, there is a single, surprised birch, no taller than a ladder, growing out of a gutter, its leaves blanched.

Vegetation is sparse otherwise. A crust of lichen on the north stone. Beard-moss hanging from the conveyor frames like wet wool. Dark liverworts in the brick seams. The mere at the bottom has a fringe of something pale and rush-like that doesn't need light much.

But the fauna —

You hear them before you see them because the town is mostly quiet and slow.

There are no engines running, not the way engines are supposed to run. In the powerhouse, four big flywheels, each taller than a man, are still turning. Just barely. One revolution per minute, maybe. Driven by water weight? By some last pressure in some pipe? The belts creak. The dynamos hum low enough to feel in your teeth rather than hear. The whole hamlet ticks over at this patient, unambitious pace. Click... wheeze... drip.

In that quiet, the place rustles.

Pigeons — thousands, but not flocking — roost individually on every ledge, every beam, each with its own space. White and slate and rust-red, they match the biofilm. They watch. Eels, fat and black as power cables, move through the mere and through the gutters and through the flooded basements, tying all the waters together. Salamanders, translucent pink with external gills like tiny fern crowns, cling to the weeping walls by the hundreds, each one at a drip-point, drinking the rock. Moths the size of your palm, dusted the exact color of cinder, cling to the brick and only move when your breath hits them. In the powerhouse, a colony of small, soot-colored bats hangs from the rafters above the slow wheels, waking and sleeping in time with the creak.

And rats, of course, but they move like clerks, unhurried, along dry lines only they remember.

Nothing eats anything else quickly here. It's too cold for quickness. It's early November cold, the kind that lives in stone. Your exhale makes a brief cloud that is immediately taken by the general damp. The water is 50 degrees, the air 52. You can feel the cool as a weight.

The strangeness is not in any one thing but in the agreement they have all made. The pigeons don't foul the machines. The eels use the old storm drains as if they were streams they evolved for. The moss only grows on the north side of moving iron. The entire bowl seems to be breathing in slow reverse: water condenses out of the air onto stone, runs down to the mere, evaporates — no, not evaporates, is lifted — as a fine cold mist back up to the stone by the heat of the dynamos, which is no heat at all. A closed loop.

The rules here:

1. Nothing is allowed to finish. No rust-through, no final collapse, no last drip.
2. Water must always be moving downward, but so slowly that it might be moving upward.
3. If you live here, you keep a distance. One per beam. One per drip.

You stand on the second street which is not quite a street because it has a channel down its middle. Across from you, in the window of what was an office, a pigeon and a salamander look out at you at the same height. Behind you a flywheel turns a degree.

You listen for the town you thought you would hear — hammer, forge, crowd — and instead you hear this:

Drip... (pause)... tick... (pause)... wings shuffling... water finding a lower level... your own coat dripping onto cinder.
