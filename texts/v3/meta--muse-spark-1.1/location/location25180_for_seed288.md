It is a city below the sky, and it has never known rain.

You have to climb down to it. Not far - the world went down, not up. A long, shallow sink, a collapse so old that its shoulders have been worn to a grade less than a hill. The limestone gave way a few million years ago and kept giving, until it opened a bowl in the earth the size of a county. From the rim, if there were enough light, you could see the other side as a darker line against dark. You go down along switchbacks that were not cut. They were walked. By small feet. By millions of small feet over such a long time that the rock has been polished into stairs, concave in the middle of each step.

The air gets cooler as you descend. Not cold. Cool the way a cellar is cool, the way stone holds onto a temperature and never lets it go. It is dry air. That is the first thing the throat knows. There is no water here. No creek, no seep, no lake, no drip from a stalactite. The cavern - if you can call a roof so high and so complete a cavern - is sealed. Its stone was tumbled and scoured and dissolved and re-laid until it finished all the shapes water could ever make it make, and then the water left. What remains is mineral dryness: fine pale dust that never clumps, salt flowers blooming out of cracks in paper-thin sheets, gypsum beards that will crumble if you breathe too hard on them. The floor of the bowl is flat in a way no desert floor is flat. It is final-form flat. The end of erosion. A plain that has had so long to settle that every stone has found its lowest place.

And it is lush.

That should not be possible but it is, because the green here has learned to do without water as you understand it. The regrowth is not made of trees. It is made of things that drink air.

There are the hangings first - from the low, dim ceiling, and from the broken ribs of the old collapse, vast beards of lichen and air-moss, grey-green, sage-green, black-green in the gloom. They hang thirty, forty feet down, never touching the ground, swaying a little in a draft you cannot feel. Each strand is thick as a wrist, spongy, covered in fine hairs that comb humidity out of the rock's exhale. Where they brush each other they fuse and thicken into curtains, into galleries. 

Below them, on the flat, is the forest that is not a forest. Pillars of fungus, but not the soft, wet rot-fungus you know. These are hard and dry and woody, building themselves out of the dust, out of chitin and cellulose and time. The trunks are as big around as houses, twenty, thirty feet tall, capped with flat, shelf-like plates that overlap each other for acres. Under each plate is warm darkness and spore. The trunks are not smooth; they are fluted and pitted and full of windows. Every inch of them is grown.

Because this is an urban place. It is a city. There has never been a tool here and no hand was ever needed to make it.

The builders are still here, which is why you can't hear yourself think.

It hits you about a third of the way down. A low, rising burr that you feel in your molars before you hear it in your ears. Then as the slope levels and the hanging gardens thin enough to see the plain, the sound becomes total. Deafening. Not a single sound, but a wall of it, a chorus of stridulation so loud the dust shivers on the ground.

The fauna are everywhere, and they are small and numberless. 

They look at a distance like ants, like woodlice. Up close they are something else - pale, blind, long-bodied things with lacquered backs, each the length of your forearm. They move so slowly you can barely see the motion. A leg lifts, holds, sets. A mandible scrapes a grain of gypsum dust and cements it with dark spit. Then again. They have been doing this for longer than your species has had a name.

Each trunk is a high-rise. Not drilled out - grown around. The insects farm the fungus as the fungus farms the insects. The fungus puts up a wall; the insects chew a tunnel through it, line it with paste, lay eggs in the wall; the fungus grows around the eggs to seal them in. Generations compress. The air inside smells sweet and sour, like dry hay. In the larger trunks you can see avenues that have remained open for a thousand years, their floors worn concave by thousands of overlapping, patient journeys. Balconies on the outside where the shelf-plates meet, where the elder broods sit and stridulate to make that endless, rattling roar. Every one of them is singing. It is not communication, not anymore. It is how they breathe. It is how they say *we are still here*.

Between the towers there is space. That is the sparse part. For all its size, the city is mostly emptiness - long, salt-white plazas of compacted dust between fungal trunks, where nothing grows by mutual agreement, where the wind has been kept out for so long that a single tiny feather could sit there for a century and not move. You could walk for half an hour between neighborhoods and hear nothing change except the chorus shifting behind you, in front of you, from one side to the other as different clusters rise. The light never changes.

Light comes from the fungus itself, and from the lichen curtains. No sun. No lamps. A dim, cool phosphorescence like foxfire caught in the flesh of the plants - a greenish, tired light that is just bright enough to see the architecture by. It doesn't cast shadows. The air glows. Near the ground, where the builders have spread their waste, the glow is brighter, a faint carpet that shows every footfall you have not yet made, and the footfalls of millions before.

Nothing happens quickly here. A plate grows a millimeter in a year. A tower will lean an inch in a lifetime. A female will lay her eggs and guard them until they hatch eight seasons later. The song has probably been exactly this loud for a hundred thousand years, rising and falling only with the humidity, which itself rises and falls on a cycle of decades as the rock above inhales and exhales with some surface summer you will never see.

There are rules here, though no one wrote them.

First: nothing is wasted. There is no water to wash anything away, so every corpse, every molt, every spore that doesn't take, is eaten, or built into the wall.

Second: what is built stays built. The erosion has stopped for everything except intent. The city is its own geography now. It holds up its own roof, holds in its own air.

Third: you whisper even though whispering does no good. The roar swallows everything, and in the dim, in the cool, under the miles-wide weight of the earth, under the slow, old towers made of spit and time and hunger, to whisper feels correct.

Far toward the center, you think you can see a larger darkness - a trunk so wide its far side is lost in the hanging mosses, a trunk that must be a neighborhood, a downtown, a capital, built and lived in and rebuilt continuously since the Pliocene. You start walking toward it, slowly, because the dust gives a little and you have learned from everything around you to move slowly.
