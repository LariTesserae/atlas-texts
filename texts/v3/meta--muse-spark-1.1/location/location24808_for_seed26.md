It is below.

Not deep in the romantic way of caverns, but below in the way a basement is below. You go down in structures made for going down. A switchback service road of poured concrete that never sees sun, still bearing the grain of the wooden forms it was cast against. An elevator shaft big enough for trucks that has never had a mirror or music. A stairwell that sweats.

The whole valley is down here. That's the first thing you have to understand to see it right.

It is not a cave that someone built in. There is no cave. There is no rock. Someone decided there should be a low place, a wide, shallow bowl a mile across and three hundred feet deep, and then they *made* a low place. Lined it. Everything you can touch is made. The floor is slab, not bed. The slab was poured in sections the size of city blocks, and you can see the seams, and where those seams have cracked from the cold. In fill. Under the slab is another slab. The walls that hold the earth back are stepped back in enormous terraces, each one twenty feet high, raw concrete the color of wet newspaper, with tie-holes still open, weeping water like eyes. No one ever took the rebar caps off. No one ever smoothed the pour lines. That's how you know this place is young. Its edges are still sharp enough to cut a glove. The corners where terrace meets terrace have not gone soft. The steel is not yet rusted through.

It is cold down here in a way that is a system, not a weather. Your breath never stops making a small cloud. The air is just above freezing, always, because the machines want it that way and because the earth overhead wants it that way and the two wants have reached an agreement. A cold that lives in the floor and comes up through your boots.

And it is wet. Not flooded. Wet the way a laundry room is wet, or a throat. The atmosphere is overfull. The exhaust of the low engines hits the cold concrete and gives up. Water beads on every underside, on every pipe and joist and light housing. It gathers and runs and drips. The terraces never dry. There is a continuous black shining on them, a film of water moving sideways as much as down, finding the tie-holes and pouring out of them in little clear springs that should not be there. You hear it everywhere before you see it: ticking, plinking, a scatter of drops on tin, on slab, on water.

Because there is water on the floor too. Not deep. A sheet, an inch or two, that never drains quite right. It covers the lower slab from one end of the made-valley to the other, not as a lake but as a presence, broken by islands. It reflects the low lights dully. Everywhere you walk, your boots make a V.

The lights are sparse and industrial and never off. Sodium heads on bent stalks, every hundred yards, caged in rustproof wire. They buzz. They throw a dim amber that doesn't reach far before the damp air eats it. Most of the space between them is just gloom and moving water and concrete geometry receding. You can see all the way across to the other terraces — this is an open place, a town-sized place — but you lose detail after the second or third bank of lights. That sparseness is part of the architecture. Things here are far apart on purpose.

Up near the top ring, where the service road enters, there are manufactories built directly into the terrace face. Shed after shed of corrugated steel, all new, all loud. They are not making anything you can name. They take in water and cold air and power from the cables that run in proper, labeled troughs along every wall, and they give back more cold air and more water and a low, bone-conducted roar. Inside their open roller doors you see identical engine blocks, ten to a shed, turning flywheels the size of houses. Not for transport. For circulation. For keeping the air wet and moving. Condensers. Heat pumps running backwards. Their belts slap. Their generators whine. The sound stacks up in the bowl and has nowhere to go. It hits the far wall and comes back slightly late, so there is always a double beat. You have to raise your voice without meaning to.

Between the sheds and the water, on the flat margins that are almost dry, there is growth. Sparse, because nothing was invited. Black mold in the joints. A stubborn, pale lichen that likes concrete. And in pans where someone once set up silt traps, reed-mace, cattails — the kind that grow in ditches. They are stunted by the cold, only knee-high, their brown heads not quite ripe. They shiver in the artificial wind thrown off the flywheels. The seeds never seem to land anywhere else. This is their whole marsh, a few acres of incidental marsh in a concrete basin.

Animal life keeps its distance. That's a rule here, almost written. A single gull you could swear you saw once, very high, in the dark above the top lip where the real world drips in. Less rare are the small, practical things that came down with the workers and stayed after: a rat that has learned to drink the drips off a tie-hole, its whiskers beaded with water. Midges that breed in the sheet-water and die quickly in the cold. A pigeon that nests in the I-beam of a gantry crane that has no load to lift. There is so much empty space between them that you notice each one.

And the crane is worth noticing. Nothing here is to human proportion except the doors into the sheds. Everything else was built for a larger logic. The gantry runs on rails the width of a road, spanning a quarter of the basin, but its hook hangs low and empty, dripping. Under it, the water sheet ripples on its own, because down here the water cycle is short and fast and strange. It evaporates off the warm backs of the engines, fogs out into the cold, condenses on the ceiling of the world — which is the underside of the next slab up, the lid of this whole place, fifty feet over your head, a flat, endless, made sky stenciled with old chalk marks from the surveyors — and then it rains. Not like outside rain, not weather from the horizon. Straight down. Droplets forming on concrete dimples and failing. A constant, quieter rain under the permanent, dimmer dark, feeding the sheet on the floor that feeds the engines that make more rain.

If you stand in the middle and turn off your lamp, the amber fades and you are left with the sound of it all working. Drip from the ceiling-sky. Trickle in the walls. Slap of belt, whine of flywheel, spreading wash of water under wind. Your own boots in it. Cold in your teeth. You can understand that this place was finished very recently and then left running, and that it will need a long time before anything about it becomes soft, or natural, or quiet.
