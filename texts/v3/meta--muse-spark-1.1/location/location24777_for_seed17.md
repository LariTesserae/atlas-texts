It is an ocean on top of the world.

Not metaphorically. The world ended and a new one started on top of it — a basalt continent thrust so high and so fast that the sea did not have time to leave. That is what elevation means here. You are two miles down but also two miles up. The datum below is dead rock. The datum you are in is water so vast it has its own weather, held in place by a bowl of young mountains that have never learned to be round.

This ocean is young. That is the wrong word for water, but the right word for everything it touches. The seafloor — if you can call it a floor and not a freshly cooled forge-table — is not sand and drift and ooze. It is black glass. Volcanic glass that shattered when the cold hit it. When the continent tore itself apart and bled out new crust yesterday in geologic time, the water quenched it. The result is a landscape of knives. Ridges 800 meters tall with edges you could cut your gloves on. Chimney spires that never had time to build sulfide cathedrals, so they are just straight spears of pyrrotite. Graben valleys that run in perfect, fresh lines for hundreds of kilometers, their walls still terraced with cooling steps. No beach. No silt. No wearing down. The water is trying to erode it, but it hasn't had time to learn how.

The water itself is impossible. It is cold — one degree above freezing at all depths — and it is EMPTY. At 2.7 water, you expect gloom, soup, opacity, the weight of life. This ocean is almost distilled. Near void-clear. No plankton bloom, no marine snow, no suspended grit, because there are no rivers to feed it and no soil to wash in and nothing to die at scale. Light — flat, white, midday normal light at 2.0 — falls through it like it's thin air. You can see three, four kilometers down-current until the blue just runs out of blue to give. It makes the scale obscene. 2.9 vast. You look out and you think the water ends close by. It doesn't. That ridge you think is a hill twenty meters away is a wall ten kilometers long and half a kilometer high. Your brain fails parallax. There is too little in the water to give it distance.

And there is almost nothing per volume. Density 0.1. You can drift for an hour and see not a single particle. Not a fleck. Then the fauna comes.

Vegetation is 0.2 — which here means zero. No kelp. No algae crust. No sea-grass. The glass is naked. Black eating white light. What thrives instead is mineral logic and animal.

They are abundant at 1.5 because they don't need plants. They need the machines.

The built is 0.6/3 — scattered. The tech is 2.0/3 — industrial. Someone found this perched ocean when it was still steaming and understood what it was: a planet-sized geothermal battery, fresh cracked open. So they dropped factories.

They are not cities. They are single buildings, unmanned. Each is maybe the size of a cathedral, anchored by four harpoon cables into the glass, floating a hundred meters above the floor so they don't get entombed when the ridge spreads another inch. They are unpainted steel gone orange-black with cold and bacterial rust. They do not have windows. They have intakes and they have STACKS, and from the stacks pours not smoke but superheated, mineral-saturated water. You see them first by the shimmer.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, into the blue distance, as far as the vanishing light allows — each alone, each humming, each spaced so far that no two share the same valley. No cables between them. No roads. They do not need to talk to one another. They are all doing the same job: drinking the cold void through one end and exhaling a black, roaring jet of 400-degree water out the other. They are not drilling. They are farming the thermal gradient itself.

That is the sound. Sound: 2.9 — deafening.

You would think water muffles. It does not here. It conducts. In air, the plant 2km away would be a hum. Here, the water carries its full spectrum and delivers it into your bones. Each factory has a low industrial thrum — turbines bigger than houses turning over — and a high scream where the superheated jet hits the 1-degree ocean and collapses, cavitates, shrieks. The implosion bubbles make a continuous white crackle like gravel in a cement mixer the size of a moon. Because the water is so empty, there is nothing to damp it. Because the valleys are so sharp and young, the sound reflects without losing edge. It comes at you from every direction, even when the plant is behind a ridge. You feel it in your molars. You cannot hear yourself think for more than a blink, and then you stop trying.

And yet — dynamic: 0.4 — never changing.

That's the weirdness, 0.9, the part your mind snags on.

The jets do not sputter. The turbines do not rev. The animals do not flee. The water does not warm.

It is in equilibrium. A frozen equilibrium. The cold ocean is so huge it can swallow any amount of heat the factories pour into it and instantly return to one degree above zero. The sound is deafening but it is the SAME deafening roar it was a hundred years ago and will be a thousand years from now. If you drop a stone, the glass pings and the ping goes on and on, reflecting, never quite decaying. You swear you can still hear a bolt you dropped an hour ago bouncing in the distance.

There is no current. There are tidal forces but the bowl is so vast they cancel. No seasons. No storms, not in water — the surface, kilometers above you, is locked in ice, a white lid that lets the normal light through but no wind. So you are submerged under a sky that is ice. A high-altitude, luminous, acoustic ceiling.

The fauna are fitted to that stillness.

They are pale, almost translucent — because why make pigment where there is no cover — and mineralized. Not fish as you know them. More like swarms of blank, industrial shrimp the size of your arm, their carapaces clouded with iron oxide, gathering in clouds of ten thousand around each stack to feed on the chemosynthetic bacteria that bloom only in the exact mixing zone of hot and cold. The bacteria make no mats, so there is no vegetation; they exist only as a rust-colored blush in the roiling water, there and then gone. The shrimp harvest it mid-water, never landing.

And the bigger things — the things that make the empty feel empty because they crisscross so much nothing — are huge, slow, neutrally buoyant ribbons. Thirty meters of segmented white tube with a mouth like a factory intake. They don't hunt the shrimp. They filter the water itself, taking the faint chemical whisper of hydrogen sulfide that escapes the plumes. They move without moving their bodies much; jetting is too costly here. They just hang in the void, ciliate cilia rippling, being cleaned by the shrimp in turn.

They are not afraid. Nothing has ever changed enough for fear to have a purpose.

If you stay and watch, you understand the rule of this place: Preservation through indifference.

The mountain grows one micron a year. The factories roar at exactly one volume. The water stays cold. The animals circle the roar and have always circled. You are the only thing that expects variation, and so you are the only thing that suffers from the noise. Your bubbles rise to the ice-roof and get trapped there, making small, sharp pinging noises as they strike the underside of the lid and never pop.

From far off, through the deafening, unchanging blue, another factory comes on. Or it was always on and you only just resolved it against the void. Light falls straight down, clean and normal, through water that shouldn't exist this high, onto rock that shouldn't be this sharp, onto engines that should have rusted out and didn't.
