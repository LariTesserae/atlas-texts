It is a low place. Not low in the way a valley is low between mountains, but low the way a floor is low beneath a ceiling. A made low.

The whole of it is built. There is no exposed ground here. No bedrock to touch, no dirt that isn't mortar-dust. Everything you can see and step on was carried and placed by hands.

You are standing in a basin the size of a county, shallow-walled, open to a dark sky that does not give light. The basin is terraced and subdivided and re-subdivided down to its bottom, which you cannot quite see from its rim. It is a town that is also a single building. A hive of cisterns.

The material is almost everywhere the same: a cool, blue-black stone, rough-split, still sharp at its edges because it hasn't been weathered enough to soften. It was quarried somewhere above and lowered down here, block after block, in primitive fashion — you can see the rope grooves burned into the capstones, the iron Lewis holes where they were lifted, the chisel chatter still bright on the faces. The mortar between them is not yet cured properly. It's damp and pale. The builders here work only with weight and friction and water.

And water is everywhere without being anywhere. This place sweats.

There hasn't been rain, not today. There is never direct rain this deep in the darkness. But the air is cool — cool enough to make your breath visible, cool enough that the stone never warms — and all that cool, dark air moves. It condenses. Every horizontal surface beads. Every vertical face weeps a thin sheet that finds the joint below it. Every underhang gathers a belly of water until it lets go.

*Drip. Drip. Drip.*

Only it's not that quiet. Not with this much surface.

This is a loud place.

The basin is a drum. A hundred thousand drips strike stone at once and their sound doesn't die, it collects. In the upper terraces, where the construction is still embryonic and raw, it is a constant ticking, like a hall of clocks. Lower down, where the water has had time to gather itself into runs and threads, it becomes a rush and a clatter, water finding the spillways cut for it — shallow V's chiseled by hand into each terrace lip. Water leaps one level to the next, slapping stone. Somewhere in the middle distance, a full temporary cascade has found a fault in the work and pours as a white curtain three or four terraces high, hammering the basins below into froth. The sound of it carries across the whole open bowl, bouncing between the close-packed walls.

Because density is the rule here.

Nothing is free-standing. The cisterns, the pens, the empty reservoirs, the houses that are not houses but dry-storage vaults, the stairwells that turn back on themselves — they are all built shoulder to shoulder, wall sharing wall, with alleys between them no wider than a person turned sideways. From above it looks like a honeycomb that forgot to be regular. From inside, you walk a labyrinth where your elbows brush both sides and the stone above your head is another person's floor, slick with condensation.

There are no plants. Not a weed in a crack, not a lichen. Everything mineral. The only color is the darker green-black where the stone is wettest, and the rust-red of the iron tools left leaning against walls. Some of the lower basins have a faint fur, a mineral scum or a cold-water bacterial mat, gray and trembling, but nothing that counts as vegetation. It is a world made before soil.

And yet it is active, terribly active. This is not a ruin. An unfinished thing.

You hear them before you see them. The makers. The maintenance. In the dark — and it is dark, truly dark, the light is only what leaks from fissures in the roof of the world far, far above, a slate-gray starless dusk that never gets brighter — you hear soft bare feet on wet stone, the grunt as a block is nudged by wooden levers, the dull *tunk* of a maul on a chisel. They are always adjusting grade, always cutting a new channel because the water has decided to go elsewhere. Primitive work. No cranes, no engines. Just rope and counterweight and many hands.

It feels strange because the place is too new to have been worn into sense. Erosion hasn't had its smoothing turn. Every channel is still angular, every pile of tailings is still conical and sharp. Water hasn't learned the basin yet. It overshoots. It pools where it was meant to run. One terrace will be dry on one side and ankle-deep on the other. And when it pools, it holds for a while in perfect, black, still mirrors that reflect nothing because there is no light, only the dark vaulted overhead and the close, wet, looming walls.

In those mirrors, rarely, something moves. The fauna is rare and shy and adapted to this total stone. A pale amphibian, nearly transparent, its heart visible through its side, slipping from one basin to another. A congregation of blind, white woodlice the size of your thumb, clustered under a lifted slab, processing the mineral fur. They scatter when your light — if you have any — touches them.

The rule of this world is simple: everything that stays up must be held up by something else that was made. Nothing rests on natural ground. If you were to pull one keystone wall near the rim, the failure would propagate down and down in a slow, wet, grinding landslide of perfect, hand-cut blocks, filling the basins one after another, until the bottom — which no one has reached in a long time — was choked flat. So they are always building. A tower of maintenance in a place of permanent damp.

You keep descending. The air gets cooler, louder. The condensation thickens until it is almost a mist, until the stone under your hands is not damp but running. The sound of the high cascade fades and is replaced by a deeper, wider sound, as if many basins below you are all at their brim and spilling at once, a low chord of water hitting water.

The basin goes on. It is valley-wide, town-wide, but still toothed with the texture of individual blocks, each one still showing the mark of the hand that placed it.
