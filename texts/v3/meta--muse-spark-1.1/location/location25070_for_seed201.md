It's not a valley. It's not big enough to be a valley.

It's a bite taken out of the mountain near the top, where the air has already given up. You're at about five thousand meters, maybe more — high enough that the sky isn't blue anymore, it's gone to a thin, hard indigo that hurts if you look at it too long, and the sun is not warm, it's bright. That is the first rule here: light is not heat.

You can see the whole place at once, from one edge to the other, like a room. A single, high-walled, wind-carved bowl, maybe ninety meters long and half that wide, open at its lower end to nothing but falling. Three walls of it are new. That's the second rule — this place has not been here long. 

The rock is still angry from being made. Black basalt and ash-gray tuff, blocky and fissured in clean, vertical lines, unsoftened. No talus slope yet, no grinding down. The scars where frost has popped plates off are still light-colored and sharp enough to cut your gloves. The edge of the bowl is a knife. You came up over it on your belly. Behind you, the mountain falls away in one long, unbroken, glittering drop into empty atmosphere. Ahead, the bowl tilts slightly inward, making a palm.

And the palm is holding something that should not be here.

Vegetation. Lush in a way that makes no sense for this cold — this deep, honest,  -20, -30 cold that sits in your lungs — but there it is because the bowl is a trap.

The sun, that brutal, bright, high-elevation sun with no filter left, beats straight down into the black rock all day. The black rock takes it. At night everything flash-freezes again to a nail-hard frost. Every morning at dawn, that frost goes directly from ice to vapor and then back, weeping. Condensation. Not rain. Never rain. The entire hydrology of the bowl is breath.

The walls exhale in the morning. You can watch it. A film of rime suddenly goes silver and trickles, not flows, not streams — a dampness. It beads on the rock, darkens it, and vanishes into moss.

Because the rock is carpeted. Not forests. Not trees. There's not enough time or dirt or heat for that, and the scale is wrong. This is small-plant lush. An alpine cushion-bog, but dense as a mattress. Mosses so deep and green-black they look like spilled ink. Crust lichens blazing orange and bone white. And everywhere the tight, domed cushions of androsace and saxifrage, hugging the stone as if holding on against the void, flowering despite themselves with tiny, impossible stars of pink and white only a centimeter wide. When the sun hits them at noon, they steam.

It is empty. That is the strangest part. You can see everything and there is almost nothing in it. One small place per many cubic meters of air. A stone. A cushion of moss. A long space. Another stone. The world here has a low density; matter has not been filled in yet. It is hollow, and the hollow is filled with light.

Then something moves, and you remember the abundance.

The moss quivers, and it's not the wind — there is almost no wind down in the bowl, only a thin, high jet hissing over the rim, making the prayer flags crack — it's life. First the insects. In the hour of most brightness, the surface of the cushions boils with them. Black alpine bumblebees the size of your thumbnail, furred against the cold, moving slowly, drunkenly, buzzing at a frequency so low you feel it more than hear it. Midges in a column that hangs in the sun like dust. Flightless beetles like polished beads.

And where there are insects, there are birds. Small, fat snowfinches and rosy-finches in flocks of twenty or thirty, that live up here permanently, their blood different. They don't sing. In this thin air you can't afford to sing. They make single, glassy *pink* notes and land and hop and stab at the moss. They are utterly indifferent to you. Their chicks are in burrows under the moss, peeping silently with open mouths.

Under every mat, pikas. You hear them before you see them — not calls, but the softest rustle, the cacheing. They have built haypiles of dried saxifrage stems everywhere, tiny perfect haystacks, against the future. A fox has been here too. Or a snow leopard. You find the scat once, frozen, full of feathers and white hair. You don't see the owner. It is elsewhere, but it uses this as a pantry.

The process is frantic, compressed. This is a place that only lives for five hours a day. Dynamic, in a fast, panting way. Freeze at night. Thaw at 9am. Insects from 11am to 2pm. Birds feeding. Flowers opening toward the light with audible pops. Water creeping by molecule from rock to moss to air to rock again. Then freeze at 4pm when the shadow of the west wall cuts the bowl like a blade. All of it quick, then stop. Quick, then stop.

The built things are few because nothing lasts up here and there is little to build with, and because the scale of human attention here is brief, visiting.

Three low, dry-stone windbreaks, C-shaped, back to the prevailing wind, made of the same black basalt but lichen-patched, so older than the fresh scarps. Primitive, hand-built. Inside, flat stones that might be seats or altars. A heap in the center that was an attempted hearth, but wood doesn't grow here — what burned was maybe yak dung hauled up from far below. Around it, small things tucked into cracks with a care that makes you lower your voice: a stack of three slate beads, a wooden butter churn stave, still greasy, carved with a simple spiral. A cairn with a piece of blue plastic caught under it — a single new prayer flag, long since bleached white by the 2.9 sun, barely fluttering.

Someone comes here, or came here. Not to live. To mark that life persists at the edge.

Or maybe the weirdness: stand at the bottom of the bowl at noon and look up. Because the air is so cold and so still inside the rim, and the sun is so intense on the black rock, there is a temperature inversion a meter above your head. You can see it shimmer. And in it, the light breaks. The empty air refracts little brief, low, horizontal rainbows that appear and vanish with no water, no mist — just cold doing something to light that it shouldn't know how to do. They hover at ankle height, at eye height. When a flock of finches flies through one, their shadows hit the moss with prism edges.

The silence is complete enough to hurt. The hiss over the rim. The click of basalt contracting. The almost inaudible scrape of a bumblebee's feet on a flower.

You feel, standing there, that you are inside something very young that is teaching itself what living is, using spare parts. Black rock, white sun, green fur, a breath of water that never gets to be a river.
