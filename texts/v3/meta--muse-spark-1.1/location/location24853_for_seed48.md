It is low country. Not low in the way of hills rolling down to meet it — it just never was anything else. A flat so wide the surveyors gave up contour lines.

The town — city — sits at zero, or close enough that zero has to be dredged and defended. You can tell how long it's been here by how soft everything is. Not ruined; settled. This is the 2.2 erosion place, the kind of industrial age that has gotten tired of being new. Every concrete edge has lost its corner. Every steel I-beam wears a skin of rust as thick and established as bark would be somewhere else. The canals were cut straight a hundred years ago and now they meander within their concrete jackets, undercutting them. Nothing has collapsed. It has just all agreed to be rounded.

It's hot. The kind of hot that starts before dawn. 38C air, more than that off the tin and the blacktop, and wet — not flooded, but wet the way a towel left out never quite dries. The groundwater is six inches down. You dig a heel in and it fills. The city weeps.

There is no vegetation. Not by ordinance, by failure. The ground is a cap: poured slab, slag, salt-pounded aggregate, cinder, more slab poured over the failure of the last. In the low places the water sits in sheets and you can see the sky reflected in it, but there's no green in the reflection. No reeds, no algae mat thick enough to call a plant. Where life tries it, the runoff kills it — oil, brine, lye from the soap works. On paper this is urban. From a plane you would see the grid and the bigboxes and the refinery squares and the rail fan still gleaming where wheels polish it. On foot, it feels empty.

That's the density trick. The city was built for a lot more mass than is here now. The lots are huge, the factories are huge, the sheds could house zeppelins. Streets are four lanes wide for trucks that come twice a day. Between any two things is a hundred meters of nothing — cracked concrete, heat shimmer, a chainlink fence with no sign on it. The buildings themselves are mostly hollow. You open a service door and there is a volume of dark air the size of a cathedral with one pump working in the middle of it, ticking over. It's not abandoned. It's just sparse. An urbanism of voids.

The light is dim despite the heat because the sky never clears. Low cloud holds down low smoke. The sun is a bright place behind steam. Mid-day looks like late afternoon in November, except the sweat. All color is cut by half — grays and soaked browns and rust reds and the silver-black of wet concrete. The lights in the factories and the pump houses burn all day long, bare bulbs and sodium, buzzing.

And then there is the animal.

You don't notice it first because of the quiet. Sound is kept down here — no wind to speak of, sound swallowed by wet air and by all that empty space. The machines are industrial but old and well-latched; they hum, they don't clang. The big diesel lift-pump in Ward Four makes a noise like a man breathing through his nose while he sleeps. The condensers drip. Once in a while a gull complains.

Then you see that every surface that isn't in direct use is moving.

Fauna: 2.6/3 is not a figure of speech. Crabs first. Half the city is tidal crab, the flat white kind with one oversized claw, and the small brown mud-crab. They cover the canal banks in such numbers that from a distance you think the slope is gravel and then it resolves into legs. They live in the weep holes of the retaining walls, in the expansion joints. They are over the roads at low water, thousands, shoulder to shoulder, moving sideways with purpose, quiet because chitin on wet concrete is quiet.

Over them, the birds that have learned not to call. Gulls, terns, boat-tailed grackles, night herons — but sparse in the way predators are sparse when prey is endless. They stand on the rooftops in rows, facing the wind that isn't there. They watch.

In the buildings, in the airspace between joists, in the high hollows where the hot air stratifies, bats and swifts in numbers that make dusk flicker even in the dim, pouring out of the busted clerestory windows of the machine shop in a river. On the ground floors, rats on the same plan as the crabs, using the roads at night as highways. They don't squeal. In this heat, nothing wastes moisture on noise.

Insects do the rest. Not as individuals but as weather. Midges in pillars over every standing water, cockroaches the length of your thumb that farm the damp under the pallets. The dumpsters have their own gravity.

There is no plant life to mediate between them, so the food chain is short and industrialized. Detritus goes to crab and roach, crab and roach to rat and gull, gull to nothing but age. The pumps bring in nutrient from the estuary twice a day and dump it over the town as wet air. Everything condenses and feeds something.

The rule of the place is keep the water moving or become water. That is the only civic law still enforced. The pumps can't stop. If they do, the low city that has waited so patiently to be marsh again will become it in a week. You hear them at night when everything else is at its quietest — not loud, but everywhere. A low chatter of bearings and the chuck of water into water, out in the dark between buildings. The men who tend them live on the second floors of buildings whose first floors are already given over to crabs. They leave their boots outside and the crabs clean them.

If you walk long enough, past the roller doors all open for ventilation, past the canal where the water is the color of cold tea and hot, past the yard where they stack empty chemical totes ten stories high, you get to the edge where the concrete simply stops being maintained and turns into flat, wet mineral mud that still has tire tracks coagulated in it. Beyond that, the estuary, brown, hot as bathwater, meeting a sky the same color, with no line between. 

It is a big place. Big enough that you can walk all day and still be in it, in that valley that isn't a valley, in that town that has more air in it than building. Big enough for millions of small lives to transact all their business without ever needing to be loud about it.
