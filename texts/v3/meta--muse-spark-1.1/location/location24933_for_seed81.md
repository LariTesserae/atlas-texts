It is high. Not a peak, but a plain that got lifted wrong.

A basalt tableland, or the filled caldera of something so old its rim has weathered down to a soft, black horizon-line in all directions. The air should be thin here, but it isn't — it's heavy. Hot. The kind of hot that comes from below, not above. The rock underfoot holds heat like a stove eye left on.

You are standing in a city that was built for the wrong density.

This is urban by every measure of intention. Streets laid on a grid that follows the curve of the old crater. Blocks. Avenues that run for kilometers, straight as poured. Infrastructure stacked on infrastructure — aqueducts over aqueducts, pipe run on scaffold over older pipe run fossilized inside mineral. Factories the size of cathedrals, and then cathedrals of factories, their roofs gone or never built, their I-beams extending up into dim air. 

But there is nothing in it. That's the first false note. The ratio is off. The space between column and column is too vast. The streets are too wide. The doorways lead into halls so big any human figure in them would be a speck. It wasn't built for a crowd. Or it was built for a crowd that was mostly air. The city is empty in the way a lung is empty — a structure defined by the volume it holds, not the matter.

And what it holds is water, and heat.

The light is low. Permanent low. The sun — somewhere above the haze — is a cold coin you never see directly. What light there is is filtered through a miles-thick condensation layer that never quite becomes cloud, never quite rains. The whole tableland exhales. You can see it from any high scaffold: the entire bowl of the city gently smoking.

Because of the heat. Low, industrial, everywhere. Not fire. Geothermal. The rock sweats. The concrete sweats. The old basalt weeps.

This is the wet. Not a river — though there was a river once, you can see its engineered channel still cutting the city diagonal — but a wetness that is atmospheric, architectural, lithic. The stone is always damp to the touch and warm. Water beads on the underside of every pipe, every girder, every lip of weathered brick and never falls, or falls far too slowly. It gathers in sheets on vertical surfaces. On the leeward side of the cooling towers — there are dozens, maybe hundreds, all cold now, all still dripping — black water runs continuously down in a skin, without source. The air itself condenses on your skin. Your shoulders are wet within minutes. Your boots are wet but you have not stepped in a puddle. The ground is dry-ish porous tufa and scoria that drinks everything and then lets it out again somewhere else, as a seep, a wall-spring, a groan.

There is no green.

Not no trees, no grass — no green at all. No lichen, no moss, even though this should be perfect for it. No mold that you can see. The mineral has won. Or made something else. The surfaces that look from a distance like moss are iron bacteria, rust colonies, sulfur crusts, a thin, oily bacterial felt that grows on the wet concrete and is dark red, ochre, white. In the gutters there are mats of something that pulses faintly, but it is not plant. The city was built out of the rock to farm the heat, and the heat sterilized it before anything green could learn the new rules.

And yet it works. That is the industrial part. That is the active part.

The factories are not dead. They are idle. There's a difference. You can hear it.

The place is mostly quiet — that 2AM quiet of big structures settling, ticking with heat. A drip every three seconds that echoes for twenty. The long sigh of wind across the rim, miles away, that arrives as a change in pressure rather than a sound. But under it, always, a low operational thrum. Pumps. Somewhere below street level — below even the old service tunnels — deep pumps still move water from hot to cold. Geothermal engines that don't need tending turn condensation turbines that have turned for a hundred years. The aqueducts gurgle and swallow. A relief valve hisses open for a long minute, three blocks over, spraying a plume of clean steam thirty meters up that hangs, heavy, and then precipitates sideways as hot rain onto a single intersection. Then silence again.

The buildings have weathered in place, not ruined, but resolved. Edges rounded by mineral deposit. Rebar exposed and then sealed by calcite flow. Corners where twenty years of dripping have built stalactites of Portland cement a foot long. The city has reached a settlement with erosion. Water decided how it wants to move through here, and the city has allowed it. Channels cut through plazas. Basements have become cisterns. Upper floors have become humid caves.

That's the strangeness that is higher than the rest. Not that a hot, empty, high, wet industrial city exists. But that it breathes backward.

The condensation doesn't fall down so much as form in midair and migrate. In the largest open volumes — inside the great turbine halls where the air is clearest — you can watch it. Droplets nucleate out of nothing, hang, join into little free-floating lakes a meter across, wobbling, reflecting the dim sodium glow of a pilot light that never goes out, then drift toward the closest cold surface, as if attracted. The water knows where to go. Sometimes, when two of these floating lenses pass through each other, they make a tone.

You see one animal. In a whole day.

Something like a heron but made of folded paper, or like folded paper made to stalk fish. It is white against the black rock, impossibly tall and thin, legs too long for any bird. It stands in the shallow warm overflow of a settling pool where the red bacterial mats are thickest, utterly still. It doesn't hunt. It just is there, drinking heat by some other process. When you get too close it unfolds — not flies — unfolds into the empty air and is gone between two pipes without a sound, leaving a quick puff of steam where it was.

You realize then the rule of the place: this was never built for people, or not only for people. The scale of the doors, the emptiness, the way water moves without needing ground, the way heat is kept and husbanded without ever being used to make anything now — the city is a machine for making a specific kind of weather. A pocket of hot, dim, wet, mineral atmosphere on top of a cold mountain world. It makes itself. It maintains itself. The factories aren't factories. They are lungs, and dehumidifiers, and sweating walls.

And you are just condensation, briefly here, beading on its skin before running down to join whatever comes next.
