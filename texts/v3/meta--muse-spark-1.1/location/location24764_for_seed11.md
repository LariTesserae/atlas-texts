On the Roof, the mountains are already dead.

That is the first thing you understand. Not dead like killed, but dead like finished. This range went up when the world was young and hot and has been sitting here ever since, getting smoothed. The elevation is still extreme — you are four kilometers into the sky, higher than weather should be — but there is no peak to it anymore. No teeth, no cliffs. Just a single, planetary-scale swell of stone that has been left out in the wind so long it has lost all its edges. 

Ancient basalt and gneiss, but weathered into something that doesn't look like rock. It's soft. Rounded into great sleeping backs and domes the size of towns, dimpled and pitted like old bone, cut through with slots and flutes and holes where the wind has been playing the same note for a million years. From a distance it looks almost flat. Up close you realize the ground is falling away from you in every direction, a bald curve into cloud.

There is not much water. That is the second thing. No lakes, no rivers that keep their name for more than a morning. The air at this height can't hold a river.

What there is, is damp. The whole highland is a condensation trap. The warm air that climbs the southern scarp hits the cold lid of this place and tears. All afternoon the clouds come — not as rain, not really, but as a sudden, blind white that rolls up and over the world-rim and condenses on everything. You can wring water out of the moss. You can watch it bead on the black stone and run in nervous, temporary fingers until it finds a crack and vanishes. In the deeper wind-flutes, where the stone has been undercut into long, north-running troughs, there are chain pools, brown and still, each one holding the sky for a day before it soaks away.

And yet it is lush. Impossibly so.

Because life here learned to drink the cloud directly.

In the hollows where soil has finally, after eons, managed to collect — a thin, dark, peaty skin — the vegetation is frantic. Not tall. Tall can't survive the sound. But dense as a drowned carpet. Giant rosette plants with waxy leaves as big as shields, each one a cistern holding two liters of trapped fog. Ferns that grow as thick as a man's thigh, but horizontal, crawling along the rock rather than up. Beards of gray-green lichen that hang three meters long from the windward lips and thrash like prayer flags. Everywhere a deep, springy mattress of moss that when you step on it sighs and exhales water under your boot. It is all warm to the touch. The stone holds the sun's heat, the air is warm, the moss steams gently when the cloud lifts.

And it is almost all plant. The density rating of this world is wrong. For a place so lush, it is empty. There is a kilometer between stands of the shield-plants. A hundred meters between anything taller than your ankle. The landscape is open in a way lowlands never are. You can see for a whole valley — a slow, ancient bowl of stone three kilometers across — and see nothing in it but rock and moss and one lonely green island of growth. No people. No herds. No insects in clouds. You walk and your own presence feels statistically impossible.

What little fauna there is, is rare and large and quiet, and you only see it as a rustle in the moss that is not wind. The white root-mice that live their whole lives inside a single rosette plant. The gliders — flat, manta-like things the size of a blanket that ride the updraft off the scarp and never land, feeding on the airborne plankton of spores. They don't call.

Because nothing can be heard over the Sound.

The Roof is deafening. The ancient erosion didn't just smooth the rock, it tuned it. Those flutes and tubes and keyholes are hundreds of feet deep, some of them, and when the trade wind hits the scarp — which is always — the entire plateau becomes an organ. It's not wind noise. It's a chord. A low, immense, ribcage-rattling howl that comes up through your soles, plus a hundred overtones screaming through the slots at different pitches. At noon when the heating is strongest, it is a physical assault, a white roar that makes you grit your teeth and your vision shimmer. You have to open your mouth to equalize. The plants have all grown low and aerodynamic because anything that sticks up gets shredded acoustically as much as physically. The moss vibrates.

And this is why the place is turbulent despite being so old and so empty. The sound is not steady. It never settles. Clouds rip over the lip at sixty kilometers an hour and the organ changes key. Shadow moves a handspan and a new pipe starts singing. The light is normal, a flat highland white sun, but it strobes as cloud tears. The mat of vegetation is never still — it shudders and ripples and lies flat and springs back, an ocean of green in a gale that never stops, hyperactive, frantic. Condensation forms and is ripped away before it can drip. The gliders tumble. A sheet of moss the size of a house, undermined by water, will suddenly delaminate and take flight like a sail and be gone over the edge in seconds.

Everything here is stable and everything is about to change.

The built things lean into that truth.

Scattered. You find one every half-day of walking if you know how to look for where a person would look for shelter from a noise that can crack rock. A low ring of stones, dry-stacked, no mortar, barely knee-high, but on the lee side the moss grows thick and the wind cannot find you. Inside, a hearth-blackened slab. A cache pit lined with the big shield-leaves, still holding a few shriveled tubers. A cairn of balanced, wind-bored stones that marks a water crack.

Higher up, where two great flutes cross and make a momentary quiet node, someone has stretched a frame of cured, black wood — not from here, carried up — and lashed it with cord made from lichen fiber. A windbreak and a listening post. From it hangs a string of little stone whistles, each carved by hand to match a different pipe-tone, primitive tech, ground with sand and a harder stone. When the wind blows through them, you can, for a second, hear the mountain not screaming at you, but with you. An attempt at translation, or appeasement.

No villages. No roads. The people who come here are occasional, solitary. They come up for the cistern-plants, for the tubers that only grow in this thin air, for the silence you can only find inside such deafening sound. They do not stay. The Roof does not allow staying. It is too old to notice you and too turbulent to keep you.

When the cloud comes — and it comes every twenty minutes, rolling up over the world-edge like a cold, white avalanche — the entire open valley disappears. You are left in a five-meter sphere of damp green and screaming stone. Water runs down your face, not raining, just arriving. The moss at your feet glows with sudden wet. The wind drops for one breath, the organ holds a high, impossible note —

— and then the cloud breaks and the scale returns and the sound crashes back and you are alone again on the ancient, warm back of the world.
