It is low the way the end of the world is low.

Not low like a valley. There is nothing higher to be low between. This place has been eroding since before there were mountains anywhere else, and now there is just the final grade left - the peneplain that underlies all other landscapes. Basement rock laid bare and worn down to a tolerance of inches. On a map with no contours. On a planet that has forgotten how to lift itself up.

You can't see curvature because the air is too thick to see far, but you feel it - a plain so vast it generates its own weather, its own pressure system, its own dim, amber-gray light. The sky never clears. It's not cloudy, exactly. The air itself is wet. Warm saturated air, body-temperature warm, holding more water than air should be able to hold, right at the point of condensing. So it does. Constantly.

This is the sound that breaks you first.

It is deafening here.

It is not the roar of a waterfall or the crash of surf. It's the sound of everything, everywhere, making its tiny water-sound at the same time. The air condenses on itself with a faint tick - trillions of ticks per second - and falls a foot, or an inch, to land. Everywhere, the rock is weeping. The rock is not porous; it is ancient shield granite and ironband, polished by eons to a greasy sheen, and water beads on it and slides. It weeps out of the horizontal joints, it sweats out of the stone. A low plain that produces water out of atmosphere and out of stone, a planet sweating.

And under that hiss is the other sound: the rock itself. This ancient final plain is not still. It is packed. 

That is the contradiction you feel in your molars before your eyes understand it. There is no room here. The plain is tiled, edge-to-edge, horizon to horizon, with plates. Not tectonic plates - flagstones. Lily-pads of stone, each the size of a table, a house, a field, fitted together with no gap for soil. A tessellation of dark, warm, wet rock that goes on without end. They are the erosion product, the last thing left when everything else has washed away: iron-hard scales.

And they are moving.

The plain is ancient, yes, settled into its final form, but final does not mean dead. Because it is so flat and so wet and so warm, there is no friction to hold it. The plates jostle. They lift a centimeter on hydrostatic pressure and sink. They click against each other. They ride over each other with a long, drawn shriek of wet stone on stone, a sound like continents being shuffled. All that packed density, all that mass in motion at once:

click - hiss - shriek - click - hiss - shriek

in rolling waves that travel for miles in the stone. A slow, terrible, ant-hill energy. You stand on one plate and watch the plate ten feet away rise slightly, spilling its skin of water, and then settle. The pattern never repeats. Weather moves across the plain not just in the air but in the ground.

Water: it lives in a film. Never more than ankle-deep, mostly shin-deep in the low joints, never less than a wet sheen that mirrors everything. You cannot step without splashing. You cannot stay dry. It is warm as blood and smells of rock and time. Where two plates meet, the water runs faster, little braided streams inches wide that are somehow loud as rivers. Where a plate has tilted, there is a shallow pan that holds sky.

There is almost nothing alive to drink it.

Vegetation is a mistake that the plain keeps correcting. In the seams between plates where a little silt has gathered - black, ground-rock silt that took a million years to accumulate - a reed grows. One reed. Then another, a hundred yards away. They are not green so much as dark rusty red, salt-stunted, tough as wire, with roots that grip the crack and try to hold a plate still. Sometimes you see a low bush, a mangrove-like thing with stilt roots kneeled over water, holding open a small darkness beneath it. They are all far apart. The plain will not allow a forest. When plates shift, they shear the roots. The bush dies and becomes a slick of fiber that is quickly pulped by warm water.

Animals are rarer still. You hear something large splash once a day and never see it. When you do, it's wrong with distance. A white bird that is actually three miles away but looks close because there is no perspective left. Or a crab-like thing, translucent, flat as the plain, that lives in the water-film and is itself a kind of moving plate, edged with cilia. It passes over your foot and you feel the plain's density through it - no room between presences, even living ones fitted into the cracks.

And there are structures.

Not built by people, not really. No tech, no tool marks. Scattered once every few miles, you find cairns that were never piled. A small hut-shape of plates that have leaned against each other in a temporary equilibrium, making a three-sided hollow just large enough to crouch in. Inside the hollow the air is quieter, and the rock is dry, and you find marks: a spiral scratched with another stone, a line of five dots of darker iron. Someone else tried to mark this place that cannot be marked. The water erases it within weeks, but someone always makes another.

Because the weirdness here is not an aesthetic. It is structural.

The light is dim - a sun that is just a warmer patch in the haze at zenith - but reflections are not dim. The water-film on each plate is a perfect mirror when it is still for half a second between shifts. It does not reflect the sky. Or rather, it reflects a sky that isn't this one. Some pans hold stars at midday. Some hold a second, fainter plain above the first, with its own scattered reeds. Some hold you from behind, looking down at yourself. If you stand still long enough - and it is hard to stand still while the universe clicks and hisses around you - you see your own heat shimmer making duplicates.

Distance breaks. You walk toward a bush and it walks away, maintaining scale. You turn around and your footprints in the water-film are already gone, but a new set leads away at an angle you didn't take. Sound arrives before the event that caused it. The plates are so flat that Euclidean geometry becomes a suggestion; parallel seams meet far away.

At the scale of continents, physics gets tired and improvises.

At night - though day never got bright - the condensation intensifies. The air rains upward as much as down. Warm droplets form on your eyelashes, your elbows, and fall up to join the cloud that lives on the ground. The clicking of the plain becomes louder, because cooler water makes the plates contract a millimeter. The deafening becomes a chorus, a bazaar of stone merchants shouting in a language of friction.

And through it all, the plain is making and unmaking itself. A plate rises, dries in the wind, cracks calmly down the middle. Two plates fuse under pressure into one. A new pan opens and immediately fills with warm, sky-reflecting, wrong-sky-reflecting water. The ancient, final, near-flattened world is hyperactive with old age, like an old animal kicking in its sleep.

You learn to move with it, not across it. To step when the plate beneath you sighs. To not shelter in a hut of plates because it will collapse and become something else. To not look too long into any one mirror of water, because the version of you in there is still standing still, and is getting concerned.

It is very warm. It is very wet. It is very, very loud and very full and very flat and goes on forever.
