You are very high up.

Not high like a mountain has a top — high like the world never finished below you. The plateau you are on is still being pushed up. It hasn't learned to be a plateau yet. It's a blister.

The air is warm. Not hot like noon, but warm like skin held close in the dark. A mammalian, close, exhaled warmth, though nothing here lives to exhale it. The warmth comes up through your boots. Through the soles, through the basalt itself. The rock is the heater.

It is dark. Not absolute. If it were absolute you wouldn't see the edge of anything. This is the darkness of a deep overcast just after sunset, when the light has technically left but the world is still holding its shape from memory. The sky is not sky but a lid — low, heavy, a uniform bruise-purple-black with no stars. The light you have is from the ground. Veins of it. A few hundred meters out, and a kilometer out, and at your feet.

The ground is new.

That is the first rule here: nothing is old enough to be round. Erosion hasn't happened yet. There has been no rain to soften anything, no river, no wind long enough — and in fact there is no water at all anywhere in this place to do the work of erosion. The rock is all edge and snap. 

You are standing on a crinkled sheet of black glass. Pahoehoe that froze mid-roll. It still holds the rope texture, glossy in places, matte in others where ash settled. Everywhere it is cracked into sharp, upturned plates, like a frozen mudflat scaled up to the size of a valley. When you take a step, the plates flex. Somewhere under you, they tick.

And then the plateau breaks itself open again.

That is the second rule: stillness is temporary. This is turbulent terrain not because the wind is fast — the wind is barely there — but because the ground itself cannot decide to stay still. Every few minutes something gives. A seam a hundred meters long will sigh open with a clean, glassy *chink* — no explosion, just a fissure unzipping — and from it a dull orange light wells up from below, lighting the undersides of the fumes. The warmth spikes. The air shivers. You can see dust motes that have been sitting for hours suddenly rise and fall in the convective lift. Then the light dims, the rock congeals, and another fresh knife-edge ridge is left jutting an inch or two higher than everything else.

No water. The air tells you that before your eyes do. Your lips are dry within minutes. There is no smell of wet mineral, no salt marsh, no snow. It is the smell of hot iron and old kiln. If you put your hand to a fresh crack, the heat rolls over it dry as paper. Any water that was ever here boiled out long ago, or never condensed.

Because there is no water, there is nothing that needs water. No scrub. No lichen even. No green smear in a crack. The only color that isn't black or orange is a pale, sickly yellow-white crust that forms around some of the cooler vents — sulfur and salt minerals sublimating out of the rock and frosting the edges. From a distance they look almost like lichen. Up close they are dry crystals, needle-fine, that shatter if your shadow falls over them.

It is open. For all that you are on top of a monstrous elevation — air so thin you feel your own pulse in your ears, every breath a little too shallow — you can see a long way. The blister-plateau you are on runs away in all directions until the dark swallows its details, maybe three, maybe five kilometers. It is not flat. It is humped, and fissured, and studded.

The studs are what you mistook for buildings at first.

Scattered across the plain, maybe one every two hundred meters, are chimneys. Not made. Grown. When the gas inside the rising rock found a pinhole, it built these things as it escaped — narrow towers of welded spatter and mineral lace, hollow, some as tall as a person, some as tall as a house. They lean. Some have collapsed into a ring of daggers. Some are still growing: you can hear them hissing faintly, almost below hearing, and see the cherry-red tip pulsing as a new bubble of basalt pops and adds another inch of height. They are sparse enough that you can walk a long way with nothing between you and the dark, and then come upon one and feel like you have come upon a hut, or a shrine.

They are empty inside. You look in one. The interior glows.

There is no other life. No insect strays here. No bird crosses that lightless lid of sky. The only movement is mineral. Even your own presence feels intrusive — a soft, water-filled thing in a place whose chemistry has no slot for water at all.

It is mostly quiet. That is what makes the sudden changes feel personal. For ten minutes at a stretch, the only sound is a low membrane hum of heat, and the thin blood in your ears because of the altitude, and the occasional tinkle of a sulfur needle collapsing under its own weight. Then a section of crust somewhere behind you will contract and make a sound like a rifle shot, and a puff of black glass will hop an inch off the ground and resettle. Then quiet again.

If you stay long enough, you notice the oddness that is not in any one thing but in how they add up: how can it be so warm this high and this dark? How can the ground be so sharp and still so soft underfoot? How can something that looks so much like a ruined village have been made with no hands at all, with no plan except gas finding the easiest way out?

The air gets warmer. Another seam is opening, twenty meters to your left. The light wells up, dim, steady, orange, throwing the shadows of the nearest towers long and thin across the glass. It shows you — briefly — that the plateau you are on is not the top. Farther on, beyond the haze, there is something higher still, still forming, a raw black spine pushing up into the dark lid of sky, un-eroded, unsmoothed, still wet with its own becoming.
