It is a room that decided to be a forest before it ever learned what a forest was.

You come down into it — not far, the land here is low — through a cut door in the outer weave where the wall has been forced open with a hand-axe. The air on the other side is warmer by a full breath. Not hot. Warm the way the inside of a bread oven is warm hours after the fire is out. Dry, still, held.

This is the whole place. From wall to wall you could throw a stone and hit wicker, but you would not throw a stone because there is no room to throw. It is hamlet-sized, or cathedral-sized, which is the same kind of size: a single interior with boundaries you can always see. A low bowl, perhaps forty strides across, and nowhere in it higher than twice a person. Low. Close.

And everything in it is made.

Not made *on* — made *of*. There is no ground beneath the ground. The floor is not earth. It is wattling, tight-coiled, the base of the basket. You can see where the withes were bent and bound when they were still green, now old-yellow and hard as bone. They are laid in concentric rings, each ring stitched through the last with blackened twine and hammered copper staples, simple work, primitive work, fingers and awls and patience. Over that, a second floor has been laid, and a third: mats of beaten reed felt, then lattices of split cane, then more mats. The walls climb the same way, curving in without ever becoming roof — they just lean and weave and lean until they lean on each other mid-air. The whole bowl never tore, never finished. You can still see the fresh, sharp cusps where a stave was snapped off and left, its fibers fraying white. Young, this place. Newly made and then abandoned right after. No wear at the corners, only knife-sharp edges that have never been sanded by wind.

And it is lush.

That is the wrong word until you touch it. There is no water here. Not a trickle, not a spring, not even the ghost of damp under the mats. The air is mineral dry, salt-pan dry. Your lips split if you stay too long. The basket itself sweats no moisture. And yet from every seam, every hole in the weave, every staple-point, vegetation pushes.

It is not green the way a jungle is green. It is green the way felt is green, or lichen after a year with no rain. Dry. Dense. Thirstless. It grows not *with* water but *instead* of water.

The dominant form is a kind of ladder-moss, though it's not moss. Foot-long fronds of layered paper, sage and dusty olive, that unfold in tight rosettes directly out of the wicker. They are not soft. They rustle like old books. If you break one, it doesn't bleed sap — it sighs out a puff of warm, cinnamon-smelling spores and reveals a pith of airy white fiber, dry as loft insulation, laid in perfect hexagons. It grows so close you cannot put your palm flat anywhere without crushing three rosettes at once. It grows on the floor, the walls, it hangs from the pseudo-ceiling in beards and curtains. In places it has matted itself into trunks, false boles as thick as a thigh, made entirely of its own pressed, interwoven dead. These trunks are not rooted. They are nailed to the basket by their own growth.

Between the rosettes, finer things: black wirevine, a hair-thin creeper that clicks as it lengthens, its segments jointed like an insect's leg, with no leaves at all, only tiny amber bells that never open. And the lamp-blooms — round, waxy, pale yellow fruit the size of a child's fist, growing directly from the basket's knots, which at midday flush with light from inside, though normal light filters from somewhere above. They are warm to the touch, each one like a small sun that forgot to burn.

Abundant life, as promised by all that cover. But nothing drinks.

The beetles come first. They are flat, lacquered, the color of the copper staples, and they live inside the paper rosettes, chewing slow tunnels. You hear them only if you hold your breath: a faint *chit chit chit*, like an old clock eating. When they move, they leave a contrail of fine dust that hangs in the warm air for minutes.

Then the hoppers — pale, almost transparent things with hind legs too long, that do not jump through space so much as they *reconsider* their position and are then elsewhere in the foliage, a handspan over. Their wings are dry leaves themselves.

And the little lizards, dozens of them, the size of your thumb, skin the exact same beaten-reed color as the mats, with eyes like polished seeds. They do not lick moisture. They open their throats to the air and you can see the spore-dust swirling into them. They are fat. Sluggish. They watch you without blinking and then all tilt their heads the same direction at once, even when you made no sound.

Because it is mostly quiet. This is a quiet place to an aggressive degree. The weave drinks sound. Your footsteps become little thumps, then nothing. The bearded fronds overhead do not sway; there is no breeze. The loudest thing is your own blood. Then, if you stand still long enough — truly still, the way the lizards are still — you start to hear the slow things.

The basket easing.
The plants adding one more paper cell with an audible, reluctant *tick*.
The lamp-blooms rotating a degree.

The dynamic here is patient to the point of insult. Growth is measured in weeks per millimeter. The wirevine might need a whole afternoon to encircle your ankle if you let it. Time is not frozen; it just considers rushing vulgar.

And then the wrongness.

You notice it first in the corners, because even though this is a bowl, it has corners. Where two wall-weaves meet, the angle doesn't quite add up. If you line up your eye with one seam and walk to the opposite wall, you arrive back at the first wall too soon, or the seam is now on your left when it should have been right. The room is denser than its outside. There is more surface in here than a hamlet's-worth of wicker should contain.

If you stare at a patch of rosettes and then look away quickly and look back, there are more of them. Not grown — just *more*, as if they were waiting for you to not be counting.

Light falls normally, warm and diffused from the open top, but shadows do not. The shadow of one of the false trunks breaks halfway down and continues two feet to the left of where it should, as clean as if cut with scissors. If you place your hand in that dislocated shadow, your hand gets cooler.

And gravity. Gravity here has been persuaded, not enforced. Dust does not settle straight down; it drifts on a very slight curve, all of it, as if the bottom of the bowl were two feet to the north of where your feet say it is. The beetles walk just fine on walls that should be vertical. The hoppers, when they reconsider their position, sometimes reconsider upside down and cling to the underside of a leaf for a while, unconcerned.

The rule, if there is a rule, is this: this place was built by hands that wanted a garden but did not know that gardens need rain, or soil, or level ground, or straight space. So they made a garden out of the only things they had — wicker and will and patience — and they made it so completely that the world relented and let it live anyway, but on a different contract.

No water will ever come here. If you poured a cup, it would vanish before it hit the weave — not evaporate, just edit itself out.

No plant here will ever die of thirst. They are not thirsty.

Nothing here will finish growing. It will just become more crowded, more layered, more itself, until the quiet is so thick with leaves and beetles and misplaced shadows that the small bowl holds a whole, dense, warm, dry forest that never needed a planet underneath it at all.

You will have to cut your way back out the way you came. The wirevine will already have stitched shut the little door you opened.
