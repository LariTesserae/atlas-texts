It is below.

Not just underwater, but *under* — below the water column, below the water table, below any idea of ground. There is no surface here anymore, or if there is, it is a rumor hundreds of meters straight up, a white press of light that never arrives.

You are inside a room.

The room is the whole world. That is the scale of it — not an ocean, not a city, but a single, vaulted volume, maybe thirty meters long, maybe less. A cathedral for a machine. Everything is built. There is no bottom, in the geological sense. No sand, no silt bed, no bedrock. The floor is steel grating and then another floor, and then poured concrete that has gone soft at the edges. The walls are riveted iron plates. The ceiling is the same, low, bowed inward under centuries of weight. If there ever was a natural substrate around this place, it has been replaced, tiled over, concreted over, welded over. The world here is manufactured down to the last bolt.

And it has been drowned completely.

The water fills it absolutely, cold and heavy and clear in the way deep water is clear — a cold that gets into the joints of your jaw even if you are not breathing it. It's the kind of cool that feels deliberate, preservative. This is an old place. You can tell by how water has been allowed to take its time with it.

Nothing here is sharp anymore. This is what ancient means underwater. The erosion isn't violent, it is patient. Every edge has been lapped down. The stair-rail that runs the length of the room, the steel lip of a catwalk, the toothed wheels of a control valve — all of it is blunted, swollen, simplified. Rust has grown over rust until it has become a new geology, thick as bark, dark red-black and pitted with the mouths of a million small things. The concrete corners have dissolved to a smooth radius, like river stone. The bolts are blind domes. The whole room has settled into its final shape. It is not collapsing anymore. It finished collapsing a long time ago and decided to stay like this.

It is dim. The light that exists is not sunlight. High on one wall, behind glass as thick as your hand that has gone cloudy, two emergency cage lamps still burn. They were never meant to burn this long. They run amber, weak, underwater, fizzing. They throw a cone perhaps two meters into the water and then the water eats it. Everything beyond their reach is blue-black, lit only by the disturbed motes. When a fish passes through the beam, it throws a huge, fast shadow.

And it is loud. That is the wrongness you notice after the cold. The deep sea should be silent, but this room is industrial and it is still working.

Somewhere behind the wall, a pump is still running. It has been running for longer than anyone has been alive to hear it. It doesn't pump this room dry — it never will again — it just cycles, groans, turns over. You feel it in your sternum, a low, uneven thud: *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK... thrum...* The entire iron envelope shivers sympathetically. Water inside the room moves with it. And then there are the secondary sounds: the tick of cooling and contracting metal that never actually cools, the ping of a chain, long detached, swinging in a slow current and tapping the catwalk. The hiss of water forcing itself through a valve that is almost, but not quite, shut.

The current is the weather here. Cold water pushes in from a grated intake on the east wall that is mostly choked with growth, pushes across the room, and exits through a lower outflow on the west, where the grating is gone and there is just a dark square mouth. It is a slow, constant river through a single room.

Because of that current, there is life. Abundant, busy, utterly normal deep-water life, filling the emptiness exactly as much as emptiness allows.

There is not much plant. No kelp forest could root here; there is nothing to root in. Only a sparse, meager vegetation — thin green-black films of algae on the lights themselves, drinking that dim amber, and in the corners where the current is weakest, tufts of something hair-like that wave. That's it. The room is too made to be green.

But animals — the animals have come in force. They love made things.

Mussels have paved the lower three feet of every surface, closed tight, black shells layered on ancient black shells. Barnacles like white teeth on the pipes. A cloud of thumb-sized shrimp hangs in the middle of the room, always in the same place, kiting in the drift. Larger things go around them: sluggish cusk, blind-white, bumping along the railings; crabs the size of dinner plates picking their way across the grating, claws loud against steel; an eel, as thick as your arm, that lives in the pump housing, quick as oil when it darts.

There are hundreds more you don't see at first, until your eyes adjust to the dim. Anemones open and closed on every bolt-head. Worms building brittle tubes along the seams of the walls.

The room is mostly empty space. That's part of it. The machines were removed, or dissolved — you see their ghosts: four massive circular mounts on the floor where turbines used to sit, each one now a nesting pit for fish. A control pulpit, tipped sideways, its levers all smoothed into nubs. Long stretches where there is nothing but water and rust and that throbbing sound.

The rules of this place are simple:

1. Everything that is here was brought here or built here.
2. Everything that was built here is now older than any person who would know how to use it.
3. Water gets in, but work continues anyway.

If you stay still, the shrimp cloud will forget you. The pump will go *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK*. The cold will hold you, perfectly, at the edge of preservation.
