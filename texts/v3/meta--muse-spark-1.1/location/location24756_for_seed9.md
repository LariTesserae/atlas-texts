It is not a mountain anymore.

It was, but the top was taken off.

What is here now is a bowl set into the high air — a single perfect room the size of a small hamlet, a cathedral without a roof, cut into the sky. You can walk its entire circumference in ten minutes if you don't stop, which you will. The walls are its boundary, poured concrete, eight meters high and stained the color of old teeth. Beyond them there is nothing but cloud. Below them, cloud. The world dropped away while this place was being made.

Everything here is made. That is the first rule. There is no bedrock, no soil that wasn't carried up, no stone that wasn't cast. The floor is not ground. It is floor — slab, then slab under that, then ironwork. Even the slope is intentional: the whole bowl tilts very slightly inward, a Palm of God that would take a hundred years of rain to measure.

And it does rain. Constantly, but not from above.

Warm air comes up the mountain all day. It hits the cold iron of these high walls and gives up. Water beads on every surface. The atmosphere itself condenses. The concrete weeps. Rivets weep. The undersides of leaves gather a drop, hold it, let it fall. You are wet within minutes here, not soaked, but misted, as if you've walked through a just-watered greenhouse. Which you have.

The terraces are what it was built for. They step down in tight rings, three, four, five levels deep toward the center, each one a narrow industrial garden bed made of riveted steel troughs and brick, all of it humming. Not loud. The hum is underneath hearing — pumps in the walls, slow industrial hearts. Black rubber hoses fat as your wrist run along every lip, pierced every foot to let water sigh out. The beds themselves are dense beyond sense. 

Someone engineered this place to be lush. No, over-lush. Lush like a mistake.

Taro and rice in standing water on the second ring, their roots rattling against the metal bottoms. Ferns growing out of the motor housings. On the third ring, tomatoes staked with rebar, vines already escaping their ties, leaves big as dinner plates, fruit splitting open from too much water, too much growth in the warm air. The heat is wrong for this elevation. At this height the air should be thin and cutting. Instead it is warm, bathhouse warm, held by the walls and by the waste-heat of the machinery and by the glass panels that some prior hand hung on hinged arms above the beds, not a roof but a suggestion of one. They are all fogged.

You can smell the iron, the hot mineral tang of fresh concrete still curing, the green rot of leaf-matter mulching back into imported soil. Everything is near-touching. The beans climb the pump housing. The pump housing sweats onto the beans.

Because it is all so new. That is the strangeness you see in second glance. For a place so thick and growing, nothing is settled. This place is embryonic.

The concrete still shows the grain of the wooden forms it was poured in. Where water has run for a few months, it is only just beginning to mark — dark dendritic stains spreading down the walls like first attempts at rivers. No real erosion yet. No gullies, no smoothed edges. Instead, a raw, construction-site geology. A pile of fines and aggregate in the corner where a trough was overfilled. A rebar end still bright and unrusted where it sticks out of the eastern wall like a root that hasn't decided what to be. The bolts have no rust. The brass valves are still machined sharp.

It is a world under construction that is already being devoured by what it was constructed to grow.

At the very center, at the lowest point where all the weeping and seeping and hose-water gathers, is the engine. Or the cistern — they are the same thing here. A low, round, riveted building the size of a house, half sunk into the floor. From it comes that mostly-quiet: a low thud thud thud, unhurried, more felt in the soles of your feet than heard, and the tick of cooling metal and the drip from a hundred leaves. No birdcall. Almost no animals at all. You're high enough that nothing much lives up here uninvited. Maybe a single dragonfly, lost, hunting gnats over the rice water. Maybe the distant, above-cloud glint of one of the big high lammergeiers circling the bowl's walls without ever crossing them, as if it knows this is not a real meadow.

Inside the cistern building — you can look through the open service door — the water is black and deep and moves. Chains move through it slowly on sprockets, pulling up clots of compost, algae, duckweed that have been harvested by another chain. There is nowhere for waste to go, so it is made to go in circles. Everything feeds everything else. The water feeds the plants, the plants clog the filters, the chain drags the clog back into the water.

The light here is ordinary, mid-afternoon light even when it's morning. No dramatic beam, no gloom. The sky is a flat white plate directly overhead. The walls cut the horizon off. That makes the place feel smaller and far larger at once.

The rules are simple and you learn them quickly with your body:

Stay on the catwalks between troughs — the soil is not for standing, it's too wet and will remember your foot.
Don't turn any valve — they all do something and they all are already open the right amount.
Don't harvest too much — the system is running so close to overflow that abundance itself is a kind of load-bearing structure.

If you stand still long enough, water that condensed on your hair will run down your face like tears. The place builds itself around you, leaf by leaf, stain by stain. If you left it for a year, the vines would pry the glass off its hinges, the roots would jack the terraces apart. If you left it for a hundred years, it might begin to erode for real, might become a hill again. For now, it is new, wet, warm, breathing hard in the thin air, a small, totally made garden trying with all its pumps and green mouths to decide if it is a farm or a swamp.
