It is not so much a valley as a cup left behind.

High up where the air goes thin and cool, the plateau breaks. The basalt is young here — not the soft, worn-down teeth of old mountains but a fresh fracture, a fault-block that dropped last week in geological time. Its edges are still sharp. You can see where it sheared. The walls of the bowl are black glass and columnar steps, still holding the chisel marks of whatever lifted them, unsoftened by wind. Nothing has had time to round them. It is maybe three hundred paces across, rim to rim. You could throw a stone from one side and have it land in the other's moss.

That is the scale of the whole world here: one room, with a roof of low cloud.

The light never quite arrives. You are above the cloud deck but below the second one — caught in the condensation layer. The sun is a cooler, whiter idea behind felted gray. Everything is dim, not dark, but like late evening that goes on for days. The kind of light where colors deepen instead of washing out. The walls sweat.

Water lives here as a habit, not a feature. There is no river. There is no lake. In the cool there is breath. Fog rolls over the rim at intervals and pours down the sharp walls and beads, and condenses, and finds its way down in black threads. It drips. Every surface has a second surface made of water. In the evenings — if you can call it evening — it rains for eleven minutes, straight down, cold and vertical, and then stops as if a tap were shut. The ground remembers it. Shallow basins in the basalt, pecked out by hand, hold it for a day.

And it is lush. Absurdly, impossibly lush for how sparse it is.

Because the bowl floor should be bare rock. Instead, there is moss, but not moss as you know it. Here it grows in pillars. In towers. Each colony rises from a crack like a small, soft tree, a meter high, two meters, trunks of dense felted green and rust-red, caps that unfurl into broad, damp fronds that catch the mist. They do not touch. That is the rule here: nothing touches. Each moss-tree stands alone in its own circle of black gravel, twenty feet from its neighbor, precise as if planted. Between them the rock is naked and clean. The effect from the rim is of a sparse orchard, an orchid-garden, a colonnade holding up nothing.

And in that orchard is the town.

Built, yes. Urban in intent if not in sprawl. There are twelve, maybe fifteen structures, which is many for a place you can cross in two minutes. They are organized — you can see the plan in it immediately. Pathways of flat stones set with care between the moss-towers, drainage runnels chipped alongside, low walls that terrace the slight slope toward the center. The houses themselves are made of what is at hand and what can be made without fire hot enough to melt stone: dry-stacked basalt shard and woven living moss. They cut the moss while it is alive and bend it. It continues to grow, so a wall is also a garden. Roofs are stretched hides, or more often, a lattice of thin bone struts thatched with lichen shingles that quiver and drink fog. There are workshops with stone looms, smoke racks with no smoke, communal cisterns for that eleven-minute rain. It is a city in miniature, complete with infrastructure, with zoning, with a small silent plaza in the center where the largest moss-tree has been hollowed while still living.

The people are sparse too. You might see two, or none, moving between towers with their shoulders hunched against the cool. They wear felted moss-fiber, heavy with water. Their tech is hands and patience. And they are not alone in this town. Not even close.

The fauna is the thing. The overwhelming thing.

They are everywhere and they are quiet.

Not loud birds. Not howling things. The bowl hums at the very edge of hearing — a wingbeat, a brush. The air is thick with moths the size of your palm, and moths the size of dust. White, and gray, and a green that matches the towers exactly. They hatch from the moss. They live in the moss. Trillions, maybe, in this one small bowl, cycling through their instars in days. They do not call. Their wings are felted to silence. They cling to every surface in such numbers that at first you took their pattern for bark, for stone.

And beneath them, things that eat them.

On the basalt, beetles with long, glassy legs like black ice, picking their way between the gravel with absolute deliberation, each step placed an inch from the last one's. In the cisterns, newts translucent as the water, with gills like ferns, opening and closing. On the moss-towers themselves, sloth-slow climbers — you think at first they are part of the house — that are six-limbed and covered in symbiotic moss of their own, so that building, tree, and animal blur until you cannot tell where craft ends and creature begins. They browse. They prune. The people let them.

The strangest are the listeners.

They hang from the underside of the moss caps, in the darkest, dimmest pocket under each frond. They look like empty, wet leather pouches, veined with pale light, no larger than a fist. When the fog pours in they swell. They fill with condensation until they are heavy, taut as bladders. And then, impossibly slowly, they wring themselves out. A single clear drop falls from each one, down onto the root mat below. It is how the towers get water that the rock will not give them. The pouches are animals. They drink cloud and urinate for trees. The people collect the overflow in cups and drink it themselves.

Everything is moving, despite the silence. The moss-towers grow visibly, a fraction of a millimeter a day, pushing their stone chips aside. The moths emerge in a constant snow of wings, are eaten, lay, die. The walls of the bowl are still settling, and sometimes a sharp *tick* goes through the whole cup as a new cooling crack opens. A beetle will freeze. A person will stop on the stone path and place a hand on the ground and wait, listening to the mountain finish its thought.

That is the rule of this place: first there was the sharp, fresh, empty cup. Then came the damp, the cool dim that never leaves. Then life came too fast, too much, too quietly, and learned to stack itself. Now there is a town inside a garden inside a snarl of wings, all in a place no larger than a cathedral nave, at the top of the world. It doesn't echo. Sound gets swallowed by all that soft, wet green. Even your breath goes quiet here.
