It's lower than it should be.

You notice that first, before the heat hits you. The horizon doesn't break. It just goes. In every direction, a straight line where pale, dusty sky meets pale, dusty land, and the line trembles a little with heat. There is no up. You are standing on the floor of something continental, a basin so wide that it makes its own haze. The maps would call it lowland, 0.8/3 — less than a hundred meters above a sea that is either very far away or no longer there. The air stacks up here and gets thick and hot.

2.7/3 hot, but not a desert hot. It is a kiln-hot, a greenhouse hot, a wet low hot that catches in your throat at noon. The kind of heat that makes things grow whether they want to or not.

Because the ground is not ground.

That's the second thing. You kneel and the whole logic of landscape comes apart.

There is no rock here. No sand in the mineral sense. No native soil. What you are standing on is made. Entirely. To the horizon and down as far as you can dig with your hands, everything is constructed, woven, layered, pressed. 

It is root. It is rhizome and mat and felted fiber and dead fiber and living fiber, in a slab.

A long time ago — or continuously, now — sparse things grew here and put down roots not into earth but into each other. A woody shrub with iron-hard black branches about as tall as you, every half kilometer or so, maybe a kilometer, throws out laterals that run for hundreds of meters just under the surface. Where they meet the laterals of the next shrub, and the next, they graft, they knot, they fuse. On top of that, a thin skin of liverwort and crusted lichen and a low succulent creeper lays itself like a scab and glues it all down with exudate. Under that, the dead layers: generations of the same mat, compressed to a corky, peaty wood-concrete, warm to the touch, slightly sprung, like standing on a book with a million pages.

You walk for hours and it is all under you. A planet-sized raft. No substrate. If you peel it, it peels in sheets. The sheets smell like hot paper and tannin and fresh-cut sap. In places the surface blisters up into low, broad domes where gas from decomposition is trapped, until your weight or the afternoon heat pops them with a soft sigh. This is a world that built itself without ever inventing a tool.

It is sparse. 0.8 density. The eye starves a little because there is so much room between presences. The shrubs — let's call them anchor trees, though they are not quite trees — are solitary and identical and far apart, each holding down its own territory of woven earth with a crown of dusty green leaves the size of your thumb. Between them, nothing vertical for minutes at a time, just the undulating, dark-brown floor, pocked and seamed. Light falls straight and unsparing, normal daylight, yellow-white, high at 1.9/3, so every little crack has a sharp little shadow. You can see too far.

Water lives here in a stingy, intermittent way.

There is no sea. There is no permanent river. What there is is damp. In the late afternoon, clouds that were born inside this basin — because a thing this big breathes — stack up and bruise and finally spill. The rain, when it comes, is hot and brief and straight down. It doesn't soak in. The mat doesn't absorb quickly. For twenty minutes, the whole low world sheets over with a skin of warm water, ankle-deep, reflecting the anchor trees like mirrors, and you hear the only loud sound this place ever makes: a low, continuous hissing as water runs over waxed lichen.

Then it drains. Not down — there is no down — but sideways, toward the embryonic places.

Because this continent is still under construction. Erosion is at 0.3, which is to say it has barely started to learn how to erode. In a true geology, water would have cut canyons by now. Here, the water has only just discovered it can cut. Where two of the great root-slopes meet, the runoff gathers and begins to incise a channel, a shallow, raw groove a meter deep and ten wide, its sides not rock but exposed, pale cross-section of all those compressed layers, like looking at the rings of one vast tree. At the bottom of the groove there is silt, but the silt is sawdust. Fine, fibrous, lignin-rich. The channel meanders for fifty kilometers until it just... stops, feathering out into a damp fan where a colony of dark green mats holds the moisture longer than anything else. A riverine place without a river. A pocket.

Those pockets are everything.

In the bottom of a new gully, or in the shadow of a blistered dome, or in a cup where three anchor roots surface and intersect, you find damp that lasts through the dry days. Condensation also. At dawn, the mass of the mat is still cool from the night and it sweats. Beads of water form on every lichen crust, run together, drip down into little bowls that the mat has grown to hold them — shallow, watertight improbable basins of living cork. For an hour after sunrise, the whole basin glitters at ground level, as if someone scattered glass.

In those bowls is where the other sparse green lives: a red-tipped moss that only grows in the cups, a thread-like fern that unfurls overnight and curls and crisps by three p.m., waiting alive inside its own dead shell. Vegetation overall is 1.1 — sparse, yes, but not absent. It's just contractual. Every leaf negotiates for water with the terms of the mat.

There are no animals. 0.5 fauna, which is effectively zero. No insects whine. No birds cross the white sky. No humus-turners, no grazers. It’s why the mat can get so thick — nothing eats it faster than it grows. Occasionally wind flips a curled fern frond and there is no scramble away. The silence is the kind you attribute to deserts at first, but it isn't empty, it's just quiet by design. 0.5 sound. What you hear, when you stop walking, is the creak of the anchor trees expanding in the heat, a noise like old rope stretching. You hear the faint tick-tick as the surface crust contracts and minute cracks open. You hear water blinking out of existence, drop by drop, back into vapor.

And yet it is dynamic. Very dynamic, 2.3, active.

Stand for a day and you will see growth happen. An anchor root, warm from rain, will add a visible knuckle, pushing up the mat into a new ridge. The black shrubs transpire so hard at midday that the air above each one shivers into a little storm cell, a personal thunderhead that may or may not rain back onto its maker. The new gullies lengthen by a handspan in a single shower, undercutting, collapsing in mats. The whole plain, if you could time-lapse it from orbit, would look like a slow, breathing lung, a fabric being knit from the inside out even as water learns, for the first time in this world's history, how to un-knit it.

The rule here is simple: everything was made by something that does not know it is making. There is no quarry, no mine, no factory, no hand. There is only accretion. Lignin and root-hair and lichen-cement and time. It is a total built environment — not a city, but a carpet the size of a country that mistook itself for geology. If you walk to the edge, if there is an edge, you would not find the end of the mat. You would find it getting younger, thinner, softer, more like a net than a floor, until finally there are only runners reaching out over damp clay, touching nothing, still trying to make more world out of air and heat and intermittent rain.
