It is not outside.

This is a room inside the mountain, but the mountain is made.

You come to it by the service ladder - three flights of open steel, high enough that the air gets thin and cold in your mouth, 2,800 meters or more, where the cloud stops being weather and just hangs against the concrete. The maintenance door is too small for two people, gasketed, and it sighs when you un-dog it. Then the sound hits you.

It is a wet room. Not a flooded room, a *weeping* room. A pocket.

Someone, on some blueprint a long time ago, called it Stilling Chamber 4-B. A cube, maybe four meters to a side, maybe five - big enough to turn around in with a pack on but not big enough to ever feel empty. Everything in it is built. Floor, walls, ceiling, the central thing that gives the room its reason. There is no rock, no dirt. The building goes down and down and out through the dam face, and this little box is just an embedded knot in it. A hidden place.

The central thing is a weir. A sharp, young concrete lip, not yet rounded by decades, still showing the wood-grain of its formwork in places, still showing where the aggregate was a little too coarse. It is a perfect, intentional notch, 80 centimeters wide, and over it comes the mountain.

Water that your eyes can't track properly. Up here, water doesn't behave like lowland water. It is snow-melt and cloud-catch and reservoir bleed, cold enough to make your knuckles ache if you leave your hand in it for ten seconds. It arrives through a black iron throat in the ceiling - a 60-centimeter pipe sweating condensation, shivering in its brackets - and falls a meter into a fore-basin, and then has to find its way over that sharp concrete lip.

That one-meter fall is why you cannot hear yourself think.

The sound is total. It isn't a roar - a roar has a shape - it is a white, physical pressure. Water striking concrete at full industrial volume in a sealed reverberant box. The pipe itself hums and bangs with cavitation, a deeper hammer underneath, *thud-thud-thud* against the drone, and the rebar in the walls carries it. You feel it in your molars. If you try to speak, the word is taken off your lips and shredded. If you stay an hour, your ears will ring through sleep.

It is cold. Not freezing. Just 8 or 9 degrees held year-round, the cold of deep water and shaded concrete. Your breath doesn't quite fog, but the air wants to. The humidity is absolute. Every surface is running.

Because it is so dim. A single bulkhead light in a wire cage, 40 watts behind thick, pitted glass, on the wettest wall. It buzzes, of course - everything industrial here buzzes - and it throws more shadow than light. The wet black concrete drinks it. The water itself is dark until it breaks, then it is white. The glow catches on the moving skin and nothing else.

And yet it is active, ceaselessly active. The room has a weather system.

Water comes from above, sheets off the pipe, bursts over the weir lip, churns in the lower basin, and vanishes through a grated slot in the floor that goes back down into the guts of the hydro works. That is the main cycle, but around it there are dozens of smaller ones. Condensation beads on the low ceiling and falls without pattern - fat, cold drops that find your neck. Spray from the fall fogs the air and settles on every bolt-head and runs down. Seepage comes laterally too, not from mountain springs - there may not be any mountain left here that isn't replaced - but from the concrete itself. The walls weep. Dark patches bloom and run clear.

The terrain in here is all made-terrain, but it is still terrain. The floor is broom-finished concrete scored with anti-slip grooves, and those grooves have become tiny river systems, each carrying a thread of water to the grate. The fore-basin has a lip of sharp, un-eroded cement that has only just begun to be etched by the water, still almost knife-edged. Where the main jet hits, the cement is already a little pockmarked, the sand exposed, young erosion happening fast in front of you. A year ago this edge was sharper. In ten years it will be a smooth pour.

Life is sparse, as sparse as it can be without being sterile.

There is no animal here. No rats, no insects, not even a spider - too loud, too wet, too clean in the way that industrial water is clean: no food. But there are a few presences.

In the corners where the spray doesn't directly scour, a thin felt of dark green holds on. Not moss, exactly; something closer to industrial slime, algae that has learned to grow on Portland cement. It is almost black in the dim light. In one joint between wall and floor, where a hairline crack has let a little lime leach out, there is a true moss, a single tuft the size of your palm, bright and startled-looking against the grey. It shivers each time the pipe bangs.

That is all. One moss, three smears of algae, and the water.

The rule of this place is simple and total: water moves through built space, and built space accommodates it. It was built to be wet. The iron throat is bolted with six bolts the size of fists, each weeping rust. The cage around the light is rusted. The grate in the floor sings when the flow is heavy, vibrating in its seat. Everything is doing a job. There is no decoration, no intention beyond throughput. Up above, down below, there are hundreds of rooms like this, or different, all humming and pouring, making electricity out of altitude. From the outside, the dam is a serene grey curve in the cloud. Inside, it is this - a thousand deafening closets where condensed mountain weather falls endlessly over sharp new concrete in the dim.
