It is high up.

Not high like a peak you climb to. High like a table left out in the weather, after everything else eroded away. A basalt mesa, or what was a basalt mesa before it was completely taken. You come up out of the lowland haze — hot, white, close — and you break into cooler air that is somehow hotter, because the sun is unfiltered here and the rock, where you can still guess at rock, holds heat like a kiln.

The mesa is not flat. It is young. That is the first wrong thing about it. High places should be worn. This one isn't. The edges are still a fresh break, sheer and flaking, black glass and rust-red scoria that has not learned to be rounded yet. Blocks the size of houses have calved off yesterday, last hour, and lie tipped against the escarpment below, already being taken. The whole place still has the geometry of violence that made it.

But nothing here is rock anymore, not on top.

The water is everywhere first, and then the green.

The air at this altitude should be thin and dry. It isn't. It's wet, heavy, condensing. The heat coming off the dark mass of the mesa hits the cooler updraft off the lowlands and stacks cloud on cloud at knee height, at chest height, at head height. You walk through cloud as if through hanging laundry. Everything drips. Water is not in rivers here — there is no slope for a river to organize itself. It is in the air, and then it is on things, and then it is inside things, and then it is in the air again. The plants weep constantly. The underside of every leaf holds a bead that swells and falls, swells and falls. The sound of it is too much, like rain, but there is no rain.

And the plants —

There is no ground. That is the rule.

Somewhere underneath, under all of this, there is basalt. You will never touch it. What you touch, what you stand on, what you walk through, is plant, built on plant, built on plant. Not forest. A forest has ground and then trees. This is plant as substrate, plant as architecture, plant as previous bet.

It started simple, you can see the logic in the younger sections nearer the edge: a mat of heat-loving moss, thick as a mattress, that sealed itself over the rock to hold water. Then a fleshy creeper that stitched through the moss to hold itself up, then a larger thing that used the creeper as soil, then a larger thing still. Now it is a city with no planner, made entirely without tools, entirely without hands.

The builders are sparse. That is the second wrong thing, and why you can see at all. For all that it is total green, absolute cover, it is not dense. It is open. The biomass is vertical and spaced, like cathedrals built too far apart on a plain.

Each one is an individual. It's hard to call them trees.

One: a colonnade of still-living trunks, hollow, fused at the top into a bowl a hundred feet across, that catches cloud and holds it as a lake. The lake is green-black. Its sides are leaves, not bark. The leaves are as big as doors and they sweat water down the outside of the trunks day and night. Inside the lake, a second tier of smaller, paler plants grow upside down, roots in the air, leaves in the water.

Another: a tower that did not grow as a tower but wove itself as one — vine thick as a torso spiraling around nothing, and nothing, and nothing, until the absence in the middle was a shaft. Wind goes through it and whistles, and the whole thing shivers. From its sides hang curtains of fine hair-roots, forty feet long, that never touch any surface. They harvest water straight out of the air.

Another: a fallen one, which is the loudest. Because nothing here is stable. This is a turbulent place, hyperactive, too hot and too wet and too high and too alive. It grows faster than it can understand its own weight. So you hear it constantly — a creak like a ship, a sudden rifle-crack as a tension limb snaps, a long tearing as a leaf the size of a sail delaminates and falls. The fallen builder is being unmade in real time. Its interior, which was a lattice of damp chambers like lungs, has collapsed open. Already the moss is covering it, already the next builders are punching seedlings into its flesh.

Between them, between these sparse and lonely buildings, there are expanses of nothing that is not nothing. Springy plains of interwoven root and dead leaf over void — you walk on it and it bounces, and water squelches up around your ankles with every step, warm. If you kneel and pull it apart, there is no soil, only more root, finer and finer, disappearing into damp darkness.

There are no animals. You keep listening for an insect, a bird, even a fly, and there is nothing. In a place this hot and wet there should be a scream of life. There is only the water and the plant-noise. Drip and creak and groan and pop. The pop of seed pods as big as your head that open in the heat with a cough. The low hydraulic sigh when a trunk decides to move water from its base to its crown. The hollow drum of water falling from leaf to leaf to leaf to mat. It is loud without any voice in it.

The light is ordinary — white-yellow high noon sun through the mist — which makes the strangeness worse. This is not the alien light of a dream. This is daylight showing you something that should not be able to work, but does. Condensation soaks your shirt clear through. Steam lifts off the upper surfaces. The air smells like crushed green, like hot tea, like iron.

If you stay until late afternoon, the cloud deck below rises to meet the mesa's rim and then overflows. Water runs up. The builders, spread across their valley-sized roof, throw shadows across each other, long and thin. You can see for a mile in any direction and you will count maybe seven of the big ones, and maybe thirty of the smaller attempts, and then a lot of bright, empty, trembling, soaking, steaming green that is itself a building that has not decided what it is yet.

And tomorrow, when the heat comes again, one or two of them will be gone, and the shape of the empty space between the others will be different, and something new will have knotted itself up out of nothing, fresh and sharp-edged, dripping.
