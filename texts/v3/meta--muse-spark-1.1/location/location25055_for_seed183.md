It is high. High enough that the world has thinned out.

You are on a tableland that should not be able to hold water, but it does. The air is bright to the point of pain — not warm-bright; cold-bright. At this elevation the sun is a hard white coin and the sky around it is a saturated, flawless violet-blue that feels closer than it should. Light comes from everywhere, doubled off wet stone, so there are no real shadows, only places where the light is slightly less insane. The air itself is cool — maybe 7 or 8 degrees C — thin, clean, with that iron taste you get above ten thousand feet. You breathe and it hurts a little. Your ears pop constantly.

This is not a peak. It is a broad, open bowl in the roof of the world, a valley the size of a small country, rimmed by further white ridges that never end. The scale makes no sense at first because everything is so close together.

The ground is dense.

I mean literally crowded. The plateau is not flat. It is packed — jammed — with forms. Knee-high to house-high mineral growths, but not plants. There is no plant life here at all. No lichen, no moss, no smear of green. What grows here is stone in its own patient habit. Thousands upon thousands of weathered pedestals, drip-spires, and bowls, all touching shoulders, leaning into each other, an in-place forest of rock that has learned to approximate a forest without any biology.

They are layered: underneath your feet is a pavement of dark basalt, polished by age until it shines. Out of it rise nodules of a paler, granular stone — pitted, pockmarked, weeping. And out of those rise finer structures: translucent chimneys of silica, ochre turrets of ironstone, black glass blisters that popped and froze. Everything is wet. This is a wet place, impossibly wet for this height.

The water doesn't come from rain, exactly. Or not only. The atmosphere is at its dew point. Clouds are here, physically here, moving at ankle height, at chest height, snagging on the stone teeth and condensing. You watch it happen: a puff of nothing catches on a cold spire and becomes beads, becomes rivulets, becomes a steady weep. The rocks themselves sweat. Every surface drips. The sound is everywhere — not a roar, but a loudness made of a million small louds. Tick, hiss, patter, gurgle, splash, the hollow *plok* of a droplet falling three meters into a mineral cup, repeated ten thousand times out of sync across the valley. The stone forest is a percussion instrument the clouds are playing. It is LOUD. You have to raise your voice.

Because the stone is in balance — this place is old, established, erosion and deposition long ago made a truce — the water has carved its paths and they stay. There are no cutting gorges. Instead there are shallow, braided runnels, glass-smooth channels the width of your hand, linking bowl to bowl to bowl. Each bowl is full to the brim with clear, icy water, then spills over its lowest lip in a beaded curtain to the next bowl below. A staircase of mirrors descending for kilometers, open and visible all the way to the violet horizon because the land is open, because the air is bright. You can see the whole watershed at once. Each pool duplicates the sky, but wrong.

That is the first breakdown.

At first you think it's just reflection. Then you notice some of the pools reflect a sky with two suns. Some reflect night. Some reflect you from three seconds ago, or from an angle you are not standing at. If you stand perfectly still beside one of the larger pools — a basin big enough to lie down in, rimmed with a smooth lip of rosy calcite — the water in it is so still and so clear that you can't tell where the surface is. And your reflection does not quite sync. Reach down and your reflected hand reaches up, but not from the same side.

Water here does not always obey grade. On the eastern slope of the bowl, you can see a series of pools where the water runs uphill between them for a few meters before continuing down. Not an optical illusion; you can put a leaf — if you had a leaf — and watch it climb. Here, gravity is negotiable, a local ordinance poorly enforced. The builders must have noticed.

The built.

There are scattered marks, primitive, almost lost in the density until your eyes learn the difference between weather and intention. Cairns. Not tall or proud — low, hunched stacks of wet black stone, three or four rocks high, placed where a larger bowl overflows. Someone, something, put them there to make the overflow sing louder, or quieter. Someone arranged a line of flat basalt slabs as a walkway that avoids the deepest pools, though it is now half-sunk and beaded with silica.

In the lee of a cluster of spires, there is a hut — or the attempt at a hut. Dry-stacked walls, waist-high, no roof, or the roof was a hide that is long gone. Inside, on the wet pavement, are tools: a hammerstone, a core of chert worked with a few brutal flakes, a shallow cup ground into a piece of sandstone to catch condensation. Simple, hand-made, pre-metal. The kind of things you make when you have just invented making things. No bones. No hearth — it would never stay lit here. Whoever lived here did not eat plants, did not hunt animals, because there are none. Not a bug, not a bird. The only motion besides water and cloud is wind.

But they stayed. They made more marks. On the smooth inner face of some bowls, you find spirals pecked into the stone with that hammerstone, line after line after line, each spiral centered not on the true bottom of the bowl but on the point where the impossible reflection shows the second sun. They were mapping the wrong sky.

If you stay until late afternoon — though afternoon does not dim the light much, only slants it — you understand why. The reality breakdown is not constant; it breathes. The cool air sinks, the condensation accelerates, the loud dripping becomes a roar, and for about twenty minutes the whole valley flickers. Distance goes soft. A spire ten meters away appears to be both ten meters and ten kilometers away at once. You look down at your hands and see the light passing through them, braided with the water. Sound lags. Your own footsteps return to you from a pool you haven't stepped in yet.

Then it passes. The bowls settle. The clouds lift slightly and you see, beyond the rim of this high valley, not more mountains, but more valleys like this one, tiered up and up, each one full of dense stone teeth and bright water, disappearing into the bright.

The rules here are not the ones you learned below:

1. Water is plentiful but never still. Even the stillest pool is moving through somewhere else.
2. Stone grows toward wetness, not toward light.
3. What you build will be annotated by water within a season, and will become part of the instrument.
4. When you look into a bowl, something looks back, but it is not an animal.

You get up from the ruined hut. Your boots are soaked. The rock under you continues to weep.
