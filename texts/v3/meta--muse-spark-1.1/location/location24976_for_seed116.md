At the very top of it, there is a room full of water.

You have to understand what "top" means here. This is not a mountain. There are no mountains left. Or rather, there was a city that kept building itself taller when the water came, and kept building itself taller when the water kept coming, floor on floor on floor on tower on bridge on scaffold until the pile of itself became its own geology. So tall that the air gets thin on the climb. So tall that you climb for a day through dead levels of housing and factory and storage that have all, over immeasurable time, weathered into something like a natural canyonland.

That's the erosion. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Concrete has lost its corners. Rebar has sweated out of slabs and been polished by wind and false rain into smooth, dark whale-bones curving from walls. Steel stairwells have been worn into slick U-shaped troughs by feet and then by water and then by nothing. The whole mass has settled. It doesn't collapse anymore; it has found its final, stoop-shouldered shape. It is a mountain made of apartment blocks. Its top is extreme, it is a summit, and it is completely artificial from bedrock to flagpole.

And hidden just under the summit cap - under the last maintenance dome that nobody had a reason to enter - is this one pocket.

You find it because of the sound. Long before you see the hatch, you can hear it hammering: a deep, industrial heart beating too loud for such a small thing. *CHUD. CHUD. CHUD.* Not an echo. A working thing.

The hatch itself is small, man-sized, set flush in a wall that has eroded so smooth it feels like river stone. The wheel is brass and has been worn to a soft lump. It still turns. You have to put your back into it and when it breaks loose, warm water weeps instantly from the seam and runs down your front.

Inside is only one room. That's all this place ever was.

It's a sphere, perhaps six meters across. Utterly built - there is no natural surface anywhere in this world, but here it's absolute. The floor is poured tile, white ceramic gone yellow with age. The walls are riveted boiler plate, every rivet a perfect, pitted dome the size of your palm. The ceiling is the same. A single industrial room, intact, intimate as a chapel, embedded like a pearl in the top of this ancient, flattened mountain of construction.

And it is completely submerged.

Not flooded, in the sense of a disaster. Submerged as a condition. The sphere was designed to be full. When you open the hatch, you don't pour water *in*, you are *let into* the water that is always there. It holds against the door for a second with skin tension, and then gives, and you slip through into warm.

The temperature hits you first. It's bathwater. Body-warm, engine-warm. Roughly 30 degrees, kept that way by the machines. It has been warm for so long that the room has a smell even through the water - hot iron and old tile and green life.

Light comes in normally — not dark, not glaring. Daylight, real sun, from directly above. The top third of the sphere is a thick lens of glass and resin, a skylight, and above that is only the thin air of extreme elevation. So the sun pours straight down in a hard column through miles of clear sky, then hits the water in this hidden cup and shatters. It makes slow moving bars inside the sphere. Moteless water. You can see every particle in it.

And because the room is full, the light behaves wrong. You are buoyant, you tumble a little when you enter, and your bubbles don't rush — they wobble up reluctantly through syrup-thick water. Sound is monstrously loud in here. That hammering from outside is inside now, doubled by the water. It is the pump on the far wall, a single huge industrial engine that should have died a thousand years ago and instead is kept alive by its own eccentricity. Black iron, as big as a car, bolted to the curve of the wall. Its piston goes CHUD and the whole sphere flexes, just a little, and a low note goes through your teeth. CHUD. CHUD. The water is its working fluid, its coolant, its product. It pulls from somewhere even higher — condensation nets on the dome? — and pushes it down, down through the whole mountain-mound of the city to feed whatever still needs water below. This one hidden cistern at the absolute summit is the heart. It never empties.

Because it is that old, everything built has become terrain. The tiles on the low side have lifted and re-set at odd angles, making little terraces. In the seams between them grows vegetation — lush, absurdly lush for such a contained place. Not algae. Something that learned to be plants here. Long kelp-like ribbons of dark rubbery leaf that root directly into rust, that have stripped chlorophyll and replaced it with something redder. They grow in slow spirals, sparse. There is a lot of empty water between each one, because nothing crowds here. One ribbon might be two meters long and there is nothing for a meter around it except clear water and light. Further down, near the drain grate, a cluster of what looks like tomatoes, but black and translucent, each the size of a fist, hanging from a vine of copper wire. The vine is part of the pump; the tomatoes grow off the wiring harness.

And fauna. Abundant, once you stay still.

The water itself is alive but not densely so — there is a philosophy of space here. A creature, then a long pause, then another creature, so you can appreciate each.

Shrimp the length of your thumb, completely transparent except for their full green guts, commuting in small slow fleets between kelp fronds. Pale catfish with whiskers as long as their bodies, blind, their skin the same yellow-white as tile, sucking at the bacterial felt on the walls. Snails — hundreds of them — but not fast. Each has a shell made of a perfect coil of porcelain, or perhaps actually made from the tile itself, rasped off and re-secreted. They move so slowly that you think they are still, until you watch one spot for a minute and it has crept a centimeter. And in the open water, in the sun column itself, clouds of silvery fry that hang motionless until the pump hits its beat — CHUD — and then they all flinch together, a single organism, flashing.

Everything is sparse and unhurried and loud. The pump, your own breathing magnified inside your head, the low creek of the ancient sphere expanding a fraction in the heat and contracting again. Beyond the lens above, wind over the summit makes a thin whistle, but muffled by water into a whine.

The rules here are simple and strange, and you understand them immediately in your body:

1. This water does not fall. It belongs up here. At the highest point. Gravity is still here, but this place is exempt by agreement with the engine. If the engine stopped, the water would remember and drop, and the whole ancient mountain below would briefly become a waterfall.

2. No substrate is natural. If you dig under a tile, you find more tile. If you peel back the kelp, its roots are threaded through bolt-holes. Even the fish, if you looked closely at their bones, would have tiny stamped serial numbers.

3. Erosion is finished. Nothing gets smoother. Nothing gets rougher. The rust has stabilized into a skin harder than steel. The tile has been worn until it can't be worn more. This pocket has achieved permanence, and so it will stay like this, warm and full and beating, until someone very deliberately breaks it.

You tread water in the middle of the sphere. The light holds you. A shrimp passes a few inches from your nose, indifferent. A snail beneath your feet has been traversing the same tile for what might be a decade to get to the better algae patch near the motor warmth.

It is a single, hidden, man-made forever-tidepool at the top of the world, and it is completely unbothered by you being in it.
