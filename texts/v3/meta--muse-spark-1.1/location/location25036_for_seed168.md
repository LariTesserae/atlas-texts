This is a high place.

Not a mountain exactly — a lift in the world, a broad, unfinished table that has been pushed up into warm, thin air and left there to cure. The light here is clean and direct and ordinary, the kind of sun that makes edges sharp, and it falls a long way. You can see too far. The horizon doesn't haze. It's just a line where the pale ground meets the pale sky, and between you and it there is almost nothing.

The ground is raw. Not sand, not rock in any finished sense. It's basaltic foam, still holding the bubble-memory of when it was extruded, pitted and full of vesicles, the color of kiln brick and pencil lead. There are no riverbeds. No salt crack. No beach. It has never flowed. The term "erosion" does not yet apply to it because nothing has been taken away from it yet — this is all addition, emergence, a place still in the act of being drafted. Ridges are really just wrinkles where the cooling skin folded over itself. When you put your hand on it, the stone is warm from the sun, and from underneath.

Because underneath is the factory, except there is no factory.

There are no buildings here. No walls, no frames, no scrap, no ruins. No sign that anything was ever made. And yet the whole plateau is industrial.

It grows.

That is the difficult thing. In the lower latitudes of this table, where the crust is thinnest, the vegetation begins. From a distance it is vegetation — unmistakably lush by any measure of cover and color and abundance. A valley, maybe three kilometers across and ten long, choked with it, dense as jungle at its center, thinning at its edges into lonely outliers that stand like sentinels in the open waste. It has a canopy. It has an understory. It has texture. In the midday light it is deep green-black and iron-red and a tarnished verdigris green, matte and slightly oily.

Up close it is not plants.

Each one is a self-extruding heat-exchange tower. A trunk is a chimney of laminated iron and slag, twenty to forty meters tall, slowly growing upward by accreting its own smoke. It has no rings. It has weld seams laid down in spirals by no welder. At its top, where the interior draft becomes efficient, it mushrooms into a vast crown of leaves — but the leaves are not leaves. They are vanes. Fins. Radiators. Thin sheets of iron silicate and carbon, feathered to increase surface area, quivering with heat. Some are as broad as a hand, some as broad as a door, fringed with more delicate dendrites like ferns made of graphite. They don't photosynthesize. They thermosynthesize. They are cooling organs for the forge below.

And there is no water anywhere in this. Not a molecule made available. No dew. No sap. The air is utterly dry, warm as skin, a perfect insulator. These iron trees don't need water; they breathe the hot exhaust that rushes up through their hollow cores. You can stand under one and feel it — a slow, steady column of superheated, bone-dry air rising from the deep mantle, turning the vanes just slightly, carrying up with it a fine dust of metal sulfide that settles on everything and is then re-absorbed into the trunk. That is their whole economy: hot vapor up, cool metal down. Perpetual.

It is lush because it is efficient, and there is a lot of waste heat to dump. Where the upwellings are closest together, the crowns interlock and make a true jungle dark. Light filters through in slatted bars, dappling the unfinished stone with moving shadow. The trunks tick. That's all they do. A quiet sound, very quiet. A contraction tick as a fin cools. A soft ping, like a hot tin roof adjusting at dusk. The whole forest ticks in its own heat, out of sync, never loud enough to be a noise. The silence in between is complete. There is no birdcall, no insect whir, no footfall. There is no fauna. Nothing has ever moved here voluntarily.

And it does not change. That is the most unusual part. An industrial landscape should stoke and clatter and output, should progress toward failure or product. This one doesn't. The draft is constant. The temperature is constant. The growth is exactly balanced by spalling — each tree sheds a skin of oxide flakes as large as plates, which fall and lie around its base in a perfect circle and slowly sink back into the porous ground to be melted and re-extruded. The forest is in equilibrium. It has always looked exactly like this and always will, an equilibrium jungle, a mature system at birth.

If you walk open to the edge of the valley where the trees thin out, the emptiness becomes explicit again. For a kilometer you might have only one young spire, no taller than your waist, its first leaf still soft and coppery and curled like a fern head, ticking bravely to itself. The sky above it is large and empty and warm. The ground between is so empty you feel the planet's curvature.

There are no roads to it and no reason to cut one. The rules here are simple: heat comes up, metal comes up with it, it must be cooled, so a shape that cools it grows. No water is required. No animal is required to carry seed. No builder is required to design the tower. Industry happens because geology requires it, not because anyone built it.
