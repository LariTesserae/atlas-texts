It is a room that the earth made by accident, and then left open.

You go down to it — not far, but far enough that the air changes. The entrance is a throat in the ground behind the old works, a young collapse, maybe ten or twenty years old at most. The edges haven't learned how to be soft yet. Limestone and poured concrete sheared together when the floor gave way, and the break is still sharp, white rock teeth and rebar with rust still blooming fresh. It is perhaps sixty feet across. A single place. You can stand in the middle and see every wall at once. A cathedral pit, a hamlet-sized hollow.

It is hot down here. Not the clean hot of desert, but sealed hot, the kind that has been sitting. Industrial hot. The air is heavy and still and damp, 98 degrees and holding, and it does not move unless you move it. The rock sweats. The machines sweat. Everything condenses. 

This was a sump room once, a service under-cavern for the factory that used to be above. That is the scattered built part — not a city, not ruins, just the leftovers of one function. A catwalk, sheared off mid-span, jutting out from the north wall like a diving board with its ladder still hanging, ending in air. A pump housing the size of a small house, dead, its black iron skin gone orange and scaly. A switch cabinet, door open, full of dark fuses. A concrete plinth with four bolt-holes where something bigger was taken away when they abandoned this level. Electrical conduit still stapled to the rock in neat, pointless lines. It was all built to move water, and now water moves itself.

There is very little light. From the collapse above, a crooked shaft lets in a column of dusty outside air, but it is afternoon-dim even at noon, just enough to see the motes hanging. The rest is darkness, thick as cloth. The only real light down here is industrial and stubborn — two caged amber workbulbs on the pump housing that never went out. They are on a separate circuit, or a battery that won't die, and they buzz at a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear. Their light doesn't reach the walls. They just make two small pools of hot yellow on the wet iron.

And it is lush.

That is the strangeness that takes a minute to process. There should not be this much green with no sun and no animals. But there is. The damp is enough. The heat is enough. The darkness is almost not.

Fern trees, or something that decided to be fern trees — black-green trunks as thick as your thigh, but soft, made of matted root-hair and trapped moisture, not wood. They grow sparse, far apart, one here, one twenty feet there, each one taking its own space and needing it. Their fronds are enormous, waxy, seven feet long, unrolling slow. Between them, on every surface that stays wet, a carpet of moss so thick it looks poured. It grows over everything indiscriminately: over rock, over fallen concrete slab, over the pump, over the railing of the catwalk. Especially over metal. It likes iron. Where the moss grows thickest, it sprouts pale, translucent pitchers that hold condensation — little cups of clear water, perfectly still.

No soil. Not really. Just a thin slurry of limestone dust, rust flake, and rot from its own dead fronds. It doesn't need more.

There are no animals. That is the first rule of this place and the one your body notices last. No flies in the heat. No echoing beetles. No birds nesting in the catwalk. No rat droppings on the switch cabinet. Not even a gnat drowned in the pitcher cups. The cups are clean. The fronds are uneaten, perfect-edged. The air should be loud with insects in this kind of hothouse, and it is silent instead. So silent your ears invent a hum.

What sound there is, is tiny and slow.

Drip.

Not a steady drip. The room breathes moisture. Water gathers on the undersides of fronds, on the sharp young edges of the collapsed ceiling, on the cold conduit, until it is heavy enough to fall. A drop hits a pitcher cup. *Tok.* A minute later, another hits bare iron far across the room. *Tik.* The moss itself makes almost no sound as it grows, but if you stand still long enough in the amber light, you can hear the occasional low *tick* and *ping* of the old pump housing contracting in the heat, metal remembering it was once hot for a different reason.

Everything here moves on a slow clock. The ferns grow an inch a month. The rust creeps a millimeter a year over its own paint. The condensation cycle — gather, swell, fall, evaporate, gather again — is the fastest thing in the room, and it takes hours. The collapse is still settling, micron by micron, sending down a puff of limestone dust once or twice a season. It is a young place, geologically newborn, its edges still sharp enough to cut your hand if you brace against the wall, and it is patient enough to outlast the factory, the lights, you.

If you sit on the concrete plinth, the air holds you like warm wet hands. The smell is iron-blood and green sap and hot stone. The two amber bulbs make the green look black. Above you, the small, sharp-edged hole out is a pale gray coin, far away, the only proof that there is a surface. 

The rules are simple here: Water never leaves, it just moves lower. Light never really arrives. Nothing eats and nothing is eaten. Growth happens without hunger.
