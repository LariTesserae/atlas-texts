It is night there most of the year.

Not the high, star-thin night of space, but a low, held-in darkness. This is a basin, a long, low trough between two black ridgelines maybe fifteen kilometers apart, the floor of it almost at sea level though the sea is far to the north. In late winter the sun drags itself for an hour along the southern ridge and then gives up. The light that remains is not starlight — the cloud deck is too thick, too wet — it is a deep indigo with a wash of green from the aurora caught and diffused in the mist until the whole sky glows like a wet road under a streetlamp. You can see, but distantly, as if through frosted glass.

The basin is new. Geologically new. You can tell because nothing has had time to be made kind. The rock is basalt, still sharp enough to cut boots, a froth of recent eruption, torn open last decade or the decade before when the rift under this place hiccuped. The edges are scoria, glass, aa lava with razored crusts. There are no soft hills. There are plates tilted up and left half-buried, there are pressure ridges that rose when the center of the valley sagged, and between them are cracks meters wide that go down into steam. Water has not had time to make valleys, so it makes a skin. It sits everywhere. The basalt is too young to drain well, so the floor is a cold marsh, a muskeg, a reed and sphagnum sponge over rock. You step and the ground sighs and the water that wells up around your boot is black and very cold. The air is too cold to hold much, so it condenses constantly. The rocks weep. The air itself weeps. Every surface drips even when it isn't raining, and it rains often, a fine, needle rain that is half snow, that never quite decides.

And everything grows on it. 

That's the strange part for how cold it is. The temperature never gets much above -2C, sometimes -15C at night, but the vegetation is furious. A boreal hyper-lush. Not trees, not really — black spruce and larch, stunted and dense and leaning, all hung in beards of old-man's lichen so thick you can't see trunk, but underneath and between them: moss. Layers of it. Pillow moss, stair-step moss, peat moss meters deep, saturated, acid bright green and rust red and a yellow so pale it's almost white. In the cracks where geothermal heat leaks, there are ferns, knee-high, unfurling through ice. There are dwarf cranberries, cloudberries, things that shouldn't be able to photosynthesize in this dark but do. The density is oppressive. There's no bare basalt in the central kilometers, not really; it's all covered, growing on growing, dead moss compressing under living moss, roots in air,Everything touching everything else. You can't walk in a straight line. You have to push through curtains.

The reason is under it.

Scattered across the valley, maybe one every three hundred meters, is a pylon. From a distance they look like nothing — a cairn of local rock, three meters tall, covered in the same moss and lichen. Closer, you see the rock isn't stacked, it's grown, lattice-braided. The surface, if you brush the moss aside, is warm to the touch and perfectly smooth, and it hums against your palm not with vibration but with a faint resistance, like pushing two magnets together. If you dig - and you shouldn't - you'll find it goes down a hundred meters, straight down into the hot layer, and at the bottom it does something that isn't quite heating. It edits the local thermodynamic cost of things. The field they make together, these hundred or two hundred quiet cairns, lowers the energy needed for cell division by a fraction, and raises the energy yield of condensation. It coaxes the air to give up its water as heat. It's old infrastructure from the Leaseholder days, post-fusion, post-entanglement, built to quickly make soil on a raw world or a ruined one. They are not maintained anymore but they have not stopped.

So the valley creates and destroys itself hourly. This is a turbulent place, not metaphorically. It is why you cannot sleep here. The sound is physically hard to bear without a hood.

First, the ice. Water near the surface in -10C with heat below makes ice plates that form and are ripped apart continuously. All night you hear it — *tink-tink-CRACK* — loud as gunfire, sometimes a deep boom that travels through the basalt into your molars when a new crevasse opens.

Second, the steam. When water finds the hot rock unmediated by a pylon, it flashes. Small phreatic explosions happen every few minutes somewhere in the basin, throwing up fountains of black gravel and hot mist that condenses instantly and falls as warm rain in a cold circle. The geysers are white noise, then suddenly screaming.

Third, the wind. The heat from the pylons makes permanent updrafts. Cold air from the ridgelines rushes down to replace it. The valley breathes hard, always. It moans through the spruce, rattles the lichen beards, tears the low cloud into shreds.

Fourth, the animals. Because it is warm, because it is wet, because there is a carpet of plant matter appear where ten years ago there was bare lava, fauna has come in dense and desperate. Geese and crane that should have migrated south were never told this place was winter. They huddle in the warm pools by the hundreds, honking, screaming, quarreling. Under the moss, lemmings and voles run tunnels like a city. Snowy owls hunt them and shriek. The marsh is stitched with their trails. In summer — or whatever passes for it — the insect hatch is biblical, black clouds of midges that live off the condensation and lay eggs in the warm water, and they make a wall of sound by themselves even when everything else pauses. Which it never does.

You are never alone in the volume. Every cubic meter holds six living things and two forms of water and one piece of hard, new rock trying to become soft.

There are other marks of people, older than the pylons. When this was just a rift and a metal prospecting claim, trappers built huts. You find them, collapsed, one wall still standing, sod roof now fully alive and hosting a small spruce grove on its own. A rusted snowmachine. A plastic shrine with a battery long dead. A boundary marker post leaning, its paint gone. No one lives here now; it's too loud, too wet, too actively making itself. Even the technicians who come to read the pylon field don't stay longer than an hour. They wear deafening protection — the only way to stand it — and they wade through thigh-deep bright moss and black water under a sky that glows, and they listen to the valley crack and scream and grow, loud enough to feel like it is building itself directly out of sound, and they get out.

At the lowest point of the basin, where all the weeping converges, there is a lake not yet on maps. It is shallow, hot at the edges and rimed with ice in the center, and it steams violently in the dark. Around its edge the moss has become something like trees overnight — pale stalks rising and falling when their bubbles burst. The lake will be gone next spring, split by a new pressure ridge, or doubled in size. That is the rule here: nothing is allowed to be finished.
