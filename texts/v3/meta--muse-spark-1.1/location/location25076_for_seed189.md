It wasn't a cave until last week.

Up on the surface it's just forest floor — warm dirt, maple roots thick as your thigh — and then the ground gave way. A clean, sharp-edged mouth, about as wide as a kitchen table is long. Fresh. The break is still raw. You can see where the loam sheared off the limestone in a single wet slab, where the root hairs are still white and reaching for soil that isn't there anymore. Young rock, young hole. It hasn't learned how to be a cave yet.

You drop down into it.

The air changes three feet below the lip. It's warmer here, not cool like a cave should be. Warm-blood warm. And damp, not wet. The kind of damp that lives in the air itself — that beads on your forearms and the back of your neck, that makes every surface slick to the touch but never quite drips. Except when it does. One drop at a time, from somewhere above you that shouldn't have anywhere to collect water.

And then the chamber opens.

It shouldn't. From above it looked like a pocket, a little gut in the earth, maybe big enough to stand in if you crouched. Down here it is *empty* in a way that makes a wrong shape in your head. The walls are too far apart. You can touch one wall of this hidden room and the other wall is too far to see clearly, and yet if you stood on the surface and threw a stick across the sinkhole, it would have landed in two seconds. It's a pocket that is bigger inside. An intimate room that forgot how to stay small.

There is no stone left. That's the first thing you get wrong. You think you're seeing stone covered in moss. You're not.

Everything is plant.

The substrate is plant. The architecture is plant. The walls, the floor, the ceiling — low and vaulted and breathing — are not *covered* in vegetation, they *are* vegetation, woven into a single, continuous living mass that decided to grow itself into a hollow. Roots the diameter of your wrist braid with other roots and then flatten and then become something like bark and then become something like leaf and then become root again without ever stopping. There are no individual plants. There is one plant-thing that has tried every shape at once.

It is euphorically, impossibly green in normal, easy daylight.

The light comes from the hole you came in through, a ragged circle of white summer light overhead, but it doesn't behave right. It falls straight down in a column and then spreads. It doesn't fade into cave-dark. It stays. The air itself seems to carry light a little farther than it should, bouncing it between wet leaves until the whole void is day-lit, shadowless, like noon under water. You can see dust motes, except they aren't dust, they're pollen and tiny, transparent seeds drifting too slowly, as if the air were thicker.

The floor gives under your foot. Not soft — taut. A trampoline of living rootlets stretched over nothing. Under it, you can feel a deeper emptiness. If you kneel and part them, you see down through layers of leaf-latticed dark descending. The plant is hollow and hollow and hollow again.

It is loud.

You expected the muffled quiet of being underground. Instead the place is working, and you can hear it work.

Creaks. Not wood creaks. Growth creaks. The sound of fibers tightening, of a root tip forcing its way one cell at a time through a space smaller than itself and splitting what is in its way. Pops of seed pods opening with a rifle-crack that echoes and re-echoes in the too-big space. A low, sustained groan as two vast leaf-walls, each the size of a house but light as a lung, rub against each other and then slip. The constant ticking of condensation swelling on the underside of leaves as big as blankets — a thousand tiny metronomes — until they get too heavy and fall. Plip. Plip. PLIP. Onto lower leaves, which shiver and dump their own load.

Warm sap-wet air moves in slow, inexplicable drafts, despite there being only one opening. It smells green in a way that hurts: crushed stem, hot earth, lily pads baking in sun, the iron tang of fresh-cut wood.

Nothing has built here. There is no path, no altar, no carving. But there is primitive making. Near where the root floor is thinnest, the plant has made things that look like tools without ever having seen a tool. Thorns grown into hooks. A leaf rolled and hardened into a tube, holding a mouthful of that rare water. In one place a branch has swollen into a perfect, smooth club, fibrous and heavy. Not used. Grown in case it is needed. The plant here invents hand-tools because it doesn't know what hands are yet.

Animals almost never come down here. It's too new and too loud and too wrong. But you see signs: a single fat white beetle, blind, dragging itself across a vertical leaf-wall that should be a floor, confused by gravity. A thread of ants that found the entrance yesterday and are now marching in a perfect, pointless circle around the lip of a pitcher-shaped leaf that holds a little amber pool of rainwater from three days ago. The pool has its own sky in it. Tiny, furious life kicks inside it. The pitcher will close soon.

The rules are loose here. That's the weirdest part. You came down six feet and you're in a hollow that could hold a small church. The light reaches everywhere without a source. Water is everywhere and nowhere — everything is wet but there is no stream, no puddle except the ones the leaves make to hold their own idea of water. Up is not quite up; if you stay still long enough, the root-trampoline under you adjusts, and you realize it tilted a few degrees while you were breathing, trying to get a better angle on the sun.

If you put your hand on the wall, the wall is warm. If you keep it there, the little hairs on the leaf-wall lean, almost imperceptibly, toward your dampness. If you stay too long, you will get that feeling — not that you are being watched, but that you are being *included*. That the plant has decided your empty pocket of space — your lungs, your mouth, the hollow behind your knees — is part of its system of hollows.

You should climb out before the light moves. The hole seems smaller than it was.

Above you, the forest is ordinary and dry. Down here, everything sweats, cracks, unfurls. The chamber makes a sudden loud sigh — all its leaves shifting at once toward the light column — and for a second you can see straight through them, see that each leaf contains another tiny hollow chamber, lit inside, green inside green, and inside that, impossibly, another.
