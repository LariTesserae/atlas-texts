It is smaller than it first feels — you think at first you have come into a valley and then you realize you can see the whole rim. 

This is a bowl, high up. A hanging caldera, maybe three hundred strides across, cut out of basalt that has had a long time to forget it was ever molten. The elevation is what you feel first in your knees and lungs. The air up here is thin and warm and a little sour, like the air inside an old kiln. Far below, through breaks in the rim, the low country is a blue haze. You are above the weather and below the stars; the light never really arrives properly. The sky is a deep, bruised violet, not night, not day — a permanent high-altitude dusk. The sun is a dimmed coin somewhere behind a roof of still, high cloud that never rains. There hasn't been rain here in a geological age. There is no water. No spring, no creek, no damp under stone. The rock is dry to its marrow.

And yet it is lush.

That's the wrongness that hits second.

The ground of the bowl is not ground. It is a deep mattress of weathered pumice and salt-fine gravel, soft and established, rounded by eons of dry wind, not cut by water. Warm to the touch, it holds heat like bread from the oven. From it grows a forest that has nothing to drink.

They are not trees. From a distance they look like huge ostrich ferns or bottlebrushes, each one solitary, ten or twenty feet apart, so sparse you can see straight across the whole bowl through them. Up close each one is a revelation of dry engineering. The trunk is a bundle of black, waxy cables, not wood, more like felted hair and cured resin. At the top it explodes — a great, shaggy crown of fronds that are not green so much as oil-black, rust-red, amber. They are lush in the way a pelt is lush. Each frond is a dense comb of filaments, thousands of them per blade, each coated in a micro-felt that catches wind and charges.

This place lives on static and dust. 

The wind is constant and chaotic over the rim — that is the third thing. It pours over the western lip in a silent river, hits the warm bowl, and shatters. It has nowhere to go, so it turns, and spins, and tears itself into vortexes. It never stops. Inside the bowl the air is a bazaar of eddies. You can see it because the fronds are built to show it. They throw long streamers, spider-silk, pale as dandelion floss, ten, twenty feet long, and they never hang straight. The air twists them. The whole grove shivers, combs, bows. And as the wind rakes across those billions of dry, resinous hairs, it makes charge. Late in the dim, you can see it crawl — faint violet St. Elmo's fire ticking from tip to tip.

The plants drink that. Or rather, they do something with water that is not drinking. On windless nights — rare — a cold fog forms without ever becoming wet; the air simply becomes fat with suspended, unbound moisture that never condenses. The fronds go rigid and sing. Their filaments are hygroscopic in a violent, non-aqueous way — they rip the hydrogen right out of the moving air with electrostatic hunger and catalyze it on the spot with the salts in the stone dust they also collect. No puddle ever forms. No drop ever falls. Inside the trunk, the chemistry is more like hot wax and slow battery than sap. If you snap a frond it does not bleed; it smokes faintly and seals itself with sweet-smelling resin.

Because of this, the air in the grove smells strange: warm iron, hot beeswax, ozone before a storm.

And it is LOUD.

The wind in those combs is not a rustle. At this scale, each frond is a harp the size of a chapel organ. The filaments are tuned. When the vortexes hit, the fronds roar, keen, drone. Low thrums you feel in your chest give way to sudden, shrill rushes like air braking. The whole bowl is an instrument being played badly and brilliantly, all at once, never in tune with itself.

The fauna love it.

You don't see them at first because they are the same color and have learned to hang in the empty air between crowns. Then one moves and the whole system flickers. They are abundant — hundreds — but sparse in the way birds over the sea are sparse: so much space, so much happening.

They are mostly air. Things like torn kites, like large, translucent bats, like mantas. Six-foot wingspans of membrane stretched over hollow, whistling bone. Their undersides are covered in fine, black bristles that stand on end. They are not flying so much as being thrown and choosing how to be thrown — surfing the turbulence inside the bowl in endless, shrieking games. When they pass over a plant, they drag their belies through the streamers, and the plant crackles and discharges into them. You hear the SNAP from across the bowl, a whipcrack of bioelectricity. The fliers shudder and scream — high, tinny, ecstatic squeals — and the plant sags, satisfied, its filaments suddenly clumped and heavy with captured dust and fat.

They are pollinators, predators, parasites, all at once. Some smaller ones, beetle-thick and hummingbird-fast, live inside the crowns and chew the wax, and are in turn combed out and eaten mid-air. Everything is frantic. Nothing rests. A frond grows a foot while you watch, unfurling like a fern in timelapse, and is stripped bare ten minutes later. A flier collides with another and they tangle, spinning to the soft pumice where they shriek and thrash and then separate.

At ground level, under that chaos, it is oddly protected. The wind rarely reaches you; it is all above. There is a warm stillness and the soft crunch of the gravel, which is not gravel but the shed teeth of the grove — old filament mats, cast-off wax combs, bone-thin husks of dead fliers, all weathered to a powder that smells of cinnamon.

People have been here.

Not many. Not recently. But enough to leave faint things. On the north side, where a basalt spur shelters a larger eddy, someone has stacked low walls of the same pumice, dry-fit, no mortar, making small, round rooms open to the sky — not to keep rain out, because there is none, but to keep the wind shear off. Inside one, there is a hearth that never burned wood. It's full of black glass slag, and beside it, a pile of long needles — plant spines, carefully shaved and fire-hardened. Tools. Picks for harvesting wax. A loom of sorts: two basalt posts with crossbars where streamers have been wound, generations of them, into a thick, amber cable that hums faintly when wind hits it.

On a stone slab there are carvings, not writing, but tallies — lines counting storm cycles, or breeding swarms, or days of dim.

A ladder, made from lashed frond-cables, still hangs over the eastern rim where the wind is cruelest, its bottom end frayed to nothing by air. Whoever used it liked the view, or needed to leave, or needed to watch for something coming up from the blue below.

The weirdest thing is the light and the weight. Because the bowl is charged, because the air is so thin and full of fine salt glass, the dim violet light does not travel straight. Around the largest fronds, you see faint halos, echoes a second late. If a flier darts past fast enough, it leaves two or three afterimages that catch up. If you stand still and breathe the warm, wax-ozone air, your own hands look slightly double. And if you jump — the pumice is so light, the warm updraft so strong — you hang a fraction too long.

The rule here is not: life needs water. The rule here is: life needs flux. Give it a gradient, any gradient — hot rock and cold wind, charged air and salt ground, stillness and turbulence — and it will find a chemistry to run on, and run so fast it screams.

The whole small country is screaming now, in organ roars and kite-squeals, fronds thrashing, streamers snapping, dust devils full of light being born and dying between the ragged trees.
