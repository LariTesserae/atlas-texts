It is not a mountain range. It is *a* mountain.

You are in the bowl at the top.

You climbed for a long time through thin, cold, switchbacked air to get here, and then, within ten steps of the rim, the air changed. It got thick. It got warm. Not hot-spring warm, not jungle warm — work-warm. The kind of warm that stays in your chest.

The bowl is small. You could walk its circumference before your tea got cold, if you could walk straight, which you can't. It's maybe a hundred and fifty meters across, a nearly perfect circle that shouldn't exist up here. The rim is a sawblade of young rock, extreme-elevation black, fresh enough to still cut. This isn't old, weather-smoothed country. The caldera or crater or blast-pocket — whatever uplifted it last year or last decade or last century — hasn't learned how to be a hill yet. Every edge is sharp. The basalt is glassy where it broke. The ash is still half ash, not yet soil, black crystals between your fingers.

And yet it is absolutely, impossibly full.

Water is the reason. It shouldn't be wet at 5,000 meters. But the bowl sits right where the clouds snag. They come up the southern face in a solid river and hit the rim and fail. They condense on contact. All day, water beads and weeps out of the rock itself. The walls sweat. The air drips even when it isn't raining. You hear it before you see it — a constant, low, loud mutter of trickle and spill. Runnels cut the inner cliffs in bright vertical lines, ten meters long and fresh and white. At the bottom, there is no lake, because something is drinking faster than it can pool.

That something is green.

The vegetation in here is emphatic, layered, elbowing for light. Lush in a way that has nothing to do with the alpine pastels below. Mats of moss a foot deep, so saturated they squelch and spring back under your boots, neon under the dark rock. Ferns the size of doors, unfurling from cracks. Vine-things that don't climb so much as plaster themselves to every surface, their roots like green wire in the wet stone. Everything is growing on top of everything else. Epiphytes on ferns on moss on rust on iron.

Because of the buildings.

They are not a village. There are too few of them, and they are too purposeful. Scattered — one here, one cut into the rim opposite, connected by catwalk. Three pump houses and a central processing drum, all built out of that late-industrial language: riveted plate, corrugated steel gone copper with rust, concrete poured in hurry, thick insulated cable. No houses. No one lives here. Or if they did, they left the machines running.

And the machines are still running. That is the sound.

Each of the weeping walls has been capped with a steel lip, a collector. The water is caught and run through gutters into a turbine housing the size of a grain silo that squats in the exact center of the bowl, where all the gradients meet. It hums. Not a high electric whine, but a deep, patient, industrial *throb*, the sound of a flywheel the size of a room that has been turning for years without stopping. Its vibration travels up through the moss into your teeth. From it, a single pylon throws cables up to the pump houses, which in turn push the water — now churned white and warm from the work — back out through ranked sprinkler arms that sweep, slow as clock hands, back and forth, back and forth, wetting the gardens that have overgrown them.

It is farming, of some kind. Automated. The arms pass over wire racks that hold more moss, harvesters that cut it in strips. Chutes that carry the stripped wool down into hoppers where it ferments. You smell it — sweet, sharp, yeasty. The whole bowl smells of green blood and hot metal and wet stone.

The light is ordinary daylight, and that is what's strangest. Up so high it should be violent, ultraviolet, thin-blue. But in here, filtered through the perpetual condensation halo and the steam off the machines, it is just... day. Soft, white, normal. 2PM on a warm Tuesday. You can see motes of ash and spore turning in it. Your shadow is not crisp. It diffuses at the edges.

It is slow. That is the rule of this place. The rock is young and sharp and wants to keep being rock. The water is patient and wants to make it dirt. The machines are slower than both — the sprinkler arms take an hour to cross their arc, the flywheel turns once every seven seconds, the hoppers fill over days and empty with a sigh. The vines grow over the railing you are holding one millimeter at a time. If you stood here long enough you would see them take it.

Fauna is rare. The air is too busy and the ground too full for most things. Occasionally a black beetle the size of a thumbnail, polished by the wet, navigates the moss like a tank. Once an hour a high-altitude swift mistakes the cloud-trap for sky and shoots over the rim, confused by the warmth, and leaves again without landing. Lichen tries to colonize the steel but the vibration shakes it off.

If you crouch and put your hand down, you feel three things at once: the warm basalt underneath, still holding the heat of its making; the cold water running over it, new from the air; and the buzz of the machine underneath both, making them meet.
