It is high.

Not high like a mountain range — high like a lifted world. A plateau that is a continent, that is the top of the world, where the air is thin and cold and absolutely clear, so bright it hurts. The light comes unfiltered, cool-white, 10 a.m. light at all hours. No haze to soften it. Shadows are cut with a knife.

There is no water here. Not in any way you can use the word. No river, no ice cap, no damp soil. The ground is a dry mineral cold — basalt ground down over a billion seasons into a fine, soft, salt-pale sand that hangs in the air, alkaline on the tongue. The sky is a deeper blue for it. Anything that would have been a lakebed is a perfect flat of salt and cracked clay, ringing underfoot. The air itself is bone-dry; static lives in everything and you can feel yourself desiccating, lips cracking open within an hour.

And yet this is a jungle.

That is the first contradiction you have to let go of.

The vegetation is total. Not as cover. As substrate. As world-stuff. There is nothing else that is the ground. What looks from a hundred kilometers away like sparse, lonely towers is actually an old-growth forest where every individual is the size of a small state, and the spaces between individuals are prairies.

They are called Pillars, because you have no other word. They are not trees — they have no leaves, no bark as you would understand bark. They are columns of living braid, of interwoven fiber. Imagine a banyan and a cactus and a loom had a child and that child decided to become architecture to spite God. Each Pillar starts wider than a city is, a buttress-rooted skirt of dead, cured fiber that has petrified into a sort of cork-stone, weathered into dunes and terraces and wind-caves. That skirt IS the land. You walk on the plant. The erosion has had its way with it for so long that the roots are hills, the fallen limbs are canyons.

Then it rises. A kilometer, two, five — straight up, narrowing, twisting slightly with Coriolis like a stalk. Its surface is not smooth. It is made of countless vertical spars, green-black and silver-blue, each as thick as a redwood, bound together by smaller vines the thickness of your body that cinch and cross-brace them. Through the gaps you can see sky on the other side. And between the spars, empty air whistles through.

Because they are dry-world plants. They do not drink. They have never touched water. Their chemistry runs on oil and wind. In the bright, cold light they photosynthesize, but what they make isn't sugar — it's wax, and turpentine, and a volatile clear sap that beads on their inner surfaces and evaporates before it can fall. They take carbon straight from the thin air and dust straight from the storms. For water they harvest the only water that exists here: the hard exhalation of animals, which they trap in felted nets near their crowns.

That is why they are sparse. A Pillar needs a hundred kilometers of empty mineral pan to feed from. From the top of one you can see the next four or five across the curve, blue-white pillars of plant-smoke in the distance, standing in a sea of nothing. That nothing is not empty, though. Between them, the ground — which is also plant, the dead fused root-mat — is covered in low things. Knee-high, rolling things. Tumbleweeds the size of houses, but light as paper, that never stop moving. Ground-creepers that are all air, lattice balls of thorned fiber that flex. Spool-mosses that are not moss, but tight, dry carpets of filament that retract when your shadow passes.

There is no building here. No wall, no road, no cut mark. Nothing has ever been built.

Because the factories were already here.

The Pillars are industrial.

This is the second contradiction you let go of when the wind hits you at this altitude and you hear it.

Each Pillar is a machine and an engine and a factory with no engineer. The wind that never stops at this elevation — cold, world-rounding, relentless — pushes through the gaps in the sparse towers. It turns something inside them. Long, dry fronds, kilometers long, hang in the interior hollow of each Pillar like loom reeds, like sails. The wind makes them spin. They beat against each other. As they beat, they ratchet. The entire trunk creaks and groans and

*thunders.*

That is the sound. At ground level between two Pillars it is deafening. Not loud like a waterfall. Loud like being inside a bell that is the size of a continent, struck continuously. A deep, industrial clanking, a shuddering THRUM-THRUM-THRUM of fiber against fiber under planetary tension, wood against wood at pressures that would crush steel. Then higher: the screaming chatter of a million dry leaves — not leaves, but dry, chitinous pressure-valves — opening and slapping shut, venting turpentine gas with a hiss that becomes a shriek. Then the bass again as a major spar shifts its weight half a millimeter after three hundred years. The air itself vibrates. You feel it in your molars. You cannot think in full sentences here and nothing lives here that tries to.

The mechanical action does work.

Up near the crown of each Pillar, where the bright light is almost violet, the wind-looms are weaving. They are not weaving cloth. They are weaving air. The ratchet-motion pulls thin filaments from glands and spins them. They make sheets of aerogel felt, kilometers wide, gossamer and terrible — filters. And they throw these sheets off into the wind.

The sky is full of them. You hadn't seen them at first because they are almost invisible, bright against bright. Gigantic sails of plant-spun plastic, drifting, tumbling, snagging on the next Pillar, dissolving into dust eaten by the next generation. The whole atmosphere churned by this manufactury, this textile mill without seamstresses, a static-electric bazaar of sails being born and shredded and reborn. That is the turbulence. This place is not stable. It is in violent, creative overproduction. The wind between the sparse Pillars is an anthill, a storm of half-finished product, of pollen that is actually industrial grit, of seeds that are riveted bolts of hard wax corkscrewing in the air looking for a place to land that doesn't exist.

And the fauna love it.

They are abundant, and they are all deaf. Or they would have to be.

They are not birds. Nothing flies with feathers in air this thin and angry. They are kite-things. Leather made of the same filament as the Pillars, stretched over hollow bone. They have no lungs — they have bellows, driven by the same wind that drives the Pillars, fluttering open and closed. You see them by the hundreds between the Pillars, riding the turbulence, screaming — because everything screams here to be heard — a high, rusted-iron shriek that cuts the deeper thunder. They are grazing. They eat wind. They slingshot their barbed tongues out and tear loose a mouthful of drifting factory felt and absorb its wax. Their exhalation — hot, just humid enough — is caught by the Pillar's waiting nets a kilometer above.

Some are huge. Inter-Pillar hunters, translucent, with a wingspan that blacks out the sun for a second as they pass, the bright light flaring through their veins. When they collide with a Pillar — which they do, constantly, and the Pillar barely shudders — they explode into a cloud of ribbon and then re-form minutes later downwind, reassembling.

Some never fly. The ground between towers boils with them. Bowl-shaped rollers, insectile, made of interlocked dry plates that clatter ear-splittingly over the root-ground. They chew the dead cork, they process it, they extrude it behind them as a compacted brick of perfect fiber-board, which the wind then buries. Lattice crabs the size of houses that climb the skirts of the Pillars and groom the spars, biting away old struts. Where they bite, new growth screams out — fast, visible. An inch an hour, creaking.

Everything creaks. The whole continent-creaking.

You stand on what you thought was soil, which is the petrified shoulder of a root older than any human lineage, in the cold bright deafening wind, in a desert that is a forest, in a factory that was never built, watching the sails of a plant that is striving to weave the sky closed, and you understand the rule of this place:

It does not need water. It needs motion. It has motion for eternity.
