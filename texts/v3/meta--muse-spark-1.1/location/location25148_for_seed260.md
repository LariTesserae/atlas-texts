It is very high.

Not high like a mountain has a top. High like the world forgot to stop building itself upward. You are on the Spine.

The basalt here is new. Raw, unweathered, still holding the heatless snap of its making. The rock came up yesterday or ten thousand years ago, it doesn't matter — time is slow here — as a smooth, black bubble the size of a parish, shouldering through older bubbles. Nothing has had time to wear it down. There are no rivers to cut it, because there is no water. The air is so dry it will crack your lips to paper in minutes. It is cool, a permanent late-October cool that lives in the stone itself. If you press your palm to it, the cold comes out of it for a long time after.

This is an alpine desert, but the wrong kind.

From a distance the slopes look like they are covered in dense, dark forest. That is the first lie the place tells. As you get closer, you see it isn't forest as you understand forest. It is lush — obscenely lush for a place that has never rained — and sparse at once. 

The vegetation grows far apart, each organism given its own huge circle of empty rock, as if courtesy demands it. No underbrush. No crowding. Just a single, massive presence every hundred meters or so, and then naked stone between.

They are not trees. They are anemones made of glass and leather.

Each one is twenty, thirty feet tall, rooted not with roots but with a fused, circular foot that has melted itself directly into the basalt and sealed. From that foot rises a trunk that isn't wood, but a tight column of layered felt — thousands of dry, overlapping leaves pressed together into a pillar. At the top, the crown opens: hundreds of long, frond-like blades, each as wide as a door, each thick and waxy and a deep, light-starved green-black.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. The air is void-dry, salt-desert dry, high-gas-giant dry. Instead they drink the sound.

That is the thing you notice next, after the height and the cold and the impossible plants. The roar.

The Spine is a flute. The new rock is full of holes — not eroded, not yet, but *emerging*. As the bubbles of basalt rose they trapped vast pockets of vacuum and thin air, and as they cooled they burst. Now the whole plateau is pocked with embryonic valleys, rimless shafts, and long, perfectly cylindrical vents that go down further than light will go. And through them all the time, the upper-atmosphere wind is falling.

It comes from above, where the air is even thinner, and drops down the side of the world, and hits the Spine, and is forced through every hole. The sound is not wind-sound. It is organ-sound, lung-sound, choir-sound. A deafening, continuous, low chord so loud you feel it as pressure on your molars. It changes note over hours as the wind shifts a degree, grinding from a B flat that makes your vision blur to a C that sits in your sternum like a second heart. It is so loud conversation is impossible. Thought is difficult. The fronds of the glass-leather trees vibrate in it.

They feed on it. That's the only way to say it.

Each frond is covered in microscopic cilia, stiff as quartz hairs. When the Deafening Note hits them at the resonant frequency, they shiver violently and generate a faint triboelectric charge. You can see it in the dimness — the whole crown of each plant corona'd with St. Elmo's fire, a slow, cool, blue-green glow crawling over the edges. That is their photosynthesis. Not light. Sound into spark. Vibration into life. That's why they grow lush in the dry and dim. The lower the light, the clearer you see them glow.

The light here is dim by distance, not by cloud. The sun — if it is a sun — never rises above a thin smear on the horizon, a cold white line like a distant city. The sky is not blue. It is the color of old slate, immense and uniform and wrong. And it breaks down if you look at it too long.

This is a 3.0 weirdness place. Reality has a thin spot.

Elevation here is not just height. It is a disagreement about direction. Gravity is supposed to pull down. Down here is mostly down, but not always. Stand between two of the great sound-plants and you will see dust you kicked up fall sideways for a moment before remembering itself. Look out over the open valley — a regional, multi-ridge expanse of emerging, bubble-rock hills stretching thirty miles to the haze — and sometimes a further ridge will be *above* you when you blinked and it was below. The world hasn't decided on its coordinate system yet. It was built too fast. The lichen on the rocks grows in two directions at once.

If you climb a little higher, to where the air gets even thinner and the sound gets even louder, you find the built things.

There was never any animal life here to build them, and there still isn't. The ground is innocent of footprints, scat, burrows, bones. Nothing eats the sound-plants; they live and eventually topple and become new felt for new plants. Nothing hums or howls except the rock itself.

But someone came, or something did, a long time ago, while the rock was still soft enough to work with hand tools.

There are cairns. Not trail markers. Each cairn is a careful tower of basalt shards balanced impossibly — a tall, precarious stack that should fall over in the wind but doesn't, because the gravity here is polite enough to look away. Near each cairn, sunk into the rock around its base, are shallow, bowl-like depressions, polished by hands, primitive work with stone hammers. In each bowl is a pinch of fine, pale powder. It isn't dust. It is ground-up frond — the builders harvested the sound-plants.

And arranged around the bowls: loom-weights. Simple, hand-knapped basalt doughnuts, with a groove for twine long since gone. No skeletons. No houses. Just these little workstations, one per plant, scattered sparsely across the open highland, hundreds of feet apart, like a hermit craft-village that wanted nothing to do with itself.

What were they weaving? The fronds are fibrous when dead. When you pull the dried layers apart, they separate into long, strong threads that hold a charge. If you braid enough of them together and string them between two vents, they'd sing louder than any single rock could.

In the center of the valley there is one such thing. I don't think the builders made it. I think the place made it out of their attempts.

It is a web, or a harp, or a loom the size of a hamlet. Cables of braided black fiber as thick as your wrist, strung from plant to plant to stone spire, vibrating so violently in the deafening wind that they are a blur. They do not break. Where they intersect, reality breaks instead. The eye slides off those nodes. The angles are wrong. You can see, through a gap in the weave, not the other side of the valley but the underside of the rock you are standing on, as if the world was folded and pinned there with thread.

The whole thing moves slow. That is its dynamic. The cables sway one inch per hour. The glow on the plants brightens and dims over days. The basalt underneath you creaks once a century as a new bubble shoulders up a millimeter more. It is not a fast place. It is a patient place, becoming itself loudly and without water, without animals, without hurry.

If you stay long enough, your bones begin to vibrate in sympathy. Your teeth glow faintly. The cold gets less important.

You understand the rule: Here, sound is water. Weight is a suggestion. And anything that can be still enough, long enough, gets to become real.
