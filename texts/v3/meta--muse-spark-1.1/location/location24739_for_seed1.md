It is low the way the bottom of the world is low.

Not a valley cut between mountains — there were never mountains here, or if there were they gave up a billion years ago. This is the final shape of rock when it has had all argument eroded out of it. A shallow, shield-wide dish in the craton, so broad you only know you are in it because the horizon is slightly higher in every direction than where you stand. The stone is black and purple and rust-red, gneiss polished to a water finish, pitted with the kind of pits that take glaciers to make. Near-flattened. A knee-high ripple might be the highest feature for a mile. In the dim, it looks wet even where it isn't.

And almost everywhere, it IS wet.

The air is cold in the way that air gets when it never really warms up — not bracing, not sharp, just a deep, old, sub-zero that has settled into the stone. Your breath falls instead of rises. The sky is low and the color of wet newspaper, and it sweats. Constant condensation beading and running out of nothing onto everything. The rocks weep from their pores. Shallow pans of water that are almost but not quite ice stand in every imperceptible low. Brine-slush, oil-slicked with bacterial films that are not plants, that are not quite anything. Where you step, if you can find a place to step, water squeezes up between your toes from rock that looks solid.

There is no plant life. None. No lichen even. No green. No brown dry stalk. This is a mineral place. Water on stone on water on stone.

And it is full.

The first thing you misunderstand is that you are looking at terrain. For a long stretch to the horizon, what you thought was terrain is animal.

They are packed. Not herd-packed, not flock-packed — saturated. Tessellated. There is no room between presences. A living pavement that extends past the immediate, past the middle distance, past where the dim eats detail. Hundreds of thousands of them, maybe millions, shoulder to shell to flank, occupying every inch of the dish.

They are big. Dog-big, man-big, small-truck-big. At first, in the gloom, you think they are seals, or maybe a rookery of penguins that have forgotten how to be birds. Smooth backs slick with condensate, blubberous, huddled against the cold. Then one shifts and you hear it.

Sound here is a physical weight.

A low iron clank as a massive flank moves. A pressure-valve hiss. A cough that is a piston firing. A deep, arrhythmic chuffing that you feel in the ancient rock under you.

They are industrial animals. There is no other word for it, although there was never any factory that made them and there is no structure anywhere to house them — no wall, no hut, no ruin, not a nail. The industry is in them.

You get closer because you have to. The one nearest you is on its side, wedged against two others so tight its skin dimples. Its back splits along a seam that isn't a wound. Inside, you see it — a boiler. Not like a boiler, a boiler. A ribbed, soot-caked iron lung, glowing a sullen orange at its core despite the cold, fed by the mineral slush it snouts from the cracks. Its heart is a forge. Its breath, when it exhales through a short stack that rises from between its shoulder blades, is a gout of steam and coal-smoke and hot, wet air that instantly condenses and rains back down on all of them. The smell is startling: hot iron, blood, wet earth, ozone.

Another one, smaller, ratchets open along its belly and an assembly line moves inside it. Tiny, blind, mewling things ride on hooks, getting stamped. A leg attached. A vent hole punched clean through.

Another one is nothing but a mouth and a grinding wheel, slowly, endlessly lapping at the ancient stone, turning it to slurry it excretes into the shallow water, which the others drink.

They are abundant because they eat each other only incidentally, but they eat the world constantly. They eat the water, the brine, the dissolved metals. They excrete heat, which is why the water stays liquid in this cold. They are the reason nothing else needs to grow here. They are predator and prey and factory and product. The biggest ones lie still for days and get colonized by smaller ones that build nests of thin wire and carbon fiber extruded from their own spines.

And none of this is stable.

This is the 3.0/3 part, the part where reality has been rubbed as thin as the stone.

If you look down the length of the basin, perspective is wrong. A creature a quarter mile away appears larger than the one at your feet. When one of the large boiler-backs vents, the steam doesn't diffuse — it hangs, then peels sideways into a sky that is lower behind it than in front. Sound arrives before the action that made it. You hear the clank of a massive one rolling over ten seconds before it actually rolls, and when it does, the place where it was remains occupied by a warm, after-image animal that you can still hear huffing, until you blink and it sputters out like a bad bulb.

The rules here:

1. Heat flows backward. The animals are hottest on the outside. The stone is coldest just under the water.

2. Nothing is built because it cannot be built — anything stacked or joined becomes alive and walks off to join the packed crowd. If you piled stones, by morning the pile would have grown a small smokestack and be shivering.

3. Water remembers being worked. If you cup it and hold it up, you can see tiny toolmarks in it for a second before it drips.

4. There are more animals than space, so space compensates. You can walk between two that are touching and find there is a mile of ringing, steaming, clattering alley between them that shouldn't exist, and when you come out you are somewhere else in the dish, but still surrounded.

You stand on a tiny exposure of true rock — a palm-sized island — and you are shoulder to shoulder to hot, breathing, clanking sides. The noise is immense now. Not just loud — encompassing. Clinks and groans and releases and the unimaginably loud slosh of thousands of bodies in brine. The dim light catches every plume of breath and makes the whole valley, this town-sized living mill, look like it is inside a breathing machine.

The condensation rains down again. The rocks weep. A gigantic creature near the center of the bowl, barely visible, lifts a crane-like neck that ends in a hammer and brings it down on the anvil of its own back, ringing the basin like a bell that has always been ringing.
