It is not deep. It is just below.

You go down not very far — three flights of iron stairs from the street-level maintenance door that someone left propped with a brick years ago, down past the OFFICIAL USE ONLY stencils bleached to ghosts — and the air changes. It gets warm. It gets soft. The street noise doesn't fade so much as it gets translated.

This was a service vault. A storm cistern, a spur of the old transit cut, a municipal lung built in brick and poured concrete a century ago and then forgotten when they rerouted the river. Cathedral-scale, not city-scale. One room that thinks it's a town. About a hundred meters long, twenty across, vaulted. You could fit a hamlet in here if you didn't mind the ceiling. And a hamlet is exactly what grew.

**The substrate.**

Everything is plant, but not feral in the way surface ruins go feral. This is settled. Established. Long-existed and in-balance with its own weight.

The brick walls are still there, sweating. You can see them, but only in patches, where the root-mats have pulled back like a curtain. Mostly the walls are bark now, or something that decided to be bark. A fig species that learned to be architecture — its aerial roots dropped from the dark vault overhead, thick as your thigh, fused together into pillars, into buttresses, into walls that hold and are held at the same time. The original columns are inside the trees now, the trees are inside the columns. You can knock on a trunk and hear concrete.

The floor is not floor. It's a woven deck of living wood and dead wood, rhizome and rebar. Where the engineers left catwalks, the vine has taken the line and made it structural. Mangrove-like knees rise out of shallow, oily water, then flatten into walkways, then rise again into huts. The huts are not built on the vegetation. They are grown out of it and then parted out with tin and salvaged brick — an urban logic still legible. A street with addresses. A corner store, a workshop row, a little square where three root-pillars leave a gap. Doors cut into living trunks and fitted with industrial jambs that creak as the tree grows around them.

It is sparse. That's the strange thing for a place so full. There is a lot of emptiness between things. A big open dark down the middle of the vault where nothing stands, just humid air and hanging roots that never reached the bottom. Ten or twelve structures total, scattered like they are being polite to each other. Much more space than occupants. The town could hold more, but it doesn't.

**The damp.**

It is never wet, not really. Water: 1.2. The river is not here. But the vault breathes condensation.

Warm air from above falls in through the grates and the elevator shaft and hits the cool mass of root and concrete and exhales. All day, all night, it beads. The undersides of every leaf, every pipe, every leaf that is a pipe, drips. A slow, irregular tick, tick, tick on the tin roofs, on the deck, on the tarps. Occasional rain comes straight down the old overflow channel at the north end — a brown, sudden pour that lasts twenty minutes, fills the low pockets between roots into temporary amber pools, then is gone, drunk completely by the mat. The rest of the time the pools remain as damp circles, a foot across, full of wriggling.

You smell warm compost, iron, green sap.

Light is dim — 0.6 — and it comes from the wrong places. A few high grates still open to the street, far above, throw down solid white columns in which dust and seed-fluff and wings turn. Work light — caged, amber, industrial bulbs — strung on sagging cables, powered by something below. They buzz and swing a little when something big flies past. And the plants make some of their own: pale, veined lantern leaves that swell with bioluminescence when touched, then fade slowly. The children here — if you see children — slap them as they pass to light their way to the latrine.

**The factory.**

The tech did not die. It was adopted.

Down at the south end, where the vault narrows, are the machines. Not ruins. Running. Industrial, earnest, scaled for production, 1920s to 1950s heavy iron that was meant to pump water and now pumps something else.

A boiler, fed not with coal but with compressed leaf-litter bricks and rendered oil from fruit that smells like hot butter. A belt-driven system of looms and presses and grinders that should have seized decades ago, but the vines have taken over lubrication. They ooze into the bearings, a clear sap that smokes slightly. Flywheels turn, wrapped in strangler fig. Pistons sigh. The whole system is making... things. Not for export. For itself. Veneer sheets pressed from leaves. Cables twisted from fiber. Jars of dark honey and sap-oil stacked on pallets.

The plants need the machines as much as the machines need the plants. The roots have learned to fruit directly into the hoppers. One species — glossy, with leaves like conveyor belts — grows only along the vibrating handrail of the main engine, its seeds germinating in the grease.

Nobody tends it constantly. It tends itself. When a belt snaps, a man or a woman comes out of one of the scattered huts, with a wrench that is also a pruning hook, and fixes it. Then goes back inside and leaves a lot of space around themselves again. Sparse.

**The overwhelming.**

The fauna is not background. It is the weather.

At any moment, the air between structures — that polite emptiness — is taken. Not with birds, though there are birds: soot-black swifts that nest in the brick and never see the sky, fat doves that walk the root streets. Mostly insects, bats, amphibians.

Moths the size of dinner plates, the color of wet concrete, that cling to the lights until their dust makes a second dim light. Beetles like polished bolts, clicking along the pipes, farming aphids that farm the fig. Clouds of gnats that move as one body and then, inexplicably, spell a shape in the air and break. And bats — hundreds of small, warm, leather-faced bats that pour out of pipe mouths at the false dusk and stitch the space between pillars with screaming you feel in your teeth. Tree frogs, thousands of them, each no bigger than a thumbnail, living in the bromeliad cups formed where root meets wall, all inflating their throats at once.

The sound is loud. 2.5. You have to raise your voice.

It's not white noise. It's layered, industrial and animal and arguing. The clank-thump-sigh of the engine, the tick of condensation amplified by tin, the sawing chorus of crickets living inside the boiler insulation, the frogs, the bats. The figs, when they grow fast — and they are growing fast, always — creak loudly enough to sound like doors. The whole vault hums, actively, dynamically. Things eating, things being eaten, things being pressed into veneer.

And weirdness — 2.4 — because none of this should hold together. The rules here are a little bent.

Gravity is slightly wrong for plants; they grow sideways, down, then up again for no reason. Some of the figs have fruits that cure into perfect, threaded machine screws. You find them rattling around in the oil pan. The water that condenses on the walls sometimes condenses upward, crawling toward the ceiling before it drips. And in the largest empty space in the middle of the vault, where nothing has been built, there are no moths, no bats, no sound. You can walk through it and feel the vault listening through you. People in the scattered houses leave that space alone. They built around that absence the way a town builds around a plaza.

If you stay long enough — warm, damp, half-deaf from the racket — you start to understand the settlement logic: sparse because anything denser would be drowned out, would disturb the balance. This is an urban place that has become an ecosystem that has become a factory that has become a grove. It doesn't want to be bigger. It has been here long enough to be weathered, to be exactly what it is. Established.

When you climb back up, the street air feels thin and too bright and completely empty of wings.
