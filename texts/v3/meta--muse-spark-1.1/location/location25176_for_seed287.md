It's below. Not deep in the way a cave is deep — just low.

You come down by a service stairwell cut straight into the laterite, its steps worn into shallow bowls in the middle. The concrete retaining walls weep. At the top it was 36 degrees outside and dry, a white sky; every meter you drop it gets hotter by a degree and damper by a third. By the bottom the air is soup.

The place is small. You can see its whole boundary at once if you step back, which you can't really do. It's a single industrial pocket, a perfect oval like a kettle sunk into the earth. Maybe forty meters across, maybe less in the haze. Old plans, if there were any, would call it Settling Sump 3 or Condensate Reservoir C. Now it's just The Hot Room.

It was poured, not dug — you can see the shutter marks in the walls, though they have been softened. Everything is established, settled, in-equilibrium-with-itself. The erosion here isn't violent. It's patient. Sixty years of hot condensation running down vertical concrete has rounded every sharp edge into a lip, a flowstone drape. Rebar shows here and there like old bone, rusted into smooth stalactites. The floor, which was once flat, is now a shallow basin with channels cut by water that isn't quite a stream.

There is no stream. Water: 0.8 out of 3 is what this is. It's not a lake, not a river. It's the air being too tired to hold itself. It collects on the vaulted ceiling thirty feet above you — a ceiling made of dark brick and soot-blackened iron beams — and it gathers, and it falls. Drip. Drip. Drip from a thousand points. It hits leaves and makes them shudder. It hits hot metal and vanishes with a hiss. In the lowest groove of the floor, there is a runnel maybe as wide as your boot, dark water moving slow as oil, disappearing under a grate.

It's dim. Up there, the rectangular mouth of the stairwell lets in a brutal column of daylight that turns to amber three meters down and dies. The only other light is industrial and failing: a caged sodium work-light still burning on a leaning stanchion, buzzing orange, and a few thin shafts where roots have forced the service hatch. That is not enough light for what is growing here.

And yet it's lush. Obscenely lush for a room with no sun.

Because there is no animal life. None. That's the first wrong thing you notice after the heat. No mosquitoes in the damp. No cockroaches under the boiler housings. No rats, no pigeons that fell down the stairs. Not even a fungus gnat. The air should be whining. It is silent except for the machines and the water.

So the plants have not been checked. They have grown with no mouth to eat them.

They came from above — wind-borne, water-borne, boot-borne — fern spores, philodendron, pothos, some kind of wilting taro that shouldn't live in this heat. In the absence of predation they have become something else. They grow dense, layered, hundreds of presences per cubic meter. Not a jungle floor but a hanging building.

Every surface is occupied. The walls are felted with a moss that is almost black-green and shines like wet velvet. Out of that grow fronds, some as fine as eyelashes, some as big as doors, overlapping, touching, holding each other up. Vines as thick as your wrist have found the old pipe runs and followed them, not strangling them but using them as trellises, spiraling for twenty, thirty feet without a single leaf lost to insects, so every leaf is perfect, waxy, entire.

They have learned metal. That's the strange part.

The built remnants here are scattered — not a town, just leftovers. A pump house the size of a garden shed, its door rusted open. A catwalk that goes nowhere now, its grate woven through with aerial roots. A workbench, a toppled locker spilling orange coveralls that have become a planter. And the machines themselves: three huge condensate pumps, each as big as a truck engine, still turning.

They have been turning for decades.

Someone left the geothermal loop on. Deep heat comes up, drives a piston slow — one stroke every twenty seconds — turns a flywheel black with grease and leaf-mold. The flywheel drives a belt. The belt is gone, replaced by vine. The vine is being pulled, slowly, slowly, around the wheel. *Weirdness 2.3 is that negotiation.* The plants aren't just growing on the factory. They are apprenticing to it.

Leaves near the boiler — which still sighs out a low, superheated breath from a cracked seal — have come in copper-colored, with a sheen like verdigris, and they ring faintly when water strikes them. Roots have grown into the control panels, and where copper wire is exposed, root hairs have tinned themselves, bright and conductive. The pump that still moves the floor runnel has a pressure gauge, and a fig leaf has grown directly over its face, translucent, so the needle's shadow can be seen through the leaf when it twitches.

It is loud. Not human-loud, not city-loud. Industrial-loud-while-empty loud. The drip from the ceiling is a constant rain on taut leaves, a thousand drums. The big pump does its asthmatic *CHUFF— hhhh— CHUFF— hhhh* with a deep subsonic knock that you feel in your molars. The hot water hisses where it meets cooler air. The sodium light hums at 60 hertz. And underneath it all, because the air is so saturated and dense with foliage, there's a low, almost inaudible crackle of growth, of turgor pressure, of cells dividing. It is deafening in a way that makes you hold your breath.

Everything is slow. That is the rule of this pocket world.

Water never rushes, it accumulates and falls. The flywheel never spins, it creeps. The vines do not shoot, they inch — but because nothing ever eats them back, an inch a day for twenty years is a wall. You can stand here for ten minutes and see nothing move except water, then look away and realize a tendril has found your boot eyelet.

The other rule is: no animal may enter. Not because of a sign. Because the air itself is tuned wrong. There is too much oxygen here, too much exhalation with no inhalation. Ferns have always exhaled soft poisons to protect themselves — tannins, alkaloids. Here, with no animals to cull the bitter individuals, those chemistries have concentrated. The black moss smells faintly of garlic and pennies. The damp tastes metallic if you breathe through your mouth. It itches at the back of your throat. You understand after a minute why nothing sings here. This lush cathedral defends itself not with thorns but with heat and copper and a little quiet toxicity. It's not hostile. It's just full. There's no vacancy in its dense volume for anything that moves on its own.

If you stay long enough — and the dim orange light and the thumping pump and the dripping make you want to stay, make you drowsy — the condensation on your arms beads up and does not evaporate. You start to feel like another damp surface for things to grow on.
