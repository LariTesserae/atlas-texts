It is not on the mountain. It *is* the top of the mountain.

Someone took the peak off at about four thousand meters and capped it with a cylinder. Not a building on a summit — a summit replaced by a building. From below you would think it was a water tower, or a grain silo made for a god. A perfect round wall of new concrete, still the color of cold ash, still sharp enough at the lip to cut cloud. No softening. No lichen yet, no smoothed corners. The pour lines are still visible, the tie-holes still dark, like a thing that was finished last week and then immediately abandoned.

Inside is one room.

A single cathedral-volume, maybe thirty meters across, open to the sky. And the sky is inside it, because the room is full.

Not flooded. *Full*. To the brim and slightly over, a lens of water held by the wall itself with no roof, no cover. At this elevation the air is thin and fast and should be taking it away minute by minute, but it doesn't. Surface tension on an impossible scale. The wind hits the sharp lip of the concrete and parts around it. The water holds.

You understand it is built when you get close enough to see the bottom, because there is no bottom that is earth. Everything under the water is made.

The floor is a grate, then another grate, then machinery. Industrial, not sleek — riveted steel, enameled tanks, old green paint bubbling under water, great slow screws and chain drives and heat-exchange manifolds the size of houses, all packed together without a centimeter wasted. It is a factory floor with no floor and no workers. Pumps the size of trucks breathe once every few minutes. Valves turn themselves one degree per hour.

And from every joint, every seam, every bolt-head, grows something.

This is not a place where vegetation has overgrown the machine. It has been *grown into it* as a component. Lush does not cover it — lush is insufficient. It is packed. There is no room between presences. Freshwater kelp, or something built to act like kelp, rises in warm chestnut and acid-green curtains from filter housings. Mangrove-like prop roots split poured concrete and plunge into oil sumps, white root-tip and black oil coexisting. Vines as thick as your wrist — not woody, but watery, translucent — have been trained along every pipe and catwalk, zip-tied and clipped with industrial plant ties that have now been swallowed by their own growth. Sheets of floating heart-leaf cover the surface near the edges, a living lid, trembling.

The water is warm. Bath warm. Blood warm. At this height it should be ice, but waste heat from below holds it at late-summer. You can see the thermoclines as refractive shimmers when a pump shudders. Light comes straight down, normal daylight, high-mountain white-gold, and it goes through ten meters of clear, warm water and hits the factory-jungle and comes back green.

There are no animals. That is the first thing your body knows is wrong. No guppies, no larvae, no water beetles, no birds landing to drink. Not even a mosquito. Only plant, and machine, and water. The leaves move but not from current — from their own slow hydraulic pressure. You see a seed pod the size of a fist open over the course of a minute and release a puff of oil that becomes a silver bead and rises. You see a root crack a gauge face. Absolutely silent work.

Because it is quiet here in a way that high places rarely are. The water kills most sound. The machines are designed to be quiet, or they have grown quiet, their bearings now sheathed in biofilm. What you hear is your own blood in your ears, and when you put your ear to the water, the faint, very distant tick of metal contracting, the sigh of dissolved oxygen leaving a leaf. A single bubble ascending through vine tangle, touching leaf after leaf on its way up: *tap... tap... tap...*

If you climb down the interior ladder — they left a ladder, its rungs already half-consumed by a bright, fleshy moss that is not moss — you are submerged without getting wet in the way you expect, because the pressure doesn't change much. It's like descending into warm air that has weight. Your hair lifts. Dust motes that should sink hang suspended, because every cubic centimeter is already occupied.

The weirdness is not that this impossible pond exists at 4000 meters, held up by a sharp new wall. The weirdness is that it makes perfect, practical sense once you are inside. Of course you would build a total environment. Of course you would eliminate natural substrate, because natural substrate is inefficient. Of course you would stack your life support — grow chambers, heat loops, scrubbers, pumps — into a single dense plug. Of course you would fill it with water, because water is structure and filter and womb. Of course you would leave out animals, because animals want, and want is noisy. Plants accept.

It is young. You can tell by the edges. Nothing has been eroded. The concrete lip is still a knife. The serial numbers on the pumps are still legible. The place hasn't had time to become ruin. It's still becoming itself. The vines are still learning which pipes are hottest. The water is still learning how to be still.

It changes slowly. Over a day, a valve stem extended by a plant will have rotated a quarter turn. Over a week, a new leaf will have sealed a catwalk. The surface of the water is always exactly at the top of the wall, never spilling, never low — a circular horizon of water that meets sky. If you stand on the outside service rim — thin air burning your lungs, the entire range laid out below you like crumpled paper — you can put your hand out and touch the meniscus where it bulges just over the concrete, warm and impossible and perfectly flat, and on the other side of that transparent skin, three feet down, a flower like a white lamp is opening in total silence, for nobody.
