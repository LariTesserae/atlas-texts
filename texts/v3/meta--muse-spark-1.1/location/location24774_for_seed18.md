It is not on a mountain. It is a mountain, or what is left of one.

Somewhere very high up — high enough that the air has thinned and gone blue at the edges — someone built a room where a summit used to be. Not a building on a mountain. A replacement. The work is total. There is no rock underneath, no soil, no scree. If you peeled back the floor you would find more floor. If you drilled down you would find girders and struts and the undersides of other floors, all the way down to cloud level and past it.

They built it at hamlet-scale. You can walk its entire circuit in twenty minutes if you don't stop, and you will stop. It has boundaries. A parapet, rust-kissed and thick as a house wall, runs the whole rim, and beyond that there is just fall and sky. Inside the parapet is a single, vast bowl — not quite a crater, not quite an atrium, more like the inside of a cooling tower that someone forgot to roof.

That is the shape of the place.

And because it is high, the wind never stops trying to get in. It comes up the manufactured cliffside — ten thousand feet of riveted iron and concrete weathered to the color of old teeth — hits the outer wall and spills over the lip in a continuous, deafening pour. It is not a howl. It is too big for that. It is a low, white roar, a physical pressure that makes the walkways tremble in their brackets. Under it, under that sky-voice, is the other sound: the Works.

In the center of the bowl, taking up most of it but not touching the sides, is the Engine.

I say engine, but it is more like a mill that mills air. A lattice of black iron pistons as thick as old trees rises three stories and then stoops again, breathing. Slowly. Oil-black cylinders lift, sigh, and sink on a cycle so gradual you can barely see movement until you realize the shadow has shifted. It has been breathing this way long enough that its joints have polished themselves into mirrors. It has been here long enough that it has stopped being a machine and become a kind of weathered geology. Its paint has blistered and gone. Lichen has failed, but rust has learned patience. The rust here is not flaking. It is smooth, established, like varnish.

Nothing in this place is new. Everything has settled into the shape the wind and weight and heat allow it to have. Beam-ends are rounded. Bolt-heads are soft. The floor plates, each the size of a dining table, have bowed almost imperceptibly in the middle from a century of feet and warmth.

It is warm. That is the first strange thing about this height. It should be freezing and it is not. The Engine sweats heat. Even when the pistons are at the bottom of their slow exhale, the iron stays too hot to touch in places, just hot enough that when the clouds come — and they do, every afternoon, boiling up over the parapet like a slow tide — they don't rain.

They condense.

Water was never brought here, there is no river, no aquifer this high. Water arrives as fog. It rolls over the rim and meets the hot iron and the warm air above it and becomes, briefly, actual rain. Not a storm. A thought of rain. It beads on every surface. It collects along the under-edges of girders until the surface tension gives up and it falls in fat, irregular drops onto what grows below.

Because everything grows.

This is the second strange thing, and the one you notice immediately after the noise. The bowl is lush in a way that has nothing to do with soil. There is no soil. The entire substrate is manufactured: steel grating, concrete aggregate, iron mesh. And from every joint, every seam, every place where two plates meet and allow a millimeter of shadow, vegetation thrusts out with a kind of insulted abundance.

Not weeds. Jungle.

At this altitude there should be lichen and a brave pine. Here there are ferns with fronds two meters long, still dripping. There are vines as thick as your wrist that have learned to use conduit pipe as a trellis, wrapping it until the pipe has disappeared. There is moss — sheets, mattresses, entire small hills of moss — so deep and saturated that when you step on grating you expect a clang and instead get a soft, sucking hush. The leaves are large, waxy, designed to catch fog, to tip, to funnel. They have evolved, in the last hundred years, to live off rust and condensation and heat-haze. Their roots have no ground to invade, so they have become epiphytic on industry. They root in rust dust. They root in each other.

The bowl is almost empty, and that is what makes it feel vast. The density of it — there is almost nothing per cubic meter. If you stand on the maintenance walkway that rings the Engine, you look out across twenty meters of humid, warm, empty air to the inner wall of the bowl. The air itself has presence. Dust motes and seed-hairs and rust flakes hang in the light, lit from above, not blowing because the wind roars *over* but not *into* — the parapet cuts it. Inside the bowl the air is suspended, thick, still except for the engine's slow push. You can see a single dragonfly cross that space for a long time.

And there is fauna. So much of it that you realize the place is, in fact, crowded — just not with things at human scale.

Every leaf has something on it. Beetles with iridescent backs that match the oil-slick rainbow of old grease. Ants running in black rivers along a seam that must be, to them, a continent-long canyon filled with brown sugar rust. Small frogs, impossible at this elevation, fat and green-gold, that live in the water that pools in the cupped leaves of bromeliads that have somehow found their way up here and now grow directly out of the side of a boiler. They call when the fog comes, a small desperate chorus that you can only hear if you press your ear close during a lull in the engine stroke.

Above them, birds. Not many species, but many individuals. Swifts that live forever on the wing outside, dipping in over the parapet to snatch moths. And moths — big as hands, the color of concrete, that rest on the hot iron until their wings are warm enough to beat. Their caterpillars eat the ferns and have incorporated iron oxide into their silk, so their cocoons look like little rusty thimbles screwed onto pipes.

Even higher, things that shouldn't be. Bats, or things that have become bat-like, that cling to the underside of the pistons and ride them up and down, licking condensation from the steel.

The rule of this world is simple and was written before anyone alive remembers: keep the engine warm, but not hot. The plants understand it. The animals understand it. If the engine ever cooled, the water would stop. If it ever got too hot, the mist would burn off and everything would dry and crisp. So the bowl exists in a moving balance.

The vines have learned not to jam the pistons — or rather, the vines that jammed the pistons died when the piston scraped them off, and the ones that grew *around* lived. Natural selection in an industrial garden. There are clean channels worn in the green where steel moves against steel, edges kept neat as a haircut by slow abrasion. In places the maintenance crew — long gone — left their tools hanging on hooks; now there are bird's nests made of stripped wire insulation and fern fiber balanced on the handles.

Light falls straight down. At noon it is just white, normal, honest mountain light, too bright to look at. It makes the wet leaves steam. The steam rises a little and then condenses again. A small cycle inside the big, slow one.

The dam comes every evening when the clouds clear. All at once the roar outside gets louder as the outside air cools, and the inside bowl, still warm, exhales its dampness in a single cloud, straight up, like the Engine is breathing out a ghost. Then the temperature drops a few degrees, the frogs stop, and for a few minutes you can hear the metal ticking as it contracts.

Then the pistons lift again.
