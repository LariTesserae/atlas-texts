It is not the top. The top is above this place and is uninhabitable — just wind-scoured hornblende and snow that never softens, cutting the sky.

This place is just below the top, where the mountain decided to hold its breath.

A bowl, maybe forty meters across. A cirque bitten clean out of the ridge, open on one side to a drop that falls away into white distance. You can see the curve of the atmosphere from here. The air is thin and terrible and bright. The light at this elevation has no mercy, no diffusion. It comes unfiltered through six miles of nothing and strikes rock and ice and makes them ache. Even on overcast days it is bright — snow-light bounced up from clouds below you. The shadows are knife-edged. The stones cast shadows that look solid enough to hold.

It is cold in the way high places are cold: dry in your lungs, and yet — damp. That is the trick of this bowl.

The wind comes up from the lowlands, forced up the cliff, cooling as it climbs. By the time it spills over the lip of the cirque it is exhausted. It stalls. And in stalling it gives up what it was carrying. The whole bowl sweats. Condensation beads on every surface that isn't in direct sun. In the shade of a rock you can watch it happen: a film, then a bead, then a bead that swells and runs. The rocks here are dark — a dense, black-green basalt veined with glassy quartz — and they are always wet on their north sides, always rimed with a caul of clear ice on their south.

No soil. No lichen even, not here. Nothing that lives by root or leaf could take the UV and the nightly freeze. There is only mineral.

And what has been done to mineral.

The bowl is crowded. Not with people — there is no one here now — but with structures. Hundreds of them. Low, dense, near-touching. Cairns, but that word doesn't do it. A cairn is a marker. These are small houses for the wind.

Every visitor, over centuries perhaps, has added one. Flat stones, hand-split with mauls, stacked without mortar. Some are only knee-high, three stones high. Some are as tall as your chest, complicated towers with lintels and small, deliberate windows all the way through them, so that when the wind pours through they make a low chord. Most are bowed under a coat of ice that has grown season after season, melt and refreeze, until the stone inside looks like a bone in amber. In the shadows between them, pockets of water persist — not ponds, not streams. Lenses. A cupped stone holds a palm of water that will be ice by nightfall. A crevice holds a tongue of old snow that never quite leaves, just shrinks to gritty beads and swells again.

The density creates its own climate. Outside, on the ridge, the wind is a howl. Inside the bowl, among the stones, it is braided. It splits into a hundred small winds. That is the sound here: mostly quiet, but not silent. The sound of high places when they are trying to be quiet and failing.

Tick. A stone contracts.
Drip. In a sunlit window, an icicle loses its grip and falls three inches onto older ice.
A sigh, as wind moves through a hollow tower and out again.
Under it all, a faint, almost inaudible fizz, like soda water — the sound of condensation forming on cold basalt, thousands of microdroplets appearing at once.

The stones themselves are weathered in the way things get when they have stopped eroding and have come to an agreement with the world. Their edges are round. Their tops are dished where water has sat for a thousand melt-freeze cycles and patiently pried. They lean, but they don't fall. The whole bowl feels settled, established — not ruinous, but completed. As if it reached the number of towers it needed and now just maintains.

Scattered among them are the other marks. A low wall, no higher than your calf, in a rough C-shape to break wind, with a blackened patch in front of it where fires have been made from things carried up from below — you can see the faint soot stain on the rock, and the chips where someone knapped a flake off a quartz vein and left the debitage. A ring of stones large enough to sit in, lined with a skin of ice that your body heat would melt in seconds. A wooden staff left leaning, its base frozen into the ground, its top bleached white by sun, prayer ribbons long since shredded away to colorless threads that snap in the wind.

No one stays. The rule here is written in the cold and in the light: build, but don't remain. The brightness will burn you even through cloud. The water will not be enough to drink, not without melting, and the melting will cost more heat than you have. The altitude will make a simple stacking of stones leave you gasping, head pounding.

So they come up, stack their stones close against the others — so close you have to turn sideways to move between them — add their weight and their intent to the mass, and go down again before evening. The bowl remains, damp and glittering and crowded and empty.

All day the ice grows and shrinks. All day the light moves and makes the quartz veins flash, one after another, like signals.
