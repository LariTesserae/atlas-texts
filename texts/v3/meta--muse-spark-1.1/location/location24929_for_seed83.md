It is below.

Not deep in the planetary sense. Just below - the first layer down where the building stops pretending to be for people and starts being for itself. You get to it by a door that was never locked, just heavy, and a short stairwell that turns back on itself twice so you lose which way is street-side.

The room is small. You could throw a voice across it and hear it hit the far wall in half a second, deadened. If it were a church it would be a chapel, not a nave. If it were a town it would be one intersection. About the size of a water cistern, because that is what it was.

Everything here is made. There is no exposed earth. The floor is poured concrete that was troweled smooth fifty years ago and has since gone soft at the edges. The walls are concrete too, and then, where something leaked and they patched it, brick, and where the brick failed, steel plate bolted over. The ceiling is the underside of whatever is above - thick pipes lagged in canvas and wire, crossing and recrossing until there is barely a patch of ceiling left that is actually ceiling. That is the terrain here: not ground, but enclosure built on enclosure.

It is dense. That is the first thing your body knows in the dark. Not crowded with people - with presence. The air is taken up. At ankle height, the old low-pressure returns, fat as thighs, running in parallel. At knee height, a rack of smaller copper lines, green-black and sweating. At chest height, the pump housing itself, a squat industrial block the size of a chest freezer, its paint blistered to alligator scales, its inspection plates labeled in a stencil font that no one uses anymore. Above that, electrical conduit, fire lines, a newer plastic trunking that is already yellowing. You have to turn sideways to move. You have to duck under a union joint that drips. Nothing was planned to fit together; it all accumulated until the space is more material than air.

It is hot. The kind of hot that lives in basements in August and never leaves. The pump has not run in years, or maybe it runs at 3 a.m. on a timer - it doesn't matter, the heat is residual and geologic now. Concrete holding summer. Iron holding friction. The air is 102, 104, but it doesn't move, so it lays on your skin. Your sweat doesn't evaporate, it stays and salts.

And it is damp. Not wet. There is no standing water, no stream. This is a drier place that remembers water. The moisture comes as condensation because the cold line is still cold somewhere upstream and the hot air touches it. Beads form on the copper, fatten, and fall. One every twenty or thirty seconds in the left corner. Tink on the steel plate. There is a place on the floor where this falling has been happening for a decade and the concrete is pitted into a shallow, dark cup that holds maybe a spoonful. When it overflows, it finds a hairline crack and is gone. Along the base of the walls, a tide-mark of calcite and rust, white and orange lace. The air tastes like hot wet stone and old oil.

There is almost no light. 0.1 is the right kind of number for it. There is a dead bulb in a wire cage over the door. There is, on the pump itself, one red pilot LED the size of a grain of rice that never turns off, fed by some trickle on a different circuit. It doesn't illuminate. It just locates itself. When your eyes adjust after ten minutes, you start to see the room by it - the way the sweating copper catches that red and turns it into a dull line. You see by heat, almost. The rest is darkness that your hands have to learn.

The erosion here is established. This isn't collapse. This is the opposite - things have settled into being this way. Rust has come and done its work and stopped where the oil was. The concrete has spalled to reveal smooth river stones of aggregate. The floor where people walked once is hollowed a half inch lower. Mineral deposits have built tiny stalactites off the canvas lagging, not sharp, blunted, the color of teeth. Everything has a skin. If you knocked a pipe with a wrench it would sound solid and thick, not hollow and compromised. The room has reached an agreement with time.

Vegetation is sparse but it insists. There is no soil, so it makes do. At the seam where the coldest pipe sweats directly onto the concrete wall, a band of black mold, then beyond that, a moss. Not bright green - a starved olive felt that grows only at the edges of the damp, no thicker than paper. In one place, where the red light reflects up off a puddle, a single fern. Maidenhair, maybe. Pale, etiolated, three fronds reaching sideways toward a light that isn't there. Its roots are in nothing - just a crack that holds dust and water. It shouldn't be alive. It is, barely, because hot and damp is sometimes enough.

Fauna is even rarer. You will not hear scurrying. At first you think nothing lives here. Then you notice the pale things that have decided density is protection. A woodlouse, almost transparent, moving infinitely slowly across the pump housing. A spider in the angle between conduit and ceiling that is the same color as the concrete dust, with no web because there is nothing flying - it waits, weeks at a time. Once, a cockroach antenna touches your shoe and withdraws. The air is too hot and too still for anything that needs to move fast.

Sounds: mostly quiet, which is different from silent. In utility rooms this dense, you expect hum, but the breakers here are off. So you hear what the building does when it's supposed to be off. The tick of cooling metal. The drip, always the drip, but irregular enough that you can't anticipate it. The occasional sigh of the canvas lagging as it takes on water and sags a millimeter. Your own breathing, which gets loud. Far above, through four walls and a floor, a truck passes and you feel it in the soles of your feet more than hear it.

The dynamic is slow. If you came back in a year, the cup in the floor would be a fraction deeper. The moss would be maybe a half-inch wider. The rust scab on the steel plate would have flaked off and landed an inch to the left. The fern would have one more frond or would be dead and replaced by the ghost of its mineral shadow, same shape. The room does not change - it accretes.

The rule of this place is: everything that was installed to move something - water, air, power, people - has stopped moving it, and now it only moves itself. Heat moves downward. Water moves in circles from air to pipe to floor to air. Dust moves into cracks. Nothing enters except through condensation and the slow percolation of heat from above. Nothing leaves except as faint steam when a particularly hot day makes the floor puddle vanish all at once.

You stop crouching. You stand with your back against the pump that is still warm, shoulder to shoulder with pipes. The darkness has weight. A new bead of water gathers on the copper above you, swells, catches the tiny red light and holds it for a moment, and falls.
