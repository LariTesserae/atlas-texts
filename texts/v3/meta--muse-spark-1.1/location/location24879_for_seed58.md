It is not a cave. It was never a cave.

Three streets ago the surface gave way — not slowly, not carved — but dropped. A fresh fault in the basalt, a clean, sharp-edged bite taken out of the hardpan. The edges are still edges. You can see where rebar snapped like dry spaghetti and where the road's yellow line ends mid-sentence over nothing.

You go down.

The air gets heavy after the first ten feet. By thirty feet it's a different country.

The Hollow is hot. Not desert hot, not sun hot. Geothermal, womb hot. The kind of heat that comes up out of the rock itself. Old lava that cooled too fast left this chamber behind, and then the surface remembered it was thin here. The walls are raw and black and still angular, not smoothed by any real time. Young stone — glassy where it broke, stair-stepped where it cooled, sharp enough to cut your palm if you brace wrong. There has been no time for wind or water to be polite about it.

The town is inside that one room. One chamber, maybe a hundred meters across, maybe less. You could shout across it and someone would hear you. That is the whole scale of it — not a city that sprawls, but a city that stacked.

Because it is dense. It has to be. They didn't build out, they built *in*, layer on layer inside the throat. Catwalks of iron grate welded to the living rock, then shacks welded to the catwalks, then more catwalks hung from the shacks. Stilt-houses and cages and corrugated tin cubes leaning into each other for support. Every inch is taken. From the upper lip of the collapse you look down and see rooftops that are also floors that are also the ceilings of someone else's workshop. Extension cords like vines. Pipes like bones. Laundry lines that will never dry. If you stand on the main mesh deck in the middle — there is only one — you can touch two opposite dwellings without stretching.

It should be loud. It isn't.

This is an industrial town without animals. No rats, no pigeons, no dogs, no flies. Even the cockroaches couldn't take the heat when it first opened. The absence is audible. You notice it in the way sound doesn't get interrupted.

What you hear instead is active quiet. The place never stops moving, but it moves quietly.

Drip.

The 0.6 of water. There is no river here. There will never be a river here. But the Hollow breathes. Hot rock below, slightly cooler air funneling down from the surface at night. Condensation beading on every piece of metal that isn't already scalding. It gathers on the underside of every catwalk and falls again. At 2am, when the surface desert finally exhales, there is a rain that is not rain — a sudden, interior weather — and for twenty minutes the whole stacked hamlet glitters with a thousand falling drops. They hit tin roofs with soft ticks, collect in oil-drums, in cut-open water tanks, in the chiseled sump at the very bottom where the pumping engine lives.

Hiss.

The engine is always on. Low, industrial, an old single-piston steam pump that pulls that sump water back up through a jacket around the generator to keep it from seizing, and throws it back into the air as hot mist. That mist doesn't leave. It hangs in the dim for a while and then becomes drip again. The cycle is tight. Water here doesn't flow away; it loops. Damp, not wet. Your shirt never fully dries. Your lips crack anyway. Salt blooms white on anything black.

The light is left over. Strung bare bulbs, 40-watt, amber, on jury-rigged lines that sag under their own heat. And the furnace glow from below — the lower workshops where they cut this raw basalt into brick, where the saw smokes. No sun reaches past the first tier. The shadows are stacked as densely as the buildings. Down near the sump, in the deepest dim, things grow because condensation is dependable.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Not green — at this heat and with so little light it can't afford green. There are black-green mats of something that is almost lichen but plates like rust. There are white rootlets that have come up not from seed but from nowhere, from cracks where the surface soil fell in, hanging down three feet without soil, drinking air. In the corners where mist pools, there is a fern that forgot how to be soft. Its fronds are waxy, narrow, curled inward, beaded always with water it refuses to drop. No one planted them. No one tends them, except by not scraping them off.

And the strange part, the 0.7 that stays with you: the rock is growing faster than it should be eroding, or the town is. In the hot damp quiet you can hear ticking. Fresh basalt popping as it cools, even now. Little flakes of new mineral spalling off at night and landing on tin with a ping. And next to that, the town adding a new joint, a new brace, a new tarpaulin, every hour. No master plan, no mayor. Just that the heat makes you work or makes you sleep, and when you work you build. Scaffolding extends half-finished into empty air because yesterday someone thought there might be room for one more platform there.

There is no wind. No birds. The rules here are simple and local and non-negotiable:

1. What falls from above will fall again.
2. Metal rusts from the inside out.
3. You do not build a house, you build a shelf and hope someone else builds under you.

It feels not like ruins and not like a beginning but like something held exactly in between — a young place that already learned how to be crowded, a factory that only ever made more of itself, breathing its own damp hot breath in the dark just below your feet.
