There is a tableland so high it has left the rest of the world behind.

You don't climb to it. The continent cracks upward in one young, unfinished gesture — a shield lifted on a bubble of hot mantle — and suddenly there is a new sky. Three, four kilometers up. The air is thin enough that the light comes down unfiltered and too sharp. White that is not white. At noon the sun is a cold arc-welder spilling across everything, and the basalt doesn't warm under it. It only glints. The sky above is dark at the edges even in the middle of the day, a deep, bruised indigo.

This place is too new to be softened. Erosion hasn't learned it yet. Everything is fresh breakage. The plateau is a maze of columnar basalt and flash-frozen lava froth, of sawtooth ridges where the rock cooled too fast, of glassy plates still holding the stress of their birth. Canals were torn open when the lift happened — long, straight, kilometer-deep chasms with walls like stacked knives. No rivers run in them. River is the wrong word for what water does here.

Water here is a rumor. 0.1 bar of atmosphere can barely hold it. There are no lakes, no snowmelt rivers. Instead, every dawn the whole tableland sweats. The cold air mass that lives on the plateau — its own private weather system, vast enough to have high and low fronts that never leave — hits its dew point just before sunrise and precipitates out of itself. Not rain. Dew. A planetary condensation. It beads on every edge, runs down every needle and hair and spine. For an hour, everything drips. Then the sun hits and it flash-freezes again or is gone straight to vapor. In the deep fissures, where the sun never reaches straight down, the damp persists. Little hanging bogs live there, suspended in shadow.

Because what grows here has learned to drink air.

And it grows. God, it grows. In the cold you would expect scrub, lichen, a heroic little flower. This is not that. This is jungle packed into a freezer. The cold selects for black. The vegetation is black-green, black-purple, black-red, so dark it looks like it was charred and kept growing anyway. Dense felt-mosses a meter thick that you sink into to your hips. Tangleliverworts that build walkable mats over chasms. Tree-forms that are not trees — they are columns of stacked fern, each frond the size of a door, spiraling up three meters and then collapsing under its own weight to root again. Every surface is occupied. There is no bare rock that stays bare for more than a day. Spores land, moss takes it. Moss is undercut, lichen stitches over it. The understory under the understory has an understory. It is fully, obscenely packed. You cannot see soil because there is no room for soil. Everything is growing on top of everything else. It is lush in the way a culture plate is lush.

And it is packed because the animals do not allow any space.

There are no buildings here. Nothing was ever built. No walls, no roads, no tool marks. But it looks industrial from a mile out and the illusion doesn't go away when you get closer. It looks industrial because the fauna *is* the industry.

The dominant animals — if you can call them that — are not moving through the landscape. They *are* the landscape. They are mountain-size in aggregate and elephant-size in particular, slow, low, broad, and back-to-back from one horizon to the other.

Imagine a bison crossed with a blast furnace, crossed with a coral polyp. The herds — the continent — are called Furnacebacks by anyone who has tried to name them and failed. Their dorsal plates are black iron, scabbed with mineral accretion. Their ventral sides are deep in the moss, rooting, digesting. They stand shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, so close that the moss squeezes up between them like grout. You could walk across the plateau for a week stepping from back to back and never touch plant.

Each animal has a stack. A chimney of living ceramic that rises two, three meters off its spine, and from that chimney comes a plume. Not smoke — steam, and iron vapor, and a bright, bright heat-haze. Inside them, lithovores, they are smelting. They take basalt in their huge, shovel mouths, grind it with basalt teeth, and in slow, cold-defying guts that are forges, they reduce it. They exhale reduced iron that plates out on their backs and on each other. They exhale wet, warm air. That wet hot breath is the only real water this place has. It rises off the herd in a collective low cloud that never leaves the plateau, that hangs a hundred meters up and rains back down as that daily planetary dew.

You hear them before you see the edge of the tableland. It's loud, but not in a way that moves fast.

A deep, low, continent-wide grinding. Enamel on rock. The clank of those iron backs knocking as animals shift an inch per hour. The long sighing jet of their chimneys, all slightly out of phase, making a chord that beats. A hiss when a steam plume hits cold air and condenses. The crack of frost-heaved moss splitting. It is the sound of a factory district heard from a valley over, but with no shift whistle, no end. And under it, the insect noise.

Because the Furnacebacks are themselves jungles. On and between them live things.

Mites the size of rabbits that polish iron. Moths whose wings are sheets of mica that drink the furnace light. Glass worms that live in the chimneys and chime when the draft catches them. Birds — or bird-ideas — made of soot and magnetite that never land, only cling to magnetic field lines and filter spores. Everything is covered in everything else. When one Furnaceback finally, slowly, topples and dies — a process that takes about eight months from the first listing to the final settling — it does not fall. There is no room to fall. It sinks, and is immediately overgrown, and its iron is immediately seized and incorporated into the living backs around it. Its chimney gutters and goes out.

No part of this is fast. That is the rule here. In this cold, in this thin air, chemistry is patient. An animal takes a step every twenty minutes. A forest of moss advances a centimeter a year because there is nowhere to advance except over its neighbor. A mountain on this plateau does not erode; it is eaten, atom by atom, and breathed out as weather. The whole system turns over on a century scale, like a single slow breath. The herd migrates in a great circle around the plateau that takes three hundred years to complete. You could sit on one back your whole life and think you were sitting on a hill.

The light never lets you forget how high you are. At midday everything has no shadow except directly under itself. The moss glitters with trapped ice that didn't quite evaporate. The iron backs throw back the sun in painful white flares. The air smells of hot rock and wet cloth and ozone. It is freezing cold and the ground is warm to the touch from the animals underneath.

If you stand at the rim, where the new basalt drops five kilometers back to the old world in a single, still-sharp cliff, you see the cloud that the plateau makes for itself spilling over the edge like a slow waterfall, evaporating on the way down. Below, there is summer. Up here, there is this: a black, steaming, clanking, jam-packed mossy engine the size of a country, with no builders, no blueprint, no off switch, making its own damp out of stone and cold light, and ringing loud enough to hear the planet hum.
