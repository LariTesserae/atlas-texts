It wasn't dug. It fell.

A circle went missing from the plain all at once, recent enough that the grass at the rim still hangs in the air over the edge, root-balls drying into fists. The break is raw. You can see it in the strata — a clean shear through red soil, then limestone, then a darker, wetter basalt that never learned to be smooth. The walls are young, still sharp, still flaking. If you put your hand on them the stone comes off on your palm like cold soot.

The pit is maybe three hundred meters across, but it feels smaller because it is so full. A hamlet dropped into a cup. A city that only ever had one room to be a city in.

From the top, in the hammering afternoon heat, it looks almost empty: a well of shadow. Then your eyes adjust and you see it is packed solid to the lip with structure. Not ruins. Occupied.

They built down here after the fall, or as the fall was happening. No streets as you would think of them. The buildings are scaffolds first, houses second — iron frames bolted directly into the young cliff, then crossed and re-crossed with corrugated sheet, rebar, salvaged concrete slab, kiln-brick, all of it stitched together by pipe. Everything touches everything else. There is no ground level. There are levels. A lattice of platforms, walkways, water cisterns, drying lines, engine housings, all descending in a dense, handmade geology down to the dark bottom you can't quite see. If you live on the third tier you walk across the roof of the second to get to work on the fourth. Ladders become hallways. A child's ball dropped from a balcony will never hit bottom — it will land on a tarp, roll onto a boiler, fall into a gutter, be caught by someone's laundry line.

It's hot in a way that has weight. The heat comes up from below and sits. The air down in the dense layers doesn't move unless coaxed. It's a kiln heat, a greenhouse heat, a lung heat — ninety percent of the work the old engines do down here is just moving that hot air two meters to the left so people can breathe.

And yet it is lush.

The blessing of the collapse is that the plain above is dry and this wound catches everything. At night the hot rock exhales, and by three in the morning every surface is running. Condensation forms on the underside of every sheet of metal and falls in a constant, slow metronome. They have learned to farm it. Gutters cut from split pipe run everywhere, fed into barrels, fed into tanks, fed into root. It is not raining; the pit sweats.

So the city sweats green.

Anything not oiled weekly grows something. Black ferns unroll from the bolt-holes in I-beams. Strangler vines as thick as your arm find the gaps between brick and muscle through them, splitting walls over years. Breadfruit trees wedged into old engine blocks with their roots in grease. Moss makes every north-facing corrugated wall a soft, dim emerald. Banana leaves, huge as doors, push out of alleyways and slap you in the face as you squeeze past. The vegetation doesn't reclaim the industry; it works with it. The wires are trellises. The steam pipes are warm beds for epiphytes. The filters of the big loom-house on the sixth tier down are deliberately clogged with a flowering vine that filters better than the mesh ever did. The smell is hot iron, wet stone, sweet rot, and leaf.

Light comes straight down, but only for two hours at noon. The rest of the day it's a dusk, a deep diffused dim reflected off a hundred thousand wet leaves. Work lights burn everywhere — bare, yellow, filament bulbs on long looping cords — but they are low and half-shrouded by foliage, so you move through pools of kitchen-light surrounded by deep, hot shadow. In that light the condensations glitter. If you stand still long enough, you can see the path a single drop takes from the lip fern fifty meters above, hitting leaf, pipe, helmet, shoulder, barrel.

The machines are old and slow and kept alive out of stubbornness and pressure. No roaring. That is the strangest thing: for an industrial pocket, it is mostly quiet. The main mill engine, a hulking black thing at the bottom, turns at two revolutions per minute on a geothermal sigh, driving a shaft that climbs the whole city-center, creaking, with leather belts disappearing into thickets of vine. The presses press. The looms clack once, wait, clack again. Everything is geared for torque, not speed. Things happen across seasons. The bore-drill that is trying to widen a new water cistern on the west wall has been chewing the same meter of basalt since spring. The metal screams quietly.

No one shouts. Sound doesn't carry well in the packed, wet density. It is absorbed by leaves and cloth and rust. You hear drips, the tick of cooling metal, feet on wet board, the whisper of a machete trimming back a shoot that will be back tomorrow. 

Animals are rare. Too hot, too crowded, too little sky. There are the small white lizards that live in the engine grease and come out to watch you. There are the gnats that live in barrels. Sometimes a falcon drops off the rim, circles once in the column of humid air, realizes there is nowhere to stoop, and leaves.

The rule of the pit is: there is no outside. No empty space. If you pull a board, you find someone's wall behind it. If you clear a vine, you find conduit. If you leave a cup anywhere, a root will grow into it. Space is made, not found, and made slowly — by agreement, by carving a little more sharp rock away, by coaxing a platform to sag just enough that a new hammock can be slung in the gap.

People know the shape of the heat on their skin like a map. They know which hour the sun will find their tier. They do not think of themselves as living underground. The rim is the sky. The pit is the world. It is young and still settling, still flaking at the edges, still dripping itself into habitable form, and it will be full long before it is finished collapsing.
