It is a low place.

Not low like a valley that you descend into, but low like the land itself forgot to rise. A shallow dish in a plain of hard earth, no more than a breath lower than everything around it. You wouldn't see it from a distance. You would only find it by walking until the ground under your boots changed.

The rim is not a ridge, it is a rounding-off. The topsoil, where there is any, is thin and stony - pale caliche and compacted silt the color of old paper - and then it gives way at the lip, where water, sometime, has done its work. Sandstone. Soft. The kind that holds the shape of whatever has passed through it for a long time.

The basin is perhaps forty paces across, if you walked it rim to rim. Smaller than a house-lot. A single room, open to the sky.

Inside, the walls have settled into themselves. They are not sheer. They are undercut and bellied and smoothed, established. Water has worked here for longer than anyone would remember, not as a river, not as a flood, but as a habit. A seep after rain, a breath of damp that sits in the cool of the low and then is gone. So the stone is weathered into a continuous curve. No sharp edges left. At the base of the wall the sandstone has darkened, foot after foot, in a tidemark that never quite dries. It is damp there, always.

This is where the water lives. Not as a pool, not as a creek. As a condition.

In the deepest quadrant, where the wall leans furthest inward and makes a kind of roof, a low ceiling of stone, the air is cooler by a few degrees, and the stone sweats. Condensation beads on its underside in the warm, still air and gathers and falls without sound. One drop every minute or so, more than enough. It has made a shallow pan in the stone floor, not cut, but polished — a rust-colored hollow perhaps as wide as your two hands, an inch deep. It holds water that is dark, clear, and perfectly still. It never overflows. It never goes dry.

Around it, the rest of the basin floor is dry again. Packed sand, fine silt, loose plates of sandstone that have spalled off the walls. When you step on them they do not crunch. They shift and settle.

The light in here is dim, even at midday. Not because of depth — the walls are only a little taller than a person, maybe two at the south side — but because the air itself holds a faint haze. Dust, moisture. The sky over the opening is white, not blue, a warm white without sun in it. The light that makes it down is indirect, bounced from wall to wall until it becomes even and tired. There are no hard shadows. Everything looks like late afternoon, all the time.

It is warm. The stone is warm to the touch, even the damp part. If you put your palm on the undercut you feel the warmth of it first, then the cool sweat of it. The air does not move.

There is almost nothing growing. There is too little water for much, and too little movement for much else. A few things have found the terms acceptable.

Lichen, flat black-green, spread like spilled paint on the north-facing stone where the seep is strongest. A clump of bunchgrass near the eastern lip, bleached at the tips, alive at the root. Three or four wiry stems of something with tiny, waxy leaves growing out of a crack that has no soil in it that you can see. They are spaced far apart, each one owning its own stretch of emptiness. The pattern is so sparse it feels deliberate, like offerings set down with space between them.

No animals. Not even insects, not here. No tracks in the silt around the water. No birds cross the white opening overhead. There is a completeness to the absence that stops you from waiting for a buzz or a chirp. It is a place that does not support moving life, or it has negotiated its stillness.

There are marks that people have been here, but they are part of the same weathered state as the stone.

On the west wall, at about waist height, someone has worked a small niche into the soft rock. Not carved — rubbed. The hollow is shallow and rounded, big enough to fit a bowl, or two cupped hands. Its edges are as smooth as the rest of the basin. Inside it is dry, and its floor is stained a little darker, as if oil was once set there. Below it, on the ground, a few stones have been set together, not as a wall but as an attempt at leveling — three flat slabs arranged to make a low platform, now half-buried in silt. No mortar, no tool marks you can still read. Nearby, a single post-hole, its wood long gone, filled with fine wind-blown sand.

No devices. No rust, no plastic, no glass. Nothing that has outlasted the patient work of condensation and dust. Just the idea of staying.

The sound is what the stone does when you are not moving. Which is to say, nothing. If you hold your breath you can hear the air itself, a very faint hush, and every minute or so, the tap of a single drop falling into its own pan. It does not echo. The soft walls take it.

And nothing changes. The water does not rise after rain, because the plain around drinks most of it before it reaches the lip. And even if a hard rain did come and fill the basin for an afternoon, you can tell the basin would not keep it. The sand would take it, the overhang would keep sweating at its own pace, and by the next morning the small pan would be exactly as full as it is now. The grass does not spread. The lichen does not recede. The warmth does not lift or fall. The dim, even light without shadow.

It has found its shape and it stays in it. A damp place in a dry world, a dim place in a bright plain, a still place. The world outside can weather and move and burn and build itself new, and this one small room with its one cup of water and its rubbed niche in the wall will keep being exactly this, neither ending nor beginning, only continuing the terms it has already set.
