It is a bowl, but not a bowl that water made.

High up where the air thins and warms strangely, a piece of the world has been pushed up too fast. The plateau is still new. You can tell by the edges — the basalt hasn't learned to be soft yet. It comes up in a single, clean lift, a blister of dark rock a few hundred meters across, and then stops. Sheer on three sides, dropping into pale, dry nothing. The fourth side is lower, a lip of shattered clinker where the lift cracked, the only way in, if you were something that needed a way in. The light up here is ordinary daylight, high-altitude clear, but the heat stays. The black rock drinks sun all day and gives it back slowly all night, so the air in the bowl never really cools. It sits. Warm, thin, still.

And inside the bowl, a city that was never built.

The bottom of the depression is not soil. It is fresh, sharp, vesicular basalt, still showing the bubbles of its birth, still with glass edges that would cut your feet. Nothing should grow here. There has never been rain in this bowl; the wind that crosses the plateau is stripped dry a hundred kilometers before it arrives. There is no spring, no seep, no stream bed. When you put your hand to the rock, it is completely dry and faintly hot.

But it is full.

It is so full you have to stop at the rim to understand how to look at it, because there is no space between things. Lush is the wrong word for something without water, but it is the true word for density and daring. Everything that lives here lives on air and stone and each other.

The builders are the plants, and they are not plants as you know them. They are low, and then high, and then low again — a settlement of layered towers packed street-tight together. The foundation species is a matte-black columnar thing, as thick as a house, as tall as three. It has no leaves. Its skin is felted with tiny, upright black hairs that vibrate almost invisibly, combing the wind for what little moisture the wind still carries, and for dust, and for nitrogen. The heat of the rock helps; what it catches, it does not drink — it seals into a clear, bitter oil inside its body. Cut it, if you would, and it would bleed not sap but something like warm glycerin that never evaporates.

From the sides of these black pillars, the rest of the city grows. That is not a metaphor. Horizontal branches, thick as your arm, reach out and graft themselves to the next pillar over, and the next, forming second stories, cross-streets, plazas. And from those branches, other things grow in dense thatch: curtains of silver air-moss that hangs in still sheets and never sways; hard, coral-like shelves of a rust-red lichen that makes its own stone and layers it; bunches of bladder-flowers like paper lanterns, translucent amber, each a single huge cell full of that same oil, catching the sun and glowing. They are all epiphytes, parasites, partners. They have agreed to become architecture.

It is urban in every sense except intention. There are avenues, but they are the narrow clefts between pillar-trunks where the black hairs part. There are courtyards where four or five trunks lean away, leaving a patch of basalt roofed over by interwoven branches. There are towers — some pillars that kept growing, twenty meters up, tipped with a crown of those light-filled bladders like a streetlamp. The whole hamlet, cathedral-close, could fit inside a church nave if you folded it a little. You could walk it edge to edge in three minutes if there were room to walk. There isn't. Every surface is occupied.

And it is inhabited to saturation.

At first you think nothing moves. The silence is so complete it edits your expectations. High places are supposed to have wind. There is no wind inside the bowl; the rim kills it. What sound there is, is so quiet it happens inside your own head: the faint tick of cooling basalt, the nearly inaudible creak of a pillar putting on a micron of girth. Then, after you have stood long enough for your breathing to slow, you see them.

The fauna here do not fly quickly or sing. They cannot afford to lose water to sound or speed. So they have become quiet and abundant beyond counting.

The air-moss is not moss. Under a lens it is thousands of moth-things, each the size of a rice grain, each with wings like felted silver that interlock with its neighbors to make a continuous sheet. When one detaches, another moves to fill the gap, so the curtain seems eternal. They detach only to drift — slowly, so slowly — to another pillar, a relocation that can take an afternoon. They graze on the dust the pillars comb.

On the red lichen shelves, herds of glass-backed beetles, each as big as a knuckle, walk in tight procession, tending gardens of a darker fungus in grooves. Their shells are the exact red of the lichen, and also faintly translucent, so you can see the dark oil they have drunk inside them. They move at the speed of a minute hand. When they meet, they touch antennae for a long time and do not make a sound.

Higher up, in the lantern-bladders, things live inside the light. Small, pale salamander-like creatures with no eyes, with skin that never dries because the interior of the bladder never dries. You can see their shadows when they turn over in their sleep. They are born there, they die there. One bladder, one animal, for years.

The rule of this place is: keep what you catch. Water is a story nobody here tells. Instead there is oil, and wax, and glass. To live here you must seal yourself. You must build inward and upward, not outward. You must borrow the structure of your neighbor. You must not waste motion or voice. The top of the plateau is still sharp because nothing has worn it away; this city is still sharp for the same reason. It is young, geologically newborn, biologically first-draft. It has not yet learned erosion, or waste, or noise.

If you sit in one of the little basalt courtyards, where the light through the amber bladders paints everything the color of honey, you can feel the whole hamlet breathing — not an in-out of air, but a slow, patient swelling. The pillars imperceptibly thicker than last year. The lichen shelf a millimeter closer to closing its alley. A felted moth drifting two meters in an hour, utterly silent.

It will be here for a long time, getting denser, until there is truly no space left at all.
