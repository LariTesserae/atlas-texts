This is the Top.

Not the top of a mountain - there are no mountains here anymore. There was a time when there were, a long time ago, but that time has been edited out by wind and time and patient chemistry. What remains is what ancient high places become when they have finished becoming anything: a Plain, at an impossible height.

Elevation here is not a feature. It is the condition. The bedrock is continental shield, gneiss and anorthosite and vein-quartz gone soft at the edges, planed down by a billion years of nothing-in-particular until it is functionally level. You can see the planing. The exposed rock flows, not because it was ever liquid, but because it has forgotten how to be sharp. Every ridge is a long, low molehill, a hundred meters tall and sixty kilometers long. The horizon does not break. It smudges away into sky because you are too high for the world to have a horizon anymore.

The air, if you can still call it that, is cool and thin and perfectly clear. Not cold enough to bite — 8, 10 degrees above freezing in the sun — but it never warms you. The sun is a normal white-yellow sun, a little smaller than it should be. Light is ordinary daylight, flat and honest and white, and that makes it worse. There is no haze to make distance poetic. You can see thirty, fifty kilometers with awful clarity and understand that there is nothing there to see.

There is no water. Not in the sense you mean.

In the lowest languid folds of the plain, where the ancient peneplain dips a hand's breadth for a thousand hectares, there are salt flats. Not salt as ocean leftovers, but salt as a mineral logic: lithium and perchlorate and pale brine that never melts and never freezes, precipitated directly from the air in the long nights. It lies in hexagons. In the afternoon it sighs once and cracks, and the crack runs for fifteen kilometers without stopping while you watch. 

Above that, nothing. No rivers. The ground is sealed. If you kneel and press your palm to it, the stone feels like glazed ceramic. Water would have cut it once, but there is no water anymore to do that kind of work.

So what grows?

Almost nothing. Black lichen that is not lichen. Little rosettes of silica and keratin that anchor themselves in wind-shadow behind a pebble and open only at noon. They are the color of rust dried on rust. They do not mat together. Each one keeps a respectful distance of ten or twenty meters from the next, as if they have agreed on solitude. In two days of walking you might see a hundred of them. If you go close, very close, you see they are not rooted. They are sitting on a cushion of dead air, vibrating imperceptibly above the stone so they do not abrade away. They have no roots because there is nothing to root in.

The world is empty in a way that has weight. Density is a joke here. The air between things is larger than the things themselves. A rock. A kilometer. A salt flower. Five kilometers. Everything is spaced as if laid out by a careful hand that hates clutter.

And then the buildings.

You think they are buildings. At first they look like erratics, boulders that do not belong. Low, dark, matte. Half-buried in the flowing bedrock like they grew there. There is never more than one in your field of view, but there is always one if you look long enough. Four walls and no door, or three walls and a roof that doesn't meet. Or a single column, two meters tall, that casts no shadow.

They are not ruins. A ruin implies something has fallen down. These have not fallen; they have paused.

This is where the turbulence lives.

Erosion says this place should be dead — final form, near-heat-death of topography, the last stable page. Dynamic says otherwise. The plain is violently active, but not in wind or water.

Watch one of the structures long enough and it fidgets.

The column will thorn. Without sound — and sound here is an absolute privation, the thin air and the salt take all acoustic energy and bury it — the column will unfold its sides into surfaces that were not there a moment ago. It will become a small city of lattices for eight seconds, a storm of black glass in an area the size of a town, assembling and disassembling faster than your eye can track, stacking impossible cantilevers at atmospheric pressure, and then it will think better of it and subside back into a column. Where it was busy, the bedrock now has new writing for a while: a fresh fan of fine, basalt hair, or a perfectly smooth divot a kilometer across that was not there this morning and will be gone by evening.

They are all doing this, all the time, across the continental scale. The plain has weather, but the weather is not clouds — it is construction. A front of activity moves from the north-west, invisible except that the sparse lichen-things all lean toward it, and you see on the distant flats little flashes like heat lightning with no thunder. Each flash is a structure deciding to become cathedral, to become engine, to become a brief forest of calculation, and then deciding, again, to be a rock.

This is beyond-modern tech not as chrome, but as manners. Matter here has learned to hesitate. The physics are slightly too permissive, as if someone left the constants loose in this altitude. No struts, no weld-marks, no heat. Things imply themselves and then unsay themselves. The silence remains perfect through it all. Even when a structure the size of a mountain briefly raises itself three hills over and collapses like a dropped thought, there is no noise. Only the faint tick of your own suit, and the blood in your ears.

Animals? Rarely.

Sometimes high up — and up here *up* is a long way — you see something transit the sun. A great, translucent membrane, kilometers wide, so thin you only see it by the diffraction of light at its edges. They drift in the stratosphere, feeding on the electrostatic gradient and the lithium dust. They never land. They have no reason to. Once in a while one dies and falls, and it takes three days to come down, and it drapes itself over a hundred square kilometers of plain like a fallen aurora, and the lichen-things all gather — no, they migrate, slowly, at a glacial pace, toward its shadow — and the little buildings go frantic around the edges of it, taking it apart without touching it.

The rule of this world is simple: It is very old, very high, very empty, and it never stopped being busy. Only the kind of busyness changed. First mountains. Then rivers. Then ice. Now this — this quiet, frantic origami with matter, performed by scattered devices that no one tends, for reasons no one left behind. The plain does not need you. The light is normal. The air is cool on the outside of your mask. And if you stand still for an hour, you will find, when you look down, that the stone under your boots has smoothed itself a little more, and has faintly, politely, taken the shape of your soles, waiting for you to move.
