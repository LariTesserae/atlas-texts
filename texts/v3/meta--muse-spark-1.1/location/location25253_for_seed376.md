It is high.

Not the sharp high of a peak, but the broad high of a table that was lifted a long time ago and left to weather. A plateau in the cold latitudes, or a high valley caught between older mountains. Enough elevation that the air is thin and has no kindness in it. Breathing is a shallow, careful work. The sky is normal, pale blue, close — you can see weather coming from a day away.

Nothing was ever built here. There are no cut lines, no foundations, no ruins, no straight edges at all. The place is old enough in its own shape that everything looks settled. The ridges are soft-shouldered, the basins are scooped and held. Rock shows through only where the living mass has torn itself open and not yet healed.

Because it is all living mass.

From the rim of the plateau to the far haze three valleys over, the ground is not ground. It is plant. Not forest in the way you think of trunk and canopy and floor. It is total. A deep, laminated, felted mass of boreal things that have learned to be architecture for each other.

Low at the bottom, black spruce and larch stunted by cold into things that are all root and elbow, growing sideways more than up, their wood white as bone under lichen. Above them, or through them, a hundred kinds of moss — sphagnum, feather-moss, reindeer moss — piled in hummocks and soaked hummocks and frozen hummocks, so deep you could lose a house in them and never find it. Berry creepers and dwarf willow stitch it all together with wire roots. In the wetter seams, liverworts slick as oil. Every surface is some green, some rust, some grey-green, furred with frost.

And it is damp, but not wet. Water doesn't flow here so much as it arrives and stays a while. Clouds come off the lower country and snag on this high table and die. They leave themselves as condensation. On a cold morning everything is beaded, dripping without rain. The beads freeze into a second skin of glass. Then midday, sun gets over the rim, and the whole plateau steams. Ice turns back to water and sucks back into the moss. Once or twice a week a real rain comes, brief and sideways, and for an hour there are thousands of temporary streams threading down through the plant mass, brown with tannin, audible if anything here were audible, vanishing into pockets. Pothole lakes, dark and perfectly round, sit in the moss like eyes. They never quite freeze, because of what is happening underneath.

The cold is steady, just below working temperature. 28F, 30F. The kind of cold where water wants to be ice but can't decide. Your boots never dry. The moss crunches and then squelches.

Because it is moving. All of it.

From a distance, the plateau looks still. Up close, it is turbulent in a way that doesn't make sense for plants, and certainly not in silence.

There are no animals to speak of. A white ptarmigan now and then, so still you think it is lichen until it blinks. Otherwise, nothing to eat this, nothing to stop it. So it does what abundant life does when it has no checks and no building: it becomes its own industry.

The spruce at the heart of the mass have become engines. Not metaphor. Sap in them in winter should be stopped, but it isn't — it runs under pressure, supercooled, hissing through narrowed xylem. The trunks swell and contract, swell and contract, a slow piston stroke you can only see if you put your hand on the bark for a long time. Five seconds per stroke. Ten. The wood creaks without sound. When they contract, they exhale steam through pores in the bark that flash-freezes into needles and falls.

Around them, the mosses have specialized. Sheets of sphagnum lift like conveyor belts, growing inches per hour at the forward edge while dying at the back edge into black peat that compacts under its own weight and then, months later, is thrust up again by frost heave. Vines of willow have braided themselves into drive shafts between trees, tightening, loosening, transferring torque through living rope. In hollows, you find churning pits where no tree is present — just moss and lichen and air — where the mass boils. New fronds knitting, old fronds dissolving into slurry, all at frantic speed. One pit births a hummock three feet high in an afternoon; a few yards over, another pit collapses in on itself without a splash, peat unwinding like a spool.

It is industrial scale without industry. No metal, no made thing. Just plant tissue doing industrial work: pumping, pressing, hauling, milling. The air smells of turpentine and wet iron and sawdust, though nothing has been sawn.

The density is maddening. You cannot walk in a straight line. Everything is near-touching, layered. Moss growing on lichen growing on bark growing on moss. The pale roots hang down like cables. If you stand still too long, feather-moss begins to felt over your boots. Not aggressively — just as it does to everything that pauses. The plateau is trying to include you in its loom.

And for all this churning, for all this valley-wide factory floor seething and lifting and falling, it is silent. The cold eats sound. The moss eats what is left. Snow dampens. Water is too thin to babble. The tree-pistons push against air so thin and so muffled there is no resonance. You hear only what your own body makes — the blood in your ears, the creak of your own jacket — and underneath that, if you hold your breath, a very faint, very low fizz, like soda just opened a mile away. The sound of countless cells dividing.

If you climb to one of the eroded ridges, the old weathered spine of basalt where the plant has worn thin, you can see the rule of the place plain.

Nothing is stored. Everything is in transit.

The plateau does not grow larger. It has held this extent for a very long time. What it does is grow and un-grow in the same hour, in the same meter. A frantic, freezing, silent anthill energy without ants. Creation is not separate from destruction here. A branch extrudes, freezes, shatters into compost, is taken up again before its splinters land. This is not decay, not cycle — too fast, too chaotic for cycle. It is a negotiation that never settles.

It is damp. It is cold. It is utterly alive and utterly empty. And if you stay until evening, when the clouds come back down and snag again, the whole valley disappears into condensation, and you cannot tell where plant ends and fog begins, and you understand that there was never a distinction.
