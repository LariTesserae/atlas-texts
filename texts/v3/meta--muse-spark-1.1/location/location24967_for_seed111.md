It is not a mountain. It is a single room *taken* from a mountain.

A col that didn't exist last winter. The range here is still coming up — you can feel it in your molars, that high, fresh, tectonic pressure — and a block the size of a house has sheared and lifted on a new fault. It came up straight, still sharp. Basalt so young it has no dust on it yet, black glass where it snapped, edges like broken dishes. No scree yet. No talus apron. Just the sheer cut on three sides and a low lip on the fourth where the wind tries and fails to get in.

You are at 5,800 meters, give or take the way your lungs are failing. The altitude is absolute. The air is a thin, cool wire in your throat, 2 or 3 degrees above freezing in the warmest hour, which never comes. This is permanent night-blue, though it is not night. The sun never clears the rim. What light you get is starlight bounced off snowfields a kilometer below and refracted starlight from above — a darkness that has depth to it, a blue so dark it reads as black until your eyes stop trying.

The scale is wrong for this height. You expect vastness. Instead you have a bowl, a cathedral nave, thirty long steps across, fifteen wide. You could shout and hear the other wall too soon. Its boundaries are complete. Three walls 20 meters high, the fourth wall a chest-high spill of that fresh stone, its lip still warm — not from sun, from the planet.

There is no building. No cairn, no wall, no tent ring. Nothing has ever been built here because this place is ten months old.

And yet it is occupied.

The water comes as breath. That is the only word for it. The daytime updraft from the valleys below hits this cold trap of new rock and wrings itself out. Condensation beads instantly on every edge, on every sharp crystal. It never rains. It sweats. The black stone glistens, beads, and drips. In the lee of the rim, shallow cups — not pools, cups, palm-sized — hold water that never quite freezes, because the rock itself is faintly warm and because of the bodies.

Vegetation is almost an insult. A few mustard-yellow lichen crusts that blew up here and stuck, trying to get a hold on the glass. One cushion of alpine sandwort in a crack on the east wall, stunted to a green coin, its white flower closed for years at a time. Nothing else will root in new stone.

Because the stone is not available.

Every surface that is not running with condensation is covered in fauna. Dense is not the word — *upholstered* is the word.

They are called high svear by the few shepherds who know them and do not go near them. They are not birds. They are not crabs. They are something that decided, at extreme elevation, that flight was too expensive. Big as two fists together, low and broad, covered in soot-black wool that holds the dark, with six jointed legs that end in pale, hand-like pads. Their faces are blunt, eyeless, all antennae and breathing holes. Alone, one is quiet, slow. Here, there are thousands.

They pack the walls from floor to rim, clinging to that fresh basalt with their pads, shoulder to shoulder, back to back, layered two and three deep where the rock undercuts. They cover the floor so you cannot see the floor. They move as slow as glaciers, as slow as the uplift. A leg lifts over ten minutes, finds a new purchase. A head tilts a millimeter. Their metabolism is a cool amber banked low.

This is a roost. A brood. A city that breathes.

When you entered you thought the loudness was wind. It is not. The svear stridulate, but not quickly. Each one drags a ridged plate on its belly across a file on its thigh, once every few minutes, to say *I am here. I am warm. I am still here.* One sound is nothing, a faint zip like a fingernail on comb. Thousands, desynchronized, layered, echoing off three new walls in thin air — it becomes a continuous low chord, a cicada roar made of stone and hair, so loud your teeth vibrate. You feel it in your sternum more than your ears. It never stops, because they never all sleep at once.

They are warm. That is the weirdness. That is the first rule-break.

You put your hand close and the air over a cluster shimmers. Cool you thought, but they are each 39 degrees inside their wool, and they share it. The damp condensation that runs down the walls hits the colony and evaporates again as steam, and re-condenses on the cold rim above and drips down again. The bowl has its own weather. Little clouds form at head height and rain back down as warm drizzle that smells of iron and animal.

The second weirdness is the tools.

No built structures, fine. But look closer at the densest patch on the north wall, under the sandwort. A svear — old, wool thinned — reaches down with one hand-pad and picks up a flake of that fresh basalt. A perfect, razor edge, struck by the mountain itself last winter. It holds it like you would hold a knife, and with agonizing slowness, it scrapes. Not rock. The yellow lichen. It shaves a curl of lichen and passes it to its mouthparts. Another, beside it, uses a smaller flake to crack the thin ice in a stone cup, to drink. A third uses a sharp chip to groom another's wool and pick out parasites.

No nests. No walls. No fire. But a whole sloping scree of collected flakes tucked into cracks around the roost — a cache. Knapping without knapping; selection. Primitive tech without a maker you would recognize as sentient, because the maker is the whole dense mass, learning over centuries slower than you can see.

You stand on the lip and your boots are already being investigated. The edge of the colony flows toward you, not fast. No aggression. Just space is dense, and you are a warm rock that appeared. They will cover you if you stand still.

If you stay till your eyes fully adjust, you see their backs faintly freckled with cold light. Not bioluminescence — frost. The hairs ice over with condensation and then, warmed from below, the ice crystals lift and make a faint halo in the dark. Thousands of tiny frost-halos, winking out and re-forming with each breath of the bowl.

It is a small place. You could walk across it in a minute. But it contains more living weight than the pastures for fifty miles below, all clinging to a room that did not exist, keeping each other alive in the darkness, scraping lichen with stone knives, singing one endless, low song that keeps the water from freezing.
