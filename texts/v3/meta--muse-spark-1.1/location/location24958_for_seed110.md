High up, where the wind should be cold and thin, there is a warm place that has not finished being made.

You find it by the mountain's own instructions. Above the treeline, above the last lichen-crusted switchback where the air goes sharp in your lungs, the ridge crests and breaks. The basalt there is young. Not young like a hundred years — young like yesterday. It hasn't learned to be rock yet. It is all edge and bubble and unhealed blister. Glassy black, pocked with burst gas, its ridges still have the softness of something that cooled too fast to decide what shape to be. There is no scree, no soil, no wearing down. This is pre-erosion. The mountain pushed here and stopped.

And in the side of that push is a mouth not bigger than a man curled on his side.

Inside, scale collapses.

It is a single room. Not a cavern system, not a tunnel going somewhere else. A pocket. You could touch opposite walls if you stretched your arms, but you shouldn't. Everything is too close.

The air itself is dense. Warm — 98, 100 degrees, body-warm, breath-warm — and so saturated you don't inhale it so much as let it condense in you. It weeps. That is the first thing. The walls weep, the ceiling weeps, you weep. Water is coming out of the stone, not running over it. The raw basalt is porous in a way old stone is not, and the heat below pushes water up through it in beads. At any moment there are a thousand droplets swollen on every surface, fattening in the dark, on the verge of falling but not falling. The atmosphere never quite lets them.

 absolutly dark. Not the dark of night, which has stars. Not cave-dark that your eyes adjust to. This is a dark that was sealed when the rock bubbled shut. It holds. The black is complete enough that you feel the temperature as color. The only way to see is with your hands.

And the room is crowded.

Because nothing has ever eroded, everything that has ever started to grow here is still here. From the floor, from the ceiling, from the walls at all angles, the stone is trying to make stalactites and has only just learned how. It is an infant geology. Thousands, tens of thousands of beginnings — little nubs, nipples, teeth of calcite and opal and black glass no longer than a fingernail, each one holding a droplet at its tip like an offering. They are so dense you have to turn your shoulders to move, not because they are big but because there is no space between their attempts. Brushing them would snap a hundred years of almost-formation. So you don't.

This is why the place can stay wet forever. Water arrives, beads, swells, evaporates a hand's breadth up, condenses on the nub above it, swells, falls back a centimeter. A tiny, closed sky. Outside, the high cold would strip this warm wet pocket dry in an hour, but the entrance kinks and the warm air is heavier than the cold, and the system has found equilibrium. Water near surface, yes — the surface is the whole room. Nothing leaves.

At knee height there is work.

Someone else found this before you, or many someones over a long quiet time. There are basins. Four, five. Scattered along what is almost a floor. They are not natural. The edges are pecked out with a harder stone, irregular but deliberate, shallow cups hammered into the softer basalt blisters, each just wide enough to hold two cupped hands of water. The rims are chipped smooth by use. Primitive work, hand work. Beside one, a flake of obsidian, its edge still sharp enough to draw blood, set down and left. Beside another, a mat — or what was a mat — of woven alpine grass, now black and pulpy and waterlogged, its fibers collapsed, still holding the shape of fingers that pressed it to strain water.

They didn't live here. No one could. It is too small, too dark, too perfect in its stillness. They came for the water. Up high where water should be snow and snow should be scarce in the late season, a never-failing warm spring that never flows, never freezes, never changes, that is holy or at least useful.

The vegetation here knows that. There is almost nothing, because there is no light to pay for leaves. But in the cracks where the basins overflow constantly by a fraction of a millimeter, there is a sparse, pale fur — not moss, not quite fungus. White rootlets, translucent, as fine as hair, hanging from the stone, living on warmth and dissolved mineral and the ghost of light they will never see. They tremble when a drop falls.

Fauna is rarer still. You might go a dozen visits and find nothing. If you are patient and keep your hand in one of the warm basins, utterly still, you might feel a leg. There is something that lives here, something blind and flat and the length of your thumb knuckle, with too many slow legs, paddling in the captured warmth. It doesn't flee. When you lift your hand it stays.

Sound is the measure of how little changes. There should be dripping, but there isn't. The air is too full to let drops fall often. So what you hear, in the mostly-quiet, is the infrequent *tick* of a swollen bead finally growing too heavy for its nub — one every minute, maybe two — and the softest possible plip as it lands not on stone but on another bead that catches it. The sound of water handing itself to itself. Under that, your own blood. Under that, a deeper, almost inaudible sigh: the mountain, still warm inside, exhaling ever so slightly through a million pores, keeping the room exactly as it is.

It does not flood. It does not dry. The nubs do not grow longer. The grass mat does not fully rot. It is a place in equilibrium that was left embryonic on purpose, a hidden lung of the summit, warm and dark and crowded with beginnings that agreed to never begin.
