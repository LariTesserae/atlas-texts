It is not a desert. A desert implies sand that remembers water. This place has never known it.

Low. That is the first rule here. Everything has settled downward to get away from the sky. On the white salt plain — a flat so flat it erases distance — there is a mouth. Not a crater, not a wound. A collapse. The salt crust, which is a mile thick and blinding up top, gave way a long time ago into a brine chamber that had long since dried and emptied. What remains is a hidden room.

You have to find the lip and lower yourself. The drop is maybe eight meters, not deep on any map, but the geometry makes it deeper. The walls are near-vertical, corrugated, the color of old teeth. Down here the light is used up. The sun is directly overhead, white and mean at 2.8, but it has to fall through that narrow throat, and by the time it reaches the bottom it is thin, amber, dim — more like heat than light.  The air itself is hot and stays hot. It doesn't move in wind, it hangs, heavy, close.

And at the bottom: a town.

Not ruins. Not metaphorically a town as in a town of mushrooms. An actual town, city-scale in intent if not in footprint. Because the pocket is only about forty meters across, the town had to learn to be a city by folding in on itself. Low, wide, salt-brick houses with walls a foot thick, built by hand. Primitive work — you can see the thumbprints in the bricks where they were pressed from salt-mud and dried in this same heat. No metal. Wooden scaffolds, now dark and dry as iron, holding up second and third stories that lean over narrow streets that were laid out with a plan, with corners, with a drainage system that has never drained anything. A cistern in the center that has never held water. Steps worn smooth by feet. Door lintels carved with a simple repeating pattern of three lines. Someone meant to stay.

But there is almost no one here now, and there was never much per volume. The density has always been wrong. That's the second rule: everything here is spaced too far. Vast rooms inside those salt houses containing one small stool, one empty shelf. A street wide enough for a market that holds only a single abandoned loom made of salt-cured fiber. The town was built empty, hollow, as if the idea of a city was more important than crowding it. Sound carries too well. You hear your own breath bounce off three walls before it dies.

And it is loud. For no water, for no animal life, for no people now — it is very loud.

Because it is lush.

That is the contradiction that defines the place and proves water was never needed.

From every mortar line, from every roof, from the walls of the empty cistern, from the loom itself — vegetation. Not green. The light is too dim and the world is too hot for green. It is black-red, deep maroon, oil-slick purple. A felt of fleshy, waxy things that are not leaves but are leaves. They call them ash-veils in the lowland dialect, but here they have no name because nothing lives to name them.

They are not succulents. Succulents imply storage. These do not store water because there is no water to store, ever. The plain above has a relative humidity of zero. What they do is eat heat and stillness. Their surfaces are covered in microscopic, glassy hairs that shiver even when the air is still. They are harvesting the temperature differential between the sun-baked upper air and the cooler salt below. They are thermotrophic. They grow lush — god, lush — in dense mats a foot deep that droop over eaves like Spanish moss made of smoked meat, that fur the streets, that climb the scaffolds. In the dim, their edges glow faintly with waste infrared. If you brush them, they don't feel wet. They feel hot, dusty, like velvet left in a kiln.

This is a long-established place. The erosion tells you that. The salt bricks are rounded at the corners, not from rain — there has never been rain — but from the endless, patient scouring of hot, dry air and the slow expansion and contraction of the plants as they swell at noon and tighten at night. There is a cycle here, active, very active. At midday the colony inhales heat. All those millions of hairs stand erect and the whole town hums — a low, staticky crackle like a field of cicadas made of paper. That's the loud. At sunset, when the light up above goes red and none of it reaches down here at all, the plants exhale. They all at once release a puff of fine, dry, carbon spore that hangs in the hot air like incense smoke and then settles, and the town sighs and contracts with a sound like a thousand small hands letting go.

It grows, dies, grows over its own dead, which do not rot — nothing rots without water and nothing eats it without fauna — so it composts by crumbling into a fine, black-red peat that is utterly, perfectly dry. The peat fills the houses. You step and it puffs. The city is slowly becoming a city-shaped garden that remembers the shape of a city.

There are no birds. No insects. Nothing moves except the veil, and the hot air, and you.

If you stay until full dim, when the only light is the dull radiant glow of the salt itself holding onto the day's fire, you can see why they built here. Low, hidden, hot, empty enough to be safe. The walls keep the blank white horror of the salt desert out. The plants give cover, give fuel if you know how to braid them without snapping the glass hairs, give a kind of company. Primitive tools left behind — a salt knife, a fiber spindle — are still sharp, still waiting, because nothing rusts.

It is a pocket of urban intention kept alive by a kind of plant that should not exist, in a place where water never did.
