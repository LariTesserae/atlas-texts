You come down into it without really noticing the descent. It's low country — not low in the dramatic lowlands way, just a shallow dish in the earth, a place where the land has settled on itself. A cool bowl, a degree or two cooler than the fields you crossed to get here. The air gets heavier as you drop in, not with humidity, but with that particular damp that lives in old stone.

This was a town. Is a town.

It is hamlet-sized, a full urban idea drawn in miniature and then left to weather into itself. You could walk its entire extent in fifteen minutes if you didn't stop to look. There are maybe nine or ten buildings, but they are *buildings* in the proper sense: three and four stories, party-walled in places, with streets between them that have names still baked into blue tiles, with storm drains and lamp posts and a small iron bridge over nothing. No suburbs, no sprawl. A hard edge where the town stops and the short, wind-combed grass begins, as if someone drew a circle and said *town goes here*.

Only the town is not made of town anymore.

The vegetation is total here. Not as in a jungle that ate a place — as in vegetation *as* the place. The builders learned, a long time ago, how to train it to hold the shape of construction. The walls are living wood - not logs, but coaxed trunks, braided hazel and alder and a pale river-willow that grows straight as rebar when you ask it right, pleached and inosculated over generations until the grain of the wood has fused into lintels, sills, joists. Between the trunks, a second skin: moss and a tight, flat ivy that grows felt-thick, that they plastered and combed and still whitewash every spring. The roofs are not thatched, but thatch-adjacent — a low, cropped carpet of sedum and valerian and a tough, rust-colored creeper that never gets taller than your ankle and sends its roots down into the wood to drink.

So you have a town that is all plant and entirely urban. A street of row houses whose bricks are bark, whose bricks breathe.

And it's sparse. That's the thing that catches you. For how much living there is, there is so much *space*. The buildings are set too far apart for an ordinary town, like molars with gaps. Big, resonant absences between them filled with nothing but cool air and light. You can see all the way through the town at a diagonal, house, gap, house, gap, sky. The density is low on purpose — to let air move, to keep the walls from rotting each other. Each structure stands in its own envelope of damp air.

Because it *is* damp. Not wet. There's no river here, not really — just the memory of one in the rounded way all the corners have gone soft. What water there is comes at intervals. In the morning, especially, everything sweats. The cool of the bowl meets whatever heat is left in the industrial guts underneath, and condensation beads on every leaf and runs down in slow, deliberate trails into copper gutters that ring with it. On cold days the town drips like a cave. You hear it before you see it - that mostly-quiet is punctuated by a drop, a pat, a *plink* on a leaf that is as stiff as a plate. Once or twice a week it rains for twenty minutes, hard and vertical, and then stops. The gutters engorge, the stone-sett streets — setts grown over so only their crowns show through the moss — flood ankle-deep and drain away into cisterns, and by afternoon it's just damp again. Pockets of water live in places: a barrel joint where three trunks meet that never dries, a green-lit well in the center square that smells like pennies and old leaves.

Under all this green, it is industrial. That’s not a metaphor.

The looms never stopped.

In the larger buildings — the Mill, the Dye House, the Engine Shed that leans a little downslope — the machines are still turning, powered by something low and steady. A boiler somewhere coughs. You can feel it in your sternum more than hear it. Shafts turn slowly in ceilings made of living branches, belts made from some supple vine slap gently as they spin. No one is at the stations, but the work continues: thread being made, not from wool or cotton anymore but from plant fiber, pulled fine, combed, woven on looms that clack at a meditative pace, every tenth beat off-rhythm where a tooth has worn. Great bolts of fabric — green and grey and undyed root-white — roll themselves and are moved along overhead lines by hooks that squeak.

It is an industrial town that grew its own infrastructure. The town electrified itself with what it had — copper wire stapled straight into living cambium, glass insulators nailed to branches. At dusk, the lamps along the one real street sputter on, dim amber, running on the slow current pulled off heat-differential engines wrapped in ivy.

Normal light here is soft. The sky over the bowl is often overcast, a high, cool white that doesn't cast shadows so much as let everything light itself. When the sun does break through, it comes in columns between the buildings and makes the condensation glitter. You can see dust motes and pollen and the tiny gnats that are the only fauna anyone sees reliably.

Animals are rare. That’s part of the town’s rule: this is not an ecosystem that wants animals. Birds don’t nest in these walls because the walls close up at night, very slightly, the way a leaf does. You’ll see a pigeon once, bewildered, on the iron bridge. Rats don’t gnaw living wood that can ooze a bitter white sap when bitten. What lives here are the things that can live *as* plant: lichen, beetles the size of rice grains that farm fungus in the seams, a particular small black spider that makes its web across the gutter mouths and drinks the drip.

But it is active. Fiercely, quietly active. Everything is growing and working at once. You can watch a branch thicken over the course of an afternoon, if you sit still. The mill walls creak as they add a ring. The Dye House vats — enormous, round, where the trunk-walls bulge out to make the structure — change color with the day, tea-brown in the morning, rust-red by evening as some process inside precipitates pigment. The fabric on the looms is always a foot longer when you look back. The cisterns breathe. The mosses on the streets exhale after rain.

The erosion, you notice, is not erosion as damage. It is erosion as settlement. Nothing sharp is left. Every edge has been rounded by wind and water and a hundred years of hands rubbing the same stair rail that is now as smooth as bone. The corners of the buildings have grown elbows. The iron bridge is pitted to softness. Even the sound is eroded — the engine thrum, the belt slap, the drip are all low-passed, as if traveling through upholstery.

The rule of the place, if you stay long enough to feel it, is simple: build sparsely, build living, keep it damp enough to keep it alive and cool enough to keep it slow. Let the machines tend the garden and let the garden house the machines. Don't bring animals in. Don't build past the grass line.

It is unusual only in how reasonable it is. An old, small, cool, damp, half-hollow town that decided becoming a forest was more efficient than fighting one, and figured out how to keep the streetlights on while it did it.
