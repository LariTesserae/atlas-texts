It is not a cave. It is a wound.

On the high cold plain there is nothing for a long time — lichen-crusted basalt, wind-shaved heather, lichen — and then the ground simply stops. You come to the lip without warning. The plain breaks.

Below you is the town.

A sink on the scale of a valley. Three, maybe four kilometers long, a kilometer wide, a few hundred meters deep, but the rim is so sharp it looks cut this winter. There is no softening, no talus slope of old age. The walls are black, raw. Columnar basalt and frozen till sheared clean when the permafrost underneath melted out and dropped. Young rock. Everywhere you can see where it fractured: bright faces, edges that still hold, vertical knives of stone. Water comes out of them everywhere.

Not as streams. As weeping. The whole cliff weeps. Groundwater hit cold air and the cold air wrings it out. The stone is glossy black with it, seamed with white where the water freezes and re-freezes into long, dirty icicles that never quite finish dripping. In summer they melt, in late summer they don't; they just grow thinner and lengthen. The air over the drop is always misting. Your breath makes the mist thicker.

The light reaches the bottom fully. It is open sky. High overcast, that flat northern light that has no shadows but is bright enough to see by. Normal day. Cold day. Cold enough that open water skins over with ice if it stays still for an hour.

Below, from where you stand, it looks at first like a forest that fell in. It is too full. That is the shock of it after the empty plain. There is no gap. No bare ground. No room.

The floor of the sink is a bog-city.

It is a city built with teeth and mud and time, with no hands in it at all.

Beavers built it. Or things like beavers — bigger than any you have seen on the plain, winter-fat, dark as peat. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, over generations. You cannot count lodges anymore because lodges have become blocks. Dams have become streets. Canals have become avenues. What was a marsh with scattered houses is now a continuous, interlocking architecture that covers the entire valley floor from wall to weeping wall.

The physics of it: they cut down everything that tried to grow tall. White spruce and black spruce and birch along the springs — all taken at chest height, stumps sharp and pale and fresh-looking, even on the old ones, because cold preserves gnaw marks. They drag the trunks down and weave them. They plaster them with black silt and clay carried up from the sink bottom. They pile them. The first lodges became islands. The islands grew together. Now the structure is a solid raft of wood-and-mud, twenty feet thick, floating and grounded at the same time on a slurry of peat and cold meltwater.

And on top of that raft, the second city grows.

Because the beaver city is so packed there is almost no open water left, only the narrow canals between lodges, a grid of black water that never freezes solid because it is always moving. And on every dome, on every dam-wall, on every abandoned food cache, vegetation has taken hold — lush beyond what the cold should allow. This is why the bog is warm in one way and freezing in another. Sphagnum moss in hummocks so thick they are like mattresses, orange and crimson and acid green. Dwarf willow making dense, ankle-high thickets. Cottongrass, its heads everywhere like lost snow. Labrador tea and bog rosemary and cloudberry vines threading through everything, binding it. The plant cover is so complete you cannot at first tell what is beaver and what is plant; the lodges are just hills covered in fur-like moss.

It is saturated. The rule here is: nothing is alone.

Where there is moss there are voles. Where there is water there are ducks. Where there is wood there are insects. The abundance is quiet but you feel it as pressure. Overhead — the air is full of small birds that nest in the cliff holes, martins and swifts that slice out over the sink in clouds and then dip back. Down on the raft, in the willow, ptarmigan burst and settle. In the canals, trout hold in the shadow of dams, black-backed. Dragonflies even this far north, big and slow in the cold, like sparks over the water. Everything lives on the backs of the builders.

From the rim you hear almost nothing. That is another rule. Cold and wet eat sound. The water drips, a hundred thousand drips from the walls, but it falls into moss and makes no splash. The birds call but their voices are muffled by the mist and the peat. What you hear when you stand still long enough is wood: a soft knock, a gnaw, a wet slide as a fresh log is pulled into a canal. Then a slap of a tail on water, deep and flat, and the water rings for a long time because everything else is holding its breath to listen.

If you scramble down — and you can, where a section of wall has slumped in a raw, stepped landslide — the scale shifts. From inside you cannot see the plain anymore. The basalt walls go up on all sides, black and dripping, and they hold the weather in. Cold pools here. The mist never quite clears. The light stands in the air. And the city is no longer a picturesque pattern; it is walls around you, taller than you are, damp and rooted through. The canals are deep and straight, kept dredged. The intersections have current. You see how traffic works: a beaver will pass you without looking, dragging a freshly peeled aspen limb three times its body length, and it will turn into an alley of woven sticks where another is already waiting, and they will pass without touching by a thumb's width because there is no room to do otherwise.

Lean close to a lodge wall and it is alive. The moss breathes. The wood underneath steams slightly in the cold, composting. Beetles run in highways through the thatch. The mud is still warm from a body that slept there last night.

There is no waste, there is no empty lot. Every piece of fallen leaf becomes a wall or a bed or food for something that will then become a wall. The city grows upward by inches a year, as new moss grows on old roofs and new roofs are built on old moss, and downward into the peat as dams sink. It is an urban density made without plan, only accretion, but it has neighborhoods — the older, higher, drier domes toward the center where spruce seedlings have taken hold and songbirds nest, and the newer, wetter outer ring where building is still frantic and the wood is yellow-white and fresh.

It will not last like this. That is the youth in it. The cliffs are still falling, still calving house-sized blocks of basalt that crash down and crush a district of lodges, and within weeks the crushed district is bridged over and re-inhabited. Water is still cutting new springs high in the walls, making new streams that the engineers must answer with a new dam. It is an active place. It will eventually either fill itself up and become a solid hill of peat and gnawed sticks that the forest can finally climb, or the melt below will deepen it further and drop it again.

For now it hangs cold, wet, packed, breathing, too full to be quiet and too cold to be loud.
