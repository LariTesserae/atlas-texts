It is high. That is the first wrong thing.

You are up where the air should be thin and cold and the stone should cut. You can feel the pressure in your ears, not from thinness but from weight. You are four kilometers up, maybe more, on the back of something young — and you are underwater.

Not under in the way of being at the bottom of a sea. There is no bottom ocean down there below you, no abyssal plain. The water is up here. It has stayed.

A warm, clear, immense lens of it lies cupped in the high basalt. A mountain sea, but it doesn't behave like a lake. It is too big, too deep for a lake and too high for any ocean. Its surface is far above you. Sunlight comes down through hundreds of meters of water and arrives intact — normal, late-morning light, slightly rippled, warm as light through a window. You can see motes in it. It is not dark down here. The water itself has the clarity of air at altitude.

The rock is new. This whole range is new. You can tell by the way it hasn't learned to be soft yet. The uplift is still fresh — black and rust-red pillars, dykes, stepped terraces of columnar basalt that broke clean yesterday on a geological scale and haven't yet been sanded down by wind or water. Edges are still edges. Knife-back ridges run for a kilometer and then shear off. There are no beaches, no sand, no talus slopes of tired pebbles. Instead there are cliffs under the water, sudden drops, chimneys and blades and tables that stand in place, too angular to have been here long.

It should be barren, this kind of young furnace-rock at this elevation. Instead it is choked.

Everything grows. That is the second wrong thing, or it explains the first.

From every crack in the basalt thrusts a kind of forest that has never bothered to decide if it is algae or tree. The trunks are not wood, not exactly, but they are rigid — dense, pale stalks as thick as your torso, close as pillars in a temple, branching only at the top where they burst into broad, waxy leaves the size of doors. They are dark green, almost black, and they overlap by the thousands, making layers. Under them, second and third understories: ferns with fronds ten meters long that hang in the water like banners and never wilt, moss that is not moss but a thick, velvety mat that coats every vertical face, liverworts swollen to the size of cabbages, strings of bladder-weed that grow up, not up toward the sun but in every direction, seeking any empty volume and filling it.

It is dense beyond logic. You cannot swim in a straight line for more than a body length without putting your hand on a leaf, a stalk, a curtain of root. The water between the plants is not empty space; it is filled with smaller plants, with hair-fine filaments that catch the light, with drifting colonies of green wool. The water itself feels slightly thick from how much is living in it. Near-touching, layered, saturated. The young rock is barely visible for the crush of green over it, but where it is visible, it is sharp.

The water is warm. Bath-warm, blood-warm. At this height it should be snow. Instead it holds its heat with a patience that suggests some other rule. No current to speak of. No chop. When you move a frond aside it stays aside for a long time before it eases back, the water closing after you like syrup. The slow way things happen here is everywhere — leaves unfurl over weeks, visibly but unhurried, gas pearls rise from the canopy at the speed of watching a cloud cross, the basalt itself still sighs and ticks upward a millimeter a year. Change happens, but across long durations. Nothing is in a hurry. The forest can afford to be dense because nothing falls, nothing decays fast.

There are no animals, or almost none. That quiet you noticed is not just water-muffle. You hear your own breath, the faint creak of a trunk swelling, and then nothing for a long time. Very rarely a pale something moves — a blind shrimp the length of your arm, a translucent ribbon that hangs in the water and pulses once an hour, a small white fish that has no eyes and grazes on the moss and then stops for a day. The niche for fauna was never filled here. The plants took everything. They make oxygen that stays dissolved and makes the warmth fizz faintly if you look. On a rock face you might find the round, empty cast of a snail that died seasons ago, un-eaten because there is almost nothing to eat it.

Light falls through all of this. The surface of this high hanging sea is far above, a breezeless, unbroken ceiling that light breaks across, sending down slow nets of caustic that crawl over the leaf-roofs. Because the water is so clear and the sun so direct at this altitude, the light is not blue and lost. It is normal. You can see color fully — the iodine yellow of some of the deeper fronds, the black-red of the basalt, the bright, almost painful green of the new growth at the tips. The valley — for it is a valley, or what will be a valley when the water finally decides to leave — extends away past what you can see. For kilometers in each direction the same drowned, sharp, overgrown highland goes on, ridge after ridge of submerged forest and stone, all hidden from any sky, all unmapped because nothing has ever needed a map for it.

There is a strangeness to the water staying. You begin to understand it not as a flood that hasn't drained, but as a condition of place. Here, at height, water is heavier than air and reluctant to fall. Evaporation is slow, the atmosphere above the surface already thick as a second sea of cloud and rain that never quite rains. The high basin breathes in cloud and holds it. The world has a dent in it here where the rules pooled.

There are no ruins, no cuts, no footings, no rope, no glass. No smoke. The stone was never quarried. The forest was never thinned. You are not the first life here, but you are the first kind of life that wonders about artifacts, and there are none to find. Only growth, and sharp new rock, and warm, heavy, silent water holding itself up impossibly high, doing the slow work of letting a young mountain learn how to be green before it ever learns how to be dry.
