It is high enough that the idea of height stops meaning anything.

You are not on a peak. Peaks are for looking at. You are on the back of something so broad and so lifted that your lungs have to remember how to work twice for every breath. The air is thin, clean to the point of being sharp, and cool — not the killing cool of the upper death zones, but a permanent late-October cool that never tips into winter or eases into summer. Five degrees, maybe seven when the sun is directly overhead. The kind of cool that lives in stone.

And the light. God, the light. At this elevation there is nothing left to filter it. It is 2.8-bright, a white, unpainted light that does not wash color out but etches it deeper. Every shadow has a hard edge. The basalt is blacker for it. The lichen is yellower. You squint without meaning to. Sunscreen and snow-blindness would be a concern if there were snow, but there is almost none — only old ice in the shade of its own making.

This is the Anvil. A tableland, a vaulted plain between ranges that themselves are higher still, but you cannot see them because the air shudders with distance. It reads as big, but human-scale big — you could walk from one side of the visible bowl to the other in a long day if your heart could take it. A valley that forgot it was a valley. Maybe fifteen kilometers across. Open. You can see everything that is in it at once and still feel it is empty.

The rock is what tells you how old it is. It is not jagged, not young. Extreme elevation usually means violence — new mountains are knives. This is not new. This is settled-form, weathered-in-place, in-balance. Wind and old ice have been here so long they've come to an agreement with the stone. The plateau is made of a thick lava, columnar basalt that cooled and cracked into great organ-pipes, and then those pipes have been hollowed.

That is the first thing that isn't quite normal. The tops of the columns are open. They stand like the stumps of a dead forest, knee to chest high in most places, waist-high in others, pitted and lapped smooth on their windward sides. Each one is a throat. Each one is a flute with no maker. Water would have done that, but not fast water — patience water. Condensation, seep, freeze-thaw over ten thousand seasons. The rock is porous in a way basalt shouldn't be.

And the whole plateau hums.

It roars, actually.

The sound hits you before anything else makes sense and then never leaves. The wind up here never stops, because there is nowhere for it to stop — it comes off the icefields of the true high range and gets driven across this table and down into these pipes. Every hollow column is tuned slightly differently by its diameter and depth. Some are low, a moan you feel in your molars. Some are shrill, a kettle scream. Together, multiplied by ten thousand, by a hundred thousand throats across the valley, the plateau becomes deafening. Not loud like an explosion. Loud like standing inside a pipe organ the size of a town while the atmosphere itself plays its only note. You have to open your mouth to equalize. You can't hear your own footsteps. You have to shout to hear the shape of your own thought and even then the wind takes it.

That's why, despite the abundance of life, you don't hear any of it at first.

There is no building here. No wall, no cairn, no road. Not even a circle of stones where someone tried to get out of the wind. It is undeveloped in a way that feels intentional, as if the place itself refuses to be kept.

And yet there is tech. Primitive, hand-held, opportunistic tech. Not yours.

The first time you see one of the inhabitants you think it is a raven because it is black and because it enjoys height. It isn't. It is bigger, with a white ruff, and its wings whistle in that killing light. It lands on a column and cocks its head at you, then works. It wedges a basalt flake — not a natural flake, a flake struck at a clear bulb-and-conchoid angle from a harder piece of included chert — into a crack to pry out something damp and moving. A larva. You realize the flake is a tool. The bird made it. Then another one lands and does the same a dozen columns over. Tool-use, but early, pre-metal, stone-on-stone, passed down bird to bird.

The plateau is full of animals. That is the strange math of it — density 0.5/3, empty, but fauna 2.2/3, abundant. You go a long time seeing nothing, just that vast, howling emptiness between presences, and then the eye catches and catches and catches. They are all low and spread thin.

Moss-pigs the size of thimbles that live inside the damp of the pipes. Cushion plants — sparse, 0.9 — hugging the rock in hard green-gray buns, each one a century of growth, flowering with a single white pinprick blossom that never seems to die because nothing ever changes enough to kill it. Then the grazers: a kind of thick-coated, high-shouldered ibex-thing that moves in pairs, not herds, their hooves splayed flat and silent on the basalt. Pikas with ears too big for their bodies. And the sky — when you lift your head against the roar, the sky is busy. Vast, slow gyres of thin-winged gliders, hundreds of them, riding the updraft off the table's edge, screaming back at the rocks in voices that are lost under the flute roar. Guano, white, paints the lee of every column, and where it paints, the lichen grows thicker.

Water is never obvious and never absent. Damp, 0.6. There are no lakes. No streams that you can call streams. Instead the air itself carries it. In the morning — though morning is a meaningless word when every day is identical — the inside of every hollow column sweats with condensation. It beads and runs down the inside wall and collects in a dark, tea-colored pool at the bottom where the moss-pigs live. In the deeper clefts between columns, where the sun never quite reaches, that water lingers as black ice that never fully melts and never fully freezes. You can put your hand in a column and your fingers come away wet and smelling of iron and old life. Occasionally the wind stalls in some larger channel and you hear, under the great roar, a secondary trickle. Pockets in larger dryness. The plateau is not arid; it is holding its breath.

Nothing moves fast here. The dynamic is frozen. Equilibrium. You get the sense that this place found its final shape a long time ago and decided to keep it. The ibex do not migrate off it. The birds do not leave. The wind does not vary except in pitch. Even the lichens do not encroach further on the rock. It is a stable system that has balanced erosion against growth against howling against tool-making against damp against bright against empty, and it holds.

The rule of the place, if it has one, is this: make as little as possible and let the wind do the rest.

The birds make a flake when they need a flake and then drop it. The cushion plants make one flower and hold it. The water makes itself inside the rock instead of falling from the sky. And you — you stand in that cool, thin, blinding light with your ears full of a hundred thousand stone throats singing the same unchanging chord they were singing before you climbed up and will be singing long after you have to go back down for air you can actually breathe, and you feel, for a moment, how it would be to be part of that equilibrium, to need so little.

To be sparse and abundant at once.
