It is below.

Not deep in the old, cold way — not a lightless press of stone and time. It is just *below*, a world that has only recently learned it is inside. The roof fell, or never formed.

You are standing in a valley that should not be able to exist underground. It is a long, ragged trench blister, three or four kilometers end to end, where the earth exhaled and didn't close again. The sky is still there, but it is not sky. It is a ceiling, 200 meters above your head, made of raw basalt plates, still sharp-edged where they broke. Light comes down through it in columns — not pinholes, but great shattered skylights, house-sized, where surface noon falls straight down. Normal, warm, yellow-white daylight. It stripes the air with dust and steam. Between the columns is shadow, but not dark. The rock itself sweats light a little, or the air does.

The temperature is bath-warm. The air holds heat the way a mouth holds breath. When the sun hits the black rock, it doesn't just light it, it cooks it. You can feel the warmth rising off the floor in waves.

Everything here is new. That is the strangest part.

Geologically, there should be no valley here. There is no riverbed to explain it. No wind-scour. No smooth wear. The walls are not eroded, they are *emergent*. They look poured and then snapped. Long, clean fracture faces, still showing conchoidal ripples like broken glass, stair-steps of columnar jointing where the stone decided to be hexagonal and changed its mind halfway. Pahoehoe floors flash where you scrape them, still faintly vitreous. The whole place has the look of something that finished cooling last week. Edges are too sharp. Gravel hasn't learned to be round yet. It's a world in its first draft, all construction joints and flash lines, un-sanded. And because it is so young, water hasn't yet found its route.

Water here is not a river. It's an intermittent rumor. It is dampness as a condition. The walls weep. Not flow, weep — condensation beads on the fresh glass and gathers and falls in cold, sudden plops. In the mornings — when the surface air is cool and sinks through the skylights — the whole valley fogs for an hour, and you can hear ten thousand drips at once. The fog collects in shallow pans scooped into the basalt, irridescent with minerals, thrumming with life. Some are no bigger than a footprint, some are wide enough to wade. But they never connect. There are no banks, no tributaries. Just pockets. Sweat-puddles in a larger dryness. The air itself tastes of wet iron.

There are no plants. Not a blade, not a lichen, not even the green slick you expect on damp stone. The mineral is too raw, too hot, too new. The world here is mineral-black, rust-red, sulfur-yellow, salt-white. Everything that would be green is replaced by density of animal.

Because the valley is full.

At first from a distance it looks like the rock is moving. Then it resolves.

It is covered — *densely* — not in places, but as a rule — in fauna. The floor, the walls, the ceiling, layered on each other, near-touching, overlapping. Not one species. A living thatch. Large as dogs, small as ants, and everything between, but all built for this same task: to live on rock with no plants in warm damp half-light.

The dominant things are not insects, not exactly, though you call them that at first. They are low, broad, many-legged grazers the size of kneeling tables, with backs of overlapping basalt-colored plates. They crawl, and as they crawl their undersides rasp the rock. You can hear it — a constant, loud, industrial shhhk-shhhk, millions at once. They are eating the biofilm, the chemical skin that forms on fresh basalt in warm, wet air. The chemosynthetic slime that is not plant but is food enough. Their radulae spark faintly on the glass.

On their backs ride smaller things: white, eyeless shrimp-like harvesters that pick at parasites. In the gaps between the big grazers are legions of tight, beetle-bright runners that dart for droppings and shed plates. The walls are taken by clingers — things like bats with no wings, just huge splayed hands, translucent bellies pulsing, leaping from grazer-back to wall-crack and back. The air is busy. Not thick with birds, but thick with jumpers, gliders, casters — a constant, low, turbulent rain of bodies launching themselves across gaps, chittering, clicking. Nothing stays still. This is not a stable ecology. It is a bazaar in a storm.

It is loud. Not deafening, but insistent, like being inside a factory made of chitin. The rasping, the clicking of plates on plates, the wet slaps as something large dislodges and falls into a salt pan, the shrill peeps of the wall-clingers arguing over a condensation drip. And under it all, a faint, deeper cracking — the valley still cooling, still settling, still being born by inches.

And in it, scattered, you find built things.

At first you think they are just more mineral oddities. Then you realize the pattern is wrong. A low ring of stacked basalt flakes around the rim of a water pan, to keep the grazers out. A bridge of interwoven, discarded plates and dried gut-sinew spanning a fissure too wide to jump. Tiny cairns at intersections of grazer trails, each topped with a white pebble. Not human. Made by something with hands, but just barely.

You see them if you stand still long enough for the crowds to part around you: small, bipedal things, about as tall as your knee, with the same plate armor but bleached, using their front limbs like arms. They carry flakes of sharp basalt in both hands. They knap them against each other with a clack-clack that is almost lost in the valley noise. Primitive. Embarrassingly primitive — no fire, no metal. But they are everywhere, and they are dense, dozens visible at any moment, and they do not seem afraid of the chaos. One will walk straight up the back of a grazing giant to reach a better vantage. One will chase another and try to steal its flake-tool, and they will tumble screeching into a water pocket, sending shrimp scattering.

Their villages are not villages. They are momentary eddies where the turbulence slows: a shaded overhang under a ceiling slab, where thirty or forty have piled rocks into low walls, woven a roof of shed plates and sinew, left behind little caches of polished water stones. By tomorrow, the big grazers will have pushed through and knocked the walls flat, or a fresh rockfall from the raw ceiling will cover it all, and they will build it again fifty meters over.

There is no order here that lasts. No climax, no erosion into softness, no soil yet to hold a root. There is only heat, damp drip, hard light falling through a broken roof, and a frantic, overwhelming abundance of life that found a new warm place with rock to eat and water to drink, and decided, all at once, to be alive in it at full volume, building little temporary things out of the wreckage of the world being made under their feet.
