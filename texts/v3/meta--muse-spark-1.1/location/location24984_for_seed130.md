It was not built on the land. It *is* the land.

Low — so low you feel the weight of the air above it, so low that the world has settled here. A plain that goes on for a long day's walk in any direction, further than lamps can reach. Not flat in the way new things are flat. Flat in the way very old things are flat. Everything that could fall has fallen. Everything that could be worn down has been.

This is the Floor.

If you kneel — and you'll want to, just to hear something other than the roar for a second — you see it's not one material. It's slabs, plates, panels, poured sheets, a reef of concrete generations laid over iron generations laid over brick and block and aggregate, fused by time and pressure and repair into a single, continent-thick substrate. There is no soil under it. There are just deeper floors. The edges are softened. Corners have become quarters. Expansion joints have widened into canyons a hand-width across. The surface is pitted, scalloped, honeycombed by a billion years of cold condensation chewing at lime.

Because it weeps.

The air is cool enough to make your breath short, cool enough that sweat never dries, and saturated. Not raining, not exactly. The sky above is a low, dark vault — you can't tell if it's cloud or smoke or just the roof of the next level up, so far away its lights are only a bruise in the darkness. Water is born in the air itself. It beads on every exposed girder, on the underside of every pipe, and falls. Constantly. The Floor weeps from a thousand low points. Sheets of water form on the faint gradients and move, without hurry, toward the seams.

And the seams take it away. Between the plates are the Drains, long, straight, perfectly parallel where they haven't slumped. They run for kilometers toward a vanishing point in the gloom, three meters deep, ten across, full of black water that moves quickly and silently — in contrast to everything else. Your boots splash through an inch of moving film everywhere else.

The built-ness is total but the density is sparse. That is what makes it so lonely. Whoever made this place — or whatever made itself here — did not pack it tight. Out on the Floor there are Things, spaced like trees in a desert on that same low horizon.

A gantry the size of a cathedral, standing on four legs. Its paint is gone. Its joints are swollen with rust the texture of fungus. Its head traverses back and forth on a rail it has polished to a mirror, twenty meters a minute, back and forth, back and forth, lifting nothing. It has been lifting nothing for a very long time. The cable sways and drips.

Further off, a stack, a thousand meters tall but worn down to a blunt stump, still exhaling a column of hot steam that condenses instantly and falls as a circle of perpetual warm rain around its base. In that warm rain, things grow.

Vegetation here is sparse and apologetic. No grass. No trees. Black-green microbial felt lines the north side of every ripple in the concrete. In the splash zones of the steam stacks, there are reeds — not true reeds, but industrial reeds, things that learned to grow in aerated concrete dust and iron water. Hollow, jointed, pale stalks with no leaves, leaning all one way from the endless wind made by moving air through moving machinery. They tap against each other. You can hear them tap if you get close enough and put your hands over your ears, which you have to do often.

Because of the Sound.

It is not one sound. If you stand still and let your skull adjust, you hear layers.

The deep bass of the Drains reverberating in the Floor itself, a subsonic shudder through your molars.
The high, deafening scream of steam relief valves somewhere beyond sight, never closing all the way.
The mid-range clatter and bang and clang of that gantry and three others like it, working, working.
A sixty-cycle hum from high-tension lines that sag so low you could touch them, though you wouldn't, their insulation long since gone, crackling with St. Elmo's fire in the wet dark that throws blue light in pulses across the puddles.
All of it echoing off nothing, because there is no wall to stop it, just distance and damp.

And still, it is active. This is not a ruin. Ruins are quiet. This place is alive in the way a factory that has been left to run with no workers is alive. Winches turn. Pumps throb — you can see the bulge of water in the Drains pulse every seven seconds. Floodgates open and close somewhere with a clang that rolls across the plain like thunder. A line of riveted tanks ticks as it cools and heats. Water condenses, falls, gathers, drains, is lifted as steam, condenses again.

There are no animals. Nothing watches you. Nothing scurries. The only motion besides machine and water is the slow black growth at the lip of a puddle, and your own shivering.

The light never really comes. What light there is is made here: the amber sodium glow leaking from a cracked inspection window at ground level that shines underwater for a few feet; the intermittent white arc where a switching yard throws sparks the size of cats; your own lamp, which makes everything beyond its circle darker. The darkness is wet. You can feel it on your skin, particulate, heavy.

If you stay long enough you understand the rule of this place, because the place has worn its rule into itself: everything that was built will continue to operate until friction and water unmake it, and water will unmake it very, very slowly, particle by particle, and then the water will carry that particle to the Drains, and the Drains will carry it somewhere else on the Floor, where it will be laid down again as silt, as new Floor. 

It is the end of erosion. Not mountain to pebble to sand — but factory to slab to powder to factory, round and round, smoothing itself toward a perfect, dark, wet plain that will roar and weep forever with no one to hear it but the reeds.
