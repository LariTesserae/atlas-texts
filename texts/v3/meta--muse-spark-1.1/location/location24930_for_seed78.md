It is not a forest on a mountain. It is a forest that *is* the mountain, and the mountain is still being born.

You are very high. High enough that the air has thinned to a cold blade. High enough that the world below, whatever it was - sea, plain, old continent - is gone behind cloud, and you are left on a young range, a fresh crumple in the planet's crust. Black rock, still sharp. This is not old, weather-rounded high country. Erosion hasn't had time to learn patience. The basalt and glassy schist come up in knives, in teeth, in unfinished towers, in fault scarps that run straight for a hundred kilometers without a curve. Scree fields that have never been lichen-softened, only shattered again last winter. If you put your hand on it, it cuts. The planet pushed this up recently and hasn't decided what shape it wants yet.

And over - through - *as* - all of it: the Plant.

At first you think density will be choking. Total coverage, from orbit, looks like a second topography, a deep green-black felt thrown over the range. But that is a trick of distance. Up close the forest is almost nothing. It is empty. It is hollow like lungs are hollow.

The dominant form is not trees. There is no soil enough for trees up here, and there is almost no light. What grows here is a cousin to moss and a cousin to kelp and a cousin to nothing you know. Each individual is vast. One trunk - if you can call it a trunk - can be sixty meters across at the base, ribbed, dark green so deep it reads as black, shaggy with frost-feathers. It narrows as it rises for half a kilometer, then a kilometer, then it splits into three that split into twenty that split into a lacework of filaments as fine as hair, two kilometers above you, still climbing. And it is empty inside. The walls of the plant are only a meter thick, sometimes less, a tough, water-proofed rind over a lattice of living fiber. Inside each there is air heated by slow fermentation, and empty space, and condensation.

So the range is filled with these. Hundreds of them. Thousands, across a continent of new mountains. Each one is a cathedral you could walk into if you cut it, but you don't, because they are alive and they are the only windbreak you will ever have. Between them: gulfs. Real, vertiginous emptiness. You can see ten, fifteen kilometers through the forest because there is so little in between. Just cold, moving air, and the next lit pillar of plant, and the next, and the next, until curvature and darkness take them. It is a vast, vertical savanna made of living towers that are themselves empty.

It is always dark here. Not starless dark, but dusk-dark all day. The sun is either far north or simply occluded by the permanent weather system this range makes for itself. The light you get is violet-grey, a high-altitude twilight at noon, starlight at night that is shockingly clear. The plants don't seem to mind. Their skin is near-black, drinking what little photon trickle there is with 98% efficiency. Some of them glow faintly when touched - not bright, not for you, a slow phosphor blush that moves under the skin like blood. It has nothing to do with photosynthesis. It's communication.

It is very cold. Minus fifteen, minus thirty on a good day, colder when the high winds pour down from the interior. Your breath freezes before it leaves your mouth and snows back onto your collar. The water in this landscape is not in lakes. There are no lakes; the rock is too fresh, too cracked, too sharp to hold them. Water exists as damp. As a rule. It exists as breath.

During the day you hear it form. The warm air inside the giant aerophytes hits their cold outer rind and condenses. The whole forest sweats on the inside and weeps on the outside. Rivulets form high up, black and shining on black skin, and run down for a kilometer in silence, gather at the elbows and armpits of the branching, and there they drip. One drop every ten seconds, each the size of a bucket, falling a hundred meters through empty air to a catch-leaf below that flexes under the weight, then tips, and spills into the next leaf, and the next. The whole range is clicking, dripping, pouring in a thousand stoic rhythms. Where the drip finally hits rock it doesn't pool. It flash-freezes into a lens of cloudy ice, then sublimates, then freezes again. Pockets of water held in living tissue, in leaf-basins big enough to bathe in, always moving before they can truly freeze.

That damp is everything. That and the rock-milk the plants make from grinding basalt with acid root-hairs. It is enough.

And so fauna: impossibly abundant for a cold dark desert at six, seven kilometers up. All adapted to emptiness and verticality and damp. You hear them before you see them. Always.

The sound here is loud. It never stops. The wind is forced through the lattice of giants and comes out screaming or organ-toned, depending on which gap it found. Each tower creaks and sighs as its inner air heats and cools - a sound like a ship's hull. Water drips everywhere with the insistence of metronomes. And the animals: white, moth-things the size of blankets that ride the updrafts between towers, never landing; things like eyeless deer with wide, splayed feet that walk *up* the sides of the aerophytes, stripping frost-fur; and colonies of something that lives in the rind itself, chewing, chirping in chorus so loud your sternum vibrates, their song amplified by the hollow body of the host. At night, which is almost always, everything clicks and calls, because in a forest that is 99% air, sound carries for kilometers. You can hear a deer-moth step on a branch three towers over.

Life is fast here despite the cold, because the plants grow fast. A fresh lava crack from last year will have a sapling - black, ankle-high, bulbous - in a week. In a month it is taller than you, hollow, thrumming. In a year it is trying to join the canopy two kilometers up. You can watch the forest rebuild a landslide scar in a season. This is a dynamic place. Avalanches, growth, wind-fall of a kilometer-tall tower - which falls slowly, like a deflating airship, and then rots into a horizontal city of moss on moss. The predators here, lean and long-limbed, have to be clever. They don't chase; they wait at drip basins, on the few true trails.

Trails? Yes. Because of the built that is scattered here.

People live here. Not many. You go a long way between signs, which is how it should be because there is so much nothing to cross. Primitive in toolkit, not in thought. They do not build towns; the forest would outgrow them before the roof was thatched. Instead you find: a rock cairn with a soot mark, marking a safe ice lens. A bridge of braided plant fiber - harvested dead rind, light as balsa, strong as cable - slung between two flutes of living tower, humming in the wind. A shelter not built but *negotiated*: a hollow blister on the side of a young aerophyte is coaxed open, kept open with bone struts, lined with felted fiber and fat. Inside, a little lamp of plant oil, which makes almost no light and that is enough, because dark eyes have learned to see. They cut with stone and cured beak. They carry water in bladders made from inner leaf.

They don't farm. They husband drip. They know the song of each tower, which tells them if it is sick, if it will drop a branch-city soon, if the inner pool is drinkable today. They move vertical as much as horizontal. The rule here is simple and unbreakable: never cut the living skin all the way through. You make a slit and you let it heal around you. If you kill a tower you kill the pocket of weather that lets you live, and the cold true air rushes in and nothing lives there for years.

At night you sit on a fiber platform halfway up a plant that is wider than a hill, your legs hanging over a drop that has no bottom in the dark, only the drip-click below and the further light - the next tower's bioluminescent blush as a big glider brushes it - two kilometers away across empty, freezing, singing air. The rock beneath is young enough to still have edges. The forest around is old enough to have learned how to be a sky.
