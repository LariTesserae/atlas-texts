It is a low place. Not low like a valley — low like a bowl that forgot how to drain.

A single acre, maybe two, sunk into warm bottomland where the water table sits an inch under the humus. You step down into it off the dry road and the air changes. Not cooler — warmer. Close, post-rain warm, the kind of wet heat that condenses on your forearms and in your throat. The light in here is ordinary daylight, white and full, but it has to fight for every foot. It comes through the upper thatch and then it diffuses, greens, scatters.

This is not a forest. It is a mass.

From the ankle of the slope to the center, there is no ground that is not plant. Not plant-covered — plant. The soil, if it exists, is a rumor. The substrate is layers of living mat: root felt, sphagnum that grew over sphagnum, bladdered vines laid down over last year's bladdered vines, all of it swollen with water that never left. You don't walk so much as depress. Each footstep sinks three, four inches and releases a cold breath of trapped air that smells like green iron and warm sap. Then the mat sighs back up.

The water is everywhere, but not as a lake. As a condition. The leaves sweat. The aerial roots weep. Every vertical surface — and there are hundreds, because this place is packed — has a constant, slow drip. Rock wool. Bark gutters. The undersides of leaves tipped with a bright bead that gathers, falls, gathers again. In the middle of the bowl, where the land touches its lowest, the matting gives way to standing tea-colored water, twelve inches deep, eighteen, choked with duckweed so small and dense it looks like a putting green until you put your boot through it and it parts like skin.

And the factory is still here. Or what was a factory.

It was small to begin with. A hamlet press, a sugar mill or an oilseed crusher, hamlet-size tech for a hamlet crop. You can read it now only as interruptions in the plant logic. There — a brick chimney, maybe thirty feet, but you only see fifteen because the fig-strangler that took it has grown its own chimney, a hollow columnar trunk that wears the brick inside it like a spine. There — a catwalk, rusted to lace, but the lace has been filled in with a flat, ladder-like creeping vine that made the catwalk its trellis and then its body, keeping the shape after the metal gave out. A boiler, huge, riveted, belly-down in the muck, its firebox open and sprouting a dense colony of ferns whose fiddleheads uncurl black with old soot. Further in, a row of gear wheels still set in a concrete plinth, half-swallowed, but still meshing — not with each other, with root cogs.

Because it is still working. That is the strange.

The tech level is not ruin. It is industrial, and it is ongoing, but it has been renegotiated. The steam engine, or what was the steam engine, is now a hydraulic engine. The warm, wet air feeds condensation coils made of living trumpet flowers that collect water and dump it, dump it, into a header tank made of a single, modified gourd the size of a car. When the tank reaches weight, a tendon valve — yes, tendon — releases. Water slams down through a sluice of overlapping elephant-ear leaves, hits a wheel made of lignified spokes and living paddle-moss, turns it one quarter rotation with an immense, groaning **creak**. The gear-tree turns. The press screw, which is now a screw-root, decades thick, descends one thread.

Pressing what? The whole biomass. The grove presses itself. A pale, sweet-sap oil beads from seams in the lower mats and trickles in runnels to the standing pool. It has been doing this for a long time. The whole arrangement is established, settled, in balance. The erosion is not cutting it down; the growth and the wear have found a pact. Metal rusts at the same speed wood grows over it.

The sound is loud for how little moves. Rare animals — there is almost nothing — a white moth, a single treefrog you will never see. The loudness is not fauna. It is water and plant and machine. The constant tip-tip-tip becomes a roar because there are ten thousand tips. The boiler ferns hiss when the sun hits them. The big gourd tank, when it dumps, makes a coughing flood that slaps and gurgles. The wheel creaks, and the vine belts that still loop between axles slip and squeak, and deep in the mat there are pops as air bladders burst. Everything is slow. You can stand and watch a drip make its way down a chain of pitcher-leaves for a full minute and still not see it fall. The wheel hasn't made a full rotation in a week, maybe. But it never stops wanting to.

Rules here: Anything that holds a shape long enough gets taken as instruction. A pipe will become an instruction to grow a hollow stem. A belt will become an instruction to grow a tensile vine. A basin will become an instruction to hold water. No one is tending it anymore, but tending continues, translated. The built is scattered like scripture verses left out in the rain, and the vegetation, which is total, which is the place itself, reads them devoutly and spells them out in its own language, packed so tight there is no space left for any other interpretation.
