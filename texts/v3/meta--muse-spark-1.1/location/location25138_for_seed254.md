It is not a cave. It is a place that used to have a roof.

The whole city block sank — not all at once, not catastrophically, just settled. 0.4 below surface is enough. Enough that the streets around it step down to it with three shallow concrete steps on all four sides, worn in the middle to a dip. You stand on the curb above and you look down into it. A single room the size of a neighborhood market hall, open to the sky but below grade, and that is the entire world.

The walls that hold the earth back are old poured concrete, weeping. That is the first thing. The water. It doesn't run in a river. It comes from everywhere at once. The air is warm, bath-warm, 2.2 warm, so warm that the concrete sweats, so warm that the underside of every leaf is beaded. Behind the retaining walls the water table presses close, and it finds its way through in dark threads, in mineral stains, in a constant slow seep that leaves calcite lace and rust lines. It beads on rebar, it travels down root hairs, it drips. Always dripping.

And you would think with all that water it would be a pond down there, but it isn't, because it is packed. 2.9 packed. There isn't room for standing water, because there isn't room for anything empty at all.

The industrial greenhouse that was built here after the settlement — after they gave up trying to keep the block dry — never stopped being a building. That is what makes it urban. You can still see the logic of settlement inside it, even though you can't see an inch of unclaimed space. There are streets, but the streets are two feet wide and they are made of pressed duckboards laid over pipes, and the pipes are wrapped in fig roots. There are houses, second and third stories still, their iron balconies and fire escapes intact but their walls now are not walls, they are sheer hedges of growth that learned to be a wall. 

This is total vegetation, 2.7, not decoration — substrate. Mangrove-style prop roots have become load-bearing columns where the original brick piers rotted out a century ago. Broad-leafed aroids and bananas were planted for biomass and never stopped. Plantain stems thick as a man's torso run up through the old elevator shafts and punch out through what used to be a fourth-floor window and then fold back down again, because there is nowhere else to go. An old American-cafeteria fern, the kind that can eat concrete, has made the ceilings. Its black felt of rhizome mats the girders and hangs down a foot thick, and from the bottom of that mat smaller ferns grow upside-down. You have to push through it. Everything is walls of living matter pressed against other walls of living matter, with just enough gap left for a human shoulder to pass sideways.

And it is still industrial. 1.8. That never left. The reason it hasn't just become a swamp and collapsed is that the pumps still run. Low down in the root mass, bolted to the original slab, are three big single-cylinder Lister engines, the kind that run forever on bad oil, their flywheels polished by rags and hands. They don't make electricity anymore — at least not much — they turn belts, and the belts turn Archimedes screws that lift the weep-water up to the skinny tin gutters that run along the top of the retaining wall and spill it back outside to the storm drain. The gutters overflow constantly anyway. The engines tick, and chuff, and the belts slap. Everywhere there is black iron pipe, sweating, patched with hose clamps and tar, sweating condensation that runs down to join the seep.

Light is dim, 1.2. The sky is a long way off even though it's only twelve feet above your head. The canopy made by the banana leaves and the tarps and the fern ceiling and the steam closes it. What light gets through is late-afternoon light even at noon, green and scattered, falling in hard-edged shafts where a frond has been pulled back for a tomato start, and full of vapor. Dust doesn't hang here — water does. The light catches the mist from the engines and from human breath and from transpiration. At night, a bare bulb every twenty feet on braided cloth wire, hot to the touch, amber, strung through the leaves.

There is no animal life. 0.3. That's what tells you how long this has been closed. No pigeons. No roaches, even. No buzz. It should be humming with insects in this warmth, in this wet, but the industrial runoff way back and the total absence of gap habitat and maybe the fungicide they used for fifty years made it sterile to everything but plants and the people who tend them. So when a leaf drops, it just falls and is caught in the mat and rots there, without being eaten. It is silent in the way only plant places can be silent — except it is loud.

2.4 loud. Not in a city way. No voices carry far because the leaves eat them. What is loud is water and the works. The constant fat plip-plip-plip from everywhere onto everywhere else. The patter of condensation off a hundred leaf tips onto tin. The chk-chk-chk of the Lister exhausts slapping against wet concrete, and the groan of roots shifting around beams. Wood and vine creak. When the screws turn they grind faint grit. You can feel it in the duckboards under your feet. You have to raise your voice two inches from someone's ear to be heard, not because of a roar but because of a dense, close blanket of sound, all drip and creak and engine knock.

The rules here are simple and established, 2.0. Nothing is new. Things have found equilibrium.

2.  You clear your gutter stretch every morning.
3.  You keep your door — which is a curtain of cut vines — trimmed so it does not seal shut.

Everything changes slow, 0.7 slow. A hallway that was passable last spring is now a solid wall of philodendron and you just go another way. A tomato variety planted in the 60s has become woody, a shrub, giving sour fruit year-round. The concrete is rounded now at every edge where hands and roots have passed. The rust has stopped getting worse; it's a stable orange skin. The air always smells the same: hot iron, chlorophyll, wet burlap, the faint sweet rot of a cistern.

If you live down here — and there are maybe thirty of you, hamlet-scale, single-feature — you know the boundaries exactly. There are four walls, a floor, a ceiling of leaf. You know every plant by where it is, not what it is. That one that holds up the southeast corner. That one that whined when we tried to run the screw faster. That one that leaks more than the others.

From above, from the dry street, people walking by barely see it. Just a warm haze rising off a deep green block, and a loud, patient dripping, like a laundry that never stops, and the slow chuff of engines from below the sidewalk that have been running longer than anyone has been alive.
