This place is high.

Not high like a peak you climb to. High like a table left out in the weather, high enough that the air goes thin and mean and bright. A manufactured cordillera.

You first understand it as a mountain range from a distance — a long, uneven interruption on the horizon — but as you get closer the line resolves wrong. There are no slopes. There are faces. Sheer, fresh, vertical faces of raw concrete and structural steel, of poured ceramic and stacked block and welded plate, rising two, three, four hundred meters out of nothing. They are not mountains that have been built on. They are mountains that have been built. There is no bedrock in them. If you cut them open you would find more rooms.

The terrain is young in the geological sense, but not in the biological one. It has never been eroded. Edges have not had time to learn softness. Every corner is a ninety-degree knife. Every re-entrant alley between structures is a slot canyon with straight walls where the formwork was just pulled yesterday — you can still see the grain of the timber in the concrete, the stipple of trapped air bubbles, the rust-bleed from the tie wire. Rebar whiskers stick out into the air and end in bright orange points. Sheet metal flashings are still sharp enough to cut. There are no smoothed river stones here because there has never been a river, only the idea of one.

And yet water is here, intermittently. On a 0.9. 

It doesn't fall so much as it arrives. The cold air at this elevation can't hold much, but when the sun hits the black tar roofs and the vast, bright, south-facing factory walls, they exhale. Then, an hour later, when a cloud takes the sun away, everything condenses at once. Droplets bead on every underside of every pipe, on every I-beam flange, on the million linear meters of conduit and cable tray. It never really rains. It sweats. Water gathers in the low spots of this built topography — in the sag of a flat roof that was never quite flat, in the V where two enormous warehouse blocks lean toward each other but do not touch, in the upturned mouths of covered hoppers — and stays there for days, dark and still, until it goes away again. The whole range smells faintly of wet concrete, like a sidewalk after a storm, but multiplied to a valley scale.

Light is everywhere. That is the strangest part. At this altitude the sun is unfiltered, hard and white, and everything is made of pale, reflective stuff. Unpainted concrete, galvanized decking, whitewashed brick, acres of corrugated aluminum. And there is artificial light too, because the industrial never stopped. Not bright floodlights, but a low, patient, everywhere glow: amber worklights left on in empty sheds, sodium lamps in stairwells with no stairs, the bluish flicker of welding still happening three blocks over, reflected and reflected again until the shadows have no source. You squint here even on overcast days. The air itself looks bright, as if the photons are crowded.

It is dense. Not dense like a forest. Dense like a lungful of scaffolding. The built things do not stand apart. They interleave, overlap, overgrow, but without any plan that would call itself a plan. A rolling mill backs directly into a row of tenement balconies. A water tower sits on top of a water tower sits on top of a boiler house. Catwalks bridge gaps only a meter wide and then continue for a kilometer, becoming streets, becoming roofs for the streets below. Nothing is ever single. If you reach out you will always touch two different surfaces at once — hot and cold, rough and smooth. There is no horizon within it because another wall is always thirty meters away, and then beyond that another, and another. It is open only in the way that a valley town is open — you can perceive that it goes on for a long way, that there are neighborhoods in this range, industrial boroughs, districts of pure infrastructure — but you can never see it whole.

It is mostly quiet. That is what industrial means when it gets this big and this slow. The engines run, but they run at idle. Vast fans turn overhead at three revolutions per minute, moving air that is already too thin and too cold. Conveyors creep with nothing on them, squeaking every third link. Somewhere a press stamps once an hour, a dull *thunk* that you feel in your feet more than hear. The quiet is not an absence; it is a pressure. Sound doesn't carry correctly here — the fresh hard angles eat it.

What lives here lives abundantly, because the place is cool and damp enough and full of holes.

No vegetation to speak of — just a sparse, opportunistic fur. Black-green lichen films the north faces of everything. White mineral crusts where water evaporated. In the seams where two walls almost touch, a single hardy weed will make a vertical garden of itself, pale from lack of direct sun, roots sunk into the concrete dust. Moss in the condensation pans, thick as carpet, bright.

But fauna — fauna has decided this is perfect. Pigeons do not so much flock as become part of the air density, their wings whistling wrong in the thin air. They nest on every ledge, in every open electrical cabinet. Mice run the cable trays like highways. Ferrel cats move slow and fat between levels. And insects — things like insects — are everywhere in the dark damp layers: pale woodlice as big as your hand under loose sheeting, moths whose wings are the exact gray of fresh concrete dust, long-legged things that tend the condensation pools and skim them. Nothing seems to eat anything else very fast. Everything moves slow. Dynamic 1.4. Processes here take months.

Because the other rule, the final rule, is that this place is not entirely right.

At first you think it is perspective. You walk down what you thought was a straight service alley between two factory blocks and after six minutes you are walking slightly uphill even though the concrete under your feet is level. You look up and the block on your left is now taller than the block on your right was, and the sun is in a slightly different place. Corners sometimes do not meet. A building you passed on the inside shows up later on your outside. If you stand in the big open assembly yards — the great flat pours that serve as this range's alpine meadows — and stare at the far escarpment of stacked housing and gantry cranes, it seems both very close and a day's walk away, until you blink and decide which one it will be.

That is the weirdness, the 2.8. Reality breakdown not as fireworks but as a construction tolerance error in physics itself. The place was built totally, from no substrate, with no reference to the underlying world, and so it doesn't quite agree where it should be. Plumb lines diverge by a degree. A room can be larger inside when the door is closed than when it is open. Light pools. Gravity is polite about it, only correcting you gently if you stray too far.

Nobody demolishes here. You don't need to. You just pour another wall against the last one while the fog condenses on the rebar and the pigeons watch and the slow engines idle, bright and cold and new and endless, waiting to become something else.
