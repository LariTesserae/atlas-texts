It shouldn't be this hot up here.

That is the first rule of this place. The second is that nothing falls.

You are on a table that the world forgot to take down. Not a mountain with a peak — a cut. A mesa so high its top has weather, not climate. You came up the switchbacks for a day and a half; the air went thin and then, strangely, went thick again. The elevation is extreme enough that your ears popped twice and stayed popped. The horizon is lower than your feet. Behind you, the lowlands are a blue rumor. In front of you, the highlands go on.

And it's hot. Not thin-sun hot. Heavy, kiln-hot. At noon the basalt under your boots is warm through the soles. The light is normal, almost insultingly normal — high, white-yellow, the kind of light that should have pine shadows in it. Here it just has rock shadows, short and hard at midday, long and absolutely black in the evening. No filter. The sun gets down here and does work.

The table is not flat. It's been here long enough to forget it was ever flat. This is old rock. Established erosion, the kind you can trust. Wind has given up gouging and now just polishes. Water — when there is water — knows exactly where to go. The whole top of the mesa is canted very slightly inward, a palm ten miles across, shallow enough that you only notice when you realize all the old flood-scars point to the center. It's not a valley, precisely. There are no ridges high enough to call it that. It's a tendency. A basin the size of a county, open to the sky, with enough room inside it for several small towns and a weather system.

You can see the whole working of it from a small rise near the edge.

The ground is red-black, a flow that cooled into rope and bubble, now weathered down to a kind of dense, pitted brick. No soil to speak of. Where vegetation tries, it does so as apology: a salt-white lichen that grows into the leeward pits of the rock, a wiry grass that exists only as a root, a single quivering green wire that goes straight down into a crack and only shows two blades above. A few stunted ironwoods at the scale of shrubs, their trunks polished like old handles. There is not enough green to soften anything. The world remains hard.

And yet it is absolutely packed. That is the third rule and the one that makes you uneasy.

Not with people. Not with buildings. There are a handful of structures scattered so far apart you could miss them if you aren't looking for straight lines: a corrugated tin leanto collapsed around a winch, a stone cistern with its lid off, a steel A-frame with a cable still humming low. You realize after an hour that what you thought were rock outcroppings are something else. Low, wide, slump-shouldered towers made of rust and brass and black iron, with fans on top that haven't turned in years and fans on top that are turning now, slowly, silently.

They are industrial, but not in the way of a town. No factories. No compound. They are machines as individuals. Condensers. It's an old word that comes to you and fits. Each one is thirty, forty feet tall, a lattice of fins and gutters and cold-coils and drip-loops, all scaled for a job bigger than any one engine should do. They were built to mine air. Stacked combustion engines down at their base like hearts, drinking what little scrub grows and the oil that seeps from the rock itself, they run hot in the hot air and make a pocket of cold around themselves. At night they tick and groan. By dawn they are weeping.

This is the water. It doesn't come from rivers here. There are no rivers. It comes from the air itself, because there is nowhere else for it to come from. Hot day, cold metal, thin air forced to do an old trick. Every morning each tower sweats a short, shocking rain into its own footprint. Intermittent moisture in the most literal sense — for an hour after sunrise, the basin mists. Condensation beads on every iron fin and runs in engineered creases, patters onto basalt, finds the old grooves. For a little while a dozen temporary creeks exist, brown with rust, and then they sink or evaporate. By ten the ground is dry again except in shadow.

In those shadows is the fourth rule: life does not waste a site.

Between the towers, in the cracks, in the gutters of the towers themselves, the basalt is covered. Not with plants. With animals. With presences. The rock is upholstered with them, edge to edge, no room left between.

They are small at first glance and then not small at all. Black beetles the size of your thumb, with elytra like enameled stove lids, shouldering each other for the damp line under a drip pipe. Lizards the color of rust, stacked three deep under a single overhang, breathing in unison. And things that are not beetles or lizards but take advantage of the same logic — things with long, jointed legs that glitter like copper wire, things that look like folded paper until they unfold and drink.

But most of all — birds. Or almost birds. Swifts, swallows, martin-things, in numbers that shouldn't be possible with this little vegetation. They have taken the towers completely. Every fin, every girder, every bolt-hole is a nest. The entire iron body of each condenser hums not with its engine — those are eerily quiet, well-made, a low thudding you feel more in your molars than your ears — but with wings. The towers are cliff-faces. The air above the basin at dawn is solid with them, a moving thatch. They dip to the momentary puddles, they scream once and then stop, as if noise costs water.

It is mostly quiet for a place so active. That is the strangest thing. A landscape with this many processes running at once — heat cycling, metal cooling, water appearing, thousands of bodies jostling, feeding, dying, engines working, stone expanding — should roar. It doesn't. The high air eats sound. The basalt drinks it. Even your footsteps are muffled. You can hear the faint spang of a cooling fin as loud as a gunshot. You can hear the beetles walking.

At noon the birds hide inside the machines where it is cool. The engines continue to thud. The water has retreated into the rock, into the guts of the lizards, into the tiny bladders of the wire-grass. The whole basin seems empty, a hot iron plain under a bleached sky. Then a shadow moves off a tower — fluid — and you realize one of the swifts has learned exactly the shape of a flickering heat-haze, or the heat-haze has learned the shape of a swift.

You start to understand why the builders scattered their work and left. You don't build a city where water has to be wrung out of the sky with engines. You build tools and leave them running, and let something else move in and keep them company. The towers will run until they wear out. The birds will keep the beetles from choking the gutters. The beetles will keep the algae from sealing the condensers. The lichen will keep score.

Nothing falls here. Everything is caught before it hits the ground — water by a gutter, a chick by its neighbor's nest, heat by a shadow, sound by the sky.
