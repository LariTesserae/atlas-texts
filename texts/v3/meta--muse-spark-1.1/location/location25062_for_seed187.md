There is a place low down in the world where the world has finished happening.

It is not a valley, not really — the country around it has been rained on and frozen and thawed and rooted through for so long that it has forgotten how to have topography. A peneplain, broad and cool and peat-dark, scoured down to its own bedrock and then covered again in a thin skin of its own dust. If you walk a mile in any direction you gain or lose maybe a hand's breadth of elevation. The sky is wide there and ordinary, the light is normal white northern light. Everything is old and has stopped trying to become something else.

In the middle of that flatness there is a room.

You don't see it until you are at its edge, because it is not built. There is no edge of planks or stone. The ground simply thickens and lifts. The sphagnum and the haircap moss and the liverwort and the low, creeping stems of things that have no proper name outside this one spot have been growing on top of themselves for so many thousands of years that soil has ceased to matter. They are the soil now. Layer after layer of living and half-living and living-again mat, braided, felted, thatched — a dome like a broad, low blister on the plain, maybe thirty meters across and ten at its highest. From the outside it looks like an especially luxuriant hummock. You could walk right over it if you didn't know to listen.

Because it is loud.

Up close you hear it before you understand it. A low, continuous industrial sound, but without any industry. No engine. No building. No artifact at all — when you pull the moss apart there is only more moss, more root, more leaf.

It is the sound of water moving under pressure through a space that is almost all plant.

The dome is hollow.

There is an entrance on the north side where the mat has never quite closed, not because something tore it but because the plants themselves hold it open, leaf-edges curled back like lips. The air that comes out is cool — even in summer it holds that 8 or 9 degree chill of deep shade and evaporation — and damp in the way a root cellar is damp. Not wet. You won't wade. But everything condenses. Your breath condenses. The inside of the wall condenses.

Inside: a cathedral scaled precisely to the absence of people. The floor is a springy mattress of living moss a foot thick that gives under your weight and rebounds slowly. The walls and ceiling are the underside of the dome, all root and rhizome and hanging curtains of sphagnum and the pale undersides of leaves that have turned inward to catch the light that filters through. Light comes through normally, because the dome is thin enough in places — not thin like paper, thin like a leaf is thin. The whole structure glows. Green-filtered, soft, shadowless, bright enough to read by, bright enough that algae freckles the air itself. You can see the whole other side at once. You can see that there is nothing in it.

That is the strange part, after the totality of the plant.

Vegetation is not *in* this place. Vegetation *is* this place. There is no ground that is not root, no wall that is not stem, no ceiling that is not interlocked leaf. Even the air is mostly spores and the faint exhalation of a thousand square meters of photosynthesis. But per volume, it is almost void. Empty the way a church is empty. A hollow made entirely of structure.

And it works.

This is where the industrial quality lives, not in metal but in function. The dome maintains itself like a factory that never needed to be built. In the thickest columns that hold the roof up — which are not trunks but compound pillars of a dozen species grown together — you can hear sap lifting with a hydraulic hiss and clunk, valving. Great bladders of water in the upper dome swell through the morning and contract in the evening, pumping. The walls creak under tension. When the wind moves over the plain outside, the whole dome flexes a millimeter and then sighs back, and trapped air is pushed out through stomatal vents in a long, low exhale that sounds exactly like a bellows, or a boiler letting off. Drip is constant. Condensation gathers at the tip of every hanging rootlet and falls, ticking against moss, against moss, against moss. Loud in the emptiness.

It has probably sounded like this for a very long time. That is its rule, its equilibrium. Nothing is colonizing it. Nothing is rotting it away. The water that falls outside once a week in a short cold rain is caught, filtered through layer after layer of leaf, and sweated out again on the inside. The temperature barely moves day to night, summer to winter. Cool, damp, stable, settled into its final form.

And it is full of animals, in the way an empty house can be full of animals.

Not large animals — there is nothing for a deer to eat here that would not also be the house. But midges in their thousands hang in the open volume, each one keeping its distance from the next, a galaxy of gnats turning slowly in the green light. Beetles as black and polished as machine parts work the walls. Small, waxy-white tree frogs live their entire lives without ever touching earth, moving from leaf-tip to leaf-tip inside the dome, drinking only from condensation. When you stand still you hear them too, the chitter and peep layered over the hydraulic sighing, abundant, indifferent to you.

If you stay long enough, the damp starts to bead on your shoulders. The moss under your boots sighs down and up again. The light doesn't change.

There is no center, no altar, no built thing to find. The place does not want anything and does not make anything except more of itself, slowly, with the patient, thoughtless force of sap in pipes, with the soft, deafeningly loud persistence of a living pump house that never needed to be invented, because in this one small flat spot on an ancient plain, the plants learned to be a room all by themselves, and then stopped.
