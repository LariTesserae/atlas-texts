You don't find it by looking for it.

Up on the high country, everything has been old for a very long time. This is not mountains as in peaks and teeth. This is mountains after mountains have given up. An ancient peneplain at almost three kilometers, where the continent tried to be tall once and then spent a hundred million years forgetting how. The basalt has weathered into soft, bald domes, like the knuckles of buried giants. No cliffs. No scree. Just long, slow swells of rose-grey rock with a skin of lichen so thin and black you think it's shadow until you touch it. Wind has had its way for so long there is nothing left to take. It is warm up here - improbably warm. The air is thin but sun-heavy, tropical at altitude, and by mid-afternoon the stone itself gives off heat like a sleeping animal.

You walk for hours and think the place is empty. Then your boot drops an inch lower than it should.

It is a mouth. Not a cave, not a sinkhole exactly. A place where the old rock finally folded in on itself and didn't quite close. A pocket maybe thirty meters long, twelve wide, hidden in the seam between two of those blind domes. From above, just a dark slit choked with green. You have to turn sideways and let yourself down hand over hand on roots.

Inside, the scale changes. The sound changes first.

It is loud.

The plateau up top was wind-silence, a high hiss. Down in the pocket, the air is suddenly thick and crowded and it vibrates. The pocket is damp, not wet. The water here does not exist as a river or a pool. It exists as a condition. In the mornings, which you can tell have happened by the way the light slides down the western wall, condensation beads on everything and runs nowhere. The ancient rock sweats. By noon it has mostly evaporated again, leaving dark tide-marks on stone. The only permanent water is held in pockets - literal pockets - little cupped hollows in the rock that never quite dry, some no bigger than your palm, some as big as a bathtub, all perfectly round because they were not carved but worn, and worn, and worn.

And in and around every one of those transient mirrors, life is being overwhelming.

The vegetation shouldn't be able to be this lush on this little moisture, but it is. Not jungle-lush, but insistently, extravagantly alive in a sparse way. There is a lot of empty space between things, careful space, as if each plant negotiated its territory. A few bent, smoke-barked trees, no taller than a man, that have grown sideways more than up, their leaves broad, waxy, dark green almost blue, turned to catch the shaft of normal, yellow-white daylight that falls straight down at noon and nowhere else. Between them, ferns that unfurl to the size of doors, but only five or six of them in the whole pocket. Great clumps of moss that are not soft but dense and springy like muscle. Everything has room to breathe. Nothing crowds. And yet everything is covered, is itself a world.

Because the fauna.

You thought "overwhelming fauna" would mean herds, swarms. It's not. It's density of being in a small place. The air is alive with it and it is deafening. Beetles the size of your thumb, iridescent green-black with backs that click like metronomes when they open. Clouds of tiny amber moths that live for a day and spend it all hitting the light. Lizards with translucent throats that inflate and pop, pop, pop in unsynchronized chorus. Something in the moss - you never see it - that makes a continuous high, glassy trill, like a wet finger on a glass rim. And wasps, long and solitary, the color of dried blood, that tend the fern fronds and make the loudest sound of all: a low, sawing buzz that you feel in your molars. There are no predators and prey here the way you think of them; it is all maintenance, tending, eating, being eaten, decomposition happening in real time, leaves skeletonizing as you watch, beetles cleaning them. The dynamic is active and constant, but not frantic. It's a workshop that never closes.

The built part - you almost miss it under the biology. 

At first you think the black threads strung between the trees are vines. They are too straight. Too still. They are filaments, maybe a few hundred of them, each no thicker than a hair, each taut between stone and bark and air, not swaying in the updraft. They are not attached. They end a millimeter before the surface and hold there, humming. When the condensation comes, water climbs them. Not down. Up. Beads of water run upward along the filament and gather at the nodes where several filaments meet in the empty air, forming a droplet that grows and grows until it is heavy and then - it doesn't fall. It vanishes. Goes somewhere else. The air around the nodes is cool, even on this warm day, and smells faintly of ozone and wet iron.

There are other things. A low, knee-high wall that runs for two meters and then melts back into native rock, its edge so eroded you can only tell it was built because the lichen refuses to grow on it. A shallow bowl in the stone floor, perfectly hemispherical, a meter across, that holds dust and three of those amber moths, dead, perfectly preserved. And sitting half-under a fern root, a device or a house or a tool - you cannot tell scale in that way - made of something that looks like folded basalt but is warm to the touch and faintly gives under your palm, like firm flesh. It breathes once every forty seconds. You can hear it if you hold your breath during a lull in the insect sawing.

No one lives here now, or maybe the concept of living here is wrong. Someone built a humidity machine, a garden trap, a prayer. A place to keep a damp pocket alive in a high, dry, worn-flat world. They built it with physics that hasn't been explained yet, that treats gravity as a suggestion for water and time as something you can stretch thin over stone. Then they left it, or became part of it.

The rules here are simple and strange:

Water remembers where it was and tries to go back up. 
Sound has weight. After an hour, your chest aches from it. 
Nothing rots without being immediately used. 
If you stay still too long, the moths will mistake you for terrain and try to drink the salt off your forearms, their tiny tongues startlingly cold.

You sit with your back against the smoke-barked tree, in the one-meter of unclaimed space, and you are loud in your breathing and sweating in this warm, hidden room that has been here since before the idea of rooms. Above you, through the slit, the ancient, endless plain glows white-hot and empty. Down here, everything is busy, damp, strange, and utterly intent on continuing.
