There is a room underneath everything else.

You don't find it by looking. You find it because in one particular corridor on the low floor of the Mill, where the air is already warm and close, there's a service door that never closes properly anymore. Behind it, a short ladder goes down into a concrete throat, and then you are in it. It's not on any current plan. It was built to be something else and then outlasted its purpose.

It is a single room. That's all. No exits except the way you came.

The room is maybe twenty feet square, but tall — tall out of proportion, like the inside of a silo laid on its side and forgotten. The walls, the floor, the ceiling, are all made. Poured concrete, old iron plate, rivets gone black. There is no natural ground anywhere. Even the floor is not ground; it is a slab poured over a slab poured over a slab, each one smoothed by a different decade of work until the edges have lost their corners. This place is ancient in the way only industrial things can be ancient — not ruined, but *settled*. The water has been working on it for longer than anyone who works here has been alive, and it has taken all the sharpness off. The concrete lips are round as river stones. The rebar shows through in places where the pour has sagged, polished bright where hands have had to steady themselves. The iron stairs that go nowhere, the catwalk that ends mid-air three feet above the floor, the sump channel that runs the perimeter — all of it is worn down to its final shape. Nothing more will erode without the whole Mill going with it.

It is low. You feel the weight of all the building above you and the closeness of the water table below. The air is warm — not hot, but warm like a greenhouse in late summer, 80-something that never breaks. And it is wet.

Everything weeps.

The walls weep. The ceiling weeps. Condensation beads on the iron and falls without ever stopping. A pipe as thick as your torso runs along the upper wall, sweating continuously. Somewhere above you, beyond the ceiling, a heat exchanger or a press or a dye vat is running and has been running for years, and this pipe is carrying its breath away. It drips, *tok-tok-tok*, into the sump channel, and the sump never gets fuller and never gets emptier. The water in the channel is clear and shallow and warm to the touch, moving because there is a pump somewhere that you can never see that pushes it. That is the sound.

The sound is deafening.

It is not a bang or a grind. It is the combined, amplified roar of water striking water in a closed concrete box. The dripping from thirty feet up hits the surface of the sump and echoes. The spillway at the far corner where the channel drops two inches — just two inches — creates a white, endless hiss. The pipe thrums. The room is a drum. When you first drop in, you have to open your mouth to equalize. You cannot talk. After ten minutes your brain evens it out into a kind of weather and you can hear yourself think again, but it never gets quieter. It is a single, perfect, unchanging white roar that has been here for decades.

Because nothing ever changes here.

The level in the sump is held by some old industrial equilibrium — float valve, outflow, inflow, all in balance. The temperature stays warm because the building stays warm. The light is normal, by accident: two wire-caged industrial bulbs in the ceiling, the kind that never burn out because the voltage is kept low, and a thick, clouded glass block window at ground level outside that lets in a square of ordinary daylight, grey-white, full of motes. The air never moves except to condense and fall.

It should be dead. Instead, it is lush.

In the emptiness — and it *is* empty, almost embarrassingly empty, a great hollow volume with almost nothing in it — whatever does manage to live has the place to itself. A mat of moss has taken the lower two feet of the north wall, so thick and saturated it looks like upholstery, deep green and dripping. Maidenhair fern grows out of the seam where the concrete meets the iron, in a perfect, trembling fringe, its black stems beaded with water. Some kind of philodendron or its feral great-grandchild has rooted in the silt in the sump channel, one plant, with leaves as big as dinner plates, glossy, curling around the pipe bracket. The floor slab is stained with algae where the drips land. It's not a jungle. There are four, maybe five kinds of plant total, but where they are, they are absurdly healthy, swollen with warm water and constant light and no competition. They are lush the way a single houseplant left in a bathroom becomes lush — indulged, overgrown, private.

Nothing else lives here. Very rarely, a cave cricket, pale as the concrete, hangs on the fern and then is gone. Once, a frog. It did not croak; the room was too loud.

The rule of this place is simple: everything here was built, and everything built has been made patient by water.

If you stay long enough, the water that beads on your forearms runs down and joins the sump. If you stay longer, the roar flattens out and you can hear the tiny differences inside it — the place where one particular bolt drips slightly off-rhythm. You understand that this room wasn't designed to be like this. It was designed to be dry, to be functional, to be visited. Now it is a pocket of final-form equilibrium, a hidden bathhouse for the building itself, doing exactly what it will be doing a hundred years from now: weeping, growing, roaring, staying warm, staying low, staying exactly this full.
