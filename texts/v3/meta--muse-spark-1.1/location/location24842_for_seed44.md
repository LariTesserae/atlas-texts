It is low. You can tell before you see it because the air gets heavier and the road, which was packed dirt, goes soft and dark. You're in the delta now, or the bottom of a dish that was once a peat cut and still wants to be a swamp. The land around is flat for miles - fields, paddies, reed - but this one place has sunk another six feet, just enough. The water table lives right under your boots here. If you stand still you can feel it pushing up.

It calls itself a town but it's really one thing: a mill and its houses, all bound together in a space no bigger than a city block. You could walk its perimeter in ten minutes if you could walk straight. You can't.

This is a cane town. Or was rice, or hemp, or whatever grows fast and wet and can be fed to teeth. The reason it exists is the screw press in the middle, still there after eighty or a hundred years, settled into its own weight.

The vegetation is total. That's the first rule: nothing here stays uncovered. The mill was built of brick and iron, standard lowland industrial - two stories, boiler house, drying floors, a line of row houses for the workers with shared walls. All of that is still underneath, but it is not the substrate anymore. The plants are.

Living fig and banyan and water-ivy and a fast, anonymous vine with heart-shaped leaves have made architecture out of the architecture. The walls sweat roots. The roofs are thatch now even where they were tin, because creepers laid themselves over the corrugation and then soil collected in them and then grass took the soil. You see a telephone pole and it's not a pole, it's a core for a column of epiphytes so thick you can't find the wood if you put your hand in. The pipes - and there are many pipes, black iron, lagged with old asbestos and new moss - are trellises. Steam runs through them still.

It is wet in every way at once. There is the canal that feeds the boiler and drains it, tea-brown and slow, lipped almost to its brim. There is the water that weeps out of the lower bricks themselves, saltpeter and condensation, running down in dark tongues. There is the air, warm - a real, bathhouse warm, 85 degrees even in the shade - so saturated that breath and boiler exhaust look the same. At noon, when the press is running full, the whole hamlet exhales and everything drips. Water condenses on the underside of leaves big as doors, hangs, falls. It condenses on your arms. On the underside of every catwalk and gantry, there are still, fat drops that never quite stop forming. The ground is never ground; it's a mat of root and pulp and boardwalk, spongy. If you pull up a plank, water fills the hole immediately.

You hear it before you see any of that working. Loud.

The engine never actually stops. They bank it at night but you can hear the flywheel turning, a low, patient thud-thud-thud you feel in the walkways. Then the belts - flat leather belts ten inches wide, running from the central shaft out in all directions overhead, slapping and creaking through slots in walls that are now leaves. The cane knives chatter. The screw press itself groans. Metal on fiber. And under that, the hiss. Every joint leaks a little steam because the gaskets are chewed and swollen and half replaced with packed fiber. White jets find the cool air and scream thinly, then vanish. The sound bounces because the space is so dense. Leaf and brick and boiler plate all throw it back at you. People shout here even when they don't need to.

And there are people. It's urban that way, not scattered sheds but layered, intentional, close. The mill houses are still houses, but their second floors have extended themselves out on frames made of living trunks, figuring that if a tree will grow right up against your window you might as well lay boards across it and put another room there. Corrugated walls made of woven stem. Light comes down in columns where they've cut it to come down. Laundry is hung everywhere, because it never fully dries. Children run on the upper walks made of cane waste compacted and resprouting. In the center, in the factory itself, the work floor is clean - surprisingly clean - swept clear of leaf litter twice a day. Old women do it with actual brooms while the press turns. You keep cutting back or you lose the town. That's the local law. Every householder has a machete that's also a garden tool. If you don't cut your frontage for three days, the vine finds the window latch.

Everything smells green, but not fresh. Hot pulp, molasses, boiler oil, wet mortar, the sweet ferment of crushed stem underfoot. The only animals you notice for a long time are none at all, because there aren't many. Too much human, too much machine, too much plant and not enough gap. No birdsong under the belts. Once in a while a dragonfly big as your hand does a patrol down the main alley between the press building and the houses, wings rattling. Rats, of course, but you hear them more than see them in the thatch. People keep cats, skinny and quiet and damp-looking.

Light is ordinary - no colored sky, no second sun. Just filtered, bounced, broken by steam. The canopy made by roofs and vines and the high carrier belt is about thirty feet up. Midday sun shafts it and turns the vapor to gold and then it is gone and it's just shade again. The far edge of the place is definite: a low earthen levee ringed with old mangrove that the company left as a windbreak. It holds the hamlet in, holds the seasonal flood out, just barely. Beyond that levee the paddies are open and quiet and too bright. Inside, it is always dimmer, denser, closer.

Nothing here is fast. The mill turns at the same speed it turned thirty years ago. The vines grow a hand's breadth a day and are cut back a hand's breadth a day. The water rises in the canal an inch by noon and falls by evening. The bricks settle another hair lower into the peat. You get the impression that if the boiler ever went cold for good, it would take the jungle exactly one wet season to finish pulling the whole hamlet down flush into the mat, without any drama. But as long as someone feeds cane in one end and keeps the firebox fed and keeps the machetes sharp, it holds in perfect, loud, warm, dripping balance.
