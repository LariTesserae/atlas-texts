It is not a cave. Not yet.

If you put your hand on the back slope of the hill where the grass has gone thin and peaty, where sheep don't stand because the ground gives slightly, you will feel it is cool. Cooler than the air should make it, even in summer. If you kneel — and you have to kneel — and pull the lip of turf and root-mat back like a badly healed scab, there is a throat underneath. Not a hole someone dug. A place where the earth hasn't quite finished deciding to be earth.

You have to go in on your belly at first. For about your own length.

Then it opens.

A single room, below surface. No bigger than a pantry, or a chapel if chapels were made for one person to sit in. You can stand in the middle if you bow your head a little, and if you stretch your arms you will not quite touch both walls at once. That is the lie it tells at first. The second lie is that it's dark down here. It isn't.

The light is bright. Unreasonably bright for being under a hill.

The ceiling is very low and very thin — a slab of new stone, maybe only a handspan thick, still soft at the edges like drying plaster. It glows. Not with phosphor or fungus, though there is fungus too. The rock itself is translucent, like alabaster held up to the sun, and the sun is right there, on the other side. You can see the shadows of grass blades up there, huge, swaying and combing over you. You can see the shadows of clouds passing. You can see your own hand's shadow from above when you put it on the hilltop, dark and enormous. The light comes through warm and directionless and white, so white it makes the edges of things hum. It has no source down here, just presence. In the brightest part of afternoon the little room is almost painful to look at.

And still it is cool. The kind of cool that lives in stone. Your breath makes a brief ghost. The floor of the room is cool clay that has never dried, smooth and slightly cupped, like the palm of a hand. It sweats.

That's the water here. There is no stream, no pool. The hill is mostly dry. But this room is damp in the way a mouth is damp. Condensation beads constantly on the glowing ceiling and gathers, hesitates, falls. Drip. Then a long time. Drip. Each drop makes a dark star in the clay. Along the lowest seam of the wall, where floor meets wall all the way around, there is a fringe of moisture that never quite becomes a rivulet. If you press your finger there it comes away cold and mineral.

Because it is so damp and so bright, things try to live here, but the room is too new for them to know how.

This is pre-erosion, embryonic geology. The stone is still learning to be stone. In places the wall isn't rock at all but compacted silt that will be rock in a thousand years if nothing interrupts it. It has the thumbprints of its own making in it, ripples and folds and little collapsed bubbles. There are no stalactites. There are only the ideas of stalactites — blunt noses of calcite pushing out a millimeter and stopping, as if losing interest. The corners of the room are not sharp. The whole place has the unfinished quality of something breathed into soft wax.

Vegetation is sparse and confused. A few roots from the grass above have punched through the glowing ceiling and hang down like pale wires, an inch, two inches long, dripping, with a single white hair-root leaf at the end that should not be able to photosynthesize down here but does, greedily, in the impossible light. On the north curve there is a mat of lichen that has come in with the air, flat and grey-green and dry-looking despite the damp. That is all. The rest is bare, shining clay.

And it should be empty. It looks empty, at first glance.

It isn't.

The fauna is abundant. Abundantly, impossibly abundant for a pocket this small and this sealed. The air, when you stay still and let your eyes adjust to the brightness — which is a strange thing to have to do in a hole — is full.

There are moths. White moths the size of your thumbnail with eyes too large, that do not fly so much as levitate from wall to wall, alighting on the glowing ceiling where they throw huge, crisp shadows onto the grass-world above. There are cave crickets, dozens, pale amber, their long antennae testing the clay. There are woodlice that have become almost transparent. There are salamanders, small and black with gold flecks, moving very slowly along the wet seam, each one carrying a bead of water on its back that it never spills.

That would be normal, for a damp bright hole.

What is not normal is how they occupy the space.

One of the salamanders walks up the wall and onto the ceiling and keeps walking, upside down across the light, unconcerned. A cricket jumps from the floor — and doesn't come down for a long time, hanging for three, four seconds in the middle of the room as if the air were thicker there, its legs still paddling. A moth passes behind another moth that is in front of it, and for a half-second both are on the same side of each other. The scale is wrong. You think at first the room is a meter and a half across, then you blink and your hand, resting on the far wall, looks like it is very far away. Then close again. The room is intimate, a pocket, a hidden organ of the hill — but every now and then, when you are not looking directly at the edge of your vision, it is larger inside. Not cavernous. Just... more room than there should be to hold all this life.

Because nothing here can stand to be crowded. The density is deliberately low. Each cricket maintains a precise, polite distance from the others. Each salamander has its own quarter of the wet seam. Even the drips space themselves out: one side of the room, then the other, then the middle, as if taking turns. The silence enforces it.

It is very quiet. The hill muffles everything above. No wind in here. The sounds that exist are small and discrete and do not overlap: the tick of a condensing drop forming on stone; the almost inaudible papery flex of a moth wing; the soft *sink* of your own weight shifting in clay; a cricket cleaning its leg with a sound like a dry leaf being folded. If two sounds happen at once, it feels like a mistake, and all the fauna pause until quiet is restored.

And then there are the structures.

Scattered. You almost miss them because they are made of what is here.

Near the entrance, half-sunk in clay, a little arch of twigs and grass stems no taller than a teacup — woven, not fallen. Further on, three pebbles stacked, too balanced to be accidental, already going soft as the hill decorates them with new deposit. Against the far wall: a shallow hollow scooped out and lined with white root-hairs in a spiral. Not human. No tool marks anywhere. No plastic, no cloth, no metal. Tech has never happened here. These are built things, but built by what lives here, in the way bowerbirds build. Offerings? Houses? Traps for light?

The moths seem to tend them. Or maybe they just rest near them.

The rules here are still being written, and the room is lax about enforcement.

Gravity is mostly down, but not dogmatic about it. Light has a little weight — dust falls slower in the brightest patch. Time pools in the clay; a footprint you make fills in with water first, then silt, then smooths itself out in about as long as it takes you to forget you made it. If you stay long enough, the damp begins to bead on you, too, on your eyelashes and forearms, and the lichen watches you with no eyes. The bright ceiling shows the shadow of a bird crossing overhead, huge and sudden, and for that second every cricket and salamander and moth stops and looks up — up through the stone at the sky they have never seen — and the whole pocket holds its breath with you.

You are not supposed to stay long. Not because it is dangerous, but because it is unfinished, and unfinished things should not be watched too closely. They become self-conscious.

If you back out the way you came, on your belly, through the root-throat, the turf settles back. The hill looks like any hill. The light out here is suddenly less bright than the light you left below.

After a rain, if you remember to look, there will be a darker circle in the grass about as wide as a body. That is the only map to it.
