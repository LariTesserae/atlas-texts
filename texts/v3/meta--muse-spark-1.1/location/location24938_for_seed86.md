It is not a cave. Not in the way you think of a cave as a place you enter. It is below surface the way a wound is below skin.

The ground opened — recently, in geologic time. A long joint in the basalt gave way and a section of the world dropped. Maybe it was lava that drained. Maybe limestone that finally dissolved and couldn't hold the weight above it. What remains is not a tunnel but a rift. A valley that happens to have a ceiling.

You can walk for an afternoon and still be inside it. That is the scale of it — town-sized, valley-sized. A low, broad trough a mile wide in places, pinched to a hundred feet in others, running straight until perspective makes its walls meet. Above you, the roof is not consistent. In places it is fifty feet overhead, rough and dripping, black where water has never dried. In other places it has fallen away entirely, and there are slots — long, ragged skylights where the surface is a pale, overcast line, too high to climb to. That is all the light this place ever gets.

It is dim. Not dark. Dim the way late evening is dim. The light that falls through the slots doesn't scatter well. It catches dust and cold mist and then it dies. In between the slots, there's a residual glow from the rock itself — not bright, not weird, just enough damp sheen to see the shape of things. Your eyes adjust after an hour to a world made of edges.

The rock is young. That is the first thing you feel with your hands. Nothing here is smoothed. The breakdown piles are not rounded river stones but fresh, angular blocks, cleaved along crystal planes, still sharp enough to cut. The walls show hackle marks where they tore apart. Chatter marks. Feathers of fresh chert. Even the mud on the floor is gritty with unweathered grit. No sand has had time to form. This place was born and then it stopped changing.

The air is cool, a steady cool that doesn't move. Fifty degrees, fifty-five. The sort of cool that makes your breath visible only if you stand under a slot after rain. It holds moisture without ever raining itself. The water here does not run in rivers. There is not enough water for that. It comes as condensation. It beads on the low ceiling in the morning — if you can call it morning when the light never really changes — and gathers and falls. Drip. Drip. Drip onto the same exact stones it has dripped onto for thousands of years. There are shallow pans in the basalt, knee-deep at most, smooth-lipped, filled with cold, clear, absolutely still water. Water so still it has a skin of limestone dust. Between the pans, the ground is damp to the touch but not wet; black silt that sticks to your boots.

And because it is damp, there is life, but sparsely. No forest. No soil deep enough for that. The plant life here has not tried to take over. It has learned to wait in corners. Pale crust lichens the color of old paper, spread thin over north-facing boulders. Clumps of a fern that never unfurled quite right — ghost-white, translucent, no bigger than your hand, growing only within three feet of a pool's edge where the splash keeps it alive. Occasional mats of moss like wet wool, clinging to cracks. A single, stunted juniper that found a chimney of light through a slot, its roots crawling fifty feet sideways across naked rock to find a puddle. Everything is spaced out as if politely. Lots of nothing between.

The density of things is low — but the density of *presences* is not.

Because the rock is covered.

At first in the dim you think the walls themselves are textured. Then texture moves.

It's fauna. Overwhelming, unignorable, loud fauna. The whole rift, from end to end, is a roost, a hive, a warren. Not in a teeming-jungle way — not a mass of bodies on top of each other. In a sparse way, spread out, with space between them, but continuous to the horizon. This place is inhabited to its carrying capacity. 

Small white cave crickets the length of a thumb, in densities of thousands per hundred feet, their antennae waving from every crevice. Sleek blind salamanders in every pool, stacked like spoons, gills fanned. A colony of pallid, long-eared bats hangs from the ceiling in a loose sheet, not clustered tight but spaced one to a foot, one to a foot, for a mile, breathing in unison. Black beetles with backs like polished coal working the silt. Something like a rat but with a mole's hands and no eyes, digging shallow scrapes.

And they are loud. That is the shock after the dim and the cool. The place is loud.

Not because any one thing is loud — but because there is no wind and no water-sound to mask it, and the sound has nowhere to go. The ceiling traps it. The crickets saw. A high, glassy trill that layers and layers until the rock itself seems to hum. The bats click and chatter to each other, a constant dry rustling conversation. The salamanders in the pools surface with soft plips, over and over. The beetles tick. The rat-things snuffle and drag. The sum is a low, broad, incessant roar like a room full of people whispering at once. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself.

There are no buildings. No walls, no hearths, no roads, no cairns. The rift has never been touched by hands that build. But it is not without technology.

Watch the beetles. The big ones, the black ones. They pick up flakes of chert — those razor-fresh shards produced by the young collapse — in their mandibles and hold them. They use them. They wedge them under the shells of snails that graze on the lichen and pry. If the flake snaps, they drop it and get another. In the silt by the pools, the rat-moles have collections — caches of particularly good stones, carried from hundreds of yards away, favored for cracking. You see the little anvils: a pitted basalt cobble, surrounded by broken shell, surrounded by a scatter of used, chipped flakes. No nests. No art. Only the earliest tool-use, hand-to-mouth, stone on stone. Primitive, manual, undeveloped. Culture that never decided to become construction. It is as it is.

And this is the law of this place: it does not change.

Drips fall but do not erode. The pools do not overflow. The light never gets brighter or dimmer; the slots are too deep for sun to move across. Nothing rots quickly in the cool. The lichen does not spread. The bats do not starve and do not overbreed. The stones are used and dropped and used again. The sound never swells or dies, it holds in perfect equilibrium, loud and even, like a held breath. You can sit for a long time on a fresh-edged block with your feet in damp silt, listening to the thousandfold saw and click, watching condensation gather on the ceiling until it is heavy enough to fall, and you will see that it falls in exactly the same place it fell last time, into exactly the same pan, making exactly the same ripple that smooths itself exactly the same way.

It is a fresh world that got stuck right after it was made.
