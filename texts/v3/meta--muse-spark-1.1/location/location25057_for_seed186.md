It is too high to be this green.

You are at almost six thousand meters, inside a bowl carved out of old, tired rock. Not a sharp, young cut — these walls are established. Weathered into a patience. The rim is a continuous basalt lip, black and frost-shattered into long, sloping shoulders, and the bottom is a mile down from it, and three miles across. A valley the size of a small country if countries could be uninhabited. The sky is low. It is always low here. A ceiling of cloud that never quite snows, never quite clears. Light doesn't come through it so much as it is remembered somewhere above it.

So this is night, but not night. A polar dusk that lasts months. The light level never rises above that hour after sunset when you can still see the shape of your hands.

And it's cold that has weight. A dry, thin cold that burns the inside of the nose, but not dry. That's the trick. The air is damp. The whole bowl weeps.

From the black rock of the rim, heat still leaks — deep heat, old heat. Where it meets that thin polar air, it condenses instantly and never stops. The valley makes its own weather in small, local loops. You breathe out and your breath freezes before it fades, but you breathe in and the air tastes wet, metallic, like licking a battery. Droplets form on everything that is not ice. If you stand still, water beads on your eyelashes. The stones are never quite dry; they shine with a film that never puddles, never runs. Only in the lee of the big things does that moisture find enough purchase to become a drip, a rill, a black creek no wider than your wrist that runs ten meters and then vanishes back into frost.

There is nothing built here. Not a cairn, not a wall, not a cut. No straight line at all.

And yet the valley is lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like something that has learned to waste nothing and still be excessive. Each plant stands alone. This is the emptiness — you could throw a stone in any direction and hit nothing but cold air and thin snow for a long time before you hit the next one. Density is so low you feel it as a kind of vertigo. The bowl is mostly void, hollow weather and basalt.

Then there are the towers.

They rise out of the frost every three or four hundred meters, and there must be a hundred of them valley-wide. At first you think they are trees and then you know they aren't. They are vents, or blooms, or factories that grew themselves because no one would build them.

Each one is a trunk of layered black ice and fiber, as thick as a house, twenty meters high, splitting at the top into a crown of leaves that are not leaves. They are broad, dark, fleshy slabs the size of doors, the color of deep bruise-green, almost black in this light, angled not to catch sun — there is no sun — but to catch condensation. They are cold traps. Water beads on them in sheets, runs down their veined troughs into the heart of the trunk. You can hear it, if you put your ear close, ticking and trickling. The leaf-plates shiver constantly, even when there is no wind, adjusting.

And under that trickle, deeper: a thump. A slow, compression release. Inhale. Exhale.

Because this is where the industrial part lives. There is no industry *in* this place. The place *is* industry.

Inside each tower, the water percolates down to the residual heat of the rock, to a bacterial mat that burns sulfur and iron with a low, furious metabolism. It boils. The tower is a boiler, a distillation column made of living pulp. Pressure builds. The trunk swells slightly. Then it vents — not from the top but from low collar pores near the base, and when it does, it sighs out a cloud of hot, mineral steam that smells like hot iron and kelp, and the steam hits the cold air and becomes instantly a fog of damp that seeds the whole bowl, that waters the next tower downwind. Each tower exhales for its neighbors.

Some have split. Their bark is a jacket of rust. When the pressure cycle runs, their fibrous plates grind. That is what you hear in the quiet.

The quiet is profound. There is no wind most of the time. The cloud lid holds everything down. Sound doesn't carry. What you get instead are these small, industrial breaths: *huhhhh... chuff... huhhhh...* from far off, from near. A piston sound. Then silence for minutes. Then a clank. Then a trickle.

The ground between them is covered in a low fur, a kind of lichen-moss that is not low at all if you get close. It is knee-high, spongy, black olive, each filament ending in a tiny glass bead of water that holds the dark. It crunches underfoot like snow that is alive, and after it springs back. This is the river system here — not rivers, but this field that holds moisture like a lung holds air.

And the animals. The valley is empty and abundant at the same time, which finally makes sense when you see them.

They are small, at first glance. Moths, you think. The dim is full of them, thousands of white specks drifting on the steam thermals, too many to count, never colliding. Then one comes close and it's not a moth. It's a thing the size of a hare, with a body made out of what looks like crumpled parchment and six long, stilt legs that never quite touch the moss at the same time. A grazer. It drinks from the leaf-towers, piercing the trunk with a needle mouth and taking the hot mineral water. As it drinks, its back glows — a dull, forge-orange bioluminescence, like the inside of a kiln seen through paper. Light made not for display but as waste product. Each drinker lights up briefly, then fades as it detaches and drifts away, its blood cooling. From far away the valley looks like someone turning engine warning lights on and off, one at a time, across a dark factory floor.

There are larger ones, too. Up near the rim, you hear the heavy ones moving though you rarely see them. Something with a shell of basalt that it has secreted around itself over decades, a walking boulder that scrapes and sparks faintly when it moves. Its back is crusted with the same moss-fur that grows on the ground, so it looks like a wandering hill. When it passes a tower, the tower shivers and works harder. There is a symbiosis happening that you don't have words for — not predation, not cooperation, but throughput. Material in, heat out, water around again.

Everything predates everything else here by eating its steam, its condensation, its waste heat. The food web is a heat-exchange diagram.

If you stay long enough your jacket gets soaked though it never rained. Droplets form on the zipper teeth and fall. Your breath joins the valley circuit. The towers don't seem to notice you. They continue their slow industrial cadence — fill, heat, sigh, condense — on a minute-hand that is not yours. Each one huge and alone and doing the same job as all the others, spaced precisely far enough apart that their fogs can just kiss.

The bowl makes itself. Established, balanced, strange. A lush factory at the top of the world, with no workers, no owners, running in the dark on damp and old stone heat, tending its silent, glowing herd.
