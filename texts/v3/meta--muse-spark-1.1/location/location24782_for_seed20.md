Up high, where it shouldn't be able to be.

You climb out of the thin pine and the scree onto the new rock — black, glassy where it broke, still warm through your boots. The basalt mesa ends abruptly. It was only made last year or last century, it is still learning how to be a mountain. Its edges are knives. No wind has had time to round it. No lichen has had time to make a map of it. It is a high place, a table set for weather, maybe three thousand meters up, and the air should be clear and freezing and empty.

It isn't.

The center of the table has collapsed.

Not into a crater. Into a room.

About as wide as a small church is long, as round as a throat. The walls drop straight down twenty, thirty meters, fresh black stone slicked and stair-stepped, sharp enough that a falling rock sparks instead of dusty. And inside that room — the ocean.

It does not fill it. It *is* it.

Water occupies the whole negative space of the pit from rim to rim without touching the rim, a vertical block of it, a standing pillar of deep water that hangs in the dim. You can see the surface tension from above like a trembling skin. It doesn't spill. The top of it is flat but not flat — it shivers, it dimples inward, it throws up occasional silent hands that become vortices that become mouths.

The water is warm. You feel it before you get close, a bathhouse heat coming off it in a thick, invisible plume. It makes the air at the edge of the precipice unbreathable in pulses — too much water in the air. The atmosphere has given up being air. For three meters out from the edge, rain hangs in mid-air and never falls, beads that appear and hang and then get pulled sideways into the mass. The light gets dim as you approach because you are walking into cloud that is also sea.

The sound is what stops you first.

It is deafening. Not loud like a waterfall — loud like being inside a bell while someone pours the ocean through it. The column itself roars without moving. It's not crashing, it's *holding*, and holding is violent work. The water is churning inside itself, a contained storm, a permanent whitewater that goes nowhere. You hear low pressure detonations deep in it, the booming collapse of vacuums that form and vanish. You hear shrieking, high and endless, as water shears against water at speeds water should not allow. You hear the rock, too — the new basalt humming like a struck plate from the strain of keeping its shape.

You have to open your mouth to keep your ears from bleeding. Your bones hear it.

When your eyes adjust to the dim — because up here should be bright sun and instead the cloud/ocean is filtering everything to a deep twilight green-blue, like light at thirty meters depth — you see that the water is not empty.

Sparse life threads it.

There are things like kelp but they are wrong, too straight, like black calligraphy strokes suspended in the column, not rooted, growing in place out of nothing and then unbecoming. Some reach twenty meters long and then in a second they divide into a spray of fibers and are gone. Near the top, where the heat makes a skin, pale bacterial mats flicker in and out of existence, phosphorescent, forming lace and then tearing.

This is a place of early-formation. The stone is young, the water is young too, or maybe too old. It hasn't learned rules yet. Its bottom does not exist. You can walk all the way around the rim and look down the side of the column and you see what you shouldn't: the water continues down past where the rock should stop it. The column hangs over a void that is also water, a throat that goes down into a darker blue that does not end. Occasionally the whole mass drops a meter with a silent jolt, exposing a fresh slick ring of basalt that steams violently, then it surges back up and overcompensates and bulges above the rim for a heart-stopping second — a dome of ocean overhanging the mountain — before sucking itself back down.

Gravity is local and undecided here. Droplets that break off don't fall. Some fall up. Some orbit the main mass slowly, until they are captured again. If you spit, your spit hangs and then joins the rain that hangs.

Fauna is rare because nothing can stand the noise except by accident. Once every hour, or once every time you blink, a thing like a fish appears. Not swimming in from elsewhere. It assembles in the middle of the column out of turbulence — a flash of silver that becomes shape. It thrashes, fast, terrified, mouth open in the roar, and then it comes apart into current again. It was a suggestion of fish that the water had. You are not sure you saw it at all because your eyes are watering from the pressure.

Built remains — there were people who found this when the mountain was lower, or before the water came.

There are three stones. That's it. Not a village. Not even a ruin. Three basalt lintels set upright near the eastern rim, away from the worst spray, each taller than you, each carved into a very simple leaning figure with cupped hands. Their faces are gone, seared away by wet heat. Between them, the ground is covered in smooth river stones that did not come from this mountain. Someone carried them up, one by one, and laid them in a spiral. The spiral is still perfect because erosion hasn't touched this place yet. The river stones are dry. The rain that never falls never touches them. The water refuses them.

That's the rule here, the only weird law that holds: the ocean that lives in the pit is not allowed to be claimed. It cannot spill. It cannot be taken. It cannot be lived in. It is a room full to the ceiling with an impossible thing, and it is furiously, deafeningly busy remaining itself. If you toss one of the river stones into it, the column will reject it — it will punch it straight back out faster than you can see, hissing.

If you stay too long, the warm wet air starts to try to make you part of the suspension. Your sweat stops evaporating. The hairs on your arms stand and collect hanging beads of water that were never rain.

You leave because the sound will not let you think of staying.
