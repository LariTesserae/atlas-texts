There is an ocean in the sky that never fell.

You are high — two miles, ten miles, high enough that the air below would be a different planet. The world has a top here. Not a mountain top, but a table, a continental shelf lifted into the upper air, and on that shelf sits water. Not a lake. An ocean that stays. Cool, cold enough to ache in your molars, clear and black-green and held up by nothing you can see. You are submerged in it and it is still high. Light comes as normal daylight, noon-light filtered through miles of water above you that never ripples with weather because the weather is inside itself.

This ocean has no floor except plants.

Vegetation total. Not vegetation *in* water — vegetation *as* water. As place. As physics. The forest here does not grow out of something else. It is the substrate. Titan kelp, or something that borrowed the idea of kelp and took it to a theological conclusion. Each trunk is wider than a subway tunnel, wider than a house, rising from below haze and rising and rising and never ending because the ends are not visible. They are spaced terribly far apart. This is a sparse forest on a continental scale, so sparse you could fit cities between two trunks and still see nothing but water and descending green shadow for days while swimming. The parameter is low density but full occupation — because each plant is a country.

Their bark is not bark. It is a wall of interwoven leaves that have become leathery and mineralized and then soft again. Each trunk exhales. Each trunk inhales. You can see it if you watch one for a long while, a slow peristalsis, a contraction that moves up its length at the speed a glacier would dream about.

And this is the industrial part. Because built: none. There is not a single cut, not a nail, not a brick, not a ruin in all this vast suspended sea-forest. But tech: industrial.

The plants *are* industry. Before humans had factories we copied this.

Listen. It's loud here. It should be silent — deep water, cool water — but it is loud and it has always been loud in exactly this way.

Every trunk is a factory that never started and will never stop. A low, deafening, continent-wide groan of hydraulics. The kelp draws cold ocean through pores the size of doors, filters it, pressurizes it, cracks it. In chambers as big as cathedrals in its sides, seawater is boiled cold — cavitated by muscle — and separates. There is the hiss of sap that is not sap but heavy oil, black-green, moving in pipes that are veins, in volumes that would empty seas. There are pistons that are not built — grown. A bone-wrapped-in-bark, a heart-valve the size of a stadium opening and closing with a perfect, thunderous *CHUK* every ninety seconds, has been opening and closing since before any calendar.

You can swim up to one and lay a hand on it and feel the machine in it. Hot, despite the cool water. It vibrates. Condensation plates that are leaves but function like cooling towers spread for acres, dripping constant rain inside the rain of the ocean. Chains of bladders lift and fall, lift and fall, driving belts of living fiber that turn shafts that turn nothing but themselves. It is electrified — you can see it. In the dim between trunks, filaments crackle with St. Elmo's fire, blue-white and constant, not flickering. Bioelectric gridlines running between forests that have agreed to be a single engine.

It has settled itself. Erosion 1.9 — this has been happening so long it has polished its own improbability smooth. The trunks are weathered. They have rinds of calcium and slime and dead symbiotes as layered as old paint on a factory that never closed. Grooves cut by nothing but water pressure and time have become canyons in the bark where whole ecologies live. The water is also in equilibrium, the soup of leaf-particulate and plankton and steam held in suspension and never clarifying nor clouding. The ocean is post-weather. It generates its own internal climate that never changes — cold convection cells the size of states, slowly turning, forever.

If it is weird, it is weird in the way a lung is weird when you think about it as a machine that wets air.

Fauna. Abundant, because of course. Nothing is built to keep them out.

Between the factory-trunks, the water is alive with things that have learned to use industry. Pale, blind, long fish like articulated conveyors that feed directly off the oil-sap seams, mouths clamped over exhaling valves, shuddering. Colonies of crustaceans in neon oranges and whites that have built no cities but cling in reefs that scrape and polish the moving parts. Whales — or things with the job of whales — big as ferries, slow, black, that pass with a periodic pressure change that thumps your chest. They drift and don't seem to eat; they filter the lightning.

They do not migrate. No thing here migrates. Migration would imply change. In this place dynamic is zero. Nothing is going anywhere new.

You can watch a school of silver darts, thousands strong, turning in unison between two trunks and realize they are turning in exactly the same place they turned an hour ago, a year ago. The leaf falls, and another grows in the same spot the same size, the chemical ledger balanced instantly. The whale exhales, and the gas is taken. The piston fires, and the slag is eaten. The sound — that huge, endless industrial hymn of cracking and pumping and sighing and electrical sizzle — is not getting louder or quieter. It has always been exactly this loud, for longer than rock has been rock.

And the light — so normal, so insultingly normal — afternoon light, 2pm on a Tuesday in May, through cold water, through atmosphere-water, making everything look like it should make sense, like you could get out, dry off, go home.

You can't. There is no surface. There is no shore. There is only the vast sparse factory-ocean held high in the cool, its trunks like pillars holding up water that holds itself up, its machines that were never made, humming, humming, humming the one note it has ever hummed.
