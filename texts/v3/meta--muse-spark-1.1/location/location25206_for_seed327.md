It is not a mountain. It is the top of a mountain where a mountain stops pretending to be anything else.

You come up through the thin and get here all at once — the world drops away on all four sides. This place is small enough to hold in your head. A single bowl cut into the summit ridge, maybe seventy meters across, maybe less. A cirque that never let go a glacier, but caught one anyway and kept its shape. The wind finds the rim and skips.

Elevation at 3.0 is a physical law here. The air is thinned out to nothing — that's your empty. Density 0.0/3. There is so much space between things. Between one tussock and the next, between breaths. The sky is too close and too far at the same time, a hard normal daylight that doesn't scatter right because there's so little to scatter through. Light is clean and unfiltered and it hits like water. You can see every grain in the rock and every mite on a leaf because the air isn't doing you any favors.

And yet it is wet.

At this height there shouldn't be this much water, but the summit catches cloud like wool on barbed wire. The atmosphere condenses here. All afternoon the cap cloud tries to form and fails and forms again, and the failure is a constant wringing-out. Water beads on every surface and runs without ever seeming to start. The rock weeps. The entire inner wall of the bowl is dark basalt, old, established — not jagged young mountain tooth, but a shape that has been occupied by wind and lichen and freeze-thaw for so long it has given up defending its edges. It's rounded, scooped, settled. In its bedding planes and weathered ledges are seams that never dry. They drool.

All that water has to go somewhere, so the bowl has a floor that shouldn't be possible up here. Not snow. A hanging marsh.

Lush, for 5000 meters. The vegetation is low and furious. Cushion moss so thick and springy it feels like meat. Cranberry-dark liverworts plastering stone. Tussocks of pale alpine hairgrass holding their own little ponds inside their roots. Everywhere, saxifrage stars, white and acid yellow, pushing out from the black. It is cool — 1.2/3 cool — the kind of cool where your hands never quite warm but the plants don't care, because the sun is ordinary-bright and the water never stops and the thin air moves fast. Everything grows sideways, tight to the ground, greedy for heat radiating off the stone.

And everything is loud.

Sound: 2.5. You wouldn't expect it from a place so empty of things. But the emptiness makes a chamber. The bowl is a cupped ear. Water ticks a thousand times — drip from ledge to ledge, seep to puddle, puddle over spilling into rill. The wind comes over the eastern notch and hits the wet wall and makes a low flute. And the fauna —

The fauna is why you can hear that the density measurement was wrong about life. It measured structure, not inhabitants. The air above the marsh is never still. There are insects up here that have no right to be up here: bumblebee queens the size of your thumb, black and orange, working the saxifrage with a desperate, audible buzz; midges in clouds that pulse like smoke and then vanish sideways on a gust; high moths with clear wings.

And birds. Too many birds for the top of the world. The rock wall is pocked with holes, old vesicles widened by that slow, balanced erosion, and each hole is a nest. Snowfinches, alpine choughs with their yellow beaks screaming as they carve air. They come here because there is nowhere else with so much water and green in a three-thousand-meter radius. The whole bowl flickers. Somewhere under a moss hummock a marmot — impossible fat — whistles so sharp it hurts your teeth. You lift a clump and there are beetles underneath, polished black, running.

There are built things, but you have to be taught to see them.

Scattered, 0.8/3. Not a village. Not even ruins. Markings. On the driest part of the rim, where the wind has planed a flat place, someone has laid a low wall, one course high, of unshaped stones — a windbreak no higher than your knee. Inside it, the moss is a different green, cropped short. A few flat stones placed as a seat. A little cist under a cairn, covered with a slate slab. Open it and there are offerings, but not for gods: a bone flute, a coil of yak-hair rope, smooth river pebbles carried up from impossible distance.

The tech is all hand. Primitive, 1.0. You can see where somebody split a hornbeam branch with a wedge to make pegs that now mark the safest line down through the marsh so you don't sink to your shins. Simple knotted cord fences to keep — something — off the moss? No metal except what's been hammered from a single small ingot and beaten into little bells that hang from juniper stakes. They ring constantly in the wind. That's part of the loudness too. Each bell is slightly out of tune.

That is the weirdness, 0.7, not strong but certain. The place is too well-tended for how empty it is. The water in the bowl doesn't drain. There's no outlet you can see. It seeps to the center where the moss is deepest and there it disappears, drinking itself down, and sometimes if you sit very still and the cloud comes down really low and sits in the bowl with you, you can hear it going down — a hollow sound, like the mountain is hollow. Which it is. The density zero told you. The old people who built the little wall knew it too, which is why they put bells up.

It's not a dangerous place. It's an active one — 2.5 — always processing. Cloud condensing, water dripping, moss growing a millimeter a year and being eaten back by birds, bells ringing and un-ringing, wind scouring and lichen repairing. It has reached such a long equilibrium that it feels intentional. You are an interruption, sitting there with cold fingers, and the bowl is working around you, loud, wet, impossibly green and crowded at the top of thin air, waiting for you to leave so it can go back to being empty.
