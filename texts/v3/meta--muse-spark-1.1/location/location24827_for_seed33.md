It is not a city that was built. It is a city that happened.

You are very high. Not high like a hill, or a mountain pass. High like the skin of the world. The air is thin enough that breathing takes a deliberate shape, a conscious act. It is cool — not biting, not knife-cold, but a permanent late-October cool that lives in stone. Ten degrees below the temperature where sweat makes sense. Your cheeks feel it first, then the backs of your hands. The air has no water in it. Not dry like a desert is dry, with cracked mud remembering lakes. Dry like before water was invented. Your tongue dries if you open your mouth.

The light is dim. Not night. There is a sky, a vast, deep-slate vault with no sun in it that you can find. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, like dusk held for hours after the sun has gone behind a range that never ends. Shadows are soft and very long and go in slightly wrong directions. Things far away are not less distinct. If anything, they are too sharp.

This high plateau, this tableland at the upper limit of where land can be, was weathered into avenues.

From a distance, for a long time as you approach — and approach takes a long time because everything here is open, regional, a valley that contains other valleys — it looks like basalt. Black, iron-rich extrusive rock, columnar-jointed from some ancient cooling. A familiar geology. Then you are in it, and the geology keeps its promise and breaks it.

The Towers. The Blocks. The Streets.

They are organized. That is the impossible part. Veridian black rock rises in rectilinear masses, hundreds, thousands of them, each between two hundred and eight hundred meters tall, sheer-sided, flat-topped, absolutely empty inside — you can tell because some have collapsed and their cross-sections show no rooms, no floors, only solid stone with the faint grain of cooling lava. And yet they are spaced. They create blocks. They create intersections that meet at ninety degrees, then fifty, then ninety again. Between them run boulevards a kilometer wide, perfectly flat, floored with a compacted mineral dust the wind has polished to the smoothness of old bone. The dust is pale, salt-white, and in it there are no footprints.

It is urban in scale. Not a ruin — a ruin implies something built and then unbuilt. This is the density of a city with none of the clutter. No rubble. No wires, no glass, no metal, no hinge, no nail. No technology at all. Pure nature that decided to imitate settlement. Established, settled-form, in balance with itself for a very long time. The wind has rounded the corners of the Towers just enough. The edges are softened. The Streets have developed a patina, shallow pitting where rarer, harder grits have been driven along them for ten million years. This place is old enough that it has stopped arguing with erosion. It has made a deal.

And it keeps going. Past what you can see. Past the point where perspective should swallow the farthest towers into haze, but doesn't, because the air is near-void. There is density 0.2 here: almost nothing in any given cubic meter. No birds, no insects, no dust motes. The emptiness between presences is so vast that your brain keeps trying to insert things. A parked car, a doorway, a person. Nothing comes.

But it is not still. It is active.

Listen — except you can't, because sound here is 0.3, a profound quiet that makes your own pulse sound impolite. But if you hold your breath for long enough, you hear the place move.

First, the wind. It does not gust. It traverses. A high-altitude, low-pressure flow from the nothing-north to the nothing-south, too thin to feel as wind, but it carries a fine sleet of lithic ice — not water-ice, a mineral frost that forms in cool dryness, that never melts, that grows in feathers on the shadowed sides of the Towers and then ablates away in the dim light.

Second, the stone itself. In this thin, cold air the Towers tick and sigh. Thermal contraction at dusk-that-never-ends. Deep within the black masses, columns shear against columns with a sound like distant bells heard through rock. Sometimes a face sloughs off. Not a collapse — a slow exfoliation the size of an apartment block, peeling in a single sheet that holds together for a hundred meters of fall before it shatters into that white dust on the boulevard. It lands without a thump you can hear. You feel it through your feet, hours later.

Third, and this is where the weirdness lives, the city breathes incorrectly.

Reality is thin here. It has 2.9 parts weirdness in 3. 

You walk down a boulevard that is perfectly straight. You count the Towers on your left. Seven. You look back. Nine.

If you stand at an intersection and look up, you can see the top of the Tower in front of you. If you stand at the next intersection north, the same Tower is somehow south. Not moved — you have been moved around its side without turning.

Gravity is mostly kind. Down is still down. But up is slightly uphill everywhere, as if elevation increases in all directions at once. It is tiring to climb the flat street.

The distance to the next block is not fixed. On some days it takes twenty minutes. On others, a full, careful hour, and you are not more tired. The Towers themselves are still. It is the space between them that is active, stretching and slackening like a lung.

There is no vegetation. Not even lichen. No water for it. The towers are bare. The dust is sterile. There was never a forest here to become coal. This is pre-life or post-life rock.

There is rare fauna, because this place cannot quite be empty.

They are not animals. They are absences that have learned to migrate. High above, between Tower tops, something long and clear drifts — a ribbon, maybe thirty meters long, translucent as heat haze, holding its shape against the wind. It never flaps. It adjusts. It feeds, if feeding is the word, on the difference between where the light should be and where it is. You see perhaps three in a day. They never land. They never make a noise.

Closer to the ground, in the lee of the biggest Blocks, there are cairn-collections. Little upright arrangements of the white dust, piled by nothing. When you are not looking directly at them they shuffle an inch. When you stare, they are perfectly still, perfectly natural, and you feel suddenly foolish for thinking they were fauna at all.

The rules:

1. Do not try to map it. The map becomes heavier than the territory.
2. Water you bring will not be taken, but it will become shy. It climbs to the highest corner of your container and freezes there even though it is above freezing.
3. If you stay still long enough, a boulevard will become a hallway and a Tower will become a wall. That is not safety. Hallways imply buildings that are occupied.
4. The place is cool, dim, empty, silent, dry, and full of a city that no one made and no one needs. It has been this way longer than any city on your world has been possible. It will be this way after.

At some point you will stop thinking of it as a city at all, and start thinking of it as what rock does when it is left alone at the top of the world to dream about being inhabited — and you will realize you have been walking for hours through the same perfect, vast, quiet intersection, and that the long black Towers, weathered and patient, have been watching you with their windowless faces, not unkindly, making room.
