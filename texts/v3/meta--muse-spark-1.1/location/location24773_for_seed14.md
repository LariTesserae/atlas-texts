It is not under the water. It *is* the water.

You are below. Not just below sea level — below the idea of surface. There is no surface anymore, or if there is, it is a cold, white rumor kilometers above, an ice ceiling or a storm-cloud that never breaks. The water here is not blue. At this depth, with almost no light, water stops being a color and becomes a weight, a pressure, a condition of being.

The place is a valley, but the word valley is something you have to re-learn. On land a valley is air with rock around it. Here a valley is heavier water with slightly less heavy water around it, cut into the ancient floor of the world. 

The floor is what stops you. You expect mud, silt, the soft universal powder of the deep. It is not that. This is old seafloor. Truly old. Erosion here has had so long to work that it has finished its work. There are no sharp edges left anywhere. The basalt of the valley walls has been worried by water until it is as round and tired as old bone. The slopes are long, low-angle, falling away for a mile before they bottom out, and they are not covered in sediment — they *are* sediment, lithified and then smoothed again, layer after patient layer, until the whole basin looks like the inside of a bowl that has been thumbed for a million years.

And it is packed. This is the strangest thing about it at first, because you expect abyssal emptiness. Instead the water column itself is full.

It is full of marine snow that never stops. Not the sparse, poetic drift you see in documentaries — this is a blizzard that never thins. Flakes of dead things, fecal pellets, specks of mineral and ice, shredded skin, all falling so thick you can barely see five meters ahead even if you had light. The darkness is not empty, it is saturated. The water between the flakes is itself full of cold, full of suspended silt so fine it makes the water feel like cold glass. When you move your hand, the water resists, then holds the shape of your movement for a second too long.

The bottom is even more packed. It is armored, completely, from wall to wall across the whole valley, maybe three kilometers across, in manganese nodules and smooth stones. No sand visible. Just stone touching stone touching stone in a pavement with no mortar, each stone the size of a fist, a head, a small seal — all of them perfectly round, deeply pitted, black-brown and slick. They are not placed. No one built this. They have settled here because this is where the currents finally got too tired to carry them any further. The oldest lag deposit in the world. You could walk on it, if you could walk under this pressure, and you would never touch the actual bedrock. You would only ever step on other stones that have been here since before the continents learned their current shape.

Sparse life hangs on in spite of it.

Here and there a stalk rises out of the stone pavement. A glass sponge, a meter tall, white as bone china and just as brittle, its lattice holding a single, unwavering flame of filtered water. Or a cluster of black whip-coral, too sparse to be called a forest — more like telephone poles left behind after a town has gone. No kelp. No meadow. Photosynthesis ended a long way above you. These things live by catching what falls. That is the only rule: everything that lives here lives on what falls from above. The only direction that matters is down.

Rare animals move through the snowstorm. You do not hear them come. Sound here is strange — it should be loud in water, but the suspended load damps everything to a low, felt hush. No echo. Even your own breath, if you have breath, is just a tight *tik tik* in your ears.

You might see a big blind amphipod the size of your hand, pale, moving from stone to stone with the deliberate care of something that lives a very long time. You might see a grenadier fish, blacker than the water, hanging motionless until a current flips a stone and it darts — a single, fast muscle contraction, then stillness again for an hour.

And the current is always.

This is a turbulent place, despite the silence. That is the paradox that defines it. The valley is a trap for brine. Cold, super-saline water forming somewhere under the ice above slips down the gentle slopes and pours along the bottom like an invisible river inside the ocean. It is near freezing, -1.8 C, -2 C, so cold it burns. It comes in pulses, in slumps, in sudden avalanches of heavy water that you can't see until they hit you. Then the entire snowstorm reverses. The marine snow that was falling straight down suddenly flies sideways. The stone pavement rattles. Nodules clack against each other with a soft, wooden sound, a sound like a million knucklebones rolling. The glass sponges bend. The whip-coral bows flat. For a few minutes the whole valley is moving, stones rolling over stones, a slow-motion rockslide with nowhere to go, endlessly shuffling itself, sorting itself, never building anything, never making a hill, just restlessly repacking the floor.

Then it stops. The snow resumes falling straight down. The water goes back to its heavy, waiting silence. The fish resumes hanging.

There is nothing built here. For all the packing, for all the density of matter, there is no wall, no post, no mark. And yet there is tool use, of the most primitive kind possible. You will find it if you stay long enough to watch an octopus — one of the small, warty, deep red ones that lives in the gaps between the big stones. It has no shell to hide in, so it makes one. It will pick up two of the smoothest, flattest nodules and hold them around itself, a portable door. When the brine-current comes, it wedges itself down and pulls its stones close. When the current passes, it will pick up a pointed chip of basalt, no bigger than a tooth, and pry at the stones to get at the blind amphipods hiding underneath. Use, then discard. The stone falls back into the pavement and becomes part of the floor again instantly. Nothing accumulates. Nothing stays made.

The rules of this place are simple and total:

1. There is only water, and water that is heavier.
2. Light does not reach here; light is a story from above.
3. Everything is touched by everything else — there is no empty space, only water with things in it and stones with no space between them.
4. Nothing new is made that lasts longer than a current.
5. Cold preserves. Pressure preserves. Old things stay here until they are worn round.

If you stay long enough, the snow will cover you. If you stay longer, the brine will uncover you again, and rattle your bones gently against your neighbors.

The valley extends on, out of your limited sight, out of your lights, a mile wide, five miles long, ten, more, a long, flattened S between those worn-down shoulders of stone, all of it doing the same thing — catching falling things, holding stones, shivering when the heavy water pours through. There is no end to it that you can see. There is only more of the same ancient, cold, packed, restless dark.
