It is not a mountain. Not anymore.

What you are standing on used to be mountains. Once, a range so high it scraped the jet stream raw, so young and jagged it could cut weather in half. Now it is so old it has forgotten how to be a mountain. Elevation without relief. A continent lifted up on a pedestal of cold, dark rock and then worn down until there was nothing left to wear except its height.

The plateau.

From orbit it would look like a shield, like the original craton of the world — a circle the size of a sea, two and a half miles up, its edges a single long cliff-fall into cloud. On the ground there is no edge to be seen, no end. Only this vast, almost-flat.Ancient erosion has done its work down to the last patience: here is the oldest stable state. The hills are not hills but long, tired swells in the bedrock a mile wide and twenty feet tall. The valleys are shallow grooves where a different kind of rock resisted a little longer. The surface itself is not soil over rock but rock that has become something like soil — basalt ground to sand to silt to flour over a billion freeze-thaw cycles until it holds a footprint.

The air is thin and very clear and cold — not a biting, windy cold, but a deep, absolute cold that has settled.  -15, -20 in the sun. Normal light, white sun high and small in a blue that is almost violet at the edges, because there isn't enough air to paint it. Shadows are hard and short. Things do not decay here. They abrade.

Water is everywhere you look for it and nowhere you can drink it easily. It does not rain so much as the world exhales. At night the sky clears to black glass and the cold deepens, and all the moisture in that thin air comes out onto everything as frost — a half-inch of feathered white on every stone, every leaf. In the morning the sun touches it and for an hour the entire plateau becomes a wetland without a lake. Water beads and runs in bright, silent threads down rock flour, gathers in shallow pans of stone, in cupped lichen, in the footprints left last week that still hold their shape. Then it evaporates straight up again, or it soaks down into the rock flour and freezes again a few inches below. Intermittent moisture. There are old riverbeds too, broad as highways, a foot deep, meandering for a thousand miles toward a cliff they will never reach, carrying only a black trickle in their center ten months of the year and then, for two weeks in high summer, a roaring, calf-deep flood of snowmelt that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere and rearranges everything.

And it is lush.

That is the shock of it. Without animals, without birds, without insects even — no buzz, no burrow, no scat — the green has had to figure out another way. And it has.

The dominant thing is not a tree. Trees require ambition and animals and a deeper soil. This is a carpet-world. A felt. A reef at high altitude. An ocean of moss and lichen and liverwort and stone-wort that grows in all directions at once. In the hollows it is knee-deep and springy, a black-green and rust-red mattress that holds a gallon of water per square foot like a sponge. On the exposed swells it tightens down into a crust, millimeters thick, patterned in explosive rosettes and fractal cracks — olive, sulfur-yellow, bruised purple — so dense you can walk on it for days and not see the rock beneath. Where the old riverbeds run, there are flags of taller growth: something like sedge, something like fern, but stiffer, with glassy stems full of anti-freeze, holding up woolly black seed-heads that never open because there is nothing to carry them. They reproduce by breaking. The wind snaps a stem and carries it a hundred yards and it roots where it lands.

Because there is wind. Mostly quiet, mostly nothing, but when it comes it comes as a continental breath and it does not stop for weeks. It has its own weather system. Clouds are born on the western scarp and drag themselves across the whole plateau for nine days, never high, never low, snagging on nothing. They precipitate as snow that never accumulates — it sublimates straight from frost to fog.

And the city.

You see it first as a mistake in the lichen pattern. A straight line where no straight line should be. Then another. Then you realize the whole swell you have been climbing for half a day is not entirely a swell.

This was urban, is urban. The density is wrong. The scale is wrong. It is a city the size of a country, but built as if materials were scarce and space was infinite. That is because both were true.

There are walls — long, low, double-wythe walls of unmortared basalt, fitted without tools better than hands should be able to do with simple hammers and copper wedges. They run for miles, ten feet high, two feet thick, absolutely straight until the ancient earth-swell beneath them bends them in a slow curve, which they followed. Inside the walls: terraces cut into rock flour and lined with flat stone to hold meltwater. Terrace farming of moss. This is agriculture without animals. They do not plow. They scrape, they transplant mats of edible crust-lichen with stone combs, they burn nothing because nothing will burn wet at this temperature.

Dwellings are semi-sunken, round, roofed with overlapping slabs that have been worn round at the edges by frost. Each one isolated by two hundred, three hundred yards of tended carpet from the next. No roads. No center. An urban area without a downtown, because when your city is 800 miles across, the concept of a center fails. You walk for a day and pass forty houses, sixty walls, three much larger communal structures — cisterns, really, huge stone bowls to catch the morning dew, covered with a lattice of bone-white poles that sing a little when the wind passes through them.

The builders are... sparse now, or were always sparse. You find evidence of maintenance everywhere: a wall recently re-stacked, the black lichen trimmed back from a doorway. A footprint in the moss that fills slowly with water. But you do not hear them. The tech is hand-work: chipped stone, cold-hammered copper, woven fiber from the sedge-flags, felts beaten from the same moss they eat. No glass. No tall fire. No wheel — the ground is too soft for it, and what would you have a wheel pulled by.

And the weirdness, which you only notice once you have been quiet long enough.

Distance stops meaning what it should. Because there is so little relief and the air is so clear, you can see a wall that is forty miles away. But sometimes you can see a wall that is behind you reflected in the air in front of you. Sometimes if you stare at the horizon, you will see yesterday's weather still walking there, a rainless cloud that has already passed you but hasn't finished passing up there.

Gravity is polite. If you drop a stone, it hesitates, as if considering, then descends a little slower than it should and lands a little lighter, so that all the unmortared walls, even after a thousand years of frost heave, stand improbable.

And the quiet. Fauna 0.5 — the world is missing a layer of noise. No animal calls, no wingbeats. Only the faint tick of ice forming and melting, the creak of stone slabs, the sigh of the carpet as it swells with water and exhales it. So you start to hear other things. The plateau is active — violently active, in its own slow way. Because when moss grows six inches a year and freezes and thaws daily and there is nothing to eat it, it moves. It crawls. A whole watershed of moss can, over a season, flow downhill an inch an hour. The low walls lean because the green tide pushes them. The great riverbeds change course overnight because a mat of vegetation dams them.

Light sometimes puddles. You will walk down into a shallow ancient valley at noon and find the light in it colder and heavier, like thin water, and your hands look blue in it. You will climb out and the dew on the rosettes will flash, too bright, too many colors, a light that is remembering something else.

The rules of this place are: Height remains after everything else is taken away. Water comes from the air, not the sky. The city grows outward because it cannot grow up. Life does not need moving things to move. And quiet is not empty.

If you stay long enough, you learn to step only on stone, not on the dense parts of the crust, because the crust is farming and the farming is someone's pantry and you are a guest. You learn to sleep in the lee of a dew-cistern where the condensation runs down the poles and drips, all night, into your mouth if you leave it open. You learn that in a place this vast, this old, this cold and lush and strange, you do not walk toward a destination. The destination, after some days, walks toward you.
