It is not a valley. It is a bowl at the top of the world.

A very old mountain, so old it forgot how to be a mountain. Its shoulders have eroded away over continents of time, its peaks ground down to a single broad back of stone, and then, at the very top, that back dimples. A shallow, almost perfect concavity about as wide as a hamlet's commons, as tall as a cathedral nave. The rim is all that remains of the ridgeline - a low, smooth lip of stone you could walk around in an hour, if you could see it. You are at high elevation and you can feel it in the emptiness of the air, even though the air is warm.

Because it is warm here. Not hot like lowland jungle, but the damp, held, mammalian warmth of breath. The stone itself holds heat. During the day - if there is a day above this place - the sun never reaches it. The mountain wears a permanent cap of cloud, thick and low and black, that sits right down into the bowl like a lid. So there is no light. Not dim, not twilight. Zero. A darkness so complete it has pressure. You enter it by stepping over the rim and dropping a foot and suddenly the sky is gone.

And it is wet. Not with standing water, not a lake. The bowl weeps.

The cloud condenses on every surface and never stops. The air itself precipitates. The basalt of the ancient bowl has been polished by age into something soft, undulant, like old molars, like river stones the size of houses, and from every pore of that stone, water beads and runs. It doesn't gush. It secretes. The walls sweat continuously. Along the lower curve, where the bowl is deepest, the sweat has become a marsh - not deep water, but a black, warm sponge of peat and liverwort and root-mat, ankle-deep, sucking softly at each step. The rock under it is still there, just below. Water near surface everywhere, but never more than a skin.

It should be dead in the dark. Instead it is choked with life.

Vegetation in this darkness has no business being lush, and yet it is obscenely lush. It has learned to eat something other than light. The walls of the bowl are curtained in hanging mats - sheets of translucent white root and fern that have no chlorophyll, or not only chlorophyll. They hang in beards five meters long, dripping, feeling downward. They are pale as cave fish, some clear enough you could see your hand through them if there were light. They pulse faintly with heat. At their tips they bead with sugars. There are broad, flat leaves at the marsh level, black-green, rubbery, spread out to catch falling condensation rather than sun. Everything is wet and swollen and overlapping, a vertical garden that grows not up toward a sun but inward toward the center of the bowl. The smell is overwhelmingly green and rotting-sweet, like a greenhouse at night.

The tech is what feeds it. You notice it only after your eyes stop trying to see.

Scattered around the bowl, half-swallowed by moss and root, are the artifacts. Not ruins, not a city. Five or six of them. Each one is about the size of a small hut, but it isn't a hut. It is a knot. From a distance in the dark, your hand thinks it is stone before you touch it and then decides it isn't. The surface gives neither warm nor cold, it just... matches. If you press your palm to it, the water that is always falling in this place stops falling around it. Droplets hang in the air, perfectly still. Dust, spores, tiny moths - all hold. There is a faint, deep thrum from inside, not a sound exactly but a feeling in your molars. They are still working.

These are what keep the bowl viable. They anneal gravity a little. They stretch time a little thin. They drink the temperature differential between the warm rock and the cold cloud above and turn it directly into the something that the plants can use. The field they make is what holds the cloud down. The plants grow thickest around them, rooting directly into their seamless sides. On one, someone long ago - or something - carved faint marks: notches at hand height, a resting place smoothed by countless touches. A shrine, or a maintenance perch. The built environment here is not buildings. It is tended objects.

And the fauna. The bowl is almost empty of matter - vast, hollow, the air so clear of particulate that sound carries absurdly well - but it is full of animals. In the dark, that means you are in the middle of a crowd you cannot see.

They are mostly small and soft and abundant to a ridiculous degree. Moths the size of your hand, with no eyes, furred and warm, that farm the fungal mats and hum as they go. Things like crickets but slower, with long, wet legs that unfold in the marsh and sing constantly. Bats, or bat-like, that hang in clumps from the fern curtains, shoulder to shoulder, breathing together. When you move, they shift, not in panic, but in a slow ripple. The sound is loud. It is the loudest dark place you have ever been.

Because all that water, all those lives, in that enclosed stone drum, makes noise. The endless ticking and dripping of condensation off a hundred hanging root-tips onto the marsh. The low, creaking sigh of the big leaves filling and dumping. The layered stridulation of thousands of insects that have never learned to be quiet because there is nothing to hide from. A deep, occasional *whoomph* from the air pressure equalizing over the rim. And underneath, the subsonic throb of those hut-knots, patient as heartbeats.

Everything here moves slowly. The water takes a long time to travel, beading its way down a leaf for an hour before falling. The moths fly as if through honey, navigating by warm and wet, not light. The plants grow a millimeter a month and are still, in total, a jungle. The erosion has stopped because there is nothing left to erode; the bowl has reached its final shape and holds it. Even rot is slow, respectful. A leaf that falls into the marsh might take a season to soften.

The rule of this place is simple: light never comes. And because light never comes, everything else had to learn to be enough. Heat is enough. Wet is enough. Touch is enough. In the bowl at the top of the ancient mountain, in the warm dark that weeps without ceasing, life has pulled the world in tight, down to the scale of a single room, and decided that one room is plenty.
