It is not that the water is over the mountain. It is that the mountain is a place inside the water where up still exists.

High. You are very high. At an extreme of elevation that should mean snow and thin air, except there is no air at all. There is only water, cold and still and utterly black over you. You are on the Final Table — the last high ground the world made before it stopped making things.

The Table is a plateau kilometers above the abyssal plain, so high its edge is a cliff that falls for longer than weather exists. To get here, water would have to fall. Instead, you climb through darkness that gets fractionally less dark. The pressure never changes. The temperature never changes. It has been cool — not freezing, but the deep cool of water that has not seen sun in a geological age — for long enough that cool feels like a kind of stability, like stone.

And there is no stone.

That is the first thing wrong, and then right. Everything is built, but nothing was ever mined or cut or smelted. Tech-zero. This is made-environment in the oldest sense: grown.

The vegetation is total. The word "forest" is insufficient because it implies trees on ground. Here plant *is* ground. The plateau itself is a braid of living matter so ancient and so densely interwoven that it has become geology. Black kelp trunks wider than houses, not round but buttressed like the walls of cathedrals, rising up from the Table, rising and rising until they vanish into the black above, where they keep rising. Their holdfasts are continents. They have fused with each other over such time that you cannot tell where one individual starts. Erosion — 2.6, ancient, end-of-life smooth — has not carved them. They have smoothed themselves. Every edge is blunt. Every angle has been sanded by nothing but time and the slow drag of water until it is patient. You touch them and your hand comes away feeling no bark, no texture, only cool, rubbery, giving mass like old cartilage.

It is sparse. That is the second paradox. For a total plant ecosystem, there is so much empty space. The pillars of kelp-forest-cathedrals stand hundreds of meters apart. Between them is nothing but clear, cold, black water that extends — you can feel it extend — for the size of a valley, a small country. The scale is open, not claustrophobic. You can see, if you can call it seeing in the dark at 0.3 light. The water is so clean it holds no particulates. Light does not come from above. There is no above you can see.

Light comes from fauna.

They are abundant, but you hear them before you see them. Sound is loud here, constant, because the state is frozen, in equilibrium, and sound is the only thing that moves. The pillars creak. Not with stress, but with breath. A low, deep, tectonic groan as water is moved through ludicrously large siphonophylls, filtering. A pop and click chorus of shrimp the size of dogs that live in the hollows. And the song — the whales are not visitors here, they are residents, pale and blind, circling the Table endlessly, calling in long, low tones that use the pillars as sounding boards. When one passes between two pillars, the whole space thrums.

And when they call, things answer with light.

The whole built structure lights up briefly, not bright, but present. Bioluminescent lace grows over every surface of the kelp-architecture: small animal, plant, neither — a living stucco that the forest grew to house. It is a cool white-blue, brief, like a window opening far away. In that flash you see how far the Table goes. More pillars then more, receding into black water. Between them, arches and buttresses and bridges — all plant, all alive — grown by the forest in its final, stable form. It built itself a city with no streets because it does not need to go anywhere.

There are terraces cut — grown — into the sides of the trunks, shallow bowls a man could lie in. There are vertical shafts where the trunk split and healed around emptiness. All empty. The fauna do not build. They inhabit. Aullid fish with fins like cloaks hover in the terraces, fanning water over eggs. White crabs walk the arches in single-file lines that have not changed course in a thousand years.

The rule here is: nothing falls. Nothing rises. Nothing erodes anymore because it has already eroded to its final shape. Water hangs. Plant holds. Animal circles. The cold holds it all in place. The dark holds it all hidden.

You are submerged in an ocean that is itself in the sky. A high place that drowned before anyone could name its height. And the forest that survived submersion by becoming the mountain.
