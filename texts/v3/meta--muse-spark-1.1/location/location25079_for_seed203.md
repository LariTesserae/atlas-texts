It is below. 

Not deep in the old, smooth, pressed-down sense of below. This is a new below. The world cracked open not long ago — geologically yesterday — and took a breath inwards. A continent fell a little. Or the crust shrugged. The ceiling is still sharp.

You are walking on the floor of a hollow that is so big it has a horizon, but the horizon is not sky, it is wall curving up and becoming ceiling. A rift-basin the size of a sea that never reached the surface. The air is cold enough that your breath makes white tufts and then vanishes, because the air is so large.

And it is bright.

That is the first wrong thing that becomes right. Bright, cold, subterranean light. Not sunlight. The ceiling is low in some places — a kilometer up — and high in others — thirty kilometers up — and it is alive with light. The roof of this world is a root-mat.

Above you, sealing the old world off, is a sheet of ice and crystal and woven root, hundreds of meters thick, and it has grown through with light-plants. Think of moss, but each frond is a glass fiber as thick as your wrist and as long as a redwood is tall, and it glows. Not soft foxfire. Harsh, white, noon-in-winter light. They crowd each other, a solid luminous meadow burning overhead. It is a sky made of plant. On the cold days, which are every day, the heat of that living light makes the underside of the ice sweat, and the sweat becomes a perpetual, almost-rain.

So there is no river here. There is damp. The whole place is damp in the way a deep greenhouse in February is damp. Condensation forms on everything, beads, gathers, falls. You hear it before you see it — a random tick, a tap on a leaf. Somewhere far off a mass of water that has gathered on a ceiling-root the size of a bridge lets go and falls for a full minute before it shatters on the forest floor, a brief, cold, vertical river that exists for twenty seconds and then is gone.

The vegetation: there is nothing else.

What I mean is, there is no ground in the normal sense. The ground is plant. The walls are plant. Most of the volume is empty air, but all the surfaces are plant-as-structure. This forest does not grow in soil. It is the soil.

The builders of this space are the Pillars. They are not trees, but they want to be called that for convenience. Each one starts from a single black-green seed the size of a house that cracked the fresh basalt when it fell from the ceiling. It puts down roots that are buttresses, sharp, fresh-edged, still splitting rock as you watch — you can hear the tick-tick-tick of microfracture. The basalt here is young, unweathered, glassy at the edges where the rift opened. The Pillars shove it aside with slow, unstoppable force. And up they go. A mature Pillar is two or three kilometers tall before it branches, and its trunk is not solid wood — it's a braid of hollow, slightly translucent stalks, each as wide as a subway tunnel, packed together like organ pipes. Inside them, cold air moves. Inside them, water trickles down in dark internal rains.

Between Pillars, emptiness. That's the density of this place. Vast, hollow, almost nothing. You can walk for a day between two trunks and see nothing but the flat, springy root-floor, which gives under your boots like deep moss on top of a mattress on top of stone. The floor is a mat, fifty meters deep in places, of interwoven smaller growth — black air-roots, white hair-fiber, sheets of lichen like leather tarps. It is alive, all of it. If you cut it, it tries to knit closed within hours.

And then, suddenly, another Pillar, so wide you thought it was a cliff for an hour before you saw it curve away from you. Its lower branches are horizontal and as big as overpasses. From those branches hang curtains — not vines, but colonial leaf-strands, each leaf the size of a sail, pale green, rimed with frost where the condensation freezes before it can fall. They do not sway in wind because there is no real wind; this continent of air is too cold and too still for wind. They shudder instead when they grow.

For this is a turbulent place, though it is quiet. The plants here are in a hurry. This below is young, newly opened, and everything is racing to claim it before the light shifts or the ceiling cracks. You can see it. Sit against a Pillar and watch. A new shoot, thick as your arm, pushes out of the floor-mat with a soft pop, uncurls, extends, seeking the bright roof. An inch an hour. It whines faintly, a cellulose squeak. Somewhere else, a branch that has grown too heavy, too fast, without enough internal structure, fails — it collapses with a long, sighing, splintering roar that fills the hollow for minutes and then stops, and within a day the fallen span is already being eaten by smaller plants that turn its death into more floor.

Creation and destruction at a speed that feels animal. The forest is an anthill if you watch it on its own timescale. It is always moving, but almost always silent.

Fauna is rare here because there is no need for it. The plants have learned to do everything. Still, there are things. You will see them only by their traces first: perfect round holes bored through a leaf-sail, a patch of root-mat that is paler where something has grazed. If you wait, very still, in the blue-white glare that has no shadows because it comes from everywhere above, you might see a grazer — something like a huge, slow sloth made of translucent jelly and hair, moving along the side of a Pillar, completely silent, licking the condensation and the algae from the surface. You might see its breath puff too. There are not many. The emptiness does not support many things that move on their own. The plants allow a few.

The built things are the most surprising, because at first you think they are also plants.

They are not. They are made.

Low circles of stacked basalt, the stone still sharp and fresh because it was broken yesterday, or a hundred years ago which is yesterday here. Alean-to made by bending living hair-roots over and tying them with more hair-root. A hearth, blackened, damp, cold. Inside one, a small figure worked from a Pillar-leaf — a doll, or a god, with its arms up toward the light-roof.

Someone lives here, or passes through. People who have learned that metal is useless because anything you leave gets grown over in weeks, and that fire is hard to keep because everything is wet and cold and too bright to need it. So they use the plants themselves as tools. A knife made from a dried and split spine of glass-fiber from the ceiling. A hammerstone with a living handle that is still faintly photosynthetic. They do not build villages. The place is too vast and too unstable to be owned. They build a place, stay until the floor starts to lift them or a curtain falls, and then they move on a day's walk to where there is nothing and start again. You find their marks everywhere but them almost nowhere.

At night — which is not night, because the roof never truly dims, but sometimes a large section of the light-moss will pulse out together and for a few hours the world is in a dim, cold, blue twilight — you can hear the rules of this world.

1. Light comes from above, always, and the goal is to get closer to it.
2. Water comes from the air, never from below.
3. Rock is temporary.
4. Everything that stands will be used as ground for something else to stand on.

The sound of the place when the light dims is not of animals, or of people. It is the ticking of water drops forming and falling over a space the size of France. It is the long creak of the Pillars shifting under their own impossible weight on a fresh, sharp, young floor. It is the almost-inaudible pop of new growth. 

It is mostly quiet. It is never still.

If you stand in the truest empty between Pillars, where the root-floor is flat for miles and the ceiling is so high the light-roof looks like a milky way, you are in a bright, cold, damp cathedral that built itself last week and is already remodeling. Your small, warm, mammalian exhale joins the condensation that will fall again somewhere else, and the forest floor under your feet, alive to its full depth, shivers slightly at your weight, and then decides to grow around your boots.
