It's not a forest. It was a forest once, and then it became a town by forgetting how to be anything else.

The place is low. Not low like a valley, low like the bottom of something that has been washing flat for a billion years. The stone underneath - you can see it in three places where the roots allow it - is black basalt polished to a soft shine, rounded, pitted with ancient potholes the size of houses, all its edges chewed off by time until it feels like old soap. The whole basin is maybe four hundred meters across, rim to rim, and you can stand in the middle and see the entire rim as a low, green-brown lip. That's the scale of it: a single room, a hamlet, a cathedral made for a weather system. It's completely self-contained. Outside the rim the world is somewhere else, flatter and drier. Inside, the world ended and kept going.

Water is the first law here.

It doesn't rain so much as the air gives up. It's warm - skin-warm, blood-warm, late August at noon warm - and saturated. The atmosphere is low and heavy and it condenses on everything. Every leaf drips even when it isn't raining. The basalt weeps sheets of clear water from invisible seams. The ground is not ground; it's a mat of living tissue a meter thick, and if you press your palm into it water wells up around your fingers like pressing a sponge. Shallow channels, braided, tea-colored, move slowly everywhere with no apparent source or mouth. They go under and through everything. At night you hear them breathing.

And then the plants.

They are not *in* this place. They *are* this place. Vegetation on a 2.6 scale doesn't mean trees. It means plant as architecture, plant as substrate, plant as infrastructure that out-competes stone.

There are maybe thirty of them. That's it. That's the sparse part. Thirty individuals, widely spaced, each the size of an apartment building, each utterly distinct. One is a fig of impossible type — its trunk is a fluted column thirty feet across, its buttress roots are walls that divide the basin into rooms. Inside the buttresses, people have lived long enough to polish the bark into doorways. Another is a colony of bamboo that is not bamboo: each culm is as thick as a subway car, translucent jade, hollow, and lit from within. Another is a curtain of aerial roots that fall like rain from a canopy you can't see, because the canopy is the sky here, a interwoven mat of leaves so vast it holds up the clouds.

Each plant is a building. Not like a building. Not metaphor. The inhabitants — because this is urban, unmistakably urban, a town — have grown them to be. And that is where the tech comes in.

The tech is 3.0. Beyond tools. Beyond machines. The fig-trunk has windows that are not cut but persuaded open; the cambium around them has been taught to grow glass. Not glass-like, glass, clear silica exuded by leaves and fused at the molecular level. Inside the bamboo culms, capillaries carry light, not down fiber optics, but down living channels that slow photons until they pool like honey. You can dip your hand in a trough of light and come away glowing for an hour. Staircases are leaf-veins enlarged to walkable scale. A market street is a single, living tendril that extends, retracts, re-routes itself hourly to connect different houses according to some argument of auxin and load-balancing algorithms that no one remembers writing.

Nothing here was built and left. Everything is being built, continuously, turbulently.

Because this place is loud and it never stops moving.

The sound is first the water — a constant low chatter of drip and runnel and slop — but over it: cicadas the size of your thumb, birds with too many hinges in their voices, the groan of wood under its own weight, the wet pop of new growth. And people. This is a town of perhaps two hundred souls, and they are as hyperactive as the plants. It's a bazaar state, anthill-energy, unsettled. The street-tendril just pulled itself away from the bread house to connect to the memory house, so a whole procession of children, old women, a man carrying a live fish in his arms, has to scramble across temporary root-bridges that are still slick and unfurling. Someone is shouting that the west fig is about to flower — an event that will happen in the next twenty minutes, that happens every three days, that will bury half the town in choking sweet pollen and edible stamens. They shout, they laugh, they move furniture made of still-living pith out of the way. No one is still.

And the fauna uses all that emptiness between the giants.

Because the plants are spaced, there's a lot of air, a lot of wet light. Normal daylight, but filtered green-gold through steam and leaf. In that light the animal life is abundant to the point of intrusion. Dragonflies with the wingspan of hawks hunt in slow, hovering patrols between trunks. Things like capybaras but with bark-colored, photosynthetic pelts wade in the tea-channels, chewing the ever-growing edges of the mat. In the canopy overhead, flocks of small, soot-black monkeys with translucent bellies flicker and call — their bellies light up amber when they have eaten the light-fruit. Ants the length of your finger march in perfect, silent lines up a root-wall, carrying not crumbs but tiny, bead-like nodes of data that glint.

The weirdness is not that any of this exists. It's how it behaves like engineering.

You watch a woman approach a blank section of fig wall. She doesn't knock. She places both hands on it and whispers. The bark blooms open — not mechanically, but topologically, as if the wood decides a hole has always been there. Inside is not a room. It's a throat of packed, warm, faintly glowing moss that exhales cooler air. A library, because the moss stores. If you touch it you can feel it accessing you slightly in return — your own bioelectric field being read and filed. It will give you back a memory tomorrow that isn't yours, useful for fixing a leak.

Rules of this place:

1.  Nothing dead stays dead. Fallen wood is immediately colonized and reincorporated. Even the basalt is being slowly digested from below.
2.  No right angles survive. The plants correct them over hours.
3.  Everything leaks. Water, light, sound, genes.
4.  The town's shape is a negotiation, not a plan. If you want to keep your house's entrance where it is, you have to keep talking to it.

If you stand still too long — which nobody does — a rootlet will find your boot and taste you to see if you are good substrate. The air will condense on your eyelashes until you cannot see. The whole small, ancient, soaking, roaring, growing cup of a world will try, kindly but insistently, to make you part of its turbulent ongoingness.
