On this continent you do not climb *to* the heights. You live in them. 

The landmass rides too high. It is a table that has been pushed up into the wrong part of the sky, a plateau so vast its middle has forgotten the sea ever existed. Ten, twelve kilometers above the reference datum — not peaks, but a country of average height. From its rim you can see weather happening *below* you, a solid deck of cloud that breaks on the escarpment like surf, dropping rain that you will never receive. Up here the air should be knives of cold, but it isn't. It's warm. Not hot — warm the way a kiln is warm hours after firing, a deep, mineral-held heat that rises from the rock itself. Day measures around thirty degrees in the shade, if you could find enough shade to matter. The sun is normal, white-yellow and steady, but the light has farther to fall and hits thinner, drier air, so shadows are hard-edged, blue at their cores, and everything beyond a near ridge sh shimmers not with water but with heat and dust static.

There is no water. Not in any way a lowlander would recognize. The scale of the dryness is older than drought. This place finished its hydrology. What runoff ever ran has cut its canyons and then vanished. The floors of those canyons are scoured and then scoured again, polished down to a pavement of wind-bitten basalt and evaporite — aeolian flutes and tafoni and balanced rocks that have been holding that impossible balance for a million patient seasons. In the deepest shade you sometimes find a weep — a brine that squeezes out of salt strata, dark as oil, and it doesn't puddle; it fuzzes instantly into needle-crystals that grow and then sublimate into the air, leaving behind a pale rash. The air tastes of salt and hot iron. If you lick your lips you can taste battery.

Nothing green has ever managed to agree to these terms. No soil forms, because soil wants water and rot. There is instead regolith, crushed basalt that grades into salt flour, into hematite dust, into dunes of gypsum that sing underfoot. The colors are the colors of unvarnished mineral: rust-red, bone-white, sulfur-yellow, the black-purple of basalt that holds the day's heat. Vegetation reads as a superstition here. Occasionally you see something that fools you at distance — a dark fan in a wind-shadow — but up close it's just a spray of manganese dendrites, a chemical ghost.

And yet it is built.

Not densely. That is the first thing you learn about its urbanism — how insulting distance is to continuous habitation. The city — if that word still holds — is not one city but a scatter of industrial districts separated by twenty, fifty, a hundred kilometers of empty, worked stone. Sparse, but unmistakably intentional. You come over a ridge of yardang and find a rectilinear grid cut into the plateau, streets that are really service ways between low, broad buildings of riveted plate and block that has been cast from melted plateau itself. The refineries, the millhouses, the liftworks — they all look like they were assembled on site and then weathered into place until you cannot tell where geologic time ends and maintenance begins.

This is industrial in the oldest sense: things that take material and make it into something else, with noise and force. The primary industry seems to be the air, and the salt. Towers of louvered steel, thirty meters tall, stand in rows like organ pipes and breathe. They are not burning anything. They are harvesting — pulling down the fine suspension of chlorides and metallic dust that this high desert constantly generates, precipitating it with electrostatic fields. Conveyor systems, almost all at ankle height, crawl across the desert on concrete pylons. Not fast. Nothing here is fast. A loaded belt might move at a walking pace. Some are kilometers long, bare to the sky, carrying a dark flow of mixed salts toward a central kiln complex where it will be sintered into ingots the size of rooms. The kilns glow even in daylight, not with flame but with contained, low, orange heat. You can stand a hundred meters away and feel your face warm.

The industry is designed for hands that are not human — or perhaps it was, and has been inherited. Pulleys are too large, walkways have a grating that presses into feet that are not booted. But the oddest thing is the quiet. For all this scale — for belts moving and electrostatic towers buzzing and kilns sighing heat — the air is too thin and too dry to carry sound well, and the machines themselves move so slowly they have solved for noise. What you hear is mostly wind over mineral edges, the occasional tick of salt expanding in heat, the distant, low whoomp of a thermal bubble breaking against a tower. Then, if you hold your breath, you can hear it under that: a faint, almost subsonic creak, as if the plateau were a drum head flexing under the sun.

Because this desert is not empty of animals. Far from it.

Fauna in the absence of plants should be impossible, but here abundance took a different contract. They do not eat leaves. They eat the city, and what the city pulls from the air.

The most numerous are the skaters. Low, flat, kite-shaped things a meter across, translucent amber, that ride thermals a few meters above the ground on stiffened membranes. They have no eyes that you can see. They tack back and forth over the conveyor belts and the salt pans, precipitating ions onto their undersides like living precipitators. When they are heavy they settle onto the belt and ride it for a while, scraping themselves clean. 

Bigger are the spoolers, barrel-bodied hexapods the size of old trucks, whose legs end in broad, felted pads that soak up brine seeps before they can crystallize. Their backs are grown over with irregular boiler-plate — not worn, but incorporated. They shed that plate in rusted sheets near the kilns, where smaller creatures — beetle-things with mandibles like tin-snips, clacking, patient — cut it up and carry it away. The spoolers tend to the machines. You will watch one stand for half a day leaning its shoulder into a misaligned belt, pushing with slow inevitability until something pings straight. Then it will sleep.

In the evening, the high fliers come down from even higher. They are sparse in the way eagles are sparse, with six-meter wingspans of black veined sail, almost invisible against the darkening basalt until they bank and catch the kiln light. They roost on the electrostatic towers and drink the charge. You learn not to stand under a tower at dusk.

The weirdness — and you feel it most when you stop trying to map it — is that elevation is doing something wrong here. Things weigh a little less when they are upwind of the kilns. Shadows linger a second longer than light geometry says they should. After a few days on the plateau your inner ear adjusts and you realize the whole table is very, very slightly hollow. It rings if you strike it hard. If you put your palm down on the warm rock after sunset, you can feel a long, slow pulse returning from many kilometers away — the low-frequency shudder of another spooler shifting a belt, or the contraction of the plateau itself as it cools. The city is not on the desert. The city is a kind of grooming behavior the desert evolved to keep its salt-skin from over-crusting.

Nothing happens quickly here. Rust advances like lichen would, in centimeters per decade. A building collapses not with a crash but with a long bow, then a sigh, then another half-century as a mineralized hump. The creatures reproduce by budding and by fission of their scavenged plates — you will never catch it in the act. The conveyors lap the continent once every three years. 

You can walk for days between districts along the maintenance road — which is just the original rock, cut straight by a wheel that was bigger than houses — and see nothing move except dust devils that collect salt needles and glitter while they spin themselves out. Then you will arrive at another cluster of low buildings, another set of breathing towers, another kiln, already lit, already warm, being tended by the same animals who do not know abandonment because the process has never stopped.

It is warm, and dry beyond thirst, and very high, and quiet in the way an engine room is quiet when all its engines are huge and perfectly balanced.
