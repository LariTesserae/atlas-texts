It is very high.

Not the peak-high of a single spire you climb to and look down from, but the high of a lifted world — a plateau that shouldn't be up this far, a table of raw continent shoved into the thin air. Five thousand meters, six. High enough that your thoughts come slow and your breath steams and does not stop steaming, because the air never gets warm enough to take it.

The rock is too new to have learned how to be mountain yet.

That's the first thing you feel under your boots. This is not old, folded, weather-beaten stone. This is embryonic. A flood basalt, black and reddish-black, that has come up recently — in the geological sense, in the last gasp — and has cracked as it cooled into vast, crude polygons the size of houses, of fields. The edges are still sharp. They haven't been softened by rivers; rivers haven't had time to exist here. There are no valleys, only the great shallow sags between cooling lobes. There is no soil, only shattered basalt grit, and then, where the rock wept and broke smaller, a paste of basalt flour and ice.

Erosion, here, hasn't started. The world is still in its formwork.

And yet it is wet.

In this cold it should be dry, locked up, a desert of stone and frost. It isn't. The rock itself is wet. The plateau is still warm inside, from its own making, and that heat meets the cold that lives permanently above it. So water never settles into one state. It comes up. It sweats out of the stone. Every low wall of basalt, every sharp, black stair-step, is beaded and sheeted. It seeps from vesicles in the rock like the rock is a sponge that has been squeezed. It runs not in streams but in films, a few millimeters thick, over everything, finding no channel and so making a million.

Where the ground dips — those broad, open sags that go on for a mile or two before they rise again — the water collects into something that is not quite a marsh and not quite a thaw. The water table is at the surface. You step and the grit takes your weight for a half second and then slowly, sighingly, fills with water around your boot. Under the skim of ice that forms each evening, there is black water and black mud. If you kneel, you can see it breathing: little gas bubbles from deeper, warmer rock popping through steadily. Pop. Pop. Pop. Like a slow boil.

The light never gets bright. The sun is behind a sea of stratus that never breaks, just thins and thickens — a pearl-grey dome from horizon to horizon. At noon it's like dusk in a northern town. At dusk it's almost dark. The air itself is condensing. You can watch it: the wind that moves over the plateau — that's the only real sound, a low, almost sub-audible drag over rock — cools another fraction and gives up a fine mist that is not rain and not fog but something in between. It beads on your eyebrows. It makes every surface glisten. Weeping rocks is right. The whole plateau weeps.

Vegetation is there because life is opportunistic, not because it was invited. Nothing tall. No tree could ever anchor in this raw, wet grit before the wind took it. But wherever a lip of rock breaks the wind, there is a crust of lichen, orange and acid green, tight against the stone like spilled paint. And further down in the wet sags, tight cushions of moss, and a black-green liverwort, and one higher plant, a kind of saxifrage that grows in miserable, dense buttons the size of your fist, leaves waxy and red-tipped to hold what little heat there is. Miles apart, then a patch. Then miles again. From a distance, the plateau looks barren. Up close, it is stippled.

For all that emptiness, it is full of animals.

Density here is a trick. Stand in any one place and you see nothing — a half-mile of wet black rock to your left, a mile of seep and cushion to your right, only wind, only water. But move a quarter mile and there is another presence. And another. And another. The scale is regional, valley-sized even without valleys, so your eye keeps wanting to count it as empty. It isn't. It's just spaced.

They are geese, mostly, when you first notice them. Or something like geese — high-altitude, heavy-bodied birds with soot-grey backs and white throats, built for cold. They are everywhere, in pairs, in lines of six or seven, dotting the wet pans. They are what makes the quiet quiet, because they almost never call. You hear the soft, careful pick of bill against moss, the shuffle of webbed feet in slurry. Thousands of them across the plateau, all quiet, all working. Why here? Because the water, for all its coldness, is rich. It brings minerals up from the new rock. The thin biofilm that grows on every wet stone is like pasture to them.

And then larger things. There are herds — small herds, four, five — of a shaggy, low, broad ungulate. Not yak, but the idea of yak, only more suited to this: legs short to spread weight, coats so long they drag in the water and are caked with black mud, faces bearded with ice. They move very slowly from cushion to cushion, cropping the saxifrage to the root and moving on. Steam blows from their nostrils in twin, continuous jets, and does not dissipate, but adds to the condensing air. When one snorts, it sounds for a second like a release valve.

Because here is the industrial thing.

There are no buildings. No walls, no chimneys, no roads, nothing made. Not a post, not a cairn. This is an untouched place in the most absolute sense.

And yet the plateau works.

It works the way a factory works. It processes.

The heat below and the cold above are an engine. Everywhere across the open table, where the crust is thin, the earth shunts. A low mound of basalt will have a crack down its center, and from that crack a column of steam tears out — not a geyser that erupts and stops, but a steady, roaring vent that keeps going, day and night, week after week. White, and loud. Not loud in a violent way. At its base it is a hiss, but standing a hundred yards away it smooths into a drone, a low heavy note. You can feel it in the rock through your feet. There are dozens of them across the visible plateau, some thin as a wrist, some wide as a truck, all firing. The condensing atmosphere is their exhaust. The whole sky is their steam after it has cooled.

Between the vents, the ground itself does work. In the wet pans, where the black mud is deepest, it churns of its own accord. Convection cells, meter across, turn over and over, slick and steaming. Mud is carried up, cooled, sinks. On the cooling cliffs, basalt contracts with a sound like a rifle shot, or like sheet metal popping in a great press — *tick-BANG* — and a flake the size of a door shears off and slides, still steaming on its underside.

And the animals fit into it. In fact, the animals *are* part of the milling. The geese dig and slurry the biofilm and enrich the pans with guano that makes the film grow faster. The shaggy grazers trample the cushions, breaking them, pressing seeds into new ground. A lesser predator, a fox-like thing you mostly see as a grey-brown smear against black rock, moves between the birds, lean, constantly casting. Nothing is fast. This is cold, high, slow industry. Growth, consumption, heat exchange, gas release, repeated at very large scale, with very little waste.

If you stand still for a long time, the cold goes into your knees, and the mostly-quiet comes back. It is not silence. Under the wind, under the hiss of steam, under the faint pocks of gas in the mud, there is a constant, low, productive hum — the sound of water moving through rock by the ton, of stone cooling, of thousands of birds feeding. The sound of a place that has just been built and is already, without pause, put to work.

Behind you, the plateau goes on. In front of you, the plateau goes on. The dim light does not change. The water keeps weeping from the new earth.
