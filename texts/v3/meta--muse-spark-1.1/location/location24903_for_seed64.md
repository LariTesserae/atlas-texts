It is not a valley. It is not even a gully. It is a seam.

High — so high the rest of the mountain has given up — where the granite backbone of the range splits along an old fault and never quite decided to close. Two walls of rock, maybe nine feet tall, lean in toward each other like hands cupped against the wind. At the top, a slice of sky, dim and bruised-purple, never more than a foot wide. At the bottom, the world stops.

You have to know it is there to find it. The wind up here at 5,600 meters has scoured everything else down to knife-edges and frost-shattered scree, but this one crack runs crosswise to the wind, and the wind goes over it, not through it.

This is the place.

The air is cold the way high air is cold — not biting, but thin, unsparing, clean to the point of pain. Each breath is work. It is -2, maybe 2 degrees, forever. It smells like cold iron and ozone and old dust that has never gotten wet. Because nothing here has ever gotten wet.

There is no water. Not in the walls, which are dry as kiln brick. Not in the air, which was wrung out a thousand meters lower. Not as snow, because the over-launch of wind sublimates it before it can settle. The stone is a dark, weathered gneiss, banded black and bone, polished by eons of dry, sandy wind into something soft-shouldered and settled. The edges are round. Nothing fresh breaks anymore. This crevice is in equilibrium, in its old age.

And yet it is stuffed. Choked. Lush beyond reason to be standing in.

It is not green. At this elevation, in this dimness, green would be a waste. It is deepest felt-black and iron-rust red and a dusty lichen yellow that is almost light itself. It grows everywhere. The walls are not walls anymore, but a vertical bog of dry vegetation.

It is not a plant you know. No leaf, no wetness. It is a mineral wool, a kind of air-moss, a branching steel-wool that grows filament by filament from spore that needs no water. Each strand is as fine as hair and stiff as wire. It branches, re-branches, fractal, occupying every cubic centimeter of air between the walls with a soft, dense, dead-silent mass that is anything but silent because of what is under it.

They are lithotrophs, or aerotrophs. They don't drink. They eat rust. They eat wind. The filaments exude a faint acid that pits the gneiss and liberates iron, and they take that iron into their bodies. The tips are black with it. The whole mass is so packed there is no room between one colony and the next — you cannot put a hand in without pushing against a mattress of dry, brittle, slightly springy growth. It is a room full of fur, grown from stone, a closet of solid moss that has grown until it has no space left to grow into, so it just holds itself, dense and compressed.

When you press your face close, you see the architecture. The outer layer near the slit of sky is tight and low, like a cropped lawn of black bristles, all leaning shut against the sound. Deeper down, where it is dimmer, the filaments get longer, more extravagant, curling into ferns, into antlers, into lungs. Some hang like Spanish moss made of copper wire. In the absolute dimmest inch at the bottom, where light barely reaches, they glow very faintly — not bioluminescence, but a static discharge, a Saint Elmo's yellow, as the wind's charge is combed through them.

There is no animal in it. No mite. No fly. No bird glances down into the slit. If you listen with your ear against the wool, you hear no skitter, no chew. Just — fullness. A vegetation that has never needed to be eaten.

That is because it is not alone in the seam.

Scattered along the floor, half-swallowed by the wool, are the remains — or the continuations — of something built.

A low, industrial god, long frozen in its own moment. An old high station, maybe Peruvian, maybe Soviet, maybe not built by anyone anymore. A single-cylinder diesel generator on a poured concrete foot, its paint long gone to iron oxide, its exhaust pipe cold. But it is running. It has always been running. A flywheel the size of a dinner plate turning and turning, driving a pump that pumps nothing, a belt that flaps because its leather is dry and stiff. Four struts of angle iron that were once a weather mast, now just a trellis for the wool, which has grown thickly around them, incorporating them, using them as iron lode.

This is the tech level here: not ruined, not functional in any human sense — just industrial, persistent, mechanized. A scattered mark. A bolt every two feet hammered into the rock with a loop of rusted cable. A tin panel that once said PELIGRO in faded letters, now totally felted over, a rectangle of slightly less dense moss. A lightbulb in a cage that never broke, but doesn't light.

The generator makes the sound.

When the wind hits the top of the seam, it does not go in. It goes over, and the pressure difference makes the trapped air inside the crevice shudder. It is a low organ note, a loud, constant, bass thrumming, as if the mountain is humming through a missing tooth. And under that, the generator — an unchanging clang-clack-clang, metal on metal, a piston that has worn itself into perfect tolerance so it never knocks anymore, just ticks like a clock that has been ticking for thirty years and will tick for three hundred more.

It is loud, in this closet. Oppressively loud, because the wool is dense but not soundproofing; it vibrates with it. You feel it in your sternum. The whole pocket resonates. And yet the sound never changes. There is no gust variation, no sputter. The wind is laminar up here. The diesel feeds on something that isn't diesel — maybe on condensed volatiles off the wool itself — a closed loop. The system has found equilibrium. Rock, rust, moss, machine, wind, drone. Nothing grows anymore. Nothing erodes anymore. The wool is packed to saturation and holds. The generator never runs out. The note never ends.

If you stay long enough, your ears stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as pressure, as place.

The rule of this world is simple: when there is no water, life does not go thirsty. It learns to not be that kind of life. It learns to eat stone and air and time. And when you are at extreme height, in a hidden pocket that the world forgot, you do not have to change. You just have to be absolutely, completely full.
