It is not a mountain that was built on.

It is a mountain that was built.

From the valley floor you can't see where geology stops, because it doesn't. There is no bedrock left showing. From the scree-line to the cloud-line, three vertical kilometers of it, the mass is all construction. Not a city on a mountain — a mountain as city, as single object.

The builders poured it in levels. You can still read their logic: vast, concentric terraces, each one thirty or forty meters tall, faced in a ceramic-concrete that was once dove-gray and is now the color of tea-stained teeth. The faces are weathered. Established. The corners have been rounded by a century of monsoon and sun and root. The aggregate is showing through like bone through skin. Settlement cracks run its flanks in dark lightning-forks, and in every crack something grows.

Because it is warm up here. That is the first strange thing you notice when you climb. It should be cold at this elevation. At 2.8/3, where the air should be thin and killing. But the mass holds heat. The concrete drank sun all day — and the light is brutal here, high-altitude bright, unfiltered, 2.6 out of 3 bright that makes the white walls painful to look at until your eyes adjust. The terraces store that heat and vent it out all night through a million small industrial mouths. The whole mountain radiates like a kiln that was turned off last year and is still cooling.

It is never truly dry. That is the second strange thing. Water: 1.4. Not wet. Damp.

There are no rivers on it. No lakes. The builders did not allow for that. What there is is condensation. Every morning before the sun clears the eastern range, clouds come and snag on the upper third of the structure. They physically catch — you can hear them catch — on the forest that crowns it, on the gantry-forest, on the rusted crane-arms that still stand above terrace 90 like skeletal trees. The cloud goes to water on contact. Everything sweats.

It runs down in black trickles, in seeps from bolt-holes, in steady drips off every leaf. The terrace floors are all tilted, just a degree or two, so that water wants to move. It moves along shallow, hand-deep aqueducts cut into the edge of each level, a chain of slow, deliberate, man-made rivulets circling the mountain endlessly downward. You never hear a roar of water. You hear ticking. Tapping. Drip, drip, drip on metal, on leaf, on stone. The mountain is always quietly dripping with itself.

And it is lush. Impossibly lush for something with no soil.

Vegetation at 2.4/3 means the builders lost. Or won, depending on how you read the contract. Every terrace plane was once a factory floor, a housing block, a rail yard. Now every horizontal surface is a field, a jungle, a garden, a mat. Not decorative planting — functional overgrowth.

The lower terraces, wider and hotter, are agriculture. Layer on layer of rice and taro growing in the old assembly bays, their roots in twenty centimeters of composted concrete dust and leaf litter that accumulated over generations. Jackfruit trees burst straight out of maintenance shafts. Banana leaves, big as doors, slap against corrugated walls. Higher up, where the wind is stronger, the growth changes: moss, ten meters thick in places, a sponge that holds the morning's condensation, and ferns that grow out of vertical walls, hanging down in green curtains over windows that never open anymore. Vines as thick as a man's waist have learned to use the industrial infrastructure as trellis — they climb the chain conveyors, they strangle the pipework, they flower with huge, waxy, orange trumpets that only open in full sun.

It is dense. 2.2/3 dense. There is no negative space. If there is a surface, something is on it, in it, using it. Pipe, root, vine, wire, and ant-trail run parallel for kilometers. The old factories never stopped, they were just absorbed.

Because this place is still industrial. 1.7/3 industrial. Not high-tech, not dead. Patient, slow industry. In the core of the mountain — and you can go inside, through archways big enough to drive a train into, where the light spears in bright columns full of dust and pollen and moths — the machines are still turning.

Huge, low-geared. Wheels thirty feet across, made of pitted iron, turning a centimeter a minute, driving looms that weave vine fiber into rough cloth. Water-lift screws, powered only by the weight of condensation dripping into counterbalance tanks, lifting silt to the upper gardens. Ventilation fans the size of houses, turning so slowly you have to watch for a minute to be sure they're moving, groaning on bearings that have been lubricated for a hundred years with pressed nut oil. Everything smells like hot iron, wet earth, and crushed green leaves.

The rule of this place: nothing leaves. Everything cycles. Water condenses, drips, is drunk by roots, transpired, condenses again on the underside of the next terrace above. Metal rusts, rust is taken up by fungi, fungi are eaten by beetles. Beetles are woven into cloth. Cloth rots into soil.

Fauna: 2.2. Abundant.

You hear it before you see it, because sound here is 1.8/3 loud — not deafening, but the air is full, weighted with noise. The low thrum of those big fans and screws, a bass you feel in your molars. Over that, the high, almost electrical whine of cicadas, billions of them, living in the moss layer. Sunbirds in metallic green and crimson flash between banana stalks. Hive wasps, big as your thumb, have built paper cities that hang from the underside of girders, gray and dripping. In the aqueducts there are small, blind white fish that were never intended to be there, eating algae and mosquito larvae. On the concrete faces, geckos, thousands per terrace, clicking.

And higher, in that bright, brutal light, the crows. Flocks of mountain crows that have learned to use updrafts off the hot concrete to soar without beating their wings, circling the summit all day in a slow vortex, calling to each other.

Dynamic is slow. 1.1/3 slow. Nothing here happens fast. You can sit on the edge of Terrace 47, where someone has set a bench made from a re-welded I-beam and planted with creeping thyme, and watch the shadow of the terrace above crawl across the rice at about the speed of a minute hand. The machines turn. The vines grow a millimeter. The rust spreads. The crows circle. A mat of water hyacinth moves six inches down the aqueduct before noon. If a terrace collapses — and they do, every few years, a weathered face sloughing off — it does so overnight, with a long sigh, and by morning the gap is already webbed with new roots, already being claimed.

From far away, from the valley town that exists only to look up at it, the whole thing looks like a single, impossible cloud-forest. Green and gray, looming, sweating in the morning, steaming in the afternoon sun, humming. You cannot tell where building ends and plant begins, because at this point, after this long in balance, there is no difference.
