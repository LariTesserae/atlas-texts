It is not on top of the mountain. It is *in* the top of the mountain.

You come up out of the treeline where the air gets thin and cool enough that your breath makes a brief cloud and dies, and the trail you've been following — black mud, white quartz grit, bruised roots — simply ends in a lip of basalt. From below you thought it was a peak. From here you see it's a broken tooth. A small, almost perfectly round collapse-caldron, maybe three hundred paces across, maybe less. A bowl cut into the summit ridge, sheltered on three sides by that dark, weeping stone.

And inside the bowl, there is the town. Or inside the town there is a bowl. It's hard to tell.

Because nothing here is cleared. That is the first law of this place.

Water lives too close to the surface at this height. It should run off, but it doesn't. The basalt rim sweats it. All day, even when the sky above is a thin, normal blue, the rock weeps — clear sheets and cold beads condensing out of the wind itself, gathering, dropping. The air inside the bowl is always post-rain. The clouds that scrape the ridge fall in here and get tangled and refuse to leave. So everything that can grow is growing on everything else that is growing.

The vegetation is not covering the town. The vegetation *is* the town. 

The floor of the caldron is not floor at all, but sphagnum and liverwort meters deep, a living mattress that quakes when you step and squelches cold water up around your ankles. Out of that mattress rise stocks of mountain bamboo, thick as your wrist, twenty feet high, so close together you have to turn sideways. But they have been coaxed. For generations — the rim is weathered smooth, lichens fat as coins on every handhold, no sharp edge left; this place has been here long enough to come to an agreement with itself — people have woven, bound, spliced, and pleached the living stalks into walls, into homes that are still growing. A house here is four bamboo clumps bent inward and tied with wet hide until their crowns fuse into a roof. The roof continues to leaf. You see a child on his knees on top of his own house, plucking the newest shoots so the door doesn't close over.

It is dense in a way that makes your eyes lose distance. There is no bare wall, no sightline more than fifteen feet. Layer on layer: moss on bamboo on vine on fern on moss again. Ladderways made of living root that re-root if you leave them too long. Platforms of woven wattle that sag under baskets of fat tubers, all suspended in air because nothing left on the ground stays unsprouted. The whole hamlet is stacked two, three stories up, but not tall — a dense, low, interlocked thicket of occupied green, humming, cupped inside the stone. Hamlet-scale, grove-scale, room-scale. You could walk around its entire outer rim in ten minutes if you could walk in a straight line, which you can't.

And everything drips.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms. It's loud here, unreasonably loud for a cool high place.

Water first: drip-drop-drone from a thousand leaf tips, the hiss of the weeping rocks that never stops, the suck and pop of feet in moss. Then fauna — because where there is this much wet living matter in one sheltered pocket, life crowds until it touches itself.

It is heavy with animals. Not big animals. There is no room for big. The air is black with cloudflies, midges in such numbers they make a visible smoke that parts around you. The bamboo whistles with warblers, mountain finches, warbling thrushes in such density that their territorial songs overlap into one constant, bright, almost frantic chatter. On every leaf there is a beetle — iridescent green, lacquer-red carapaces — gnawing, breeding, falling, being eaten. Frogs the size of your thumbnail, orange as lichen, bellowing like dogs from the waterlogged moss. Slugs as long as your forearm leave silver highways across the walkways. It smells like reproduction. Wet earth, crushed leaves, bird shit, fermented nectar.

And under, through, over all of it: human noise. This place is turbulent. Not collapsing, but never still. Anthill energy. Bazaar energy.

Nothing can be built to last because everything is alive and everything is growing and the cool mist rots dead wood in a month. So they don't build to last. They tend. The whole settlement is being constantly re-tied, re-cut, re-woven. A woman with a bone knife — copper is rare up here, iron rarer, most tools are sharpened stone, polished wood, worked antler — is severing infant bamboo shoots before they can lift her pantry platform. Two old men are shouting over the birdsong as they haul a dripping root-cable to replace a bridge that grew shut overnight. Children chase the fat bumblebees away from the drying meat with woven fans, and the bees are so numerous they don't really go away. Someone is pounding tuber to paste and the thump thump thump gets lost in the frogs. Laughter, arguing, the endless snip-snip of pruning shears made from two lashed mussel shells.

The light is perfectly ordinary daylight — cool, high-altitude, clear — but it is shattered. It comes down through the lingering wreath of condensed cloud inside the rim, diffused, then chopped a thousand times by leaves. It spots the wet walls and makes the water on them shine. You are damp within minutes. Your shoulders are damp, your hair sticks, your lip tastes like moss.

If you stand still too long, which the midges will not let you do, you feel the rules assert:

1. Keep carving your space or it will disappear. Green closes over an absence in hours here.
2. Everything that falls must be raised. If you leave a tool in the sphagnum, you will find it in three days cradled in white roots, already claimed.
3. Do not try to keep things dry. Keep them from drowning instead.

The town has no edge at the bottom and a hard edge at the top. Where the woven living walls meet the basalt rim, the rock is bare, scrubbed clean every morning by the women with moss wads, because if they let the vegetation get a hold on the rim-stone, the stone will keep the water, and the whole bowl will cup more rain, and the floor will rise. Even now you can see, where they missed a spot, a fern has split the basalt lip with green fingers.

Down in the center, where it is deepest and wettest, there is a pool you can't see but can hear. Not open water — water between leaves, water held between mosses, water lifting, black and cold. That's where they dip their jars. That's where the frogs are loudest.
