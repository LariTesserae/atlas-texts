It's not a valley. It feels like one at first, from above, but it's smaller.

You drop down off the main wash — low country, bog-flat and black with old peat — and the ground just gives. Not a cave, not yet. A lip of basalt, rounded over by ten thousand winters until its edge is soft as a shoulder, undercut by water that isn't there anymore, or not always. A pocket. A room tucked under the world.

From the outside you would miss it. The brown sedge grows right over the rim and hangs down in a thatch, dripping even when it hasn't rained. This is a damp place in a dry place. The air that comes up out of that cut is too cold.

You have to turn sideways and let yourself down on old handholds — not carved, worn. Someone's hands over a long time, foot-shaped scoops in the stone where the water ran most. That's the first built thing, and you almost don't see it for what it is because the stone is so settled into itself. Established. Nothing sharp left anywhere. Even the fractures are lichen-welded.

Then you are in.

The chamber is maybe twenty feet across. Low enough that you have to stoop near the edges. It is empty in the way a cathedral is empty — not lacking, but hollowed on purpose, with vast space between one living thing and the next. The floor is cobble and cold black sand, scoured clean, with a single shallow thread of water making its patient way from the back wall to the front, too small to call a stream. More like a chain of touches. It beads on the ceiling, gathers, drops. Condensation and seep. In midwinter it stops and becomes clear, knobbled ice on the underside of the rock.

It should be silent. It is deafening.

The entire back wall is water. Not the little thread — the wall itself is the backside of a fall. On the high moor above, the river throws itself off the low shelf and it lands right here, on the other side of this stone. The rock is thin there, maybe a man's arm thick, and shot through with hairline cracks, and the whole weight of that low, wide, white-brown river hits it and hits it and hits it without end. You cannot think over it. You feel it in your molars, in your chest. A continuous, hammering roar, not loud like a shout but loud like weather — a pressure that makes your ears fold in. When you put your palm to the back wall, it buzzes. The water seeping through there is not cold, it is vibrating.

In that roar, any animal stays away. That is its fauna — rare. Once in a long time a white moth finds its way in and beats itself blind against the dark until it settles. A dipper might shoot through the curtain outside and be gone. In summer, one hibernating clump of bats high in a seam, breathing once a minute.

The darkness is almost total because the fall outside is not a window but a wall of moving milk. A little grey light gets punched through where the water is thin, and it moves. It ripples across the chamber floor and ceiling in constantly shifting lace, never still. Not enough to see by. Your eyes open wider and never adjust. After a long time you can make out green.

Lush is the wrong word for this latitude, but here it is lush anyway. Everything that can hold holds moss. Not in a carpet — in isolated, perfect, deep pillows. One here on a boulder that fell a thousand years ago and never moved again. One there under the constant drip-line from the ceiling, hanging down like a beard, bright and saturated even in this light that is not light. Liverworts as big as hands, black-green and shining. In the cold, they grow slower than they should be able to. The air is 34 degrees, 35 degrees, always. Your breath makes a cloud that you cannot see but that you feel leave you and die.

Because it is so cold and so damp and so slow, nothing rots. It lingers.

And there are marks that someone was here and decided to keep it empty. Scattered. Primitive.

Near the side wall, out of the way of the seep-thread, three low cairns no higher than your knee, made of river stones carried in — you can tell because they are not basalt, they are pink granite from upstream. Each cairn has gone soft with moss, but the moss has been picked off the very top stone, recently. Or not recently — slow time here fools you. A year ago, maybe.

Between them, driven into the sand, are split sticks of blackthorn, the bark still on. Prayer sticks, or markers, or just something to do while you waited out the cold and the noise. They have little figures wrapped at the top with sinew: a feather, a bone button, a twist of wool gone grey. Hand-tools did this. No saw cut is clean. The wood was worried apart with a flake of stone or soft iron.

There is also a larger structure, if you can call it that. A leaning slab, propped against the wall to make a half-shelter too small for a man to lie under, but just enough to keep the constant bead-drip off something. Under it, a flat stone with a shallow ground-out bowl, and in the bowl, dark water and a few white pebbles placed one by one.

No one lives here. It is too loud and too dark and too cold. But it is visited. It has been visited for so long that the way up and down is a groove.

Nothing changes quickly. The moss advances a millimeter a season. The seep-thread moves half an inch a decade when a pebble shifts. The waterfall outside wears the lip one grain at a time, and the roar very slowly, over centuries, gets louder. You sit in the empty pocket with your back to the soft green and the cold soaking up through your pants from the sand, and you look at the shifting light thrown through the falling water, and you understand that this is a listening place, not a living place. 

You came to get out of the wind and you stay because after a while the deafening stops sounding like the river and starts sounding like the room itself humming.
