It is a bowl.

Not a valley — a bowl left in the low ground when something was taken out. The old quarry, the sump, the settling pit. You can walk its rim in ten minutes if you don't stop, so you can hold the whole shape in your head at once. Low on the coastal plain where the air never quite drains, where the cold gathers.

The walls are not high but they are complete. On three sides, quarried basalt in soft, stepped terraces, each terrace knee-high, worn round as old stairs. The stone is black when dry and blacker when wet, which is always. On the fourth side, the wall is made of things — a sheet-metal dam patched with concrete, rusted I-beams shoved into the earth like fence posts, a slope of slag and clinker that has weathered until it looks like natural scree. It has all been here long enough to be settled. The edges have lost their cut. The iron has scaled and flaked and sealed itself. Water has found the convenient path and then worn it deeper.

Because there is so much water.

It doesn't rain always; it just never leaves. The air is cool — late October cool — and saturated. Your breath comes out in a short puff and is gone. The sky is low, pearl-gray, not dark but dim the way the inside of a throat is dim, light diffused to nowhere from everywhere. The stone weeps. If you put your hand on the basalt, water beads instantly on your skin, not from the stone's wetness but from the air condensing on you, on it, on everything slightly colder than the air itself. High up on the terraces, you can see it happening. A fine mist touches the black rock and becomes droplets. Droplets join, become trickles. The trickles find old drill-holes, old bolt-holes, old seams, and thread down.

The bottom of the bowl is not soil. There is no plant life here. No grass, no moss, no reed — nothing that would be recognized as green. The chemistry went wrong, or right. The ground is a mat of industrial fines — magnetite dust, concrete slurry that never fully cured, a paper-thin bacterial film the color of dried blood and oil — overlaid by a shallow, continuous sheet of water, two inches deep, black and clear at the same time. Where the water pulls back, it leaves rust-flowers, halite whiskers, sulfur crusts. Where it stands, the bottom is pitted with thousands of tiny, perfectly round breathing holes.

This is an industrial place, but only as an afterthought. The factory that made this hole is gone, or scaled back to somewhere beyond the rim you cannot see. What is left down here is peripheral, scattered, unplanned to remain: a single pump house the size of a toolshed, its door gone, its one engine still running — not because anyone tends it, but because its intake is in the water table and its outflow is back into the bowl, so it has nothing to do but cycle. You hear its heart: *chuff... chuff... chuff...* slow, with a long pause between, like something asleep. A line of concrete piers, each topped with a ceramic insulator, marching nowhere. A half-buried chain, links as big as your head, disappearing into the slag slope. A yellow gantry arm that overhangs the north wall, frozen mid-swing for twenty winters, dripping from its elbow in a rhythm slightly different from everything else dripping around it. Faint marks in the basalt where sheds stood and were unbolted.

And it is dense. Not with structures, with presences. The water is full.

At first you think the bottom is gravel shifting. Then the gravel shifts against the current, and scatters, and recollects. The fauna here doesn't need plants. It eats air, eats rust, eats each other, eats the warm chemical sigh of the bacterial mat.

They are small, but so many that the water seems to have texture. Pale isopods, each the length of your thumbnail, white as milk-glass, their backs beaded with water that never falls off. They move shoulder-to-shoulder across the submerged bacterial skin, grazing. When one lifts, you see its underside is a frill of feathery gills, beating. In the deeper pockets — old blast holes now full to the brim — something else lives: translucent shrimp that hang upside-down from the surface tension, their gut a dark orange line of iron bacteria. Newts, but not newts — salamander-things four inches long, also white, also blind, with external gills like ferns that flush pink when they surface. They walk, not swim, over the backs of the isopods, who do not react.

Above the water, on the terraces, density becomes vertical. Black snails, cool to the touch as the stone itself, their shells pitted with condensed droplets, each shell carrying a smaller white shell, and that shell carrying a moving speck. Midges in a low, tight cloud that never rises above knee height, they don't bite; they drink straight from the air. On the underside of the gantry arm, a colony of what you thought was fungus, until it pulses. It is a sheet of interlinked, leathery bulbs that inflate and deflate, exhaling mist. When they deflate, they drip. When they inflate, they pull water straight off the steel.

None of them make much sound. That is the strange quiet of the place. The engine chuffs. Water ticks on basalt, on metal, on shell, on shell. Thousands of tiny legs move through shallow water with a sound like paper being crumpled very far away. Occasionally a salamander-thing snaps — a soft, wet clap — and the grazing mat ripples outward as every isopod takes one synchronized step sideways and resumes. The midges hum only when you are inside their cloud, and then only inside your ears.

The rules here are simple, and old:

1. Water comes down, not up, but it never leaves.
2. Nothing photosynthesizes, but everything grows.
3. The metal rusts, which feeds the film, which feeds the isopods, which feed the white walkers, which die and feed the metal.
4. The bowl remains a bowl. Water finds the lowest place and stays there because the walls have learned how to keep it.

If you stand very still, the cool begins to get into your boots, into your knees. Condensation beads on your shoulders, your eyelashes. You become another cold surface for water to find. The isopods, curious, will begin to explore the edge of your boot, tasting the salt, the iron oxide, the faint grease. The midges will settle on your hands to drink the water you have gathered without knowing it.

You are not an intruder. The pit has always included occasional large, warm animals that stand for a while, drip for a while, and leave a little iron behind.
