It is low.

Not low like a valley low, low like sea-level low, like the kind of low where the air sits heavy because it has nowhere else to go. The land here was not carved, it was poured. A shallow, sharp-edged bowl cut into black rock and then lined, badly, quickly, with concrete that has not yet learned how to be old. The corners are still corners. The lip where the spillway breaks still shows the grain of the formwork. Everywhere the aggregate is exposed in crisp points, not yet rounded by weather. It is a young place, in geological terms and construction terms. It was made.

And then it filled with water.

Not a lake. Water does not get to be a lake here. It arrives as seep and sweat and steam. The rock itself weeps. The air, which is hot — an indiscriminate, shirt-sticking, low-latitude hot that makes metal too hot to touch by noon — cannot hold its moisture. It condenses on everything. On the corrugated walls of the worksheds, on the undersides of the pipes, on the rivets. It beads and runs and drips without ever raining.

This is a town, but a town the size of a room.

You can stand at one end of it, by the rusted chain-link gate where the access road stops because the rock got too wet to pave, and see the other end without raising your voice. Although you would have to raise your voice. Four, maybe five buildings: long, single-story machine halls with sawtooth roofs blacked with tar, a smaller office with its windows painted over, a water tower that no longer towers because everything around it has sweated to the same height. They are spaced far apart on purpose. There is a lot of nothing between them — cracked concrete apron, shallow standing pans of water the color of tea, pallets going soft. Sparse, not abandoned. The emptiness is part of the plan. Room for forklifts to turn, for steam to move, for heat to dissipate without taking the whole block with it.

Because this place's only business is moving water that does not want to be moved.

Inside the largest hall the pumps never stop. They have not stopped in longer than anyone posted here can remember, and the logbook says the same thing every day in different handwriting: PRESSURE STEADY / FLOW STEADY / TEMP 41C. Industrial, in the old sense: not sleek, not digital. Big electric motors the size of chest freezers, belt-driven, housed in cages that rattle. Red wheels, greased shafts, a smell of hot oil and hot water together. A boiler for no reason anyone can now explain — maybe it was for power once — now just idles and vents. The whole complex coughs out a low white cloud that never rises. It just hangs, thirty feet up, a flat, luminous ceiling that turns the already low sun into a dim, sourceless glow. Bright noon here is like late dusk anywhere else. Dim and hot at the same time, so your skin thinks it's night but your lungs know it's mid-day in August.

That cloud is why nothing grows.

There were attempts. You can see the rebar stakes where someone tried to train vine up a wall and the vine gave up. Vegetation is sparse to the point of insult — a few clumps of wiry salt grass pushing up through the cracked apron, a single, stunted casuarina by the office that has learned to drink condensate off the roof gutter. Its leaves are always wet. Its bark is black with algae that can tolerate heat and sulfur. No shade, really. Just interruptions.

But life is not sparse.

The fauna have figured it out before the flora did. The place is loud with them precisely because it does not change. The water is always here, the heat is always here, the machines are always loud in the same way, so the animals have made a contract.

Mosquitoes and midges in permanent, lazy pillars over every pan. Dragonflies the length of your finger — burnt-orange and bottle-glass blue — hawking through them without hurry. Geckos plastered to every interior wall of the pump hall, pale and pulsing their throats in time to the piston. In the weeping joints of the concrete, colonies of dark toads the size of plums sit and let the condensation fall into their mouths. Rails and little egrets pick through the marshy edge where the bowl leaks out into the true swamp beyond the gate, always the same three birds, always in the same places, as if painted there. In the dark under the pallet stacks, rats run their highways. On the tar roof, a colony of feral cats has gone half-wild and half-deaf from the sound, sleeping in the heat with their ears flat.

And the sound. It is not chaotic loud. It is equilibrium loud. The chuff-chuff-chuff of the pumps, the steady hiss where a seal has failed and no one bothers to fix it because the leak has become part of the flow calculation, the tick and tock of cooling metal, the ceaseless plink of condensation from pipe to puddle to drain to sump to pipe again. Water moving in a circle, energy moving in a circle, turning money into steam into water into more water. You stop hearing it as noise after ten minutes and start feeling it as pressure on your molars.

Nothing is eroding, nothing is progressing. The concrete is wet but not washing away. The rust is proceeding at exactly the speed that maintenance can paint over it. The pans never flood, never dry. The cloud never breaks, never rains. The level in the sump is held to a line drawn in permanent marker eight years ago, and it has not deviated by a centimeter. The young, sharp edges of the place should be softening in this wet. They aren't. The water is too controlled, too busy recirculating through the works to do geological work.

If you live here — and two or three people do, in the back of the office where they've put a cot and a kettle and a calendar that is always correct because every day is the same — you learn the rules fast:

1. Keep to the high, dry-ish islands.
2. Don't turn anything off.
3. Don't expect it to be cooler at night. The cloud holds the heat in.
4. Wipe the handrail before you grab it. Something will be living on it.

Outside the gate, beyond the bowl, is actual low country, hot swamp going on for miles. But inside is smaller, and more complete. A hamlet-scale city that does one thing perfectly and dimly and loudly forever, cupped in fresh rock, sweating, and swarming with life that loves places that have stopped trying to become something else.
