It is not a cave you find. It is a cave that finds you when the upper levels leak.

Below the streets, below the foundations and the root cellars and the old clay pipes, there is a blister in the basalt. Not large. If you stood in the middle with your arms out — which you can't, quite — your fingertips would brush two different walls. It is an intimate, lung-sized room that got stuck while the earth was cooling and then spent the next million years learning how to hold water.

The rock here has settled into itself. The walls are not jagged. They are worn smooth and bellied, polished by nothing more violent than a million years of damp air. That is the first rule of this place: nothing sharp is allowed to remain. Even the edges of broken stone are lipped with a soft white calcite that has grown over them like healed skin. A balanced place. An old place that finished becoming itself a long time ago and now just continues.

It is cool in a way that has nothing to do with weather. The air is the temperature of deep stone in early spring, and it never changes. Not warmer at noon, not colder at night. Your breath doesn't fog, but if you stay long enough your hands ache.

And it weeps. Constantly.

Water is not flowing in here. It is *appearing* here. It beads on the ceiling — a low, black, gently domed ceiling six feet high at best — gathers into fat, bright droplets that swell and shiver and let go. It sweats from the walls in dark sheets. It condenses on every surface out of air that should not be able to hold that much moisture. There is no source and no sink, or rather, there is a perfect one of each. The floor is a shallow, black mirror, ankle-deep, cold enough to bite. But it never gets deeper. It never dries. It takes every drip without a ripple of change.

That is the sound.

In a room this small and this hard, that much water is deafening. A thousand unsynchronized metronomes. Plip. Plok. Plik on stone, plok on water, plik on clay. A drip hits a pot and you hear it in your molars. A trickle runs down a wall and it sounds like someone pouring out a jug right next to your ear. The echo has nowhere to go; it folds over on itself until the whole blister rings, loud as a millhouse in full rain. When you first drop into it — feet first through the throat-hole in the upper rock, where the old well shaft broke through — the noise is so much larger than the space that you think the room must be huge.

Then your eyes adjust.

The light is dim and it comes from nowhere and everywhere. Not daylight; daylight could never bend down this far. The rock itself carries a faint, tired phosphor in its seams, a greenish-grey glow like light through deep water, and it catches in the droplets. Every bead of water is a lens. The whole room glitters dimly, low-lit, as if seen through wet eyelashes.

And it is full. Absurdly, impossibly full.

This is a city. It was not built like a city; it grew like a city because there was nowhere else to grow.

On every dry-ish ledge, on every half-inch of slightly higher basalt, someone has stacked something. No plants — nothing grows here, nothing green has ever gotten a foothold, no moss, no root, no wood. Only mineral and made things. Little dry-stone huts no bigger than dog kennels, built up from flaked basalt and chinked with pale river clay, their roofs made of fitted slate that is always wet. They lean against each other for support, wall sharing wall, roof touching roof. Between them are alleys you could not put your hand through sideways. A dense, layered, near-touching settlement that spirals around the interior of the blister like a swallow's nest made of houses.

There are hundreds.

You crouch in the cold pool and you look closer and you see they are not for people. Or not for people now. The doorways are triangular, a foot high. The thresholds are grooved by tiny, repeated feet. Inside, black, dry floors. A hearth place with no ash, only a dark smudge. A shelf with three smooth stones sorted by size. Primitive — hand-flaked edges, cord impressions in the clay, soot from some animal-fat lamp that burned out generations ago. Simple, honest tool-work, but work nonetheless, with intent.

Nothing moves. The air currents are trapped. The clay does not crumble because it never gets the chance to dry. The stone does not fall because it has already fallen as far as it can. This is a place in equilibrium, a pocket of time that forgot to pass. The water appears, the water falls, the water stays the same depth. The little houses hold their shape. The light never brightens or dims.

It feels crowded, though nothing lives here now. The density of presence is oppressive — too many roofs, too many doors. You have the feeling that if you turned your head fast enough you would see them, shoulder to shoulder in the alleys, waiting out the drip.

If anything does live here, it is rare and shy enough to make rarity a virtue. Once, while you are crouched with water up to your shinbones and that terrible loud dripping drilling into your skull, you might see the faintest V wake in the floor-pool, away from your feet. A pale thing, translucent as the calcite, blind, the length of your thumb, that lives in the permanent wet and minds its own business. It makes no sound. It does not disturb the city. It laps once at the stone and is gone.

You cannot stay long. The cold climbs your legs. The sound gets so loud it becomes silence again, a white roar. The little town around you — that wet, mineral, careful, pointless, beautiful town built in the only dry places inside a weeping rock — simply continues, as it always has, with or without you looking at it.

You find the handholds in the rock, pull yourself back up through the throat-hole into the world where things grow and change and dry out, and the blister seals itself behind you with a drip.

Plok.
