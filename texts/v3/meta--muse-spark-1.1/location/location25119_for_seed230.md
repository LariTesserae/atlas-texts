It is high enough that you feel the climb in your chest before you see it.

The ridge keeps going — a long, wind-combed back of basalt and frost-broken scree, the color of cold iron — and then it doesn't. It dips. Not a valley. A bowl. A shallow, almost perfectly round cup in the mountain, maybe three hundred steps across, rim to rim. Old. So old the rim has softened, its teeth worn down to a low, continuous lip. Inside, the ground steps down in two broad, natural terraces, and then one more that isn't natural at all.

That is where the town is.

From above it looks too deliberate to be a hamlet and too small to be a town. That is the first confusion. There are perhaps sixty buildings, but they are not scattered. They are placed. A low wall follows the exact contour of the upper terrace, not to keep anyone out — it is only waist high, dry-stacked slate — but to keep the edge. Inside that, lanes run at right angles. You can tell they were laid out once, by people who owned a length of cord and a sense of north. There is a main way that goes straight from the east cut in the rim to the center, and three cross-ways that intersect it, and the blocks between them are filled not full, but with intention.

The houses are all stone. The same stone as the bowl, quarried from it, so at first you think the town is just the place where the mountain decided to fold itself into rooms. Lower courses are big, glacially lugged slabs, lichen-stained, their corners rounded by centuries of handling. Upper courses are smaller, flatter, chinked with pale clay. Roofs are low-pitched, framed in stunted juniper that must have been dragged up from a long way below, and covered in overlapping slate tiles held down with stones against the wind. No nails. Wooden pegs, stone weights, clever lapping. Every house is one story, one door facing south, one smoke hole. Some have a second skin of wattle daubed and whitewashed on the inside. 

It is urban, unmistakably, in a way that has nothing to do with size. There are gutters. Cut into the lane edges, shallow stone troughs that all fall, foot by foot, toward the center. There are thresholds worn down by feet into a little dip, but identically placed. There are shared walls. There is a communal oven built into the northern bank, a dome of blackened stone big enough to stand in, cold now, but swept clean. There is a place where the lane widens into what can only be a square, though it holds only a sitting stone and a post for tying things. It is a city in miniature, or a city remembered by people who had only ever heard one described.

And it is mostly empty.

Not abandoned. Kept. Between houses there are lots where a house should be and isn't, flat pads of rammed earth where snow melts first. Between presences, a lot of air. You can walk the whole main way and never have to step aside. Tools lean against walls where they were left — a maul with a polished river-stone head, a loom frame, a bark bucket — but no hand comes to take them. The space between things is the same as the things themselves; the emptiness feels built.

The air is cool and thin. It never quite gets warm here, even at noon. Your breath shows if you exhale hard. The light is ordinary high-mountain light, clean and unsparing, slightly blue at the edges, making every slate edge distinct. Shadows are short and sharp. There is no haze; you can count the flakes of mica in a roof tile twenty paces away. When cloud comes over the rim it comes as a slow, level river and pours into the bowl and sits there a while, and then leaves.

Water is what makes it possible. There is no stream, no spring you can see. Instead there is damp. It is everywhere in small ways. In the mornings, the north-facing stones sweat, and the sweat beads and runs down little nicked channels into covered cisterns — four of them, one for each block, lidded with a flat capstone with a hole just big enough for a bucket. It rains sometimes, not often, but hard, top-of-the-mountain rain that hammers for an hour and then stops, and the gutters take that water and take it too, chattering briefly, down to those same cisterns. In the center of the lowest terrace, where all the grades meet, there is a pool. It is not big — you could jump across it if you were young. Lined with clay pressed smooth and black, holding maybe five feet of water that never rises and never falls. Its surface is almost always still. On it, a thin skin of dust. Around its edge, the stone is dark with perpetual damp and bright with a tight green that isn't moss, exactly, but close.

That is most of the vegetation. This high there are no trees. What grows grows in the joints of things. The dark-green sheet around the central pool. Gray-green lichen that makes a map on every roof. In the empty house-pads, bunches of stiff, straw-colored grass, each bunch a foot from the next, shivering. A low mat of a plant with tiny, waxy leaves that holds dew and tastes of salt if you chew it. In the shelter of the southern wall, someone once tried to keep a row of root vegetables — you can see the straight, stoned-up furrows — but now only one or two volunteer leaves come up, dusky, and go nowhere.

Animals are rare because there is nothing to eat. Sometimes a small brown bird with a white stripe lifts from the rim, crosses the bowl in a straight line, and is gone. You see its droppings on the sitting stone. There are pikas somewhere higher; you hear one call once in the afternoon, a sharp single note that bounces once. In the cisterns, at night, you might hear something move, a beetle or a newt, but you won't see it.

And the quiet. It is not silence. Wind crosses the rim constantly, but the bowl breaks it, makes it go overhead. So down here what you hear is what wind leaves: the faint tick of contracting slate as cloud shadow passes, a bucket handle knocking once against a cistern lip, your own feet. If someone was here you would hear them breathing a long way off.

Nothing changes. That is the rule, more than any law anyone made. The slate does not keep eroding faster on one side. The small pool does not silt in. The empty pads do not grow over. The tools do not rot; the juniper is too resinous and the air too cool and dry except for that precise, kept damp. People, when they are here, repair a tile with another tile exactly like it. They sweep the lanes. They keep the gutters nick-free. They do not add. They do not enlarge. A house that falls in is not rebuilt larger or smaller; it is rebuilt as it was, or its pad is swept and left as a pad.

You get the sense that the town achieved a shape a very long time ago that matches the shape of this place — how much water condenses on a cold night and how much weight this stone can bear and how many mouths can drink from four cisterns and one pool — and then stopped. Not out of failure. Out of correctness. It settled. Like water in a bowl, it took the form of the bowl and now it holds.

If you stay until evening, the light goes flat and wide, and then blue, and you can see how the town is one thing, a single artifact, as if the whole hamlet were carved by a patient hand that knew no hurry and still knows none.
