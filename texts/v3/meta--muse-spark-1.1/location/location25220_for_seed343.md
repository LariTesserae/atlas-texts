It is not a mountain anymore, not really.

It was, once. You can tell by the way it holds itself. This is the top of an uplift so old the planet has forgotten to push it up any further. Extreme elevation — you are four kilometers above a sea you cannot see — but the violence is gone out of it. Wind and time and warm rain over uncountable years have taken the teeth off everything. 

The range doesn't peak. It swells. Vast, bald domes of stone the size of counties, their shoulders rounded, their backs broad and polished. From a distance they look like the knuckles of buried gods. Up close, the stone itself is strange — not granite anymore, not anything hard. It's a soft, pinkish gneiss, exfoliated into sheets as thin as paper that sigh and lift in the heat and crumble under your thumb. All edge has been taken off. Gullies that were once canyons are now just long, gentle troughs, a hundred meters deep and a kilometer wide, with slopes so even you could lie down on them. The world here has settled into its final shape. Nothing more will fall. Nothing more will be cut. That's what ancient means.

And on this worn-down roof of the world — total green.

Not forest. Forest implies trees with space between them, implies ground. There is no ground here anymore. The stone is completely buried under a living mass. The vegetation is the terrain now.

It is warm — a high, steady, late-summer warmth that never quite breaks into hot, despite the altitude. The air is thin but honeyed. And in that warmth, the plants have built a continent out of air.

The base layer is a peat-moss, but peat-moss grown monstrous. A sphagnum-like sponge ten, twenty, thirty meters thick, dark underneath, living on top, holding all the damp there is in the world. Because there isn't much water here — 1.2 — no lakes, no rivers that last the year. Instead, every afternoon the clouds come up the ancient slopes and snag on this swollen highland and condense. They don't rain, really. They settle. You get wet from standing still. Droplets bead on every hair, run down every leaf, and drip straight down into the moss, where they are held. Ridged channels in the moss, some as wide as your body, carry that water slowly, silently, for days before it evaporates again.

From that sponge grows everything else, and the rule here is: grow on what grows. The dominant form is a kind of giant heather and rope-trunked rhododendron that roots nowhere near rock. Its roots braid into the moss and into each other. Its trunks are hollow. Its bark is soft and photosynthetic. Above that, curtains of epiphytes — bromeliads big as bathtubs, orchids, lichens that hang like wet wool — grow on the heather. And on those, filmy ferns grow, and on the ferns, more moss.

So the whole plateau is one building with a thousand open floors. Because for all that the green is total, the density is empty. You push through a wall of leaves and step into a hollow cathedral twenty meters tall where a single tree holds up a vaulted ceiling of leaves, and the space between trunk and trunk is just... air, and light falling in shafts, and motes. The biomass is almost all surface. The volume is void. You can walk for an hour inside the plant, between pillars, with the sky of leaves high above you and soft bog underfoot that bounces. Then another wall of hanging rootlets, another room. It's open country, valley-sized, town-sized, but made entirely indoors.

If you climb high enough — up the braided ladders of living wood to the true canopy — you break out onto the top. Only then do you see the scale of it: a rolling, breathing plain of green that follows the exact, worn, ancient curves of the mountains underneath, extending past the immediate in every direction without interruption, softening every dome, filling every broad trough. A landscape that is not land.

You were told there would be quiet.

There is not.

The fauna here is overwhelming, and it is the reason for the hollows. This is not a place for big animals. Nothing could push through all that leaf. Instead, everything small learned to fly.

The air inside the green rooms is thick with them. Not swarms — a swarm implies a center. This is a suspension. White moths the size of your hand, each one silent. Beetles like polished seeds that click against wood as they lumber through air. And then the things that eat them: thrumming, crystal-winged dragonflies two feet long that hover with a sound like a struck wire. And the things that eat those: birds, but birds that have become something else — tiny, wren-like things in colors moss never invented, moving in flocks so vast they become a single thing, a fluid in the air.

The sound is deafening. In the open rooms under the canopy it rises until your chest hums. It is not one noise. It is millions of wings, millions of calls layered until they become a roar like a waterfall. In the lower sponge-hollows it dulls to a perpetual buzzing rain. At noon, when the condensation clouds come and the light fracture through droplets on every leaf — normal, noon-bright, sun-through-high-mist light — the cicada-analogs start, and the sound becomes a physical pressure that makes you put your hands over your ears and laugh.

You learn quickly why the only trails are vertical.

Scattered through this continent-sponge are marks. Not ruins, not yet. Just scattered, stubborn human things. A platform woven from living heather trunks and allowed to keep growing, now level and solid forty feet up, with a low hut of split pith-wood on it. A ladder made of notched poles tied with vine that goes nowhere you can see, disappearing up into a curtain of roots. Small fires have been kept in clay basins, so the moss around them is blackened and will not grow back. Shards of chipped stone — scrapers for harvesting the big bromeliad hearts, that hold a cup of clean condensation water each.

The people who keep them are not here right now, or they are and you mistake them for part of the trunk, sitting still in woven bark. Their technology is primitive because nothing else works for long — metal rusts in the damp, heavy wood rots and is eaten from beneath. So they use what can be cut and regrown: simple tools, hand-carved, cord and stone and living architecture. They don't cut the forest down, they persuade a room to have a floor where they want it.

Their rule is the same as the plants': don't try to own the volume. Live on the surface of things. Keep your spaces empty so the wings can pass through.

If you stay until evening, when the clouds lift off and the warm air finally cools enough to wring the last water from itself, you will hear the sound change key as uncountable throats come to rest, and you will see, for a few minutes, how truly high you are — above the weather, on an old, old mountain that has turned itself into a garden in the sky, and all that warm, echoing green between you and the ancient, tired stone.
