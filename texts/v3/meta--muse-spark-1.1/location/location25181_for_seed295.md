It is not a mountain that has been built upon. It is a mountain that has been replaced.

A long time ago — long enough that the wind has rounded all its edges — the stone underneath stopped mattering. At an elevation where the air should be thin and cold, it stays warm. This is a high place in the tropics, a tableland that catches the cloud deck from below, so that by afternoon you are standing above a white sea and the air around you is still and humid and warm on the skin, 28 degrees and close.

What you first take for ground is not ground. 

Every surface is made. Not assembled, not quarried — made, extruded, excreted, woven, cemented. The entire valley-sized open bowl of the summit, maybe eight kilometers across from rim to rim, is a single continuous structure. It is not a city in the human sense. There are no tools here. No machines, no cut stone, no fired brick, no metal.

It is built entirely of bodies.

The base paper is wasp paper, but old. Generations of tropical paper wasps, larger than your thumb, have been chewing the dead trunks of the cloud forest below and carrying the pulp up for ten thousand wet seasons. Their grey, fibrous sheets have been laid down over and over until they are meters thick, weathered to a soft, felted sandstone. On top of that is a second architecture of mud — the work of cliff swallows and furnacebirds. Millions of beaks have carried up river silt in pellets. Each pellet is pressed, dried. Each cup holds another cup. The cups have fused over centuries into winding, load-bearing walls, terraced and buttressed like a termite cathedral scaled up to geology.

And lace through all of it are fibers: vines that were encouraged to grow through the walls and then died and lignified, roots that were trained by leaf-cutter ants into living rebar, silk. So much silk. Cobweb and caterpillar and spider in such quantity that whole districts of the structure have a dull white sheen that tightens and slackens with the humidity.

This is a settled-form erosion. Nothing is collapsing. Everything has been weathered just enough to stay. Rain has carved gullies into the mud-palisades — slow, patient runnels that have been there for a thousand years and will be there for a thousand more. The edges are soft. The corners are all rounded. The whole mass breathes and holds. It is patient the way an old reef is patient.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no lake, no river. This is too high and too porous for that. Instead there are pockets. Damp is the law. At night the cloud deck rises and pours over the rim of the structure, and everything condenses. The silk drips. The paper swells. The ferns that cover every outward face — and it is lush, shockingly lush, a vertical jungle of epiphytes growing not on trees but on nests — unfurl and collect. The whole place glistens at dawn without ever having rained. Then when it does rain, which it does two or three times a week, brief warm downpours, the water disappears into a labyrinth of throats and cisterns the animals have left for it. You can hear it ticking down through levels below you for hours after the sky clears. In the deeper, darker galleries, there are pools where it collects: black, still, mineral-tanged from dissolving old mud and old frass, ringed by moss so thick it is like a towel.

Light does not reach far. From the outside above the clouds at midday the structure is bright enough — green with vegetation, dark brown and grey where the building shows through — but the structure is dense, and it is mostly interior. Most of its volume is tunnel, chamber, gallery, chimney, cell. You have to crouch to enter. Once you are three turns in, the day is gone. The darkness is not absolute; it is a 0.4 darkness, the darkness of a deep forest floor, or just after sunset. Your eyes adjust and you see the walls are alive with faint fungus, with the wet shine of mud. Narrow shafts angle up for ventilation and for birds to shoot through. In those shafts you can see dust motes and pollen and tiny feathers hanging in a single column of white light. The rest is dark, warm dark that smells like soil and bird and leaf-mould.

And it is full. Overwhelmingly, impossibly full.

You feel density before you understand it. The walls are not walls; they are a matrix of other residents pressed close. In any cubic foot there are ants farming fungus on root paper, beetles rolling fibers, small warm bats hanging in rows like fruit, their noses tucked into each other's wings. Then the birds: swifts that never land except on these walls, brown swallows in such numbers that their mud cups make a honeycomb you can walk across, parrots that have chewed holes into the softer paper districts and nested in them. And insects you have no name for — crickets as big as mice, moths with wings as broad as your hand that look like more paper.

And they are loud. Deafening. That is the first thing that stops you, before the darkness, before you understand you are inside something made.

The sound is not a chorus. A chorus implies timing. This is saturation. Every animal is calling at once. The swallows make a constant, high, agitated chittering that bounces down the tunnels until it becomes a hiss. The swifts scream — a needle-like shriek — as they cut through the shafts. The cicadas, fat as thumbs, drill a metallic, continuous whine that you feel in your molars. The bats click and weep. The crickets saw. The frogs, which live in the condensation cisterns by the thousands, croak in a low, interlocking throb. There is no quiet gap to measure it against. You have to open your mouth to equalize the pressure. After an hour you stop hearing it as sound and feel it as heat.

There is no center. The place is open, regional. You can walk for a day in any direction and still be in it — sometimes on top, in the fern meadows that sway over the buildings, sometimes inside, in the slow, winding throats. The structure grows at its edges, almost imperceptibly. A swallow adds a pellet of mud. A wasp adds a paper-thin flake. A vine puts on a millimeter. The whole town-in-a-valley spreads, settles a little deeper, erodes a little smoother, holds its warmth.

Its only rule is this: nothing is imported. Nothing is mined. The mountain provides nothing anymore. The forest below provides leaves to be chewed, mud to be carried, food to be flown in and excreted and re-eaten. Everything is made from what was already made. The mountain has been completely turned inside out into home.

If you stay until night — and the night here at high elevation is long and true — the sound changes shape but not volume. The diurnal birds tuck and the nocturnal things start up: the owls that nest by the thousands in the larger chimneys, the larger crickets, the soft, endless rustle of roaches cleaning the tunnels. The condensation begins again, and the whole vast, soft, high, manless city begins to drip, quietly, in the deafening dark.
