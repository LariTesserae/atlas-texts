It is high. High enough that the air has been rinsed.

You are standing on the rim of a young mountain that didn't have time to become a mountain range yet. The earth here pushed once, hard and recent, and then stopped — a single broad-shouldered upswell of basalt and still-sharp slate, its edges not yet sanded down by wind or water. The stone is black-grey and iron-red, fractured in clean vertical planes where it cooled too fast. It stair-steps down into a bowl valley maybe three miles across, maybe four — open enough that you can see the whole shape of it at once, closed enough that you know where it ends.

It is cool here, always. Not cold that bites, but the kind of cool that makes breath visible and then holds it there a second too long. The light is ordinary daylight, high and clear and thin from altitude, without color-cast, revealing everything exactly as it is. It doesn't move. The sun is directly over the western lip and stays there.

This is a city, though the first hour you might argue that it isn't.

The built thing fills the bowl. Not packed. Not dense. It is urban the way a beehive built by minimalist bees is urban — organized, intentional, continuous, but with a great deal of air between every decision. Low towers, all of them made by hand. You can see the hand in everything. Fieldstone foundations, unmortared, fitted with patient hammer-tapping. Timber frames of dark, dense mountain wood that has been adzed, not sawn. Roofs of split slate shingles, each one the size of a chest, held down with stone weights. No nail more complicated than a forged iron spike. No glass. No chimney smoke, because nothing is burning.

There are hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. They are spaced thirty, fifty, a hundred feet apart, set along the natural terraces of the fresh rock, connected by worn paths that are worn without ever having been worn down. Every tower is three or four stories, narrow, with external staircases that wrap them like vines. They look like they were built for looking out, or for drying things, or for being alone while still being inside a city. Between them: empty lots of bare rock where a building could be and isn't. The arrangement is too regular to be random, too sparse to be crowded. A city practicing negative space.

And it is damp. Not wet. The valley is not a lake country, not a river country. The water here is shy, interstitial. In the shadow of each tower's north side, the black rock sweats. Condensation beads on the slate edges and gathers into droplets large enough to fall, and then it does — one drop every long while from roof to stone basin. The basins under every eave are full to their lips and never spill. In the low folds between terraces, where the rock cracked open when it was born, there are pockets of water, black and still, as clean as eyes. No inlets. No outlets. Just held there. If you touch it, it is cold and it leaves your hand damp for a long time. The air itself at ankle height smells like water on stone.

Despite the stone, despite the thin air, it is lush. Not jungle lush. High, cool, stubborn lush. Every horizontal surface that isn't a path has a pelt of moss so thick you could lose a knife in it. Not bright green — black-green, olive, rust-green. Lichen as large as dinner plates, crusted over the tower walls in circles. Small, tough ferns in the joints of the rock, their fronds furled and perfect and not unfurling further. Alpine cushion plants flowering with these absurd, saturated purple and yellow blooms. All of it alive and in its prime and not getting any more alive than that. The moss never propagates. The flower never drops a petal. Lushness as a state, not a process.

That is the rule that holds the whole place together. Nothing is changing.

Not frozen as in ice. Frozen as in equilibrium. The water in the basins does not evaporate. The moss does not grow. The towers do not weather. You can see a mason's hammer left on a low wall, the stone chips beside it exactly where they fell, suspended in the air just above the ground, because they were falling when time decided to hold its breath.

Which makes the abundance of animals strange, until it doesn't.

There are animals everywhere. That is the other thing you notice once your eyes stop looking for people. There are no people in the towers, but there are shaggy mountain goats on the terraces between them, dozens of them, hair long enough to brush the moss. There are white ravens perched on every roof-peak, two or three per tower. Marmots as big as dogs sitting upright at the edge of pools. Larks and swifts in the air — you can count at least forty in a given piece of sky — wings extended in a glide that never ends. Everything is here. Nothing is eating. Nothing is excreting. Nothing is calling, really. The valley should be noisy, with that many beasts, but it is mostly quiet. Profoundly, thickly quiet, like a temple quiet. If you stand very still you hear the occasional soft, involuntary thing — a goat's hoof shifting a millimeter on stone with a tiny *tik*, the rustle of a raven resettling feathers, your own blood. Once in a while a drop of condensation falls from an eave into a basin and makes a sound you feel in your teeth, because there has been no other sound for a long time.

The weirdness is not dramatic. No floating islands. It's just that everything is slightly on the wrong side of possibility. The light is normal daylight but casts no shadows that lengthen. Your own shadow is fixed under you, short and dense, no matter how you turn. The water pockets reflect towers that are not quite where they should be, as if seen from a few degrees to the left. The goats have far too many eyes, or maybe they have the right number but the eyes are set in a pattern that keeps making you count six instead of two, and you cannot stop checking. The silence has weight. The valley is a perfectly preserved thought of a city, high and cool and damp and green in its stone bowl, invented by hand tools and then kept.

If you stay long enough you start to understand the urbanism of it. It was not built to live in. Or not only. It was built to be complete. A place where every tower has space to be seen, where animals and moss and people — when there were people — could all have room without crowding. A settlement at high elevation to be near the clear light, where condensation could be collected without rain, where sound would not carry and disturb anyone. A primitive, careful, sparse city that achieved such a perfect balance of weight and emptiness and damp and stone that it tipped over into forever.

You leave a footprint in the moss. It stays.
