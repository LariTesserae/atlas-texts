It is not a cave. There is nothing natural left to be a cave.

You go down into it by a stair that was cut when the stair was the quarry. Three turns down, thirty-one shallow steps of brick that never knew a hillside. The air gets warm as you descend, not cool the way underground should be. It holds the heat of the day above and never quite lets it go. By the second turn you can smell it — warm mineral air, old lime, wet earth that isn't earth anymore.

At the bottom, the room opens.

It's small. You could throw your voice from one end to the other and hear it come back twice. A hamlet-sized hollow, maybe a cistern, maybe a grain vault, big enough for forty houses if houses were built for careful people. A single vessel, almost round, with a domed ceiling that was plastered by hand a long, long time ago and has been replastered ever since. No stone shows through. No bedrock. Floor, walls, ceiling, columns — everything is made. Bricks set in rings, then coated in a blushing lime plaster that has been smoothed, cracked, patched, smoothed again for so many generations it has a topography all its own. Ripples and thumb-marks, wasp-nest bulges, thin spots where the red of the brick bleeds through like a cheek. The whole place has settled into itself. Nothing is square anymore, but nothing is falling either. It sags in equilibrium.

Light comes from above, straight down, normal as noon. Three oculi in the dome, each no wider than a cart wheel, rimmed with baked clay tile to keep them from crumbling. They are open to the sky. At midday the sun makes three white columns that move slowly across the space, full of dust and seed-down and gnats. When it rains up there — not often — you hear it first on the plain above, then it comes down the oculi in cold, straight lines, drumming on the floors below. The rest of the time, water gets in the other way.

This place weeps.

Condensation forms on the dome in the cool dark hours before morning. The plaster is never dry. It darkens with damp that beads and runs, following the old trowel lines down in slow, deliberate courses, until it drips. Every surface has a tide mark. Copper bowls, clay jars, upturned tiles, half a gourd — all set out to catch it. The floor is not flat. It was built with a subtle dish, a shallow cross of gutters feeding four deeper pits. You hear them all day. Plink. Pat. Tick. A hundred irregular metronomes. Damp — not flooded, not even wet, but permanently on the edge of wet. The air smells of it.

Because it is warm and wet and full of edges, it is full of things that live.

There was never supposed to be soil here. But soil finds a plaster this old. In every crack that has been allowed to stay open, there is a green smudge. Sparse, starved vegetation. Maidenhair fern, thin as wire, in the mortar joints near the pots. Moss like a green scab on the north curve where the sun never hits. People here grow things too, because they have to — not fields, but habits. Onions strung from iron hooks driven into the brick. A shelf with beans sprouting in damp cloth. A single small lemon tree in a cracked cistern-jar that has lived for twenty years and never gotten bigger than your arm span, its leaves dusty but alive. Every plant is accounted for.

The animals — those were not planted. They arrived.

It is dense in here in a way that is almost touching. The air is not empty. At dusk, the swifts come down through the oculi, screaming, and wheel in the dome for insects, impossible to track in the brick-round. Then the bats take over, deeper, softer, chittering. Day and night there are crickets, not one or two but hundreds, in the walls, each hole in the brickwork a tiny black mouth that sings. Geckos cling to the ceiling, translucent, their throats pulsing. Beetles the size of your thumbnail, black and polished, march along the gutters and drink at the pits. Small house snakes, pale as the plaster, move through the bowls. And people — a small hamlet's worth of people — living in rooms that are not rooms but recesses, curtains hung between columns, looms and hammers and cooking fires all sharing the same breath.

Everything is loud. The dome takes every sound and lifts it and throws it back. A spoon dropped in a copper pot clangs three times. A child laughs and the laugh lives up in the ceiling for a second longer than it should. Water ticks everywhere, insects rasp, bats squeak, someone beats a rug outside an alcove and the dust comes out in a puff that catches the sun shaft. Someone is always mending, tapping a chisel, scraping plaster.

The rule of the place is maintenance. Because everything is made, everything has to be remade. The plaster drinks water and must be fed with more plaster or it will go to mud. The gutters clog with cricket skins and lemon leaves and must be cleared. The jars must be emptied into the central cistern or they overflow and the floor, which is also a ceiling for the dark below the floor, grows soft. The people here have primitive tools — wooden trowels, iron scrapers, fired-clay knives, rope made from palm fiber — and they use them constantly. You see their handprints in the walls, still wet. A woman passes you carrying a bucket of slaked lime, and you understand: this place is not a ruin. It is not being eroded. It *has been* eroded, long ago, into the shape it wants to be, and now it is kept in balance, in that exact weathered state, by endless, tender work.

If you stand still in the exact center, where the three light shafts overlap at noon, and you close your eyes, you can feel it as one organism — the warm damp breath, the ticking water, the layered, near-touching density of living things all stacked on brick that was laid by hands and never touched ground again.
