It is high up. Not high like a peak, high like a lifted country.

You climb for a long time to get here — not up a mountain but onto it — a long basalt incline that takes a whole afternoon of switchbacks, and then the land flattens and keeps flattening, until you realize the sky has gotten much closer and larger and the air has gotten thin and warm at the same time. That kind of high tropical plateau where the sun hits hard but doesn't burn; it presses. Light is ordinary, white-yellow, high-altitude normal, 2pm forever, but it has further to fall at the edges. You can see weather twenty miles off doing its own work.

This is a tableland that weeps.

The rock underneath is old. That is the first rule here. The erosion here is not dramatic. It is established, finished with arguing. Wind and water have had such a long time with this limestone cap and the basalt below it that every edge is rounded, every channel is exactly as deep as it needs to be. There are no fresh scars. The whole plateau is pocked and lipped and terraced into broad, shallow bowls the size of neighborhoods, linked by smooth throats worn down to a polish. It looks like something left out in the rain for ten thousand years. Which it was.

Water lives just under the skin. The plateau is not a swamp, but you are never more than a few inches away from wetness. Step off the paved ways and your boot sinks a half inch and darkens. The rock itself sweats. Everywhere, low cliffs and retaining walls and the sides of old quarries are beaded with seep, the stone weeping steadily, dripping without urgency. In the mornings the air condenses out of itself and for an hour low clouds form right at head-height in the streets, not fog, just the atmosphere wringing itself out. It tastes of iron.

And yet — almost nothing grows. That's the strange bargain. Water 1.8 but vegetation 0.6; you would expect reeds, fields, jungle. But the water that comes up through this rock is heavy. It carries dissolved travertine, sulfur, old machine oil now, a century of industry. It crusts everything it touches with a thin white-yellow mineral skin. Where it pools it builds its own dam, then overtops it. So the wild plants are only the ones that can stand it: black-green mats of algae in the runnels, tough as felt. A kind of wiregrass that grows in solitary clumps, far apart, each clump holding a little dome of dry soil around its roots like it's trying to keep the water out. On the windward sides of buildings, a lichen like dried rust. No trees. No fields. The city tried, but had to stop trying.

The city.

Because this is a city. Not ruins, not outpost — urban, continuous, organized. But sparse. 1.2 density on 2.5 built, which creates a particular feeling: infrastructure for a million where only a few hundred thousand live, or ever lived. The built fabric covers the entire open scale, valley after valley of this high wet table — you can see it for fifteen miles in any direction and it is still city — but it never clots. It is all spread out as if everyone agreed to give each other a wide berth.

Low factories of concrete and riveted iron plate. Machine shops the size of train sheds, their roofs bowed and white with mineral. Apartment stacks four or five stories high, each one alone in a lot sized for four, with water streets between. Everywhere, aqueducts. That's the through-line. The industrial reason for being here: they didn't build here despite the weeping rock, they built *because* of it.

The whole settlement is a slow engine for handling water.

From any high walkway you can see the pattern: along the lips of the eroded bowls sit the pumping houses, brick, with tall narrow windows, all humming. They house low-speed flywheels twenty feet across, turning at walking pace, turning since before your grandparents were born. Black rubber belts slapping. The water that would otherwise make a shallow lake of the entire plateau is coaxed, lifted an extra three feet by the wheels, and sent along overhead troughs — half-pipe, half- Roman aqueduct, patchwork repaired — that run between buildings for miles, always with a slight descent. The water never stops moving. It falls down chutes at intersections in sheer sheets, it turns other wheels, it clatters through sluice gates. The factories underneath don't make things so much anymore as they maintain themselves; their job is to keep the water moving so the city doesn't dissolve back into marsh.

It is deafening.

That's the thing they don't tell you until you're here. 2.6 out of 3 and maybe that's low. Not sharp, not human noise. A constant, low, total roar of water on concrete, water in iron, the thrummed bass note of those flywheels and dynamos, the shiver of every pipe and penstock vibrating in sympathy. And electrified on top of it — the crackle and sixty-cycle hum of the substations that have taken over from the belts, stepping down power the falling water still makes. You have to shout directly into someone's ear. Workers wear wax and cotton, but they still go slightly deaf by thirty. So people here communicate with hands, with big painted signs that hang from aqueducts and are changed daily, with lights. White for yes. Red for not today.

The fauna have mostly left. 0.8 rare, which is right, but what remains is adapted to noise as its medium. Once in a long time you see a highland hawk, tiny in that enormous bowl of sky, circling and not landing. Its shadow crosses a hundred dripping roofs. In the troughs live the only native animal that truly likes it here: a eyeless white shrimp, almost a crayfish, that clings to the concrete and filters the mineral water, piling its little casings into ridges. Children farm them by scraping the troughs.

And the strange — 1.6, not a portal trick, just a wrongness in the texture of the real.

If you watch the weeping rocks long enough, the water doesn't only fall. Because the air is thin and warm and so heavily saturated, and because the mineral crust makes a static charge, some of the droplets bead on the lip of the rock and hesitate. They round, shiver, and then lift a millimeter, hover, before deciding to fall. On any wall at any moment there are hundreds of tiny spheres hanging just off the stone, trembling. In the late afternoon when the sun gets low and goes orange through the ground-clouds, the whole city glitters with suspended water that hasn't yet committed to gravity.

The other strange is the sound itself. At this scale and volume, slow dynamic, low frequency, it becomes a place. The roar is so steady for so many decades that you can walk into pockets where it interferes with itself — where two troughs cross, where two pumping houses face each other — and find nodes of quiet, dead silence the size of a room, that moves a foot or two each year as the travertine builds. People mark them with chalk rings. They put chairs in them to have conversations. When they fade, they leave the rings behind.

Nothing here happens quickly. That is the final law. The flywheels turn at 3 RPM. The water climbs one foot per mile. The mineral grows a quarter inch per year, laying down its pale skin over every screw head, every boot print, every faded sign. Buildings sink evenly, not catastrophically. The city is always a little more rounded than it was last year. You come here expecting to hear industry as frantic, but this is industrial as geology — patient, enormous, dripping, roaring, warm, wet rock with a town built around its pulse, listening to it so loudly it has to learn to be silent in other ways.
