It is high. Not high like a peak — high like a floor. A high flat place that forgot to become a mountain.

You come over the last rise and the valley is still there, and it is full.

Not full like a forest. Full like a mouth is full of teeth. From the rim where you stand to the far wall a mile or two across, the ground is a single continuous field of stone, packed so tight you cannot see the ground it stands on. There is no soil. There is no space. Each stone holds up the next. They are not boulders. They are not scree. They are columns — hexagonal, weathered, shoulder-to-shoulder basalt and older, paler rock underneath, each one about as wide as a table and as tall as a man, some taller, some broken short. Frost-split and water-split and time-split until the entire upland basin became this one organized thing: a cobbling of the earth itself. An established form. Old enough that the edges of every column are rounded, soft to the eye, notched where they have leaned against each other for a very long time.

And it is wet.

There is no lake. There is no river. The water does not come from anywhere and does not go anywhere, which is the first unusual rule of this place. The air is bright — painfully bright — the light at this elevation is thin and white and absolute, sun through less air, UV that makes the wet rock glare. The temperature is cool, a constant 8 or 9 degrees Celsius, cool enough that every shaded face of every column condenses. The wind that crosses the plateau is not strong, but it is always saturated. It hits the cold stone and gives up its water. It beads. It sheets. It weeps.

Every stone is weeping, all the time. A film of water a few millimeters thick moves over every surface, catching the bright light and splitting it. It does not gather into streams. It does not pool. It runs down each face and vanishes into the hairline cracks between columns where capillary action pulls it back up to weep again. If you put your hand on the rock, it is slick, not with algae — nothing grows here, not a lichen, not a thread, no green, no black, no life that roots — but with pure water on mineral. The stone itself is clean in a way stone is almost never clean, scoured of any organic stain. Just wet quartz and basalt, grey and iron-red and pale yellow.

You would think with that much water it would be silent, or soft. It is not. It is deafening.

The sound is not moving water. The water is too thin to make that much sound. The sound is the air itself being forced through the packed field. The columns are so dense, with such narrow, irregular gaps between them, that the constant gentle high-altitude wind is shredded as it crosses. It becomes a roar. Not a howl, not a whistle — a broad, white, toneless roar made of a million smaller roars as air squeezes between each pair of stones. It is at the exact pitch and volume where your skull starts to participate. You feel it in your molars. You cannot hear your own footsteps on the wet stone, cannot hear your own breath. If you shout, the sound is taken immediately and given no echo.

And here is the thing that makes you sit down, eventually, on the rim, because your ears need you to be still: it never changes.

The wind does not gust. The light does not dim. The water does not increase in the afternoon and lessen at night. There are no clouds that pass and make shadow here; the condensing happens below the level of cloud. The roar does not rise and fall. The water does not make new channels. The stones do not shift. It is a system in absolute, improbable equilibrium. The same amount of water condenses as evaporates. The same amount of air enters the field as leaves it. The temperature holds. The brightness holds. The sound holds, a single endless note played at the threshold of pain.

This is why nothing grows. Not because it is too cold or too high or too bright. There is no niche that opens and closes. There is no season, no wet pulse, no dry crack. Nothing flickers here to let something else in.

Time behaves differently on the packed floor. If you stay long enough — and the cool and the brightness and the roar make it hard to stay long — you realize the floor extends too far for its size. You can see the other side, but you cannot judge the distance because every column looks like the next, and the water makes everything glitter identically. It could be a few hundred meters. It could be a town's worth. It is open, but saturated; you can see the entirety and still not find a place to step.

Rarely, something moves. A high pale fly, almost transparent, with wings too small, blunders low over the wet teeth, deaf and cold. It lands on a column, drinks nothing, and takes off again. It does not live here. Nothing lives here. It was carried over the rim by accident and will be carried out again, if it is not first pulled down into a crack. The fly is the only fauna because it is the only thing that can be lost enough to arrive.

If you do go down into it — and you will, because the roar dares you — you find that your boots do not fit between the columns. You must walk on top, from flat wet top to flat wet top, each step with water shooting out from under your sole. The stone is not slippery; it is gripped by that same thin film. The sound grows until you feel it as pressure on your chest, but it does not get louder. The light grows until the water on every surface becomes a mirror of the sun, but it does not get brighter. The basin holds you in its state.

The rule here is simple: occupy all space, leave nothing unused, and then hold it. Perfectly.
