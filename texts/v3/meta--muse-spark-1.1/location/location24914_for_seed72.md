It's not deep. That's the first thing you get wrong.

You go down only three flights. Poured concrete stairwell, painted yellow a long time ago, the paint blistered by heat. The air gets heavier with each landing. By the second, your shirt is sticking to you. By the third, the door at the bottom is sweating.

You push it open into the Room.

You can see the whole place at once, edge to edge, if your eyes adjust. And they have to adjust, because the light is terrible.

It was cut, not found. That's obvious in the walls. This is not a cave that was smoothed by water over millennia. This is a square hole made in raw, young basalt. The cut marks are still there, perfect, concentric where the big boring machine chewed through. The stone is black-gray and still sharp, not yet worn down to softness. In places it flakes and pops in the heat. It smells like hot rock and clean dust. The ceiling is low — maybe twenty meters at the high point — flat, except where a section is sagging with dark, damp mineral. The floor is lower than you expect, too. We are below the water table.

That is the whole trick of this place.

Water comes at you from everywhere without ever being a river or a lake. The rock itself weeps. The hot air hits cold stone and gives up. It beads and runs. Up near the roof bolts, it condenses around the heads of the bolts and drips — plink, plink, plink — in no rhythm. The walls have vertical black streaks where water has been running since they cut this place. Down where wall meets floor, the cut has given up being a line and has become a trench, a shallow runnel all around the perimeter choked with green-black algae and lime scale. The floor never really dries. It's a thin skin of concrete poured over rock, and it is always damp to the touch, always carrying a sheet of water a millimeter deep toward the central sump.

It's hot. Not desert hot. Plant-boiler hot. The geothermal cooperative that cut this chamber cut it for heat. On the far wall, behind chainlink, three exchange engines sit on concrete plinths, big as houses, painted industrial green. They are not new, but they were built to run forever. They suck the low, slow heat out of the rock and make electricity and they never stop. You feel them in your molars before you hear them. A low, wide, industrial thrum — HUHUHUHUHU — so low it’s a pressure in the chest. One of them has a bearing going old, and every thirty seconds it makes a complainy whine that rises and falls. No one hurries to fix it. That's the pace of things here.

In between and around the engines is a town. That's not a metaphor. This was supposed to be an unmanned station, but people live where they work when they work every day in a place like this. So the chamber is zoned without anyone ever zoning it.

There are houses, or rather built, framed cubes on stilts to keep them out of the sheetwater. Light steel, corrugated tin, old scaffolding plank, shipping container panels. Eight or ten of them, scattered far apart. Not like a city neighborhood pressed together. Like a hamlet in a cathedral, with lots of empty, echoing air between each light. Each one has a porch made of pallet wood and a humming window AC unit that is losing its fight. Wires hang across the air in big catenary swoops from one roof to another, from roof to engine gantry. Under one wire, a single string of bare, amber work bulbs cuts a triangle of dim light across the mist.

It’s dim because nobody wants more light. The air is a hot steam bath. The only true lights are the sodium floods on the engines so the techs can read gauges, and whatever each house hangs for itself. The mist catches what light there is and holds it, makes halos. The corners of the chamber are just dark wet rock.

Nothing much grows. The backflow is too hot and mineral for roots, and the bedrock is brand new. But life finds its edge where the water slows. In the perimeter trench, a scum of liverwort and thin ferns, pale for lack of light, trying anyway. A tomato plant, spindly and yellowed, in a five-gallon bucket on one stilt porch, kept alive by someone who loves it desperately. Mostly though, the only green is the slime on the concrete.

Because the animals have won.

You didn't hear them at first over the engines. But now you do. The chamber is loud with them.

The rock walls are not black alone. They are spotted and moving. Ten thousand cockroaches, the big, glossy American kind, two inches long, drinking from the weep-streaks. They move slowly in the heat, antennae waving. The concrete floor near the trench shifts under your boot, not because of water — because of woodlice and isopods, gray pill bugs the size of a thumb, rolling and stacking over each other in the damp.

And the air — above the houses, in the big empty between — is full. Moths. Pale, dust-colored moths with bodies as thick as your finger, in a cloud that fills the entire upper third of the chamber. They churn in the humid air, all of them slow, heavy with moisture. Not panicked. Just endlessly circling the few lights, shedding scales. Bats slice through them, and there are more bats than you would have believed could fit in such a small place, a colony packed into a cut-out roost in the ceiling where the machine pulled back. They chitter and flap, their sound lost under the engines, but visible. Every surface has guano and shed wings. The sump in the center, the only real pool, is opaque and twitches with blind cave fish and the larvae of things, all of them eating each other in slow motion.

It all moves slowly. That's the rule here. Nothing can afford to move fast. The water seeps, the rock sweats, the engines turn, the moths turn, the people on their porches sit and look out into the hot, wet, dim room that contains their entire world. One edge to the other. A small urbanism in a raw, unfinished hole in the earth.

There's a rule about space. You don't build in the path of the water. You let it run to the sump. You don't kill the roaches — they clean the mold that would choke you. You don't leave fruit out.

You close the stairwell door behind you so the heat doesn't get out.
