It is high.

Not high like a peak — high like a table left out after the world ended. A meseta, a basalt shield worn down until its edges are just a long, sighing fall into darkness. The wind that comes up from below is already hot and has been hot for a very long time, and by the time it crests the rim it is stripped of anything that could ever become rain. This place has forgotten how to rain.

And it is all built.

You understand that first by touch, before your eyes adjust. Under your boots there is no soil. There is flooring, or what was flooring, three thousand seasons of growth-plates laid down and then grown over. Broad lignified panels, interlocked like the scales of a kiln, each one a meter across, each one warm from beneath. They are not wood. They are denser than that, closer to a living carbon-ceramic, built to bear load and to conduct heat. The seams between them breathe. When you stand still you can feel a deep, slow exhale push through the cracks, smelling of hot sap and machine oil.

Then the forest — if you still call it that — begins.

There is nowhere it is not. Vegetation on this mesa does not grow *on* things. It *is* the things. The entire open region, valley-wide, town-wide, farther than your lamp can throw, is one continuous, packed mass of plant that learned how to be architecture.

Trunks go up as pillars, as thick as houses, and they never bothered to become round. They are fluted and buttressed and braced, they show weld seams where they were guided together when younger. Their bark is black in the dark, and where it has spalled with age you see the underlayers: rings not of wood but of fibrous conduit, of xylem that carries not water — there is no water here, there has been no water here in geologic memory — but a slow, hot, amber sap that is mostly wax and soluble mineral and carries heat as its currency. It sweats from every cut. The air shimmers with its vapor. It smells sweet and acrid, like incense left too long in a forge.

The canopy is not a canopy. There is no sun to catch. The sky above the mesa is lightless, a deep hazed dark the color of cooling iron, and yet it is hot. The heat comes from below, from the deep basalt, from the compaction engines that still run somewhere under the floor. The plants here never needed light. They are thermosynthetic, or industrial — it is the same thing now. Their leaves are vast, black, felted with heat-radiating hairs, and they are all turned downward, cupped toward the hot floor-plates, drinking infrared. They don't rustle. They are too thick, too close. They hang in layers, meters thick, leaf on leaf on leaf like shingles in a book with no space between pages. To move forward you don't walk through them; you let them move through you, or you find where the structure has briefly yielded.

Because it is always yielding. This is the turbulent part.

The arcology-forest is in constant, quiet overturn. Nothing is stable because everything is alive and everything is built and everything is old to the point of exhaustion. A pillar-trunk somewhere to your left groans — not wood groaning, but a stressed composite — and delaminates, its outer sheath sloughing in hot, papery sheets that are immediately seized by smaller vines. The vines — which are also cables, which are also conveyor belts — torque and pull the slack into themselves, knitting it into a new bough, a new wall, a new stair that no one will use. A hundred meters away a whole ceiling of interlocked leaves collapses under its own heat-weight with a soft, massive *whuff*, like a lung deflating. For two minutes there is a hot updraft. Then the edges are already bridging the gap with fresh growth, pale shoots that harden from amber to black as you watch, steaming.

It is ancient. Every surface has been built, grown, rebuilt, grown over, sanded smooth by hot dry wind carrying fine basalt grit that never gets wet enough to stick. Corners do not exist anymore. Edges are round. The original design — you can still see it if you look at the spacing — was grid-like, industrial, rational: avenues for machines, bays for fabrication. Now those avenues are still there, but they are throat-shaped, lined with leaf-fur and sap-blisters and the bitter black fruit that never rots because nothing eats it. The factories are still running, after a fashion. An extruder halfway buried in trunk-flesh still coughs, once every few hours, a sheet of new growth-plate that will never be laid. It just curls up against itself and is consumed. A pump house made of living timber wheezes heat. Light comes only from these failures in brief, dull pulses — a seam glowing cherry-red where sap superheats, a clutch of those fruits splitting with a brief cold phosphor that illuminates nothing.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds that should be in a forest. The silence that results is profound but not empty. The packing is so complete that sound cannot carry. Everything is insulated by living cotton, by dense flesh. Your own steps are muffled to nothing. What you hear instead are the long-period sounds inside the mass: the creak of load-shifting, the soft pop of a sap blister bursting, the hydraulic sigh as a whole sector rebalances its pressure somewhere out of sight, the faint fizz of wax volatilizing from a hot leaf edge. It is mostly quiet the way a sleeping engine shop is quiet. The work never stops, but it has learned to do it without clatter.

If you stay still long enough, you realize the rule here: *growth is construction and construction is growth and neither is allowed to stop.*

There was never a distinction made between scaffolding and tree, between factory wall and bark, between engineer and gardener. They built a place that could make itself out of itself using the heat from the deep earth, sealed it against a waterless world, and left. And what they built obeyed. It kept building. In the dark, in the heat, without water, without pollinators, without plan, it got denser and older and stranger and more efficient at turning heat into more of itself, until the original mesa became just a rumor of hardness under a town-sized organism that thinks it is a city.

Above you, leaves the size of doors shudder without wind. A seam opens. Hot air lifts on your face, carrying the smell of cinnamon and hot plastic. Somewhere behind that, a new trunk is beginning, pushing up through the floor plates you stand on, slow and irresistible as a hydraulic press. You should move.
