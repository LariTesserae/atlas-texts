It is at the top. Not on top, but *in* the top.

There is a mountain here that is not a mountain. It was poured.

You find it by maintenance ladder, four hundred meters of vertical rungs in a wind-shaft that never warms, and then a hatch that isn't locked so much as frozen half-shut. You have to kick it. Inside is a room that was never meant to be a room, only a pocket, a flaw, a place where three systems needed to not touch each other and so left a space about as big as two people lying down, if those people did not mind touching.

Everything is made. That is the first rule here.

There is no rock, no dirt, no mountain. The floor is not stone but cast iron grate, thick as a wrist, still holding the burrs from the mold. The walls are raw concrete, the kind poured yesterday in geological time — you can still see the grain of the wood they pressed against it, every knot and saw mark conserved in negative, untouched by water or wind. The edges are too sharp. Corners have not yet learned to be corners; they are 90-degree statements. Bolts the size of your fist, none rusted, all still wearing their factory oil under a skin of ice. Above, the ceiling is low — you have to stoop — and made of the same riveted plate that makes the walls, the floor, the hatch. There is no outside here. Even outside is made.

It is so cold that cold stops being weather and becomes structure. The air itself is a material they installed. It is cold at 0.1 in the way that absolute zero is a direction, not a place you reach. Your breath does not plume; it shatters. It falls as snow onto your own coat.

And because it is so cold at the top, and because this pocket is high enough that low pressure air is being rammed through it day and night by a vent the size of a town somewhere below, the air weeps.

Water lives here without permission. It is not flowing. It is not raining. The atmosphere is wrung. The underside of every plate, every bolt head, every grate, beads and bulges with water that cannot decide to be liquid. The concrete sweats black, then clear. It gathers at the lip of every form-line and hangs there, fat, trembling, never quite dropping. Condensation runs down the walls in broad sheets that never get wider or narrower, a skin of moving water over a skin of ice over a skin of concrete. Under the grate floor, you can hear — actually, you can't hear it under the other sound — a shallow pan of water, maybe an inch deep, that is always exactly an inch deep. Overflow is led away at the same rate it arrives. Nothing fills. Nothing drains.

The light is a single caged work bulb, 40 watts, amber, behind wire so thick it cuts the light into stripes. It hums. It has always hummed. It will never burn out because the filament is at equilibrium with the cold. It gives enough light to see that the walls are not walls.

They are covered.

Fauna: the word is too clean. This pocket is dense the way a lung is dense — alveoli per volume, near-touching, impossible to count. They are not rats, not bats, not pigeons, though you call them pigeons at first because your brain needs a warm animal.

They are a moth, or a thing that borrowed the moth blueprint. White, the white of fresh concrete dust, each the size of your thumbnail, with wings that are not wings but curled filter-feathers to catch water out of air. They are packed onto every surface that is not actively sheeting water — the ceiling, the underside of the grate, the side of the bulb cage where the warmth is a rumor. Layers. They roost on other roosting. When one moves, the ones under it move, and the ones under them. Their bodies are dense as wool felt. They do not fly. There is no room to fly. They breathe the water film and excrete a faint black-green smear, which is the only vegetation in this world.

That smear is algae, maybe, or mold that learned to grow on industrial air. It is sparse but tenacious — a velvet at the waterline, a hairline crack of green-black at each bolt where the moths haven't licked the surface clean. It doesn't grow. It is kept at exactly the length it needs to be to feed the next layer of moths. One fern spore must have come up the ladder shaft years ago. It germinated in a concrete seam and produced a single frond, two inches long, white-translucent with cold, frozen in the act of unfurling. It never grows. It never dies.

And the sound.

You understand only after the hatch bangs shut behind you why no one stays to work here.

The whole mountain that is not a mountain is an intake. High altitude wind is forced through baffles, through turbines, through this pocket. The sound is not wind, not engine, not fan. It is all of them at once, after the walls have filtered out any variation. It is a single, total, flat roar at the resonant frequency of the room — deafening, chest-compressing, tooth-rattling, perfect. It has no gusts, no warbles, no cycles. It is the same loudness and pitch it was when this room was poured. You feel it in your molars more than your ears. After ten seconds you cannot remember silence.

It does not change because this place is finished and frozen in the instant after it was finished. This is what emerging erosion means here: nothing has ever eroded. Nothing will. The concrete has not yet cured in human terms but will never need to cure further. The water that beads never falls. The moths that shiver never need to eat more or less. The light never flickers. The sound never stutters. The fern never opens.

It is a total, built, wet, cold, dense, dim, deafening little god-chamber at the top of the world, still holding its breath from being made, packed to bursting with pale, damp life that asks nothing but to keep filtering the weeping air forever.

You leave because you can't stand to stay in a place that will never, ever change around you.
