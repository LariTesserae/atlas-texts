It is not flooded. It was built to be drowned.

In a low place — a perfect, almost circular declivity in the basalt shelf, maybe an old quarry, maybe a breathing hole where something large once surfaced — there is a town the size of a town square. The size of a factory floor. You can see its edges all at once if you have the light, which you don't.

The water above it is not water as you know surface water. It is cold that has become a material. 2.9 of 3 all the way down. Black, high-pressure, near-freezing, just above the point where it would become a different kind of crystal. It presses on everything and makes it take its final shape. It does not slosh. It hangs. When you move through it, you have to push it aside in sheets. It closes behind you without a splash.

Inside that bowl sits the Works.

It is urban in the way a circuit board is urban. Not sprawling — compressed. Buildings, if you can call them that, are not separate buildings but a single dense knot of construction that forgot to leave alleys. Iron tenement frames grown together with pipe stacks, boiler housings, gantries, catwalks that lead into walls that are themselves the sides of other machines. Concrete poured around everything to hold the pressure, now smoothed by a century of still, cold flow until all the corners are round like river stones. Established. It has stopped eroding because it has found the shape water wants it to have. Rust doesn't flake here; it forms a skin, black-brown and hard as enamel.

And it is still on.

That's the industrial part. This is not a ruin. Low, deep thrumming comes not as sound but as a feeling in your sternum — the geothermal tap at the bottom of the bowl turning, unhurried, massive. Flywheels the size of houses turning in water that should stop them, but doesn't. Piston heads rising and falling with a slowness that would be useless on the surface, but down here moves a thicket of belts and chains, all layered, near-touching. Every surface has something moving against it. Pressure shutters open and close. Heat exchangers sigh warm water that never reaches far before the cold devours it. Sparks do not happen under water, but there is light — a low, sullen amber from the furnace mouths and from cupola lamps caged in wire, each one fighting a losing battle against the dark. 0.5 of 3. Not night. Deeper than night. Light only exists within arm's reach and then gives up.

Because the sound is gone. Water has taken it. You hear what the water allows, which is almost nothing. The clack of a far valve is a soft knock on a door three houses away. The great engines are quiet as sleeping animals. You can hear your own blood. If a bolt shears off — and they do, occasionally — it falls through the water column impossibly slowly, turning, landing with a puff of silt that hangs for an hour.

Vegetation is sparse, because almost nothing can eat iron light. But something does. On the leeward sides of the warm pipes, where the exchangers bleed a few degrees, there are white gardens. Not kelp. Not algae. Thin, translucent stalks like glass noodles, each tipped with a single black bead that orients toward the furnaces. They grow in the seams between plates, in dense little congregations, shivering when the water shifts. You can see where workers — or whatever once worked here — cultivated them, scraped them into lines. They are cropped short.

Fauna is rarer still. Once in a long while a blind thing, long as your forearm, pale as the stalks, detaches itself from a dark intake and jet-pulses across the gap between two gantries, startled by your lamp. It doesn't have eyes. Nothing here does anymore. The place keeps itself.

The weirdness is not that it's underwater and running. The weirdness is that the water has learned to be architecture. In the oldest part of the knot, where the pressure is highest and the heat is steadiest, the water between two buildings does not mix. It holds an edge. You can put your hand against it and feel it give like cold gelatin. Inside that pocket of water-that-is-not-water, a different density, there is no silt. Tools hang there, suspended, perfectly still, wrenches and ladles and a single chair, as if placed on invisible shelves. The water has been still for so long it has become structure. The Works grew pipes around these still-places and uses them as storage.

You are at the bottom of a cold ocean in a room that is also a town that is also a machine that is the size of a chapel, and it is so dense with things made by hands that there is no direction that is empty. Above you, the water column — kilometers of it, black, unmarked — is a ceiling. Sometimes, far above, the ice shifts, and a needle of true daylight pierces down, diffuses, dies before it can touch the roofs. The Works doesn't notice. A piston completes a cycle that takes seven minutes. A shutter opens. White stalks lean. In the profound, pressurized silence, everything is in motion.
