It is not a summit. It is what is left after summits have given up trying to be summits.

A plateau, but that word is too kind, too flat. This is a hanging country at the roof of the world, somewhere past the last honest contour line. The air is not thin — it is absent-minded. You have to remember to breathe for it. Extreme elevation, not in height so much as in removal. The world below is a rumor of blue.

The light never gets any brighter than this. A dim, high overcast that is not cloud but a kind of permanent attenuation, as if the sun is behind frosted glass miles thick. No shadows. No noon. The hour is always the blue hour before a storm that never comes. Because of that, distance collapses. You think you can see for fifty miles — an open, multi-valley scale, town-sized bowls stepping down into larger bowls — and you can, but nothing in it gives you scale. A boulder and a mountain are the same size until you are right on top of them.

Cold that has forgotten what heat was. Not biting, not windy-cold. Just profound, intrinsic, equilibrium cold. Stone cold that lives inside the stone. 0.4 kind of cold, where water cannot commit to being water. And yet it is damp.

That is the first strange thing.

There is no snowfield. No glacier. The plateau is dry, too dry for snow to hold, scoured clean to dark rock. Basalt and slate, weathered into long, resigned shapes. Not jagged — erosion has had its long, patient way here. Established. Everything is rounded, saddled, scooped out by a wind that no longer erodes, just polishes. Gullies that found their perfect form a million years ago and stopped. You could sleep in them. Somebody has.

But everywhere the rock sweats. In the lee of every wind-scoop, in the cup of every weathered basin, the stone is dark with moisture. Not a stream. Not even a trickle most days. Condensation beads on the cold undersides of overhangs and hangs there, trembling, never quite dripping. Films of water that stay liquid by spite. In the deepest of the bowls there are tarns — flat, black, perfectly still mirrors of that glassy sky, no inlet, no outlet, fringed with a hand's-width of black ice that never grows and never melts. Pockets of water in a larger dryness, holding themselves apart. If you touch one, your fingertip comes away aching and wet long after you wipe it.

Vegetation is an apology. A crust of black lichen on the north side of everything. In the damp pockets, a sparse, low mat — not moss exactly, but something that learned moss from a story — gray-green and tight to the rock, brittle to the touch even though it is wet. A few stiff, hair-like grasses that grow one blade at a time, a foot apart, like antennas. They never seem to go to seed. They just exist, spaced so far apart that the emptiness between them is the point. The density of this place is hollow. You can walk an hour between anything worth noticing.

What you do notice, because there is so little else, are the built things. Not built, really. Left. Scattered cairns that are too careful to be random and too ruined to be shelters. A circle of stones set on end that was a wall once, now waist-high and filled with that sweating damp. Farther off, on a saddle between two nameless bowls, a single structure: four walls of unmortared slate, no roof, doorway facing nowhere. Inside, a flat stone that might have been an altar or a bed. No tools. No metal. No carving. No sign of fire, ever. Just marks — faint, wind-polished petroglyphs of spirals that go the wrong way if you stare at them. Someone was here when being here meant something, and then they learned the first rule of this place and left.

The second rule is sound.

It is loud here. Impossibly, body-loud. At first you think it is wind, that high-altitude scream through a pass. But there is no gust, no variation. It is a single, vast, low roar with a high, almost-tone buried inside it, coming from everywhere and nowhere. It does not rise or fall. It does not buffet your clothes. Your ears pop trying to find its source, and then stop trying. It is the sound of the air itself being held under tension. You feel it in your molars. You have to raise your voice to hear yourself think, and when you do, your voice sounds flat and small and instantly swallowed, while the roar never changes. It never gets louder, never quieter. It just is. A frozen chord. You realize after a few hours that you have not heard silence in so long that you cannot imagine it. The roar is the ground-state of this world.

And that is when the weirdness stops being subtle.

Because the plateau is not stable the way a desert is stable. It is stable the way a paused video is stable. Time-invariant. Nothing moves. The cloud that is not a cloud never moves. The condensation beads never fall. The sparse grasses do not sway in what should be wind.

Look down-valley to judge your walk, and the valley is both close enough to throw a stone into and a full day away, and both are true at once. Walk toward that cairn and it stays the same size for too long, then is suddenly behind you. The tarns reflect not the sky above them, but a slightly different sky — a shade darker, with a star or two you cannot see when you look straight up. If you sit very still, you can hear within the loud, never-changing roar a second layer that is not a sound but a spatial error: the echo of your own footsteps arriving a half-second before you take them.

Rare fauna knows better than to live here. Once in a long while, very high, a shape that might be a lammergeier circles — or rather, it hangs motionless against the dim glass, wings outstretched, gliding on an updraft that doesn't exist in a place where the wind never moves. It watches you. It does not blink. After a long time it is gone without appearing to move. There are footprints near the damp fringes that are not yours, not quite human, with too many toes, filled with perfectly still water that doesn't take the impression. They point in a direction you came from that you don't remember taking.

Nothing grows, nothing decays. Nothing changes. The water stays damp. The cold stays cold. The roar stays loud. The place has reached its own settled equilibrium, weathered into a shape that includes your presence only as a temporary condensation, like the beads on the rocks.

If you stay until your breath evens out and you no longer have to remember to breathe, you start to understand why the builders built so little and left it empty: any shelter here would become part of the pause. Any fire would burn forever without progress. The only honest architecture in a place where reality has thinned out and holds is a low wall that marks nothing and a cairn that does not tell direction, but just says, once, *someone else noticed the loudness, too.*
