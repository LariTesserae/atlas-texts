It is very high and it is very hot, and those two facts should not be true together.

You are on a mountain that is not a mountain the way lowlands think of mountains. A single block of the world lifted, a tepui, a horn, a wall, its sides going down for thousands of feet into a white sea of cloud that never breaks. The air up here should be thin and killing and cold. It is not. It is thick, and wet, and hot, like breath held in a mouth.

The light is normal - that is the most disorienting part. A midday sun, high and white, no color to it, not dim, not glaring. It strikes the stone and the leaves and makes hard short shadows that never move. They do not move. Nothing here moves in time.

You are standing on the lip of a valley that should not exist at this elevation. A long, open trough cut into the top of the world, maybe three miles long and a mile across, open at one end to the clouds. And the trough is a city.

Not a city built in a valley. The valley is the city, because everything that grows here builds.

There is no soil left visible. There is only what grows, and what growth weathers into. The dominant things are not trees, exactly, but they remember trees. Great fig-type beings, banyan-type beings, columnar beings of living wood as wide as houses, with buttress roots like walls, with roots that become arches, then arcades, then streets. They have grown so dense, so long, in such perfect balance with wind and water and stone that they have become settled and established. This is not a jungle taking over ruins. There is no ruin underneath. This is an urbanism without builders.

Trunks rise a hundred feet and split and re-join. Limbs graft to limbs under their own weight and make cross-vaults. Aerial roots drop like pillars, thicken, hollow, open into doorways. Every surface of them is itself covered - with epiphytes, with sheets of liverwort and moss so lush and layered you could put your hand in to the elbow and not find bark, with hanging ferns that grow in curtains ten feet thick, with bromeliads big as bathtubs. It is a town of a thousand stories and no nails, no cut, no tool marks, because there were never any tools. Nothing ever cut it. It grew into this shape and then stopped.

And it is wet.

Water is near the surface everywhere because the mountain is weeping. The rock itself - a dark, porous basalt, weathered smooth, pitted with centuries of established erosion that has not deepened in a lifetime - sweats continuously. You can see it on the cliff faces, a glassy sheen that never drips, never dries. The atmosphere condenses the moment it touches anything. Leaves are always tipped with water, always letting go of a bead that is instantly replaced. Every hollow in the wood holds a cistern of dark warm rainwater, even though it is not raining. At the head of the valley, the stone exhales a broad sheet of water that should be a waterfall, but it is too heavy with heat to fall fast. It comes out of the rock hot as a bath, steams despite the already saturated air, and spreads, and braids, over and through the root-city.

That is part of the deafening.

There are no animals here. No birds, no insects, no calling, no rustle of beetles under leaves. That absence should be silence, and the wet cathedral should be silent. Instead the whole open valley roars with abiotic sound at a level that makes your jaw ache.

The water roars because it falls forever without ever depleting - the same volume moving. The wind roars because the architecture of the forest-city funnels the upper atmosphere through streets and colonnades and root-holes, and because the leaves, dense and leathery and near-touching, every single one of them overlapping another, vibrate like drumheads. It is not a wind that gusts. It is a sustained pipe-organ chord of humid air forced through a thousand living throats. The wood itself groans - a low creak as vast trunks lean into each other with a pressure that never resolves, never snaps, never settles further.

The real sound, the one that is too loud to hear at first, is the weirdness.

At this height and heat, reality is thin.

Gravity here is tired. The water that sheets off the high roots does not always fall. Some of it travels down a foot and then, finding a patch of air that has forgotten which way down is, hangs as a trembling coin of water for a while, then flows sideways along an invisible joint before dropping straight again. You see pale spheres of water suspended between ferns, perfectly still, reflecting the normal light, never evaporating. Vines grow downwards and then up and then sideways in a single smooth curve that encloses a street, then root in the underside of their own canopy. A trunk on the far side of the valley is both far away and, if you turn your head at the wrong angle, pressing close enough that you can see the moss on it detail for detail, because distance is folded.

Light behaves itself mostly, but not entirely. Shadows are too crisp at their edges and then, at the corner of vision, double. An avenue of living pillars that you walk down for five minutes can, when you look back, be only thirty feet long.

Nothing changes. That is the rule that holds all the other impossible rules in equilibrium. 

The leaf that is about to fall never falls. The water bead at the tip of a fern never quite drops - it has been beading for an age. The cloud sea below never churns, never rises, never sinks. The sound that is deafening is deafening in exactly the same way from moment to moment to moment, so loud it has become the new silence. Your ears ring trying to find a variation that isn't there. Your skin prickles with the hot wet and never gets wetter or drier. The city is not growing. It is not dying. It is not in succession. It has already become what it is, exactly as dense as it can be, exactly as urban and organized as pure biology can be without ever inventing a tool, and it has stopped in time.

It is a metropolis of wood and steam at the top of the sky, empty of any inhabitants except the structures themselves, built by nothing but the long insistence of itself, in a place where water condenses out of hot air and never leaves, and it will be like this, like this, like this.
