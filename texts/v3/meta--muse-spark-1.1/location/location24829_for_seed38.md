It is not a mountain. Not anymore.

What you are standing on is what is left after a mountain has finished being a mountain. A continent lifted a long time ago - high enough that the air should be thin and cold and sharp - and then left there to be weathered down to patience. The elevation remains. The shape does not. 

There is no peak. There is a Table. A vastness of high country so broad it has its own weather, so old that its rivers have forgotten how to cut. They meander in broad, black, slow sheets across a plain that tilts barely feet per mile. The bedrock underneath, where it shows at all, is soft-edged and bellied out of the earth like old bone, sandstone and clouded limestone smoothed to a shine by three hundred million rains. It weeps. Everywhere, water beads out of the rock itself, not in springs, but in a perspiration. The stone is saturated, and so is everything above it.

This is a warm place in permanent dim. The light never gets bright here, not because of latitude but because of altitude and saturation. You are in the cloud itself. Not under it, in it. A low, warm, luminous fog has settled over the high country and never left. It condenses on everything. The air is  warm bathwater air, 82 degrees and breathing. You can see about three hundred yards in any direction before color drowns in white. The sun is a thought behind it, a slightly whiter patch that moves. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, filtered through ten thousand layers of leaf.

Because it is all leaf.

Vegetation here stopped being something that grows *on* the land and became the land itself. This is not a forest. A forest implies ground with trees on it. This is a mass, a totality, a deep green architecture of living matter stacked a hundred feet thick. What you walk on is not soil. Soil is ten feet down. What you walk on is mat — layer upon layer of living and half-living epiphyte and moss and rootlet and rotting leaf that has woven itself into a continuous sponge. Your foot sinks three inches and rebounds. Leaves as broad as doors overlap and channel water in deliberate ways. Trunks do not rise; they *lean* and thicken and merge. The dominant plants — if you can call them dominant — are ancient bromeliad-like forms the size of houses, their cups holding ponds. Each cup is its own lake, black tea water, leaves and silt and tadpoles and the sky reflected dimly.

And the roots. The roots have given up on direction. In most places roots go down. Here, because water is everywhere, they go *out* and *up*. Fine red root-hairs hang in curtains from higher branches into the moist air and drink straight from the fog. On some of the house-plants, the roots have grown into hollow cages around empty space where the trunk used to be, long rotted away. Now the cage is the plant.

Water is never more than a few inches away. It is falling, not as rain, but as condensation. It ticks endlessly from leaf-tip to leaf-tip to leaf-tip, a hundred quiet descents before reaching any pool. Every surface is glossed. There are no dry places. The sound of it all should be loud, but it isn't.

It is mostly quiet.

This place is loud with density and quiet with sound. The sponge swallows noise. Your own footsteps are muffled to a soft compression. Every few minutes you hear a low *ploop* from one of the cup-ponds as something breaks the surface. A bird with wings like stained glass, too big for insects and too small for prey, glides between trunks without flapping, hanging on the warm, wet air. It doesn't call. Almost nothing calls. Sound would be wasteful here, it would be lost in the leaves.

Instead, fauna moves in sight.

The air is busy, if you stand still and let your eyes adjust to the dim. The density is absurd. Not in swarms, but in layering. There are small frogs everywhere, not green but clear. Translucent amber, you can see the dark of their stomachs full of gnats. They live in the film of water on leaves. There are insects that are not buzzing but *walking* across the surface tension of the water that hangs in the air — water striders of the fog. Beetles the size of your fist, black and enormously heavy, climb absolutely vertically up the living walls, carrying gardens of moss on their backs. The moss grows there, then small white flowers grow in the moss. 

Higher, something slow moves through the canopy mass. Not climbing, but *flowing* through it, parting leaves. You see its back — grey-green, fur that is not fur but fine lichen. A grazer that eats its way through the living mat, leaving a temporary tunnel that fills back in within weeks. It looks at you, if looking is the word — it has eyes under its lichen — and keeps going at the speed of sap rising.

And then there is the strange.

Because the light is dim and the air is warm and wet and high, things here store light instead of seeking it. Many of the leaves, especially low down where almost no sun reaches, are translucent and swollen, like hands full of watered milk. At dusk — though dusk is only a slightly deeper dim — they faintly glow, not with bioluminescence but with stored daylight, phosphor-pale green, releasing what they caught at noon. For an hour after true dark you can walk by the light of leaves.

Other leaves are dry to the touch despite the omnipresent damp. They repel water completely. Water beads into perfect spheres and runs off them without wetting them, rolling along pre-grooved channels on the leaf surface to be delivered precisely to the plant's root-collar. The plants irrigate themselves.

And there are the structures.

You will not find them unless you are looking for their absence of plant. Here and there, rising out of the sponge, a shape too regular to be root-cage. Low circles of stone, four or five stones leaning together to make a waist-high hollow. Or a single pillar, basalt brought from far away — there is no basalt in this limestone table — its faces pecked smooth, now covered in a fur of liverwort so thick its original shape is lost. No dwellings. No roads. No marks of tools. These were not built with technology, not really. They were *placed*. Cairns, shrines, markers for something that moved very, very slowly across this vast, slow place. Inside one of the circles you find the ground is not sponge, but bare, weeping limestone, and in that shallow pool of rock-sweat lives a species of blind white shrimp found nowhere else, tending something.

The rules here are simple and old: 

1. Water does not flow quickly. It seeps, condenses, is passed leaf to leaf, held.
2. Everything grows into everything else. There is no space that is not alive, and no life that is not touching other life.
3. Change is a matter of decades. A leaf falls and takes seven years to rot because everything is already so saturated with life it must wait its turn. The grazer carves a tunnel and the tunnel lasts a generation. The stones grow moss a millimeter a year.
4. Nothing is in a hurry. Warmth and wetness mean you do not have to rush.

If you stay still long enough, the frogs will climb onto your boots because you are the driest high ground around, briefly, and the warm fog will condense on your shoulders and run down your back in the channels the plants taught it, and you will understand that you are not visiting this place. You are just another temporary, slightly drier surface for its water to bead on before it moves on again.
