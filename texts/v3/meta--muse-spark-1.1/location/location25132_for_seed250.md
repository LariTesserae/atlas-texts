It is an ocean that lives too high up.

Not a sea on high ground — a sea *as* high ground. The world here is altitude before it is anything else. You are kilometers up, where the air should have thinned to a blue ache and then to black, but instead you are submerged. The water starts all at once, a surface without a surface. From below it looks like a sky that has decided to be heavy. From inside it, which is where you are, it looks like weather that forgot how to stop.

Normal daylight filters down through it, which should be impossible at this depth. The light is white and everyday and wrong. It comes in columns, broken and re-broken by currents, by cold seams, by densities of suspended silt that never settle. You can see for what feels like miles straight up and then you can't — the water just gets brighter, more agitated, until light becomes a kind of full-body static. Down is darker, but not dark. Far below is the actual color of distance: a deep, cold indigo where the water is finally thick enough to forget the sun.

This ocean doesn't have a bottom in any honest sense. It has interruptions.

They rise out of the deep like the first draft of mountains. That's the geology here: embryonic, raw, insultingly new. Pillars, fins, archipelagos of stone that have not yet learned to be hills. Mid-ocean ridge stuff but lifted into the sky-ocean — black basalt and glassy flow-stone still warm in its core, though the water around it is cool enough to make your teeth hurt. The rock is sharp where it should be smoothed. No rounded boulders, no sand yet, no soil. Every edge is a fresh fracture. Cooling cracks still tick and split as you watch, exhaling little black snowfalls of fresh mineral into the water. Some of the pillars are kilometers across at the base and still so far from their neighbors that you would need a day to swim the void between them, even with help. The space between is the real terrain. The emptiness is the point.

Scale is embarrassing here. You think you are looking at a cliff face and then your eyes correct and it's a continental wall, thirty kilometers tall, disappearing upward into light-churn. You think you are looking at ripples and it's a weather system turning over inside the ocean itself, a vortex of clear and silty water the size of a country forming and tearing itself apart in an hour. This ocean makes its own weather several times over — internal storms, upwellings, downwellings, rivers *inside* the ocean that run faster than the ocean around them. It is turbulent in the way a star's atmosphere is turbulent: always in construction, never in equilibrium. Creation and destruction aren't cycles here; they are the same visible event.

And it is loud. That is the first thing that would kill you if the cold and pressure didn't. Even through the water, even with nothing alive to hear it, there is a constant, whole-world roar. Water shearing on new rock. Rock boiling against new water. Those new-born mountain-fins singing with low-frequency resonance, a sound like a cathedral organ played by a fault line. Higher up, near the light, the ocean cavitates — vacuums opening and closing in the storm-rivers, snapping shut with a crack you feel in your sternum. It never stops. There is no quiet cove, no lee side, because the water never stops moving and there is nothing soft to absorb it.

Where the stone breaks the flow and is briefly near enough to light, there is vegetation. Sparse is generous. There are no forests. There is not even ground cover. What there is is tolerance: mats of black-green lithotrophs clinging to the lee edges of basalt like a bruise, hair-fine filaments that wave but do not swim. Maybe a kelp analog, but more mineral than plant — translucent stalks that grow a meter and then are ripped off by current and tumble away, rootless, still photosynthesizing as they fall into the indigo. They are the only green in a world of black rock and blue-white water. They do not spread because there is nowhere to spread *to*. Everything is cliff. Everything is new and still hot and still too sharp to hold life.

And there are no animals. You feel it immediately as a wrongness that has nothing to do with loneliness. The water is full of motion but none of it makes a decision. No fish, not even a microbe large enough to see darting. No coral. The rock stays bare except for that sparse, stubborn felt. The water itself is sterilely clean. It's a whole ocean performing every function of an ocean except housing anyone.

That is because of the other thing, the thing you cannot see directly but that makes the light behave wrong.

There is tech here, but not *built*. There are no ruins, no towers, no roads, no wrecks. No artifact has been placed. And yet this place is post-engineered so thoroughly that nature itself reads as an artifact. The water doesn't fall. A sea at this elevation, this depth, should fall. It should have drained off the world in a single, magnificent, planet-scouring cataract on the first day. It hasn't. It is held. Not by a bowl, but by a condition.

If you watch the light columns long enough, you see the lattice. Not with your eyes — your eyes see only water — but with the way the light hesitates. A faint, regular hitching, like light moving through a screen made of gravity. At certain angles, in the clearest cold currents between the basalt towers, the water organizes into perfect, impossible hexes for half a second before turbulence shreds them. Silt hangs motionless in mid-current for a heartbeat. A bubble will stop rising and then, thoughtfully, rise sideways.

The ocean is a machine that is not made of parts. Or its parts are below the size of parts. The water itself is the mechanism, structured at a nanoscale that extends up to continental scale. A field, a coherence, a suspension — literally, a suspension in both senses — holding an entire ocean in the sky, cold and clean and empty, to see what kind of geology it will decide to be if it is not allowed to erode. The answer is frantic. The rock comes up fast and angular and unweathered, because it is being extruded from below by that same unseen process, cooling and cracking and being battered by that loud, endless, animal-less surf.

The rule of this place is: water is up, stone is new, distance is the default, and something older than structure is keeping it all in this state — cool, bright, roaring, unfinished — not to preserve it, but to watch what it does when it never gets to finish.
