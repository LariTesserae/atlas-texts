It is high up, where the air thins and the light gets hard.

The mountain was not built ON. The mountain WAS built. Or replaced.

From the pass — the only place where you can stand back far enough to see it as a whole — you understand the scale wrong at first. You think you are looking at natural terraces, a geological stair, basalt benches or shale slumping. Then your eyes adjust to the brightness, and you see the seams.

Everything is set stone. Everything.

The entire bowl of the valley, the ridges around it, the peak itself, which from a distance looked snow-capped, is covered in work. Dry-stacked walls, low and massive, platform upon platform upon platform, rising in irregular, relentless tiers from the dark lake at the bottom to the sharp, unfinished crown at the top. No bedrock shows. No soil. The ground under your boots, if you can find ground, is a floor of split slate laid tight enough to hold water. Which it does. Which everything does.

It is a cold place. Not freezing — the sun is too direct for that, too white and too close, pouring over the thin air without filter. It is the cold of deep stone that has never warmed. A cool that lives in the rock, not the air. Your breath does not smoke but your hands ache if you keep them on the walls too long.

The stone is young. That is the strange part. In a place this vast, you expect rounding, sag, the softening of a thousand winters. There is none. Every edge is still off the hammer. Faces of quarried gneiss still show the bright, chisel-scored interior, pale grey against the weather-darkened skin. Corners are square. The heavy lintels over the narrow passageways have not yet settled into their supports. Flakes of knapping debris, sharp as knives, still lie in the channels where no one has kicked them away. This place was just made. Or is still being made, very slowly.

And it is packed. That is the word that finally fits once you descend into it.

There is no space between structures because there are no separate structures. There is one continuous act of building that has saturated the topography. A house is a wall is a dam is a stair is a cistern. To move across the valley you must move through it — sideways along walls shoulder to shoulder, up steps cut for legs shorter than yours, through slots where two retaining walls almost kiss. Every surface is occupied by another surface. The sky becomes a bright ribbon you only see when you tip your head back. The density is not vertical like a city. It is territorial. No part of the mountain was left alone.

The craft is primitive. You can see how it was done, which makes it more overwhelming than if it were seamless. No saws. No mortar that holds. Stone split along its grain with wedges, perhaps of harder stone or crude iron that left rust-blooms in every wedge-scar. Lifted by hand, by ramp, by many hands. You find the depressions in the coping stones where rope dragged. Everywhere, the mark of fingers and palm. Someone, many many someones, picked up every single one of these stones and placed it. The total square footage is incomprehensible for such labor.

There are no plants. Not a lichen, not an alga in the water, though there should be. The elevation might excuse trees, but not this absolute mineral cleanliness. The stone is scoured, too cold, too wet, too new. And there are no animals, no insects, no birds riding the updraft. No footprints but yours, and those fill with water almost as soon as you make them.

Because it is wet. Profusely, aggressively wet.

The sky is bright and clear, but the rock weeps.

High air condenses on every cold face. You watch it happen — a bright film appears on a slab of dark schist and beads and runs. Springs find their way out from between courses, not because the builders left pipes, but because water near the surface has no other choice. It pushes out from the seams of the mountain itself. The entire system is a water machine.

And so they built for it, those primitive, patient builders. Every terrace is slightly dished. Every floor is canted a degree. Every wall has a weep hole at its base, a small dark mouth dribbling constantly. A lacework of rills, gutters, runnels, finger-wide aqueducts runs along the top of every wall, down every stair. Nothing is dry. Water is shivering over every horizontal surface millimeters deep, catching the fierce sun and throwing it back almost painfully bright. The great lake at the bottom is not a lake. It is the lowest cistern, knee-deep, then waist-deep, perfectly rectangular, its surface broken only by the ripples of a hundred inflows.

The sound is what emptiness does with water.

Mostly quiet. Not silent. There are no leaves to rustle, no animals to call, no wind turbines to hum. But the water never stops talking quietly to itself — drip, trickle, seep, plink. A thousand tiny overflows. If you stand still in one of those tight, tall alleys between two ten-foot retaining walls, both dark and damp, and look up at the strip of violent blue, you can hear the mountain ticking with water. Drops from above pooling on the slate before you, falling from the lip above that, falling from above that. Delay. Then another.

The dynamic is slow. So slow you almost miss that it is moving.

The pool you stand in deepens over an hour. The film on a stone thickens. The highest, newest tiers at the summit — where raw, quarried rock still lies in rough piles, as if the workers only stepped away — glisten more each minute but never quite seem to spill. The water is patient. The stone is patient. Even evaporation is slow in the cold.

You understand the rule here without being told: nothing is allowed to stagnate, and nothing is allowed to rush. Everything must keep moving, by a hand's breadth, by a drop. You step, and water takes your place. You climb, and a channel you cleared with your boot refills. The whole dense, total, hand-made mountain is a single instrument for slowing water on its way down from sky to dark, for holding it, just for a second, bright and cold and clean, in every possible stone hand before letting it go.

You have to watch where you put your feet. Not because you will fall, but because there is almost nowhere that is not already holding something.
