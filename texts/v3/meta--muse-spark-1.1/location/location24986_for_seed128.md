It is not a room. It is a caught breath.

High up in the mass — so high the outside air would be thin and knife-cold if you could reach it — there is a pocket where two systems failed to meet. The outer skin of the mountain, which is not a mountain, slopes and curves away above you, a slab of weathered composite a hundred meters thick. The inner skin, the hab's pressure hull, leans in. Between them, for a vertical kilometer, there is nothing but a baffle gap and wind. Except here, where a structural rib buttresses out and a service node swelled and then sagged, long ago, leaving a void about the size of a chapel closet. Four steps long, two steps wide, and tall enough that you have to crouch under the low side.

Everything here is made. There is no stone, no dirt. The floor is a grate, then another grate beneath it. The walls are hull, old hull, the kind they don't make anymore — poured metaplasm that was told to be solid and then forgot how to stop being slightly alive. It is dark gray, almost black, pitted like pumice from a century of condensation eating at it. White mineral beards hang from every bolt head. The whole place has been weathered into nature. Water has cut it. That is the erosion — not wildness, but time.

It is warm. Unreasonably warm for this altitude. Waste heat bleeds off the inner wall in a constant, wet exhalation. The air in the pocket never quite dries. It sweats. Beads of water nucleate right out of the air on the cooler outer plate, gather, tremble, and fall. They fall constantly. The ceiling weeps. The ribs weep. Even the handholds weep. The grates under your boots are slick with a dark biofilm and the water runs through them in engineered rivulets, ticking and gulping as it drops away into the dark below. It doesn't pool. It can't. The pocket is always just after rain.

The light is almost none. There is no bulb. The only light is what the walls themselves give off when they are stressed — faint amber fault-lines that pulse deep in the metaplasm, like coals under ash — and the light of the residents. Which are many.

You thought sparse vegetation meant empty. It isn't. A few things grow. In a seam where calcium from the weeping water has made a false soil, three fern-fronds have taken hold, but they are not the ferns you know. Their leaves are black-green and printed with circuits, each leaflet a tiny solar plate that tore off some maintenance swarm and rooted here by accident. They shiver each time a drop hits them. Beside them, a mat of white, hair-thin lichen that is actually wire, tasting the water for pH. That's it. That's all the plants that could get permission to be here.

The animals — though, are they animals? — are everywhere. This is their continent.

First you hear them before you can see them, because the sound in the pocket is deafening. The outer skin is singing with high-altitude wind, a low roar that vibrates your molars, and the inner skin is singing with the deep thrum of the city-mountain's blood moving. Between them, trapped in this tiny wet throat, the sound stacks and doubles and becomes solid. And on top of it, the cicadas. Thousands of them. They are not cicadas. They are heat-shunt beetles, each the size of your thumb joint, glossy black with heat-exchange wings that they buzz not to fly but to cool themselves. They crowd every surface, layer over layer, legs touching legs, drinking the condensation and flashing their wings. Their combined buzz is a white, rattling scream that makes your own voice disappear inside your head. When you move your hand near the wall, they part and re-form, annoyed.

Between the beetles run the mites, silver and quick, and in the water film skate little shrimp-clear drones that are cleaning, always cleaning, carrying specks of mineral away in their bellies. In the highest corner, where the two plates nearly touch, a colony of filter-bats has roosted — not bats, but folded membranes with hanging mouths — they pulse, drawing the wet air through themselves, and with each pulse they sigh out a little cloud of warm, cinnamon-smelling spores that are actually diagnostic code.

It's dense, impossibly occupied for such a small place. Every square centimeter has a presence. Your knee, braced on the grate, is already not yours; two beetles have climbed onto it to drink the sweat off your suit.

The rule of the place is simple and you learn it in seconds: nothing leaves wet. The tech that built this pocket never stopped maintaining it. The outer plate still tries to be a perfect insulator, still generates a fractional gravity shear to shed ice, though there is no ice this deep in the warmth. You can feel it as a faint wrongness when you lift your hand — a lag, as if the air is a little thicker on the way up than on the way down. That's the old impossible engineering, idling. The inner wall still tries to heal. When a beetle dies and its carcass clogs a pore, the metaplasm extrudes a clear thread and stitches over it, turning it into more wall. The pocket is eating its own ecology and being eaten by it in return. It is balanced. It has been balanced for longer than anyone has remembered this pocket exists.

You can only stay a few minutes, because the warm wet and the deafening make you stupid. The water keeps falling, line after line, cold from its birth on the outer plate, warm by the time it hits your shoulders. The beetles shriek. The bats breathe. The light goes amber, amber, amber with each slow breath of the walls. Somewhere far below, the city shifts.
