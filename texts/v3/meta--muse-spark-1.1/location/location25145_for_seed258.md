It is high.

Not high like a peak — high like a table left out after the world was made. A basalt mesa that never finished cooling, a few thousand feet above the lowlands, broad enough that you can't see its edges in the haze. The air up here should be cold and thin. Instead it is warm, wet-bath warm, greenhouse warm, because the stone underneath is still young.

This is what low erosion means here: everything is raw. The rock hasn't learned to be rock yet. In places where the weave slips you can see it — matte black, bubbled, soft at the corners like bread crust, still holding the heat of its making. It sweats. Water doesn't come from rain so much as it comes from the rock itself, from the air itself. The atmosphere touches the warm stone and wrings itself out. All day long it condenses and drips and runs. There are no rivers. There is just weeping.

And nothing is bare.

From a distance you would think this is a ruined city. From closer you realize it is not ruined, and it is not a city in the way you understand cities. It is total plant, and total built, because those are the same thing here.

The vegetation didn't colonize architecture. It is architecture.

There is one kind of building, but it repeats at the scale of a valley. Imagine a banyan fig and a mangrove and a gourd vine and a cathedral builder all agreeing on one idea. Thick prop-roots as wide as houses drop from a canopy you can't see the top of, hit the young basalt, splay, fuse. From those roots, walls grow. Not walls covered in vines — walls that are vines, lignified to the hardness of old oak, woven so tight no light passes through the braid. They grow as buttresses, as arches, as open vaults. Then another root drops a hundred yards away and does it again.

The space between presences is huge. That's the sparseness. You can walk ten minutes across a floor that is a single living leaf — a leaf the size of a plaza, dark green and slightly yielding, veined with raised channels that carry warm water — and not encounter any vertical at all except the distant, cathedral-trunk pillars holding up the upper layer. Then you come to a Structure.

A cistern, for example. A perfect sphere thirty feet across, woven out of living aerial rootlets that have swollen together, watertight. It is full to the brim with condensed water so clear you can see its living bottom. The inflow never stops: a lip at its equator exudes droplets, continuously, *plink plink plink* that becomes a roar when multiplied by ten thousand cisterns across the mesa.

Or a stairwell. A hollow column with a helical ramp grown into its interior, its steps are bracket fungi the size of mattresses, still alive, still soft.

Or a house with no inhabitants. A dome with a single round doorway, its floor is moss a foot deep and dry somehow, despite the wet everywhere else. Its ceiling is a lattice that lets in normal daylight — bright, white, midday light that doesn't move.

Because nothing moves here. Not really.

This is a place in equilibrium. That is its strangest rule.

In a normal forest, things grow, fall, rot, compete. Here growth has arrived at a shape and has decided to stay. The leaves do not fall. The water does not erode the rock anymore than it already has. The vines do not thicken. You can put your hand on a root and feel the faint push of water inside, feel it is alive, but it is not *changing*. It is holding. The whole mesa-wide organism — maybe it is one organism, maybe it is a convention of many — poured itself out in one fast, exuberant, embryonic effort and then froze in fullness. Raw geology under, total living construction over, and between them, forever, this warm wet breath.

And that is why it is loud.

There are no birds to speak of, no insects in clouds. Fauna is rare. Once or twice a day you might see something white and long — a glider, a moth the size of a sheet — move from one pillar to another without flapping, just shifting on the humid air. It makes no sound.

The sound is the place itself. Water condensing on ten million leaves and falling. Water ticking on cistern heads. Water sighing through root-pipes under the leaf-plazas. The creak of wood under tension that never resolves, the groan of a living vault that is holding itself up without ever settling. It all adds up to a steady, staticky roar, like being inside a drum made of rain. It never gets louder or quieter. The moment you think you have become accustomed to it, you realize you are hearing it inside your teeth.

The rules are simple here, once you stop looking for makers:

1. Everything you touch is alive and was grown on purpose.
2. There are no tools and no toolmarks, because the urge to make is not separate from the act of living.
3. Water comes from everywhere and goes nowhere — it circulates.
4. No new thing will be added. No old thing will be taken away.

If you stay until the light starts to thin — though the light does not really move either, the day is a long normal noon that smudges at the edges — you will see that the condensation catches the sun and makes a low ground-mist that never burns off. It collects between the distant pillars and makes the mesa look even larger, an archipelago of living towers in a white sea, town-or-valley-sized, valley-without-a-valley, repeating as far as the warm haze will let you see. And somewhere in that haze, another cistern fills, another leaf sweats, another stairwell waits empty, all of it built, all of it grown, all of it never needing to be built again.
