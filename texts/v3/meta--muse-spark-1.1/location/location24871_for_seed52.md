The city was born after the land it sits on.

That's the first thing you understand when you walk it. This is low country, almost sea-level low, but not a floodplain. The ground is too new for that. It's a fresh basalt tray, lifted or extruded in one long, warm event maybe forty years ago - black glass and scoria still angular under the wear. It hasn't learned to be rounded yet. Everywhere the plain is broken into plates the size of city blocks, separated by fissures a meter to three meters wide, with edges so sharp they can cut a boot. The plates are not quite level; one tilts two degrees north, the next two degrees south. Water lives in that wrongness.

There isn't much water, not in a river or a lake sense. The air is a humid hand on your shoulder all day, warm enough to make your shirt stick. At night the whole valley exhales. Because the sky is dimmed, the air cools fast, and condensation comes out of nowhere, beading on every surface. By dawn, the undersides of all the plates are dripping. The fissures run. For an hour after sunrise you can hear them - a thousand temporary creeks appearing in the dark between buildings, brown-black water running over glass, then vanishing as the heat returns. They leave behind silt lines and little mats of fern-spore green in the cuts. In the deeper sink-fissures where two plates have sagged together, the drip never quite stops, and you get pockets - a small, clear pool with white blind shrimp in it, then fifty meters of dry stone, then another pool.

The light never gets strong. The city made its own overcast. Far above, at what should be cloud height, hangs the Lattice - a veil of braided structure, not fabric, not machine exactly, a kilometer up and extending beyond what you can see. It doesn't block the sun, it edits it. Light comes through amber and thinned, as if through old honey. No shadows with hard edges. No glare. Just a perpetual late afternoon, even at noon. The air under it holds the warmth, 28, 30 degrees, and the vegetation loves it.

And it is lush. Not evenly. The city is sparse for a city. If you stand on the roof of one of the hab-spires you can see the pattern for kilometers - and that's the scale of it, not a megacity smear but a valley-wide scattering. One tower, then a quarter kilometer of open, cracked plain with weeds, then three low terraced platforms huddled together, then open again. But where life takes hold, it takes *over*. The buildings are not hostile to plants. Their skins sweat distilled water, and the leaves know it.

Giant taro and engineered elephant-ear, leaves a meter across, glossy and dark green almost black in this light, grow out of the walls themselves. Not ivy on concrete - the wall is a root medium. Strangler figs the size of houses have started on the older spires, buttressed and full of hanging gardens of epiphyte. In the open lots between structures, cane and wild sugar grow ten feet high in a season from the fresh mineral soil. You can smell it: warm sap, resin, the iron smell of new rock, and that wet-stone smell after the morning fissure-flow.

The built things themselves are what make the quiet feel strange.

There are maybe two thousand structures in the basin, and no two agree on what a building is. The early ones, human-normal, are concrete and steel, already being eaten. The later ones are not built. They are coaxed. Their frames are error-correcting - a dull-metal lattice that holds space in place a little too hard, so light slides off it wrong. If you stare at a corner of a new spire, your eye skips. The surfaces seem to be made of overlapping plates of air that have been convinced to be solid. Some of the towers have no doors on the ground floor; you enter by thinking the entry pattern at them and the membrane thins for you. Inside, they are bigger than outside, not in a funhouse way - in a way that makes your inner ear hurt if you think about it. The utility lines don't run in pipes; water condenses directly into basins because the Lattice tells it to condense there. The power doesn't hum.

The ground-fissure grid and the building grid do not match, and nobody has tried to force them. The streets, where there are streets, are really just the wider fissures bridged with carbon lace that you can see through to the damp below. Sometimes the lace repairs itself overnight into a new arch.

It is mostly quiet. That's the eeriest part for a town this big. Sound absorption is part of the Lattice's job, or a side effect. Footsteps are muffled. Voices don't carry. What you do hear is small and close: water ticking off a leaf into a cistern, the sigh of a fig root tightening around a tower, the soft, almost subsonic thud when a building adjusts its weight on the basalt plate. Once in a while, a high, thin whine passes overhead - maintenance kites, translucent as jellyfish, kilometers long, swimming through the dim air to edit a filament.

Life only looks empty until you sit still.

Fauna is rare, but it is present, and it is careful. No flocks, no swarms. A single amber heron might stand in a fissure-pool for hours, perfectly still, so coated in condensation it sparkles. Slider lizards, six-limbed, run between shadows and freeze. In the cane breaks, something larger moves at dusk, never seen cleanly, leaving prints in soft scoria where three toes drag. And on the Lattice itself, very far up, there are slow-moving dark spots - gardeners, or animals, or both - grazing on the light.

The whole place is active but not frantic. Things are growing. Plates are still settling with occasional deep, harmless shudders that send a wave of condensation raining off every leaf at once. A tower two streets over might be a meter taller tomorrow; the builders don't announce it. The fissure-creeks come and go every day. The figs tighten.

The rule here seems to be: new land wants to be occupied quickly, but it doesn't want to be crowded. So it made, or let us make, a city that is mostly air, mostly damp, mostly open space between quiet, impossible houses, all connected by water that only exists for an hour in the morning, all under a sky that has been politely turned down low so the young things don't sunburn.
