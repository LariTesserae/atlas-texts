It is high.

Not high like a peak. High like a table left after everything else was taken away. A final place. The world below is gone or never was, and what remains is this flat-topped height, warm stone breathing out the day's stored heat even though the light never fully arrives.

There is no horizon here because the place is not large enough to have one. It ends. You can feel it end. The scale is that of a single room, if a room could be the size of a cathedral's nave - a hamlet, a clearing, a courtyard with no village around it. But there is no clearing, because there is no room left empty.

This entire elevation is built.

Not built upon. Built. There is no natural substrate showing through. Everywhere you look, from the dust underfoot which is powdered brick and ground-down mortar, to the walls that are not walls but limits where the building stops because there is no more mountain, everything has been placed by hands. Primitive hands. Early hands.

It is packed. That is the first sensation after the height and the heat. It is saturated. The builders did not build structures with space between them; they filled occupation to its absolute limit. It is a dense, low, interlocked mass of cairns and dry-stone hives and stacked slabs and leaning towers the height of a man and the width of a shoulder, all mortared with nothing but their own weight and the skill of fitting. Flat stones the color of sun-baked bone, and darker stones the color of dried blood, and salt-white stones that crumble if you stare too long. They are fitted together like teeth. Like scales. Like a single, collective wall that forgot it was supposed to be many buildings.

There is not a handspan that is not worked. You cannot see where one maker started and another left off. The technique is old - you can see it in how low and practical everything is, how each stone has been knocked into shape with another stone, how there is no cut metal, no true arch, no straight line that lasts longer than an arm's length. And the stone itself is ancient beyond that. That is the paradox that makes it feel so still. The construction is total, but the erosion is total too.

Everything is rounded. Everything is smoothed. Edges that were once chipped sharp by primitive hammers have been softened by a wind that has had a million years to work. The cairns sag into each other. The uppermost slabs have become domes. The gaps between stones are not gaps but polished throats, lipped and blackened, whistling. The entire dense hamlet of stone has settled, like bread cooling, into its final, most stable, irreducible shape. It will not fall further. It has found equilibrium. It is the oldest thing that can be made by hands and still be recognizable as made.

The light is dim. It is always dim here, and warm. The sun, if there is a sun, is behind a permanent high haze - not cloud, not dust, but a thickening of the air itself at this altitude. The illumination is sourceless and brownish, like light through old paper. It doesn't cast shadows so much as it allows a slightly deeper dimness to pool between the packed stones. And yet the stones are warm. Unreasonably warm. They hold heat like a kiln, like living skin under your palm. When you press your hand to them the warmth comes back to you immediately. The air itself is warm and utterly, absolutely dry. Water has never been here. Not as rain, not as ice, not as stain. There is no lichen on these stones. No moss. No blush of green or black. The palette is mineral only: ochre, ash, rust, salt. The air has no smell of water. It tastes of hot rock and iron.

And then there is the sound.

It is deafening.

From the first moment you are inside it, not hearing it but being in it. It is not loud in the way an explosion is loud. It is loud in the way a great weight is heavy - total, encompassing, physical. The wind comes over the edge of this high world, over the drop where the building stops, and it falls into this dense, packed maze of throats and tubes and polished flutes and hollow cairns that have been bored out by centuries of that same wind. Every gap between every stone is a whistle, a pipe organ throat, a prayer tube.

Because there are so many gaps, and because they are all slightly different lengths and diameters, worn by chance and hand, the sound is a chord. A single, enormous, unchanging chord that fills the cathedral-scale enclosure and does not vary. It does not gust. It does not waver. The atmospheric conditions that drive it are locked. The note has been sounding for so long that the stones have shaped themselves to better resonate it.

If you stand here, the roar lives in your molars. It lives in your chest. It blots out thought, blots out any possibility of listening for something else, because there has never been anything else. No animal has ever called here. No footfall has ever been audible over it. No voice could ever have carried. The builders must have built deaf, or built in order to make something that could speak louder than they ever could, even after they were gone.

And nothing changes.

That is the law here. The warm stones do not cool. The dim light does not cycle. The dust does not settle because it is already settled. The deafening chord does not rise or fall. The packed, total construction does not expand or crumble further. It has reached the end of its life as a built place, its oldest-stable-state, and it will hold that note forever on the top of a world that has dried out and forgotten any other shape.

You have to leave by climbing back over the edge you came from, because there is no path through. There is no room between presences.
