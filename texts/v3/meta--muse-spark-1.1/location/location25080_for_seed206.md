It is not a swamp. It is a pocket *inside* a swamp.

Low country, warm low country where the air never really lifts. The kind of flat that makes water forget which direction to go. You wade through knee-high warm water the color of tea, between cypress knees and mats of floating fern, and everything is close, green, breathing on you. Light comes down normal and flat and white through the canopy, not dramatic. Just daylight.

And then the ground, if you can call it ground, gives a little under your foot. Not mud — give.

You move a curtain of hanging moss and hanging roots and there it is. A room that was never built.

It is a single chamber, maybe three strides across if you could stride, but you can't, you have to crouch. It's entirely plant.

It is not inside a tree so much as it is made of trees agreeing to stop being trees. The walls are living, coalesced. Buttress roots from six or seven different lowland giants have grown toward each other here over centuries, over millennia — that established, in-balance, long-settled shape — and fused. An inosculation. They flattened against each other, grafted, wrapped, became a roughly circular wall. Leaves roof it over completely. The interior is bark on its inner face, but bark that has been worn smooth by being interior. Not by hands or tools — no tools have ever been here — but by water, by air, by time exhaling against it. It's damp wood polished dark as old river stone, weeping.

That's the water. It's everywhere but never deep. The walls sweat it. The low ceiling sweats it. Warm condensation beads and swells and breaks and runs down in slow, bright lines. The floor is not floor; it's a live mat of interwoven root-hair, sphagnum, and something thicker, like a felt of living palm fiber, saturated but buoyant. When you press it, water wells up around your knees and then is sucked back down. The air itself is almost liquid. Your skin stays wet without ever being rained on.

And because it is all plant-as-substrate — vegetation at totality — there is nothing else. No stone. No dirt that isn't root-bound. No structure. No artifact. And yet —

It is loud, and it is industrial.

At first you think it's insects. It is, partly. But it's the plant itself.

This chamber is an engine. Or it has become one, without ever being made.

The roots that form the walls are hollow and under pressure. Warm sap, thin as water, moves through them in great hydraulic surges. You can see them swell. You hear the work. A low, rhythmic THUNK - hiss - THUNK - hiss - as valves of fiber constrict and release. High up where the wall becomes ceiling, pitcher-like nodes the size of oil drums hang, living stills. They fill with steam — not hot steam, warm plant steam, humid, fragrant, 90 degrees — and vent it with a shuddering sigh that turns to water on contact with the cooler air and rains back down. A piston chamber of wood contracts, tight as a muscle. Somewhere deeper under the floating mat, something cavitates and knocks.

No smoke. No metal. No rivets. But the logic is all industry: intake, pressure, transport, exhaust, cycle. Scaled production. It has been doing this for longer than any factory could. Photosynthesis at such density and volume that it needed logistics. Electrified — you can smell ozone after a big pulse, you can see the fine hairs on the root-mat stand up. The energy is tangible.

And it is in perfect equilibrium. Never changing. That thump-hiss has been thump-hissing at exactly this tempo, 48 beats a minute, with the same pressure, the same amount of weep, the same light, for a thousand years. Water in equals water out. Growth equals sloughing. It doesn't get bigger. It doesn't wind down. It discovered its steady state and stayed there. The wood is weathered inside itself. The erosion is finished; the shape it has is the shape it keeps.

It is sparse inside, for all that fullness. There is a lot of empty between presences. That's what makes it feel like a room, a pocket cathedral. The walls are everything, but the center is mostly humid air and falling droplets.

Except for the fauna, which are abundant. They are the workers and also the residents who don't work. Tree frogs the size of your thumbnail cling to the weeping walls in hundreds, throats pulsing, adding their own piercing, metallic chorus to the industrial clatter until you cannot tell frog from valve leak. Beetles with backs like polished gunmetal ride the root-mat, bailing water, chewing a filament here, sealing a leak there with amber sap they produce from their own abdomens. Clouds of tiny white flies rise and settle, rise and settle in the normal light that filters down through the one pinhole in the leaf-roof — a perfectly round hole that lets a solid column of sun in as thick as a post. Moths the size of your hand, warm to the touch, hang from the ceiling asleep, their wings vibrating in sympathetic rhythm with the engine. None of them built anything. They were attracted to the function and stayed.

Weirdness is low, just a tilt. Only this: that the plant is making absolutely nothing. There is no fruit here. No seed. No flower anymore. All that mechanized, scaled, abundant hydraulic power is not going toward reproduction or spread. It is just cycling. Intake, pressure, exhaust. A factory whose product is continuing to be a factory.

If you stay long enough — and you have to stay on your knees, because there is not headroom — the sound reorganizes. The hiss is no longer hiss but long inhalation. The thunk is a heart, or rather, many hearts agreeing to beat as one. The water that beads on your arms beads on the walls at the same rate. The warm is the same warm inside and out.

You are inside a living bottle, in a low place, after the rain that never stops being after. It was never built, and it will never stop running.
