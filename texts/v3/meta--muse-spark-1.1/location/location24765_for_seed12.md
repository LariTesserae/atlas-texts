It is not an ocean. At least, not an ocean the way the lowlands understand ocean. It is higher than weather.

You climb for days to get here, up the new range. The range that wasn't here when your grandparents were children. Bare, black, unfinished rock - not yet mountains properly, more like a thought of mountains pushed out of the planet. No slopes smoothed, no valleys carved. It's all raw edge and hot glass, stillborn basalt, pillow lava stacked on pillow lava, tetris-blocks of stone that haven't learned how to be a mountain yet. The stone hisses.

And then the air changes.

At about four thousand meters, where the air should go thin and cold and bright, it goes warm and thick and dark.

You walk into the water.

There is no surface. One step you are in cold wind on sharp new stone. Next step your foot goes down and doesn't come down, it goes *in* - into warm, heavy, hanging water that does not fall. The cloud has gotten too dense to be called cloud anymore. It has become place.

They call it the Summit Sea.

It hangs in a bowl of its own making, a sea perched on top of the world. A standing lens of atmospheric water three hundred meters deep and a whole valley wide, held aloft by heat. Below, the new rock is still hot from its making and breathes up a constant warm draft. Above, the high cold presses down. Between them the water condenses and stays, too warm to snow, too dense to rain, too dim for sky.

So the high town is submerged without ever having been flooded.

The light here is evening all day. The sun is a rumor far above, seen as a paler green in the deep of the hanging sea. Everything is lit from all sides at once, diffuse and close. At noon you can see maybe a hundred meters through the warm murk before everything goes blue-black.

The town is built in the water.

Not on the rock - *in* it. Because the rock is still coming. Every few months a new shoulder of basalt sighs up from below, splitting older houses, lifting them. No one builds foundations. That would be foolish when the ground is still learning its shape.

Instead, they weave.

First comes the kelp. Not true kelp - black-stemmed, warm-water, air-filled. It roots wherever the new rock is barely cool enough to touch. It grows fast, fifty meters a season, its bladders as big as houses. Lush doesn't cover it. In a year a raw scarp goes from black glass to a dripping, hanging forest. The fronds don't float up, they hang down, heavy with warm water. Leaves like wet blankets, like lungs.

The people build in the interstices. Platforms are lashed between stems with cord made from the same kelp, seasoned until it is tough as sinew. Terraces are carved into rock while it is still soft enough to carve with shell and fire-cracked stone. Later the rock will go hard as iron and they will abandon that terrace to the forest and carve a new one. Houses are frames of raw basalt and living root, walls are woven bladder-skin that holds a pocket of slightly thinner water inside - almost-air.

You can breathe here. After a fashion. This is the strange part.

If you thrash, you drown. If you are afraid, you drown. If you come up from below with lungs full of lowland air and try to keep it, you drown. The water is warm as blood, 32 or 33 degrees, and if you let it in slow, if you have lived here since childhood with your chest stretched by your mother, your throat learns to take from it. Not well. Not for long at great effort. Old people sit perfectly still in their kelp nests and filter-breathe for an hour at a time. Young people dart and swim and must duck into the air-houses every few minutes to cough and laugh and take a real breath from the trapped bubbles they tend like gardens.

So the whole town is a slow, dense, vertical tangle. You don't walk; you haul yourself along ropes from terrace to net to living branch to half-carved basalt niche. Everything touches everything else. A house is a boat is a pantry is a cousin's house. Stone knives and basalt hammers and polished shell scrapers hang on every cord within reach, because you need them constantly to cut back growth, to harvest bladder fruit, to shave the rock before it hardens. Primitive, not because they forgot more, but because nothing else lasts. Metal rusts to nothing in a week here. Fire is impossible except in the little oil-pockets where they coax fermented kelp fat to burn with a smothered blue light.

It is quiet. That is the first thing that undoes you, after the breathing.

The water eats sound. Your heartbeat becomes loud. You can hear the mountain itself: low ticks as new rock cools and contracts, sudden deep *tok*s when a pillow bursts far below. Once in a long while, a great sigh as a whole new sheet of stone lifts the town a finger's breadth higher and all the ropes sing. Mostly there is just the slow swish of fronds, the rustle of people pulling themselves hand over hand, the soft clack of wooden prayer-bells. Every house has one, to find your way back in the dim. Different shells cut to different tones. The whole Summit Sea chimes faintly, arrhythmically, never quite in silence.

Animals are rare. Too new, too high, too strange for most things. There are pale things like transparent shrimp that live in the kelp bladders. There are the blind white fish, fat and slow, that the children herd. One kind of beetle that sealed its wing-cases and learned to swim. That's almost all. No birds. Birds cannot fly here. The air is too thick; their feathers waterlog. So over the whole dank, warm, hanging city, there is no bird-call, no insect whine. Just warm water and dim light and the patience of green things growing over raw world.

If you stay long enough, you stop looking up for sky. You start to understand the rule of the place: it is not a city under water. It is a city in the time before water and air decided to be different. The sea came first and the mountain is still being born inside it, and the people have learned to live in the interval - weaving houses out of forest, carving stairs out of yesterday's lava, holding their breath not as an emergency, but as a way of being at home.
