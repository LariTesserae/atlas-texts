It is not a valley. It is not a peak. It is a cup left on top of the world.

You climb for a long time to get here and the air should get cold, but it doesn't. That is the first wrongness. The mountain is high — high enough that the forests below are a green sea-haze, high enough that the light gets hard and close and noon is white overhead. You are above most birds. But the air at the top is hot. Not thin-and-hot, not desert high-altitude hot. Close, bathhouse hot, skin-prickle hot. The rock itself radiates it.

The top of the mountain is flat where it should not be flat. An old eruption, a bubble that rose and popped and never collapsed. A bowl maybe eighty meters across, rimmed with a low, sharp lip of fresh basalt. Young rock. You can tell because nothing has had time to be kind to it. The edges are still knife-clean, the inner walls still show the tear-lines where the stone cooled too fast. No scree slopes. No softening. It looks like it happened last year, though lichen has already started its slow argument.

And the bowl weeps.

This high there should be no water. Instead the bowl is the wettest place you've been in weeks. The air hits the hot rim and turns. You can watch it happen — late morning, the valley clouds rising, pulled up the black flanks of the mountain like breath, and when they crest the rim they condense all at once without ever becoming rain. They become wall.

Water forms in the air itself, on a lattice you can't see. It beads on nothing and then slides down nothing, gathering, becoming rivulets that run down invisible inclines, joining, falling. The whole crater pours, though there is nothing to pour from. It is a marsh-edge without a marsh. A perpetual post-rain.

The tech is the bowl, or it lives in the bowl. It isn't a machine in the way a truck is a machine. It's a field, a lattice that was grown or convinced into being. If you put your hand out over the center you feel the heat gradient step — hot, then hotter, then a seam of cool so abrupt it blisters and chills in the same instant. Dust motes in your palm sort themselves by size. Water avoids your fingers with intention. There is something here doing work that physics still considers theoretical — pumping heat uphill, wringing water out of equatorial high air, dumping entropy somewhere else. A climate siphon. A fin.

It is still active. That's the sound, or rather the lack of sound.

There is no wind inside the rim. Outside it howls. Inside, the condensing is utterly quiet except for a low, bone-conducted hum you hear through your molars when you clench your teeth. *Faen-faen-faen.* Water ticks on rock. That is almost everything. 

The ground of the bowl is not ground. It's a shallow pan of that same young, pitted basalt, and over it is a skin of water never more than ankle deep, warm as blood, constantly being remade. It doesn't pool where it should. It sheets away from low spots and climbs in silver films up tiny basalt ridges, because the field lines want it there.

And where the water climbs, life follows.

Vegetation here is not a carpet. It's sparse the way cathedrals are sparse — a few enormous presences with a lot of considered emptiness between them. In the warm sheet-water, clumps of something that is not quite fern, not quite bromeliad, grow three meters tall from a single root. Each one is its own island. Their leaves are waxy, black-green, scooped to catch the air-water. From their bases drip curtains of aerial roots that never touch soil — they end an inch above the water, drinking from the humidity. They are too lush to be up here. They look tropical, lowland, dragged up and allowed to go wild. Between them is just hot stone and clear hot water and light.

Light inside the bowl is normal in a way that feels strange after the climb. Not heavenly, not glaring. Just day. But the water on the rock throws it back twice, so the undersides of the leaves are lit as brightly as the tops. The whole cup glows from within like a lantern.

Built things: there are not many. Whoever set the siphon to work did not need shelter. Near the eastern rim, half-swallowed by a fern-island, there is a stair — three steps cut into the basalt that lead up into air and stop. Not broken off. Finished. As if the idea of a fourth step was decided against. By the north edge, a pylon leans. It was ceramic once, now its surface has rewritten itself into something like mother-of-pearl that shows heat as color. It does nothing you can name, but dragonflies — one of the few animals — will always land on it, and only on it.

The fauna is rare on purpose, you feel. This place is too hot, too exclusive. You see one thing at a time and then nothing for a long while. A white hawk cutting the outer wind but never entering. Those dragonflies, each big as your hand, with wings that do not beat so much as flex the air. A frog the color of wet rock that lives in the throat of one of the fern-trees and sings once, at exactly the same hour, a single note that makes the water ripple outward from where there is no impact. No predators. No prey. Just visitors who found the room and stayed.

Everything is moving but slowly, actively, like a system with long patience. The water condenses, runs its impossible routes, evaporates again off the hot stone, is caught again before it can leave the rim. The fern-islands grow a millimeter a day — you can hear it if you kneel very close, a tiny creak of new tissue unrolling. The lattice itself shifts its geometry every few hours, and when it does the waterfalls in midair change angle, and the hot-cold seam moves a foot to the left, and steam puffs up in a ring and vanishes.

If you stand in the exact center — ankle deep in water that is coming from all sides and going to all sides — and you stay very quiet, you can feel the true rule of this place. It is not a garden and not a ruin. It is a lung, left on, breathing for a world that no longer needs this much cooling. It was built small because only a small correction was needed. A hamlet-size thermostat. The builders left no walls, no signs, just that three-step stair as a courtesy, or a joke.

It will keep weeping here at the top of the hot mountain long after the basalt has dulled and softened, long after the fern-islands have gone to seed and tried to colonize the air itself.
