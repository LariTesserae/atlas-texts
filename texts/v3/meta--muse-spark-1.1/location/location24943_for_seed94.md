It is high.

Not high like a peak you climb to. High like a table that was lifted. A continent of tableland, tilted just enough that the weather coming off the world below has to climb for days to get here, and by the time it arrives it is tired and cold and has to set down everything it was carrying.

The elevation is so vast the air thins and then forgets to thin anymore. The light here is ordinary daylight — no filter, no second sun, no aurora — flat white cool light that makes colors honest. At noon the shadows are short and blue. At distance the light stacks, range after range going paler until the farthest ranges are the color of the sky itself, so you can't tell where the land ends.

This place is old in the way a riverbed is old. Nothing is sharp. Everything has found the shape it wants to keep.

It is called a moor because there is no better word, but a moor is supposed to be open. This is not open. This is packed.

From the lip of the plateau to every horizon, there is cover. Not trees — trees tried here and lost to wind and cold. What won instead is a low, incomprehensibly dense mat: cushion mosses the size of houses, liverworts with leaves like dinner plates, black-green bog myrtle and dwarf willow and wool-grass and a kind of heather that grows into itself, stem through stem, until you cannot put a hand between plants without pushing through five layers of living tissue. It is lush, but horizontal lush. A clipped, compressed jungle three feet tall and a mile deep if you count the roots.

And it is wet. Not with lakes. With nearness. The water table is kissing the underside of everything. Every footfall — if you could find ground to step on — would wring air from the mat with a sigh and bring up dark water that beads and vanishes. The rocks weep. The plateau is made of an old, soft basalt that has weathered into terraces and bowls, each bowl holding its own bog, each lip seeping. In the cool air, 4 or 6 degrees above freezing at midday, your breath condenses and doesn't leave. The atmosphere itself condenses; in the early afternoon, low clouds form directly out of nothing ten feet above the moss and drift sideways through it, raining where there is already water.

This is where the industry is, because there is industry, though nothing was ever built.

At first you think the ground is hissing. Then you realize the plateau is engine-turned.

The entire tableland is geothermal and hydrostatic in a way that has organized itself. Water percolates down through the peat, which in places is forty feet deep and compressed until it is almost coal, is heated by the blood-warm rock below, and comes back up somewhere else under pressure. It comes up through natural throats — smooth-walled chimneys of basalt polished by a million years of this same cycle. When it comes up it carries dissolved iron and sulfur and silica, and where it surfaces it builds.

Some throats breathe. Slowly. A column of white steam lifts twenty feet, condenses instantly into that ground-level cloud, and collapses with a soft exhale. A minute later it does it again. Field after field of them, each on its own interval, all out of sync, like a continent of lungs sleeping.

Some pump. The water comes up cold first, then hot, then a plug of mud, then water again — chuff, chuff, chuff — and with each chuff the moss around the throat compresses and rebounds. Peat-brown water spills and is soaked immediately. Around those throats the minerals have precipitated into low, round factory buildings without walls: terraces of rust-orange and yellow-white travertine, vats and settling pools tiered down the hillside, each one full of a different chemistry, each one hot enough to steam but not hot enough to kill the algae that colors it teal and blood-red.

Nothing made them. Water and time made them, and kept making them after they worked.

And they *work*. The pools refine. The pigments sort themselves by weight. Iron bacteria in sheets like rusted chainmail harvest the dissolved metal and rain down flakes that the mosses collect. The bog itself is a slow industrial press: new moss grows on top, old moss is pressed below into peat, into lignite, into jet, giving off marsh gas that pops quietly in small blue flames that never go out, wandering a few inches above the mat like lost workers.

It is all occupied. There is no empty surface. If a vent dies, in a season it is overgrown and its terrace is colonized by a different consortium of liverworts that specialize in dead-vent mineral. If a peat-pool scours open in spring melt, the water beetles — black, abundant, slick as oil drops — are in it before it stills. The midges rise in such numbers that their swarms have weight and weather. The birds that eat them — small, fat, streaked gray-brown the exact color of dead wool-grass — flicker through the mist in clouds too numerous to count, never singing. That is the fauna here: not large, not individual. Potatoes of voles that tunnel through the moss mat rather than under it, leaving soft raised highways; long, pale amphibians that live their entire lives in the water film between moss and rock, never seeing sky; and everywhere, mites and springtails in such density that the moss itself seems to move.

The sound is wrong for what this is. An industrial continent at work should roar. This one is quiet because the moss eats all high frequency, and because everything is wet, and because distance is so vast that sound has nowhere to go. What you hear, if you lie down on the springy mat and put your ear close, is a low, complex, almost subsonic activity: the chuff of water throats, the tick of cooling silica, the pop of methane flame, the soft hydraulic sigh when a cushion compresses under its own weight and releases water, the billion-legged hush of insects. No echo. The plateau is too soft to return one.

There are weathers nested inside the weather. A kilometer away it is raining. Here, it is clearing, and the low self-made cloud is burning off, and you can see for a moment the true scale — that this same wet packing, this same cold breathing factory-bog, goes on for days' walk in every direction, terrace after terrace, until curvature takes it. And over there the light catches a hillside of travertine vats and makes it look, briefly, like a city of mills.

It isn't. Nothing was ever built. It built itself, and it settled into its work a very long time ago, and it will keep working long after the idea of building is forgotten.
