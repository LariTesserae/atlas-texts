It is entirely underwater, and it has been underwater long enough that drowning is no longer the point.

You drop into it from above — not falling, sinking. The water column above is thick and warm, bathwater warm, something like 29 degrees, and it holds the light out. The sun is a rumor filtered to a green-gray diffusion about a hundred meters up. By the time it reaches here, it’s just a tinge, a dimness that is less light than a suggestion of direction. Upper is slightly less dark than lower.

This place is a bowl.

Not a trench, not an open ocean plain. A bowl cut into rock, maybe thirty, forty meters across at its widest — hamlet-sized, room-sized if your idea of a room is a cathedral. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it: the lip of basalt and old concrete where the water suddenly gets darker, cleaner, emptier. Inside the lip, everything is packed.

Below surface in both senses: under the sea, and under the ground. A flooded quarry, a caldera, a cofferdam that never got de-watered. The sides are stepped, terraced, eroded not into chaos but into a settled form. The concrete has gone soft at the corners. Rebar shows like bone at the weathering points, but it too has been wrapped over. Everything has found its equilibrium shape.

And it is full. Fully, impossibly occupied.

This was a town, or a factory that became a town. Industrial, not post-industrial. Not ruins — infrastructure still in place, still organized. Three and four stories of stacked construction following the curve of the bowl, all facing inward. Narrow alleys of riveted iron plates, gantry railings, catwalks that now hang slack with the weight of growth. Pipe runs as thick as a man, running vertically like organ pipes, some still shivering with low heat. You can feel it more than see it — the water around a particular stack is three degrees warmer and moves in a slow upwelling that stirs the silt.

It never stopped working. Not really.

Somewhere near the bottom, low and central, there is a machine — or a set of machines — that you never see clearly because the center of the bowl is the densest part. Big, slow-turning things: pump wheels, maybe, or turbines running in reverse, or just flywheels left to spin down over decades. They turn once every few minutes. They make no real noise. Water takes sound away differently here; there is a profound, padded quiet. If you put your hand on a railing you feel a faint thrum through the steel, a low *hund* that travels up your bones to your jaw. If you let go, the silence returns.

It is covered, everything is covered.

The water is not empty. It is alive and thick with it — warm-water, low-light lush. Not coral, not quite weed. Black-green kelp straps that have learned to grow on vertical walls, hanging out of windows like laundry. Beards of filter-moss on every edge. Barnacle-like growths but soft, pulpy, that pulse irregularly. And cultivated things gone wild: what must have been algae vats or biofuel curtains, now ripped loose, floating in dense, slow-moving sheets that have rooted themselves to balconies and smokestacks. They make a second forest, a vertical jungle inside the iron town. The water between the leaves is crowded with particulates — spores, shed skins, flecks of rust — so you never have more than five meters of clear sight. The rest is haze.

There is so little animal life it makes the plants feel even stranger. No school of fish streaming through. No whale passing overhead. Maybe once an hour a single, pale thing — a blind cichlid descendant, a shrimp the size of your fist — picks its way across a wall, grazes, disappears. The absence of movement is what makes the density so oppressive. Nothing darts. Nothing flees. Everything grows into everything else and stays.

The rule here is pressure and patience.

If there were people here, they would have had to breathe water. Or they would have had to never need to. The doorways are too narrow for diving gear, the stairwells fully choked with growth so you have to push through like through a hedge. The windows are open, but the rooms behind them are not empty — they are full of water, full of silt, full of more pipes, full of tangled black root-veins that have crept in from outside and made a nest of the apartment. Some rooms still have a chair, or the shape of a workbench, preserved as a hard knot around which the soft things have grown. Nothing is being built. Nothing is being torn down. Erosion has finished its argument. The town has settled into its final shape: a cup, packed to brimming, quietly overgrown, warm as blood, slowly turning.

If you stay long enough and let your own bubbles cease — if you hold very still in the middle of the flooded street where two walls of industrial terraces lean in — you notice the whole little city is breathing. Not metaphorically. The warm updraft from the machine at the bottom lifts the algae curtains by an inch. The cold water from the lip sinks to replace it. The leaves sway. The silt rises and falls. One slow cycle every seven, maybe ten minutes. Inhale, exhale. You are inside a lung, or a boiler, or a womb that forgot to open.

You can see the rim from here. You know you could kick and leave this place and go up into the open, dark blue where nothing is. But for now, the bowl holds you, packed in with all the other things that decided to stay.
