It is the bottom of the world.

Not low like a pit — low like a decision the planet made a long time ago to stop trying here. An ancient shield plain worn down past hills, past valleys, down into a barely-wrinkled skin of stone. Elevation so gradual you only know you're descending because the water gets deeper on your boots. The horizon is too far away for the light. Under a low, thick, unbroken vault of cloud the color of cold iron, the land simply goes on until the dim swallows it.

The rock is not one rock. It's everything that was left after everything else washed away — a basement gneiss, black and pink and sea-green, polished by time until its grains show like fat in meat. There are no sharp edges left. Glaciation, water, wind, life before life came and went — whatever happened here happened for so long that the concept of an edge has been forgotten. Boulders here are not boulders. They are swellings in the floor, domes the size of houses, the size of sheds, the size of a sleeping dog, all smoothed until you could run your palm over them for a mile. Between them: shallow pans, and grooves, and the faintest memory of riverbeds that now do little but hold water because they are slightly lower than everything else.

And there is water everywhere. Not as sea, not as lake — as presence. The water table is so high it breathes. You press your foot and water beads up around the sole from the saturated rock itself. It weeps. The big domes are always wet, always sheeted with a moving film that never quite dries, dripping from their undersides even though it has not rained in hours. Or maybe it rains constantly in a mist too fine to be rain. The air itself condenses on stone. The whole plain condenses. Puddles connect to puddles through braids of clear, cold runnels an inch deep and a foot wide, cutting absolutely no channel because the rock refuses to be cut any deeper. It is a marsh without marsh plants. A fen made of stone and sky-water.

There is no plant life. Not a blade, not a moss, not even lichen as you would know it. The color is all mineral. Black-green where water sheets, rust where there is iron, pale bone where silica has precipitated out like frost. If you look close, the wet stone is slick with something — a bacterial skin, amber and black, not quite alive in the way that allows for leaves. It smells faintly metallic, faintly of iodine when you disturb it.

Which makes what lives here impossible, and then obvious.

They are everywhere because they have the whole water skin to themselves.

They are not birds, not insects. They are pale, low, multiply-jointed things with translucent shells the size of your hand to the size of a serving platter — crabs that are not crabs, woodlice that never learned wood. On the dry crests of the domes they are sparse, one every few yards, cleaning their antennae. Down in the wet pans, they are dense enough to look like moving gravel. Their backs are freckled with condensed water that they drink off each other. They filter. They scrape that bacterial film with mouthparts like horn combs. They strain the water with whips and fans. They are white, and pinkish where the rock stains them, and in the dim they hold a faint internal luminescence, not light so much as a lessening of dark.

There are bigger ones too — things that move like seals made of slate, dragging themselves from pan to pan, leaving broad wet drags that fill immediately with water and with a sudden rush of smaller things picking at the fresh film.

This is why the world is deafening.

Each of the small pale things makes one sound. Either a click, the snapping of a shell-plate against water, or a thin, high chitter like wet glass rubbed. Alone, nothing. Together, in the millions across this valley-broad plain that acts as a perfect acoustic mirror, it is a roar. A constant, shifting, valley-filling roar of clicks and scrapes and chirrs and splashes, bouncing off low cloud and low rock until you cannot tell direction. It is the sound of the rock being eaten alive, one micron at a time. It rises and falls in waves as densities move. Sometimes a whole flat goes silent for a half-second as a larger grazer passes, and the silence is more shocking than the noise.

And there are structures. You see them only because the sound makes you look for order.

Low walls. Not much — one course of flat stones set in a shallow arc to make a pan deeper, a weir to hold water half an inch longer. Pits scratched into the leeward side of domes and lined with smaller pebbles. Cairns, three or four stones high, each stone wet, each stone precisely placed so water drips from its tip into a cupped stone below. Old, old work. Some of the walls have themselves been smoothed by erosion until they look natural, until you realize natural never makes a semicircle.

Nothing lives in them now that doesn't live everywhere else. The builders — if they were something other than the abundant fauna learning to farm its own food-film — are not here. Or they are here and you cannot separate them from the fauna. You find a tool left behind in one of the deeper pans: a split long-bone, or a stone that is not from this shield, its end ground to a flat comb, exactly the width of the feeding combs of the grazer-things, but hafted with sinew. It lies in an inch of cold clear water, and when you pick it up, it drips, and a hundred tiny white creatures pause in their clicking to watch you.

The rules here are simple and old.

Water will always be near. Light will always be dim — a perpetual late afternoon under cloud, too diffuse for shadow. Heat never comes; the air stays cool enough that your breath shows a little, cold enough that the water is always painful after a minute. The stone will never be sharp. Nothing with roots can hold.

The only way to be abundant is to be small, pale, wet, and loud.

And the water keeps weeping out of the rock, and the clicks keep grinding the rock into future water, and the low walls keep holding that water a moment longer than it would stay on its own, as if something once thought that if you could just keep the water still enough, something green might finally dare to grow.
