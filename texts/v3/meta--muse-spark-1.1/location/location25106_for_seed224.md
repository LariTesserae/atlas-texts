There is a place on the high ridge where the wind stops.

Not because the wind dies. The main crest is still up there — you came over it an hour ago, or a day ago, bent double in the thin, cold dark, with the basalt ringing under your boots. Up there the wind never stops. It's a high place, a last place, the kind of altitude where the planet gives up pretending to be habitable and shows you its structural stone, scoured clean, polished, pitted. Old. The rock up there has settled into itself. It isn't being carved anymore; it has been carved. Edges have found the shape they will keep until the range itself goes down.

You saw the cairn because everyone who comes this high builds one or finds one. Two slabs of dark basalt leaning together against the prevailing drift, not quite touching. A windbreak that never worked very well. From the outside it looks like nothing - a triangle of shadow maybe a meter long, knee-high, full of trapped grit and the glassy black sand that collects in all high cold places where there is no water to wash it away.

There is no water here. Not really. The air tested dry before your instruments failed. Your mouth has been dry for so long you have stopped noticing. The little snow that tries to happen here doesn't fall, it forms directly out of the air as a fur of sharp, dry crystals on the leeward sides of stones and then sublimates by morning without ever becoming liquid. No meltwater channel. No lichen. No stain. Just mineral.

You crouched to get out of the wind and that is when you found that the space behind the two slabs is wrong.

From outside, you could span it with your arms. From inside, you can't touch both walls at once.

It is still a small place. This is not a cathedral. It is a pocket, an intimate, hidden room that has been left ajar. About as long as you are tall if you lie down. About as high as your chest when you sit. You have to turn sideways to enter. Once you are in, the wind sound cuts with a softness that is not acoustic. Absolute attenuation. The silence that falls is mineral and total. You can hear the blood in your ears and then, after a minute, you can't even hear that.

The floor is the same basalt, but weathered differently. Outside, the rock is abraded to a dull sheen. In here, it has a faint step-pattern to it, like tide marks, except there has never been a tide. Each step is a millimeter high, perfectly even, running across the floor and up the walls. Established. Here for a long time. Somebody, or something, once laid a hand here and the stone remembered.

And there are things left here. Sparse, scattered, not a camp, not a settlement. A presence that did not need much.

In the back corner, where the two slabs finally do meet, there is a nest of what you first take to be wire. It is not wire. It is black filament, thinner than hair, thicker than spider silk, and it does not lie on the rock so much as it holds itself a fraction above it, under its own tension. It meets at a point in the middle of the air, about where your knee would be if you sat. At that point, nothing. Or, not nothing — a point that refuses. Light falls into it and does not come out right. The dim starlight that leaks through the entrance, and the faint airglow from the high thin sky outside, bends around it. Your eyes want to slide off it.

It is cold. Not the pleasant cold of a winter night. The deep, still cold that lives in places far from any ocean, where heat has had a long time to radiate away. You are breathing through your hood and your exhalation does not fog. There is no water in it to fog. Whatever moisture you still have freezes instantly to the inside of your mask as a white rime.

There are two other artifacts.

One: a marker, knee-high, of a material that looks like smoked glass but is warm to the touch even through your glove. It is set upright, half-sunk into the floor. No writing. Its top face is angled to point at the filament-knot, and along its angled face is a slow, patient motion: grains of light, or dust motes, or stars, rising from one end to the other, taking hours to traverse a few centimeters. You watch one travel. You think about your watch. Your watch stopped at the entrance.

Two: three small stones, stacked. Not part of the natural debris. Deliberately placed. They are not local basalt. One is a perfect cube of something matte white that takes no shadow. One is a sphere of iron that should have rusted and hasn't. One is a tetrahedron that is somehow both transparent and opaque. Scattered is the wrong word; placed is too intentional. Left.

This is the wrongness, the 2.9 out of 3 of it:

Up is not stable in here.

If you sit still long enough — and you have to sit still long enough because the cold and the silence make you still — you notice your sense of balance quietly detaching from the rock. Up drifts. First a degree or two, as if the pocket were slowly rotating. Then it stops, then it over-corrects. Not enough to make you fall. Enough to make you feel that weight is a negotiation here, not a law. The little stone cairn sometimes weighs slightly more on your retina than it should, as if it is leaning into a gravity that lives under the floor.

Second: the entrance. From inside, it is narrower than your shoulders. When you look out through it, you do not see the ridge you came from. You see another night. Lower, dimmer. A flat plain of the same black sand under a sky that has too many fixed stars, not moving. No ridge. It has been that way since you came in. You are not worried yet. The slow dynamic of this place does not admit panic quickly. Things change here on the order of centuries. The filament knot has been contracting, you think, by perhaps the width of an atom since you arrived. The light-grains on the marker are still climbing.

The rules of this place, as you piece them together in the silence:

1. Nothing lives. Not here. No plant, no animal, no bacteria brave enough. The ecology is mineral and remembered. The only biology was you and you are a visitor who has already left too much water in the form of frost on your mask.

2. Nothing is lost. The sand does not leave. The cold preserves. The stone remembers every touch.

3. Small spaces can be large if you fold them properly. This pocket is a demonstration. A working. Someone needed an intimate room at high altitude, where no one would look, where erosion had already finished its work, where the planet is thin and quiet. They built — or grew, or persuaded — this fold to hold a single, difficult thing still.

The filament knot is that thing. It is not a machine as you understand machines. It is a place where physics is being held pinched, like a nerve. The heatless warmth of the marker is waste heat from the holding. The slow drift of up is leakage.

And while you watch, with your gloves getting stiff and your knees aching from stillness, the knot does something incredibly slow. It rotates, not in space but in some other way, and for a fraction of a second you see through it. Not to outside.

Through it to a room exactly like this one, but inverted, where another cairn of two slabs leans on a different high ridge, under a different dim light, and for that instant you understand scale — this is one of thousands, scattered across high cold places where the world is thin, each holding a point apart from the world, a lattice of held-open nothing that keeps something much larger from —

— and then it is a knot again.

The wind outside your non-entrance makes no sound. The cold deepens by a fraction.

You can stay here a long time. In here, a long time might not mean what you think it means outside. But the pocket was not made for staying.

It was made for leaving something alone.
