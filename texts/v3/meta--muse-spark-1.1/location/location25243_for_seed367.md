It doesn't look like anything from a distance.

You are up high. The country has been up high for so long it forgot how to be mountain. This is not a peak. This is what is left after peaks. A tableland so old the wind has sanded it down to almost nothing — a vast, warm, gently wavering plain of desert pavement and rusted sandstone, covered in a thin glaze of dark varnish where the sun and the bacteria have agreed to finish it. There are no ridges. There are no valleys. For a day's walk in any direction the ground barely changes: iron-hard pan, scattered pebbles that click together like teeth, low hummocks of salt where some ancient inland sea sweated out its last.

The sky is huge and white with heat.

And then the ground stops being ground. Without warning, under your feet, a lip.

A pocket. That is the only true scale here. 

From the plain you could step over it and miss it. A mouth in the mesa-top, maybe thirty meters long and half as wide, the edges undercut and softened by time. A solution pit, a wind pit, a bubble where softer stone gave out a hundred million years ago. The walls drop eight, ten meters, sheer in places, sloped and stair-stepped in others where slabs have fallen and learned to be stairs. At noon the sun makes it straight down for about twenty minutes. The rest of the day it is in shadow. Dim, cool-shadowed, not cold but held. That is why you feel the temperature change in your chest before you see anything else.

Inside, it is full.

At first what you see is green. That should not be possible. This is dry country. There has been no rain you can remember. The air's humidity wouldn't wet a cloth. The regional maps mark this as waterless. But down in the pit the rules bend just slightly, the way old places allow.

There is no standing water. No spring, no seep, no pool. The floor is dust and shattered flagstone and salt-fine silt. The vegetation makes its own water out of almost nothing.

It is lush in the way a desert is lush when you get on your knees. Dense, layered, near-touching. Thickets of bottle-trunk acacia and iron shrub that have swollen their bases into vast water casks, bark like elephant skin, leaves reduced to waxy needles that shine. Clumps of black thorn that grow outward then inward, making cages. And everywhere the real cover: the air-plants. Gray-green beards, rosette velvets, flat paddle cacti stacked like hands, all covered in fine hairs that comb the night air. At dusk, when the high plateau cools fast, a cold fog tries to form and fails — it becomes a low mist that sinks, heavy, and pours into this one hole in the ground. The beards drink it. They drip it to each other. They exhale oil that smells like turpentine and creosote. By morning they are plump.

The people who live here call it catching, not growing.

And people do live here. The walls are a town.

It isn't large in kilometers. You can see every house from the lip. That is the intimacy of it. A pocket-scale urbanism. But within those thirty meters, it is vertical and crowded and always being mended. Ancient erosion gave them shelves and ledges, and they have made the rest. Lower third: cut-stone footings from the fallen slabs, mud bricks made from the pan dust mixed with crushed leaf and dung, plastered until they look like the wall itself. Middle third: wattle of thorn branches, tied with cactus fiber, thatched with the gray beards dried into thatch that still tries faintly to live. Upper third: nests, not houses — shallow caves enlarged with hammerstones, mouths hung with woven screens.

Ladders everywhere. Not sophisticated — poles with notches, rope ladders, the natural stair steps worn deeper by bare feet. Tiny granaries like beehives tucked into joints. Thorn corrals. On the north wall, where sun lingers longest, a lattice of drying racks heavy with sliced paddles, with pods, with strips of lichen-meat. No metal. You see a few pieces of beaten copper brought from far away, but mostly chert flakes, bone awls, wooden mauls black with hand grease. Primitive work that is good at its job because it has had thousands of years to get good in this one room.

What makes it urban, not just a camp, is the density. Smoke from three hearths never clears; it hangs in the dim, gold in the afternoon slit of sun. Paths are worn shin-deep into stone. Every flat surface has a purpose. The bottom of the pit is not empty — it is a commons, a threshing floor, a meeting place, a garden of the most pampered air-beard mothers, set on stone pedestals like sculptures and watered by hand from jars that collect dew. The jars themselves are the technology: wide-mouthed, shallow, black ceramic set out all over the plain on the mesa at night, carried down at dawn beaded and heavy.

You do not hear wind up on the pan. Down here you hear everything.

That is the second thing after the green: the sound. Loud, but not in any human way.

The pit is overwhelmingly alive. The dim and the oil-smell and the constant mild warmth have made an insectarium. The air itself seems to shiver. Fat black bees the size of your thumb joint that do not sting, that roll in the beard-flowers. Clouds of tiny white moths that live a single day and spend it all in that one shaft of light. Clicking beetles that carry salt grains twice their size. Geckos covering every wall so thickly that at first you think the rock is spotted — then a hundred throats inflate at once. Swifts that nest in the stone houses and scream when they fold their wings; they pour out at dusk in a corkscrew, eat the day's moths, pour back in.

And the people in it. Children shouting in a pit have no place for the sound to go, so it stays and folds over itself. Women pounding pods, men calling up a ladder to take the weight, someone scraping a hide, someone coughing, all of it layered close because there is nowhere else for it to happen.

It is an ecosystem in fast motion. No water to buffer anything. A day here is a year. Before breakfast the hunters have already gone out — not for game in the pit, which is taboo to kill except the mice — but for salt lizards on the pan above, returning with them slung over their shoulders. The bee-hives swell, get robbed, swell again. The air-plants put out a new beard that is immediately colonized with scale that is immediately eaten by ladybirds that are immediately eaten by geckos that shed their skins that go into the compost that goes to the mother plants. Predator and prey and scavenger are within arm's reach of each other all day.

The rules of the world here are simple and unwritten because the walls write them.

1. Never damage the catchers on the rim.
2. No fire larger than your two hands.
3. Nothing that lives in the pit leaves the pit alive except people, and nothing dead leaves at all if it can rot usefully.

It has worked for a very, very long time. The original pit was smooth. Now its ledges are scalloped, its walls polished at hand-height. Pot sherds in the floor dust go down meters. The acacia trunks are as thick as houses because no one will cut a mature one — it is the cistern.

At night, when the plain above goes cold and hostile and the stars are too bright and dry to be beautiful, the pocket holds its warmth. The smell of warm dust and green oil rises out of it like breath. From the lip you can stand with your feet on a million years of flatness and look down into a dim, dense, buzzing room where a whole town and a whole jungle have agreed to live inside the same stone bowl because nowhere else on this ancient, finished tableland would let them.
