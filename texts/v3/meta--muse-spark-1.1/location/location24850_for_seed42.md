It is underwater, entirely.

Not damp, not flooded — drowned. A small, high tarn cradled in a mountain's palm, maybe forty meters across, maybe less. Its rim is the world-boundary. The crater is old stone, basalt and granite gone soft at the edges, lichenless inside the waterline, worn down to a smooth, dark bowl. Established. It has been holding this water for a very long time. The water itself is cold and thin and very clear, high-mountain cold, the kind that holds a lot of light and very little heat. Sun falls straight down through it at midday without shattering. Normal light, but filtered to a cool, even green.

And inside the bowl, there is no empty water. There is forest.

Not a forest *in* water. The water *is* forest. From crater wall to crater wall, from the silt bottom up to the wind-rippled surface, it is all plant. Long ribbons of alpine water-weed and cold kelp, feather-milfoil, hornwort gone massive, quillworts thick as a man's thigh. They rooted generations ago and then kept going, because there was nowhere else to go in such a small place. They became substrate, scaffold, architecture. They braided each other. They grafted when they touched, bark-to-bark underwater, and made living joints.

That is the town.

It is sparse, for a town. That is what makes it a town and not a thicket. The builders — you never see them working fast enough to catch them at it — have thinned it with primitive care: stone knives and worked bone and teeth, patient hands. They have cut channels between the great vertical trunks so you can move. They have woven the living fronds into floors and lofts, into dens and thresholds. On a human scale it is hamlet-scale, cathedral-scale. A few dozen larger structures, spaced with reverent emptiness between them. So much cold water-light between presences. You can see all the way across town in one glance if the fish are not in the way.

Each house is a single plant, encouraged over decades to become a house. A bulb-rush that was coaxed to swell and hollow before it hardened, its top bent over and tied down until it grew into a dome. A column of kelp whose stipe split and was trained into an arch, then thickened and stayed. The walls are alive and still growing, slow, slow — dynamic 0.8 slow. If you sit and watch a doorway for an hour you will not see it move. If you come back next year, it will be lower, tighter. The floors are mats of interwoven root that never quite stop feeling like root but hold.

There is no metal here. No nails. Everything is lashing, graft, weight, and growth. Rope made from stripped weed fibers, green and slick, holding a living beam in place until the beam decides to hold itself. Steps carved into living trunks with a chipped stone that was itself brought down from the rim, above water, on a rare ascent. The technology is all hand and time.

And it is loud.

For such a sparse, slow, cold place, it is astonishingly loud. The water carries everything. The great hollow trunks act as organ pipes as the very slight circulation of the tarn — snowmelt in at one lip, seepage out at another — breathes through them. A low, constant hoot in the larger houses, a fluting in the smaller. The fronds slap and sigh against each other even when there is no current, because the fish hit them.

There is abundant fauna, more animal than plant mass would suggest could be supported, and all of it is here in town. Because town is the only structure. Finger-long white alpine char whose scales are almost translucent, hanging in schools in the open avenues of water between houses, turning as one. Fat, black, slow newts that have claimed doorframes as territory and sit there all day opening and closing their throats. Freshwater shrimp in clouds so thick they look like dust until they click — thousands of them picking at the houses, cleaning them, and clicking all the while, an endless static. Snails with shells the size of fists that carry gardens of filamentous algae on their backs, scraping paths along the woven walls. And frogs. Inexplicably, impossibly, frogs at this elevation, living permanently below the surface, their calls distorted but huge, low bongs that resonate in your chest and in the wooden walls and carry up through the surface and can be heard on the scree above as a mountain humming.

Light at noon is direct, green-gold, full of particles. At morning and evening it comes slanted through the crater rim and through the weed and makes long colonnades in the water. The surface, seen from below, is a bright, trembling, silver skin. The rules here are simple:

1. Everything that wants to stay must be buoyant or tied.
2. Everything that is tied eventually becomes part of what ties it.
3. Nothing rots fast. The cold holds it. Fallen lashings, shed skins, dead shrimp — they drift slowly and are taken apart filament by filament by shrimp and bacteria. The town digests itself slowly and re-grows.
4. If you want air, you must go up into the lung-rooms. Some of the older rush-houses have learned to photosynthesize so aggressively in the summer light that they exhale oxygen into their hollow crowns faster than it dissolves. It collects there under the dome in a trembling silver bell. The frogs use them. The newts use them. If a human diver came down and stayed long enough and was trusted, they could put their head in one and breathe cool, plant-bitter air that smells of green and cold iron.

But nobody builds boats here anymore. The town does fine, high up and submerged and almost still, its streets of water between living columns, clicking and hooting and croaking louder than any mountain lake has any right to be.
