This is a city under a lid.

Not deep-under — not pressured, black, mantle-deep. Just _below_ surface, a half-step down. The roof of the world is still there, sixty or a hundred meters above you, but it is broken. It is a limestone country that has been eaten from underneath until it gave up and sagged, and then in one long, old event, collapsed in a chain. What you stand in is not a cave. It's a trench scar, a graben valley, twenty kilometers long and two wide, open to the sky through a wandering rip. The sky is a bright, thin river of white far overhead. The light comes down in columns and hits the pale floor and bounces.

That is why it is bright.

Everything below is pale stone that has become a lightwell. The walls are ancient in the true sense — not ruin-ancient, but geologically tired. Erosion 2.6. This place has finished eroding. There is nothing sharp left. The boulders that fell when the roof fell a million years ago have been smoothed into domes, into whale-backs. The original river that cut it, long gone, left its meander as a broad, flat, silt-pan floor. The cliffs that remain are not cliffs, but slopes of flowstone and collapsed benches, rounded like gums over old teeth. You could walk up most of them if your knees allowed. The place has settled into its final form.

It is cool. Not cold enough to ache, cool enough that breath makes a faint ghost if you stand still. The air sinks down here and stays. The sun warms the surface desert above — because above is desert — but its heat doesn't make it down in full. This air has weight.

And it is damp, but not wet. Water: 0.8. There is no standing lake. There is no rain down here, except when rain up top finds one of the thousand throat-holes in the roof and comes down as a brief, local waterfall that lasts an hour and then is gone. The damp is from condensation. The cool stone sweats. Every morning, and again at dusk, the walls weep. You see it as a sheen. You feel it under your boots on the stone paths — not slippery, just darkened. In the low places of the floor, where the ancient riverbed dimpled, water collects in shallow pans, clear as glass, a few centimeters deep, fringed with white mineral. Salt and calcite. The whole trench tinkles with slow drips that you have to strain to hear.

Nothing green grows. Vegetation 0.3. Zero. The light is bright but the soil never formed; the floor is ground limestone, salt bloom, gypsum dust, and water that is too alkaline. Even lichen doesn't hold. What grows instead is mineral. Needles of aragonite in corners where air doesn't move. Frothy crusts of white. Sheets of flowstone that look like frozen cloth. And in the dampest pans, a skin — not plant, not quite fungus — a brown-black bacterial mat that smells faintly of iron. The locals scrape it and eat it when the cave shrimp are scarce.

Because fauna is abundant. 1.5.

That's the surprise. In a place so mineral and empty, it crawls.

They are all pale, almost translucent, and they are all quiet. That's the rule. Sound here is 0.2, a pressed cotton silence. The stone absorbs it. The air is too still to carry. So evolution went silent.

Across the bright pans, in the inch-deep water, there are herds — literally herds — of blind isopods the size of your hand, moving like a slow tide, grazing the bacterial film. In the deeper pockets, where water collects up to your knee, fish with no eyes and long whiskers turn in endless, unhurried circles. On the walls, crabs the color of milk climb the weeping surfaces, their legs too long, harvesting condensation with hairy mouthparts. And above, on the ceiling, things you at first think are stalactites detach and glide, silently, from one side of the trench to the other — flat, manta-like gliders, papery, catching thermals from the cool sink. They make no cry. At night, beetles with cold blue lanterns on their backs come out by the thousands, but they have no stridulation. The whole teeming moves like it's underwater.

The city is in the middle of all that, and it is sparse and turbulent at once, which should not be possible.

Built: 2.2. This is urban. It was not built to be impressive; it was built because this is where the water is and where the sky can't burn you. But it is a city. The scale is valley-sized. You can see from one end to the other only as a haze of stone and distance.

The density is 0.7, though. The buildings are far apart. There is almost a reverence to the emptiness between them. Each structure is a low, thick-walled drum made from the whale-back boulders themselves — hollowed out, added to with sun-dried blocks cut from the floor, then smoothed by generations of hands until they too look like natural outgrowths of the ancient erosion. No mortar. No metal. Tech is 0.7: primitive in the honest sense. Hand mauls of basalt dragged from above. Antler picks. Ropes of braided isopod gut. Copper is known but rare — you see a few green-stained nails, a few knives. Everything is manual, heavy, patient.

Between the drums runs a worn grid of paths, polished by feet to a white gleam. And along those paths, the turbulence.

Dynamic: 2.8. The city is in constant motion, but quietly. Nobody shouts. A market has no callers. People run. Children dart between the drums with armfuls of white crabs still kicking. Old women carry pans of water on their heads at a quick trot, water sloshing but never spilling, moving from a fresh seep to a house that needs it before the sun evaporates it. Men haul blocks on sledges. You will see a house being taken down on one edge of town while on the other edge a new drum is being raised around a good seep, stone by stone. The whole town migrates a few meters every year following the weeping.

It's chaotic, anthill-busy, bazaar-busy, but it makes almost no sound. Feet on soft dust. The click of crab legs in a basket. A low hush of breathing. The occasional low tap of stone on stone, muffled as if heard through water. Even arguments happen in sharp, quick gestures, not yells. Sound is expensive here; it galls the gliders. When something loud does happen — a sledge cracking, a child falling — the whole market flinches and looks up, because the gliders start dropping.

The weirdness, low but present 1.1, is this: the light never moves quite correctly. Because it is not direct sun, but sun reflected from the white desert above and then reflected again from the pale floor, there is no single shadow. Each person has three or four faint shadows, overlapping, pointing in slightly wrong directions. At noon, when the rip of sky is straight overhead, the entire trench fills with shadowless, sourceless brightness, like being inside an eye. The crabs freeze. The humans stop and stand with faces up, eyes closed, letting the cold bright soak into their skin for ten minutes. It is the only time the city is still.

Then a drip falls somewhere behind you, loud as a bell in the hush, and the running starts again, silent, frantic, purposeful, all over that old, smooth, worn-down, water-sweating bowl of a city under the broken roof of the world.
