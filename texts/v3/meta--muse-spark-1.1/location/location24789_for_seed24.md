It is a low place.

Not low in the way a valley is low, between higher things — low in the way a shallow bowl is low, a sag pressed into the world, hamlet-sized and self-contained. You could walk its rim in ten minutes. You could stand on the rim and miss it entirely in the dark, if you didn't know to look for the change in air.

The air is what tells you. Up on the flat, the night is cooler. Down in, it is warm. A dry, held warmth, not hot enough to burn, but enough to be constant, like the inside of a bread oven hours after the fire has gone out. The ground itself holds it. During the day this salt pan bakes; at night it gives back. And here, in this hollow, the heat pools and stays. There is no wind to take it away.

The light is almost none. The sky is overcast high and thick with dust that never quite settles, never quite moves — starless, moonless, a lid. What light there is comes from the sky itself, a very faint, sourceless dark-gray, like the last light remembered by stone. You can see the shapes of things close to your hands, the pale edge of salt against basalt. You cannot see your boots clearly unless you wait. Your eyes never truly adjust because there is so little to adjust to.

The ground slopes inward in a long, soft, established curve. This is not a fresh collapse. The sides are weathered to a comfortable angle, sanded by a thousand years of the same dry air. At the top of the rim, the crust is salt and caliche, white and blistered, cracking into irregular polygons. As you go down, that gives way to fine, dead sand — tan, then umber — packed tight as old flour. Lower still, the sand is mixed with smooth, dark pebbles of basalt that have worked themselves out of the earth and have nowhere to go. They sit half-buried, each with its own little wind-tail of fine dust behind it, all pointing the same direction even though no wind blows now, and hasn't for a long time.

In the very bottom, the flat of the bowl, the material changes again. The sand is gone and it is clean, dry clay pan, fired by its own history to the hardness of a pot. It is cracked into large, quiet plates that fit together without quite touching, with hairline gaps between them where you could slide a knife. Nothing grows from those cracks. There is no water here, not even the memory of damp. No spring line, no stain. This is not a place where water has ever been. The salt is not from evaporated sea but mineral exhaled from the deep rock, dry salt, like frost that formed without water.

And yet, sparse, there is vegetation.

It shouldn't be here in this darkness, and in some way it barely is. On the mid-slope, spaced far apart — enough room that each one stands alone — are seven, maybe nine dark shrubs. They are low, knee-high at most, with many black, wiry branches that do not branch often. They have no leaves. Or rather, they have reduced leaves to thorns, to scales, tight against the wood to keep in anything that might be kept. They are the color of the basalt, so you almost don't see them until you are near. They do not cast shadows because there is no light to cast them. They do not move. Their roots must go down impossibly deep, into some dry chemistry that sustains them without rain, without photosynthesis to speak of. They are not growing. You look at one for a long time and you understand it is not growing and it is not dying either. It has found its form and is keeping it, like the slope itself. Established. Settled. In balance with emptiness.

There is almost nothing else. No grass. No lichen that you can see. No animals. No insects. No birds cross this bowl. The place has not been found to be useful to them. That is its rule: presence is expensive, so only what can persist for almost nothing is permitted to remain.

There are no structures here. No walls, no cairns, no foundations. No one lives here because to live here would require making something that this place will not allow. And yet the stones suggest a kind of tool-use, or the ghost of it.

The basalt pebbles in particular: basalt fractures in conchoidal curves, leaving edges as sharp as broken glass. Some of these stones have broken naturally — thermal stress, in the days when days still had temperature swings — and lie in flakes. A core, a striking flake, a crude blade. One of them, resting half in dust at the foot of the largest shrub, is almost perfectly hand-shaped, with a thick back where your thumb would go and a fine edge that would cut dry fiber if there were fiber to cut. No one made it. But if you were here, and you needed a knife for a moment, you could pick it up. Primitive technology without technologists. The place provides the shape of a tool without ever having been touched.

The sound is almost absolute quiet. Not the quiet of a forest which is full of small attenuations — this is a sealed quiet. If you stand still, you hear the blood in your ears. If you scuff the sand, the sound is immediate and small and then it is gone, swallowed by the clay bowl. The air itself is so still and dry it makes no noise moving around the thorn branches. The darkness and the warmth and the dryness together make a kind of pressure on the eardrum.

Nothing in the bowl is changing. The heat does not rise or fall. The light does not brighten or dim. The plants do not sway. The salt crust does not grow. The dust that is suspended high above does not settle. When you leave a footprint in the soft upper sand, you get the impression that it will stay there until the clay beneath it finally softens, which will not be in your lifetime, or in the lifetime of the shrubs. The scales of that change are too long to be called change at all.

If you sit in the center, on one of the hard clay plates that is still warm from a sun that is not up, your back to everything, you are at the exact bottom of a small, complete world that forgot to need water, forgot to need animals, forgot to need light, and kept only what it could keep without effort: warmth, mineral, a few dark, patient shapes that hold their place, and silence.
