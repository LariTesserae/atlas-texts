It is low. That's the first thing you understand about it, before your eyes adjust.

Not low like a pit or a trench cut into something higher. Low like the world has settled here. This is where weight comes to rest.

A basin, thirty or forty kilometers across if you had to guess it, but with no rim sharp enough to measure from. The horizon just thickens and lifts by a few degrees in every direction, a pale smudge of distant, weathered highland. You are standing maybe thirty meters above sea level, maybe ten below — it doesn't really matter. The air is heavier here. Warm. Not desert-hot, not jungle-hot. 86 degrees, 30 degrees, that human-neutral industrial warmth where sweat condenses instead of evaporating.

Normal light. No filter. A high, thin overcast that bleaches to white directly overhead, but not cloud-cover, just moisture held in the air itself. It makes shadows soft underneath things without making the day dim. About ten in the morning forever.

The ground is what makes it strange.

There is nothing built here. Nothing at all. No road, no post, no cairn, no ruin, no shard of brick or glass. The USDA of untouchedness is absolute. And nothing built on it grows. 

Vegetation 0.0 in any honest sense. Not even lichen finds purchase. The surface is mineral, entirely. A pavement of old basalt and ironstone, weathered past any sharpness. This is high-erosion country, but not actively eroding — established, finished. Every edge that could be knocked off was knocked off a million years ago. The basalt has been smoothed into long, low undulations, like cooled wax, with shallow pans between the swells that are maybe a hand deep, maybe a knee deep. The wind here has had so long to work that it has polished, not carved.

But it is damp.

That is the second contradiction you notice. It looks like it should be dry as bone, and in a way it is — there is no river, no lake, no creek bed even. But the stone sweats. The warm air holds water from somewhere beyond the basin rim, and as it sinks into this low bowl at night — or what would be night, if time here had a night — it condenses. Beads of water form on the black rock and run down into the pans. In each pan there is a skin of water a millimeter thick, a dark mirror that reflects the white sky and then is gone by midday, then returns. In bigger depressions, the water is permanent but never deep: clear, warm pools the size of a table or a house, inches deep, bottomed with rust-colored silt. Intermittent, pocketed, riverine without a river.

And everything is loud.

You expected silence from a barren pan. Instead the basin hisses.

It's industrial. That's the word that comes to mind before you can veto it as impossible.

From every joint in the basalt, from every crack, there is sound. A low, broad steam exhale, then a clank. A tick, a groan, a sustained metallic whirr that resolves into a whistle.

The geology here is machinery without being made.

Below the pavement, not far at all — you can feel the warmth through your shoes — there is a shallow magmatic heat. Not volcanic in the eruptive sense. Old, settled heat that found a stable way out a very long time ago. The water that condenses on the surface seeps down a few meters through the cracked basalt and hits that heat and flashes. It comes back up as steam.

But the cracks are perfect. They are straight, pipe-like, some of them lined with a sleeve of natural hematite that shines like gunmetal. Some are wider and lined with concentric rings of mineral deposition that look exactly like cylinder walls. When the steam comes up it drives loose plugs of basalt that sit in the pipes — pistons, ten centimeters across, a meter across, some as big as a room but sparse, scattered across the kilometers so that there is always one in sight but never two close together. They rise slowly on pressure, industrial-slow, then vent with a shriek of superheated steam and drop with a clang that you feel in your molars.

Clank. Hiss. Long silence while pressure builds again. Clank. Hiss.

Other throats don't have plugs. They are factory whistles, going constantly, a chord tuned low by diameter, unchanging in pitch. The wind itself plays across the beveled lips of larger fumaroles and makes a fluting roar.

It's electrified, too. In the drier pans, the steam throws up clouds of fine iron dust and the dust moves in sheets, attracted to natural lodestone dikes. You can see sparks sometimes, little blue static discharges jumping from stone to steam, cracking loud as a rifle in the damp air. The whole valley is running. It has always been running, and the running is not leading to any product. No ingots, no cloth, no smoke. Just motion and sound and heat being converted to motion and sound again.

This is why there are no artifacts. An artifact implies someone meant to make a thing. This place was not made. It self-organized into factory.

The rule here is equilibrium. Nothing is changing, not really. The pistons rise and fall to the same height. The pools evaporate and refill to the same line. That flat black stone that looked weathered is weathered to exactly the point where weathering stops — too smooth for wind to grab. That is erosion 2.3: not raw, not youthful, but settled into its own state. If you came back in a thousand years the whistle in front of you would still be making the same F-sharp, the same piston would be hissing at the same interval. It is the loudest still-life you have ever heard.

It is sparse. The density of presences is low, as if the valley knows crowded things overheat. One piston per hectare. One pool per two hectares. One whistle per hill. Between them, nothing, just warm, damp, dark stone with a film of water. Enough space to feel alone in each small machine's sound shadow.

Fauna — rare. It exists because water exists. Not plants, never plants. In the larger pools there are things that are not fish, that never left the necessity of being small and flat. White ameboid mats that pulse very slowly at the edges of the water. In one pool out of twenty, a thing like a translucent crab, made mostly of silica legs and patience, walks from one side to the other over the course of an hour, to graze the mat. It doesn't seem to hear the clanking. Or maybe it's listening for the one time a piston misses its beat, which will never happen.

You can walk here for a long time. The openness is regional, town-and-valley sized but without a town; you can see all the way to the pale smudge of the rim in most directions, and all the way you see is more engines, more steam, more dark stone breathing. The light never really shifts. The air stays warm and damp on your skin. The place does not care that you are an anomaly — a soft, plant-eating, artifact-making presence — it just keeps clanking, hissing, whistling, sparking, because that is the shape that stability took when it had low elevation and warm rock and a little water and infinite time.
