It is very high.

Not high like a hill or high like a peak you climb to. High like a place where the earth is still deciding to be vertical. The mountain is not finished.

From the lowlands you would think it is a storm caught on a nail of rock — a bruise-colored cloud that never moves. Up close, you realize the cloud is the city. And the city is scaffolding for a mountain that hasn't learned to hold itself yet.

They call it the Sill.

The Sill is an emergent range, a raw knuckle of basalt and pale, unweathered granite pushing up through the skin of the world at the rate a fingernail grows. You can hear it if you stand still long enough. Not an earthquake. A long, low grinding, a sound like molars closing. The geology is embryonic, uncut by water. There are no valleys here yet, no rivers, no softness. There are planes and sheers and sudden, right-angle steps where the rock has extruded and stopped, waiting for the next lift. The edges are so sharp they take the wind apart.

Because it is so new, there is almost no dust. No soil. The rock sweats instead.

This high, the air is cool — not the clean alpine cold that burns, but a wet, workmanlike cool, 8 degrees and damp through your coat, the kind of cold that lives in concrete stairwells. The light is dim always. The sun is a rumor above two layers of cloud deck. What light gets through is filtered to a flat, blue-grey eventide. No shadows, or rather, one enormous, diffuse shadow that everyone lives inside of. The city runs its lights at noon. Amber windows and sodium worklamps strung on cables between unfinished crags, flickering when the wind hits the generators.

And still, it is urban.

Not a mining camp. Not an outpost. A town, a real layered town, built with the conviction of a place that knows it will be here for centuries even though the ground under it will not. 

Because the mountain is still coming up, they could not build on it. So they built *around where it will be.*

Look across the open scale of it — maybe three valleys wide, maybe one town if you walked it for a day — and you see the lattice. Black iron girders, pre-stressed concrete forms, industrial gantries and poured staircases that bolt into nothing and wait. Riveted water towers perched on rock fins the size of houses. Factories with their corrugated walls already rusted, hanging in the air on stilts a hundred meters tall, their assembly floors unmoored, fabricating more stilts, more girders. The whole thing is sparse. There is a tremendous amount of nothing between structures. You will walk for ten minutes on a grated catwalk with wind screaming through the mesh, nothing below but a thousand meters of cold air and cloud, to get from a hab-block to the boiler house. The built density is high where it exists — dense as any industrial city — but the city itself is thinly sown across the emergence. It respects the emptiness. It has to.

Water is rare and everywhere. There are no rivers. There is no water table. The rock is too young and too tight. But the elevation catches the lower clouds and wrings them. At around 4pm every day, for about an hour, the cloud comes *through* the city. Not over it. Through it. You can watch it hit a vertical basalt slab, condense in sheets, and run. For that hour the girders weep. Every surface beads and streams. People put out barrels, basins, laundry. Gutters you thought were decorative suddenly roar. The rest of the time it is damp. Condensation dripping from pipes, from undersides of balconies, from the rock itself. Pockets. There is a cistern-chapel cut into the soft granite of Lift 3 that only fills in the afternoon fog. There are hanging gardens in the lee of the factories that live entirely on that breath.

And they are gardens. That is the strange part, with so little soil and so little light — the vegetation is lush. Not forest-lush. Crevice-lush. In every weld where the wind has dropped a little lichen, in every north-facing joint where the condensation never fully dries, something has rooted. Bristling mats of black-green moss thick as a mattress, ferns with leaves the size of doors that unfurl slowly, slowly, over months. Glasswort that grows on iron. Vines that have learned to eat rust, their tendrils orange at the tip. The underside of the city is a hanging meadow, inverted. You look up from a lower catwalk and see a ceiling of green dripping.

Where there are plants, there are animals. A lot of them.

Too many for this altitude, for this cold, for this sparse, unfinished world. The Sill is loud with them.

Flocks of white-grey longwings — not birds, not bats, something with both membrane and feather — that nest in the girder-holes by the thousands and scream when the fog lifts, a sound like tearing sheet metal that mixes with the factory whistles. Six-legged rock hoppers, big as dogs, their backs crusted with the same moss that grows on the factories, chewing it off each other. Condensation beetles that roll water instead of dung, pushing perfect spheres of fog-water three times their size along the catwalks to their burrows in the concrete. And in the open air between the structures, things that should not be able to fly here hover — heavy, slow, buoyant grazers that feed on airborne spores, ringing low like struck bells as they pass. The ecology is not supposed to work in a dim, damp, cold extrusion zone. It does not care. It has overfilled the scaffolding.

The machines make a different loud. The slow loud and the fast loud layered.

Fast: the clank and churn of the stamping mills, the diesel thud of generators, the wind harp shriek through every tensioned cable — a constant, industrial Aeolian wail, rising and falling an octave when a gust hits the whole valley-sized instrument.

Slow: underneath it, a deeper sound, at the threshold of hearing. The rock growing. The mountain remembering that it needs to be here. On very quiet nights — which are never quiet — you can feel it in your teeth more than hear it: a subsonic groan as a new seam of basalt inches up, pushing a factory foundation up three millimeters. The maintenance crews come out with wrenches the size of men and loosen the tolerance bolts another turn. That is the dynamic. Patient. Negligible, until you measure it against a year, a lifetime. Everything is adjusted.

This is where the weirdness lives, and it is not subtle.

This place is unfinished in a way that goes beyond geology. The rules are still curing.

On the eastern edge, where the rock is newest, gravity is undecided. Water drips up from a slab for three seconds before remembering to fall. A bolt dropped will hang for a breath too long. In the factory district, the smoke from the smelters does not rise; it pours sideways for a block and then vanishes, as if the sky has seams.

Some buildings are built for erosion that has not happened yet. There is a massive stone bridge, beautifully buttressed, spanning a gap where there is currently no gap — just a solid wall of new granite. Its builders will tell you, calmly, that there will be a gorge there. They have seen it. They plaster over cracks that appear in the stone a week before the rock moves.

And then there are the places where you can see through. Not through to the other side of the city. Through to the place where the city is supposed to be. At dusk, when the worklamps buzz on, you will sometimes look down a long alley of girders and floodlights and see not the next pylon, but a finished version of the Sill — weathered, green, river-cut, old. You can hear its river. You can smell soil. Blink, and it's raw rock and rivets again. The workers leave offerings for it: a cup of fresh water.

No one pretends this is a normal place to live.

You live here because you are part of the construction crew for a planet. You keep the boilers lit so the moss won't freeze. You scrape the condensation beetles off the cisterns. You listen to the longwings scream and the rock grow and the wind play the city, all that loud stacked up against the cold, dim, damp, high air — and once a month you go out with the great wrenches and give the mountain room to become itself.
