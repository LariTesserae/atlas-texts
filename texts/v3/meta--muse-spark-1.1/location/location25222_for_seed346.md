It is a basin town, but not a town in a basin — it *is* the basin.

You come down to it over the last lip of permafrost, where the tundra finally gives up and dips. Elevation here is nothing, a shallow bowl scooped out of the white plain, maybe thirty meters deep at most, three hundred across. From the rim you can see the whole thing at once, end to end like looking into a room. That's the scale of it — a hamlet, a grove, a single cathedral. You could walk its perimeter in twenty minutes if it would let you walk in a straight line.

It doesn't.

The cold is the first rule. Not a biting, storming cold. A still, primordial cold.  The kind that makes iron brittle and breath audible. The air sits low and heavy and damp. Not wet — damp. The sky is low and white and normal-bright, a flat noon that never quite becomes day, and everything sweats. Condensation beads on every surface and never quite freezes and never quite falls. Once every few hours a mist will pass through without wind — a riverine breath — and leave the railings slick and the stone dark for a while. In the gutters between buildings, which were never built for water, there are runnels of black ice that are somehow also liquid underneath, moving very slowly.

The city occupies all of the bowl, and more than all of it.

It is undeniably urban. Not ruins, not outpost. Dense, layered, intentional urban fabric, the density of the old walled cities — Kowloon, or medina- — but vertical in the wrong direction. Buildings lean into each other until there are no streets, only seams. Balconies become roofs become floors for the level above. Alleyways dogleg and then narrow until your shoulders brush both sides and then, without getting wider, admit that they now have four sides. You duck under laundry that has frozen stiff on the line, lichen-colored sheets and cables so fine they are invisible except where the damp collects on them.

Sparse vegetation lives in the negotiation space between structures. Not trees. There is not enough light that reaches down, and not enough time. Black lichen, grey lichen that glows faintly at its edges, a kind of wiry moss that grows in straight lines following the seams where condensation drips. In the few open pockets — courtyards no bigger than a kitchen table — someone has tried to grow something in boxes: a stunted potato plant, a single rust-colored grass. They survive because the air is damp enough.

And the buildings themselves — this is where the temperature of your mind drops lower than the air.

They are being built, but not being built in the way you know. The tech is quiet and beyond language. The walls are not brick or concrete or metal, though from far away they pretend to be. Up close they are woven. Latex-black and bone-white material that is somehow both solid and a dense swarm, like seeing a beehive from far enough away that it looks like a wall. You can put your hand near it and feel the faint heat it pulls out of you, and the material thickens toward your palm without touching you. Doorframes are thicker at the top than the bottom because gravity is a suggestion there. Some of the structures are inverted shadows of structures that are not yet there — scaffolding made of held light and held cold where tomorrow a house will be.

Nothing here has been eroded. That is the strangest part. Every edge is fresh, raw, unweathered. Corners are too sharp. The geology under the town, where you can see it between foundation-weaves, is primary basalt that looks like it cooled last week, glassy and unmarked by water or wind. Pipes and conduits emerge from that raw stone as if the stone grew around them. The town is embryonic. It is forming the very ground it stands on as it goes, laying down strata that have no fossils because nothing has ever died in them before.

Because the scale is small and the density is high, reality can't get enough leverage to stay straight. Weirdness is not an event here — it's the zoning law.

Walk down what looks like a single alley and you count seven left turns when there should have been two. Come back and count five. The same window, seen from below, looks out onto a kitchen that is larger than the building that contains it. On the far eastern edge, the town's boundary is a literal edge: a low wall, and beyond it the white plain. But if you stand at that wall and look back, the town is deeper than you came through. There are streets in it that you could not have entered from the outside. Light arrives normally — flat, white, shadowless — but it pools. You will see an old woman's doorway lit like late summer even while snow dust sits on her lintel.

Sound obeys the same quiet. Mostly no sound at all. No engines, no birds — fauna is almost absent. Once a day you might see a small white bird cut across the sky above the bowl and think better of landing. The things that live here are people, or were. They are dense too, many presences per room, but they have learned to be quiet. They move slowly. Everything moves slowly. A man re-weaving a wall will make a pass with his hands and the swarm-wall will take an hour to shiver and accept it. A kettle takes a long time to boil because heat is slow here. Conversations are held at low volume and with long pauses, because if you listen for too long you hear the rooms echo in a slightly different order than you spoke.

The rule is this: the town is a compiler error in place. A low, cold, damp pocket where someone tried to build a city with tools that act on spacetime the way a loom acts on thread, and they did it too densely in too small a knot. So the space is still learning what shape it is. It is wet because the air can't decide if it is air. It is cold because entropy is sluggish. It is quiet because sound gets lost in the folds.

If you stay long enough on the rim, you will see the light catch on the condensation over the whole bowl at once, and for a second the town will look like what it wants to be when it is finished — a perfect, impossible dense star of habitation, all doors touching all other doors — before your eyes lose focus and it goes back to being a small, damp, shivering clutch of almost-houses, huddled together against the white that is not weather, but blankness.
