It is low. That's the first wrong thing.

The world here is supposed to have mountains, high places, but this place missed them. It's a long, shallow trough gouged into the cold plain, kilometers long and a couple wide — not a true valley, not yet. The land hasn't learned how to erode. The walls of it are too new, too sharp, too vertical for how cold and damp it is. Fresh dark rock, basaltic and glassy, still showing the chatter marks where it was pulled apart. Raw geology. No scree slopes, no softened shoulders, no river meander. Just a sudden negative, like a fingernail scratch in still-soft stone. Water hasn't had time to teach it how to be a valley.

And because it is low — lower than the plain by only forty or fifty meters at its deepest — all the cold air pours into it and stays there.

The town lives in the scratch.

You see it first as a discoloration. The plain is white-grey, wind-scraped snow and black stone. Then the trough appears and it is green-black, packed, steaming faintly though the air is far below freezing. Normal daylight, flat overcast light, no shadows, the kind of light that makes colors true. Cold you can see.

The town occupies every available centimeter of the trough floor and walls, from one raw cliff to the other. There is no room left between things. That's the rule here: if there is a surface, it is occupied.

It is urban, unmistakably, but not planned. It is industrial in the way a coral reef is industrial — layer upon layer of production, all engine and pipe and scaffold, but grown, not drawn. The first layer was bolted to the fresh rock: iron walkways, riveted boiler houses, brick chimneys that never got tall because the cold caps them. The second layer was built on the first. The third on the second. Now you have to read it downwards in time, like strata.

Everything is running. You can tell by the tremor in the air, by the way the frost never quite forms on certain pipes. Vast low-temp mills, condensation traps, moss presses, rendering vats. Electrified, but with old electricity — big knife switches, humming transformers the size of houses that buzz so low you feel it in your molars more than hear it. That is why it is quiet.

The sound is wrong for such density. A packed industrial town the size of a small region should scream. This one doesn't. The cold damps it. The vegetation damps it. And the rock itself, still emerging, still unweathered, is too dense to echo right.

A low white noise: the hiss of low-pressure steam bleeding from a thousand relief valves, instantly condensing, the tick of cooling metal, the soft sigh of water moving where water shouldn't be.

Because water here is not a river. Not yet. There is no watershed old enough for a river. Water here is what the industry wants and what the air gives up. The whole trough breathes. Warm exhaust from below hits the cold sink above and weeps. Every pipe, every cable, every ladder underside is bearded with clear ice and then with droplets, dripping constantly. They have built gutters that feed gutters that feed gutters, wooden sluices black with age, tin troughs, half-pipes of repurposed boiler hull, all dripping down toward the centerline. In the center, there is a flow — not a river, a managed leak — a braided, shin-deep, ice-rimmed channel of warmish grey water that carries ash and oil and living things. You step over it with short iron bridges that are never more than a meter apart because there is no space not to build a bridge.

And everything is lush. Impossibly lush for minus ten, minus fifteen. Not trees. Trees would need soil and time and erosion hasn't made soil yet. It's a vertical peat of moss and liverwort and black-green lichen and cold-loving ferns that have learned to root in rust. It grows on everything that stays still for a week. The north-facing walls of factories are five feet thick with it, hanging in frozen curtains that still grow at the tips. The roofs — which are also floors for the level above — are gardens of that moss, cropped and combed and farmed. They farm the moss. They cook it, press it for oil, spin it for insulation, burn the waste for the small heat that never quite warms you. In the steam breathing pockets between machines, where a pipe bursts hot, jungle-like pockets explode: fronds as wide as doors, translucent, white-veined, dripping constantly, gone to black slime if the pipe is fixed and the cold returns, then reborn somewhere else.

It is all occupied. Moss, and under the moss, fauna. Pigeons by the hundred thousand, but wrong pigeons — thick feathered, small-headed, walking more than flying in the packed aisles, nesting in switchboxes for the warmth. Things that are not rats but fill the rat-shape: six-limbed, hairless, with translucent skin, running the steam pipes. And in the central water, things that look like pale shrimp, like woodlice as big as your hand, boiling in the warm chemical flow, farmed with scoops. Insects that cannot fly because the air is too cold, that crawl in black mats over the lichen, humming without sound. Everywhere movement at the edge of sight. No space un-watched or un-eating. The density is total.

Weirdness comes from the fact that the town is still under construction by the geology. The raw basalt trough is still emerging — a few millimeters a year, but it doesn't creep. It jerks. In the night you feel it: a deep pop, a new crack along the eastern wall, a slab the size of a house shucking off clean and falling into the packed city. And the city absorbs it. It must.

This is the dynamic, the turbulence: the town is destroyed and rebuilt daily, quietly, without panic. When a new wall surface appears, slick and black and steaming cold, it is immediately swarmed. Boys with hammers and pitons and bags of moss slurry. Women unspooling copper wire. The pigeons arrive before the humans do. By midday the new rock has a stair bolted to it, a steam line sweating on it, a green fuzz already adhering. When an old building is crushed by a rock fall, no one clears it. They build on top of and around the wreck and re-route the dripping gutters around it in an hour. The city has no streets, only gaps that persist between things for a few weeks before they are filled.

The town never stops. Not in a loud, shouting way. In the way an anthill never stops, or a body never stops healing. A turbulent, industrious, completely silent emergency.

The rules here are simple and no one wrote them down:

1. Cold sinks. Keep the engines low if you want to keep them.
2. Water is not from below, but from the air, and it belongs to everyone, so you may take it but you must pass it on.
3. If rock goes bare, cover it, or something else will.
4. If you have to make a noise loud enough to be heard more than two levels away, it must be worth it.

You stand on a catwalk that was a roof this morning and is already a floor, with moss coming up between your boots, and your breath makes instant snow that falls into the green, and somewhere under you a turbine big as a church turns slowly over, turning nothing but turning, and the central slow water keeps moving, and new stone cracks open behind you with a sound like ice breaking in a glass.
