High up, but not a peak. A shelf. A place where the mountain changed its mind about climbing.

You come up through thinner air and drier stone for a long time and then the ground steps back — a broad, shallow bowl cut into the flank of the highland, maybe three hundred strides across. Not a crater. A place where an old flow pooled and sagged. The rim holds it in on all sides, a low, weathered wall of dark rock about twice a man's height, rounded over at the top like the lip of a cup left out in the rain for a thousand years. It keeps the wind off. It keeps the world small. You can see the whole thing from any edge; it is a room with no roof.

And it's warm here. That is the first strangeness. Up this high it should be cold enough to bite. Instead the stone holds heat. The basin faces south-southwest and the black basalt drinks sun all day and gives it back slowly, and somewhere underneath, deeper than the bowl, there is a second warmth that doesn't come from the sun. The rocks weep with it.

Water is everywhere, not as a lake, not as a stream with a direction, but as a condition. The air comes over the rim and hits the warm stone and condenses. It doesn't rain. It thickens. It beads on everything. The basalt of the rim wall sweats constantly — dense, pitted old lava, its pores filled, its surface furred with lichens the color of rust and pale lime, and from every pore and pit a droplet forms, swells, runs. Slow dark tracks down the inside of the bowl's wall where the water has run the same way for so long it has polished little gutters into the stone. Gentle, inward, centering gutters.

The floor of the bowl is not flat. It's hummocked, settled. Old rock has sagged and been softened. There is a deep, springy mat — peat, but not like lowland peat. A thick felt of half-rotted fiber over sand and clay, waterlogged so that when you step near, not on, it sighs and dimples. Water is right under the surface, everywhere. You can press your palm and watch clear brown water well up around your fingers and stay. In the lowest places it gathers into pools that are not quite pools — clearings in the mat where water stands shin-deep, perfectly still, so clear you can see the black root tangle ten inches down, so still it holds the sky without a wrinkle.

And what grows here grows far apart.

Lush, but sparse. Nothing crowds. Each plant takes its own space as if by agreement.

There are clumps of a broad-leafed thing like giant taro, but thicker, its stalks as thick as your wrist, waxy, swollen with stored water, the leaves held up like shallow bowls, some two feet across, cupping an inch of captured condensation. The leaves are so green they look almost black until light passes through. Nothing has eaten them. Not a hole, not a chew mark, not a single ragged edge. Where an older leaf has died it hasn't been eaten either; it has simply melted straight down into dark slime and fed its own base.

Between them, in the gaps, stands a kind of fern that doesn't behave like a fern. It grows as a single trunk, six or seven feet tall, with no branches, only a crown of fronds that hang straight down, heavy and dripping. The trunks are spaced twenty, thirty feet apart. Always alone. The ground under each is bare peat, dark and dotted with its own fallen spores — countless cinnamon dots that never blow away because there is almost never wind inside the rim.

And everywhere, lower, the moss — but not a carpet. Pillows. Discrete, domed hummocks of moss the size of a sleeping animal, vivid green to yellow-green, luminous, saturated. They sit in their own wetness, some of them gently pulsing. Not with animal breathing. With water. They swell as they take in condensation through the day and contract by a fraction as dusk comes. If you sit for a full hour you can see it. You can hear it if the day is very quiet, which it almost always is.

Because there is nothing here that makes a sound on purpose.

No animals. No insects. No birds to pass over. The absence isn't like a dead place. It's like a place that was made before animals were thought of, or after they have left and the plants have learned not to wait for them. There is no pollination by wing. The giant bowl-leaves reproduce by tipping; when a leaf gets too heavy with water it dips and pours itself into the throat of the plant next to it, and with the water goes a cloud of pale, glassy seeds that sink and root. The fern-trunks let their fronds down into the standing pools and release their spores directly into the water, where they drift like dust in sunlight and settle. The whole bowl propagates through water, through slow pouring and touching and dripping. You start to realize why everything is so spaced out — each needs its own fall radius.

That is the weirdness. It knows how to live alone.

The quiet that results is total but not empty. What you hear:

The tick of a droplet from a frond-tip into a leaf-bowl.
A second later, another tick somewhere else on the far side.
The long, almost inaudible creak of peat adjusting under water.
The faint, continuous hiss of condensation forming on basalt, too soft to be called a hiss. More like the air getting heavier.
And if you stay still long enough, a low wet sigh from the moss pillows as they release a little air as they swell.

Materially, the bowl is old. The erosion is not violent. No scree, no fresh scars. Every edge that could have been sharp has long been dissolved, lichened, rubbed down to a hand-smoothness. Even the broken rocks on the floor — pieces that fell off the rim generations ago — are rounded like river stones though no river has ever run here. They have been covered and uncovered by moss and peat so many times their sides carry tide-lines of green. In the center, where the water pools are deepest, the basalt shows through the peat in one long spine, a whaleback of stone polished by endless water-sheeting, warm to the touch. On its back there are shallow, scooped hollows, not natural. Or not entirely natural. Repeated use. Places where something with hands has sat, and ground, and smoothed, using the spine as a mortar. A few stone flakes lie nearby — basalt flakes struck with care, their edges still clean but patinated. One of them fits a palm perfectly. No building, no wall, no fire ring, no art. Just that. Tool-use without settlement. A visited place. Perhaps a place that is visited only when someone needs water that has never had an animal in it, or moss that has grown without being touched.

There is no path out that is not climbing. Light here is normal, ordinary mountain light, bright at midday, pooling warmer and amber in late afternoon because the dark walls trap it. It doesn't slant dramatically. It just stays a little longer than it should below the rim. Clouds pass at eye level, sometimes lower, and when one drops into the bowl it doesn't pour in; it sinks as fog and sits, for hours, making every leaf-bowl a little lake and every moss pillow bead with silver.

It changes slowly. Over a day, almost nothing. Over a season, the pools breathe up or down an inch. Over years, a new taro-leaf plant appears between two old ones and both lean away by a degree. Over long time, the rim softens further, the gutters deepen by a grain. The stone tools on the spine get a little more polished where rain hits them, a little more sunk into peat where they rest.

You don't stay long, because the silence becomes aware of you. Not hostile. Just unused to anything that makes moving sounds, anything that exhales so quickly, anything so warm and so briefly here.
