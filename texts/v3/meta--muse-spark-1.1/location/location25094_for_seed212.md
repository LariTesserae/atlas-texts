It is low. The whole world is low — a broad, warm basin that never quite decided to be land.

It is not old land. This is new land, wracked up from underneath. If you dig down through the mat with your hands — and you can, because everything is soft and alive — you hit it within a few arm-lengths: black, young rock, sharp and unweathered, still holding the heat of its making. Glassy in places. It came up fast, cracked like a loaf, and it steams. Not with volcanoes, with breath. The plant found it before rain could round it.

And the plant is everything.

Not forest. That word is too sparse. There are no trees as distinct individuals here. There is a City made of plant, or a plant that has grown in the shape of a city. It goes to the horizon in every direction, street after street after tower after plaza, a valley-wide settlement that was never built. It was secreted. Germinated.

Its avenues are not paved; they are grown. The avenue is a single enormous leaf midrib, three lanes wide, slightly concave, waxy and foot-wet with morning condensation. Its edges curl up into colonnades — thick, living palisades of interwoven aerial roots and stipules that braid themselves into arches and balconies and doors as you watch. The buildings are not structures with plants on them. They are buds. They are tightly whorled leaves the size of apartment blocks, still furled, overlapping so densely the light has to squeeze green and gold between them. Some have unfurled completely and stand open like vast cups. Some are still closed, humming with pressure. Streets run inside them, and streets run over them, and streets run through their walls when a tendril decides a shortcut is necessary.

It is dense the way a jungle is dense and the way a downtown is dense at once. There is no empty air. Above you is always another level: a lattice of vines as thick as subway tunnels, liverwort carpets hanging like awnings three stories thick, ferns that have learned to be structural. Below you is more of it — you never truly touch the ground, you walk on layers of living thatch, springy, half a meter of succulent water-storing leaves and then fibrous root felt and then another level of dark, wet leaves that have not seen sun in weeks but are still alive.

And it moves. 

God, it moves. This is not a slow growth. This is turbulent, frantic, insulting growth. This city is having an argument with itself at full volume. A boulevard will eruct a new district in ten minutes — you will hear the complaining creak of cellulose locking, a buttress root the size of a bus shoving up through the path, leaf-bricks slapping into place, a whole cul-de-sac of soft, lime-green rooms sighing open. While you stand in one intersection trying to get your bearings, that intersection ceases to be an intersection and becomes an interior courtyard as a curtain of strangler figs drops around you. On the other side of the valley, something dies just as fast — a whole tower of brassica-like scaffolding goes brown, collapses inward with a wet sigh, and is eaten by its neighbors before the sound is gone. It is a bazaar, an anthill, a storm that builds with leaves. Nothing is permitted to be finished.

That is why it is deafening.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects even. Nothing to make noise. The sound is all vegetable violence. Growing should be silent. It is not here.

It cracks. It pops like knuckles, a thousand knuckles a second, all around you, overhead, underfoot. The hydraulic shriek of water being forced up through xylem twenty stories high. The papery thunder of a leaf-blimp inflating. The tear of two trunks that have grown into each other deciding to part. The low, constant groan of the whole mass settling on its young, sharp rock — the sound of roots learning granite. You can feel it in your molars. Your chest. You have to shout to a companion right next to you and even then your voice is eaten by green.

There is water, but not the way a swamp has water. The air itself is damp. Warm — a breathing, bathhouse warm that makes everything sweat. The City sweats constantly, because that is how it drinks the sky. On every surface at all times is a sheet of condensation in beads the size of grapes. It gathers, runs in milky rivulets down leaf-gutters, collects in cisterns that are just the axils of giant plants, amber and trembling with tannin. Sometimes it rains, but when it rains it is not weather. It is the City wringing itself out — all the stored dew dumped at once from a hundred thousand leaves in a single, shuddering exhale. For an hour there will be rivers in the streets, then nothing but dripping and sucking for a long time again.

And because there are no animals, there is no rot smell, no dung, no decay. Only green, hot, clean, living smells: cut grass and crushed pepper and sugar and iron.

The weirdness is not decorative. It is structural. The Plant City does not obey enclosure.

You walk down a long, straight aerial root avenue that should take five minutes, and it takes an hour, because the perspective is growing while you walk it. A leaf-room looks from the outside like a single-person hut, a tight bud you could hold. Inside, it is a damp cathedral, vaulted, with light falling from chlorophyll windows high above. And its walls are lined with smaller doors that are themselves full-sized boulevards. You look down a side alley and see the back of your own head a block away, because the vines have looped space.

You can get lost vertically as easily as horizontally. Gravity is negotiable where the growth is fast enough. New floors grow over you faster than you fall. Sometimes down is suddenly to your left, because the whole mass shifted.

There are rules here, but they are not human rules.

1. Everything that can photosynthesize is architecture. Everything else is scaffolding and will be replaced.
2. No surface stays. If you stand still long enough to feel comfortable, you are becoming a site.
3. Water does not belong to place. It belongs to time. It will find you, sit on you, and leave.
4. Silence is death. If a patch goes quiet, it is already being eaten.

In the open squares — and there are squares, perfect, almost Roman plazas formed by the radial crown-shyness of six titan leaves that refuse to touch — the normal light, a plain late-summer light, falls straight and golden and absurd in all this invention. It catches the sweat in the air and makes it sparkle. In that light, for a moment, you can see the whole valley at once: a low bowl of impossible, attentive, self-building green, so dense it looks solid, so alive it looks like it is trying to speak, so loud you can't hear yourself think.
