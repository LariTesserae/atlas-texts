It is not a forest.

A forest implies many trees crowded together, trunks, leaf litter, shade. This is high and open and too bright for that.

You are on a tableland so large it has its own weather, a continent lifted. The basalt underneath is old, old enough that wind has worried it into softness — broad swales, shallow bowls kilometers across, whale-back ridges polished to a dark satin where no soil ever held. The air is thin even though it is hot. At midday the sun is white and vertical and normal — not dim, not blinding — it just falls straight down and cooks the stone until it gives back heat in waves. At 3,000 meters, the heat is not humid. It is a dry kiln heat.

And then, spaced across it, the Vegetation.

From a distance you mistake them for hills. Or thunderheads. Or ruins.

There are not many. That is the first strangeness. You can stand on an outcrop that has been sanded bare for a thousand years and count maybe seven in any direction, all the way to the haze where distance swallows scale. They are too far apart for their canopies to ever touch. Between them is nothing but hot stone, lichen, and moving shadow.

Up close you realize each one is not a tree. It is a place.

The smallest are the size of a cathedral. The largest are the size of a small city, a mountain that grows. Call them Holdfasts, because nothing else in language holds. A single organism — or a colony so tightly bound it stopped being plural — that germinated in a crack where condensation could find it, and then kept growing up and, more importantly, out and in on itself.

They are succulent on a scale that breaks the word. Their exterior is not bark but plates: overlapping, living shingles the size of billboards, waxy green-black, blistered with lenticels that weep salt. Each plate is a water tank. When the intermittent rain comes — hard, fast, gone in twenty minutes, once every nine or twelve days — the plates shiver and open, channeling every drop into gutters that lead inward. The rest of the time, they make water from air. In the mornings, before the heat, their entire mass sweats. Condensation beads and runs and is sucked back in through a million mouths. If you press your hand to one in the early hour, it is cold and utterly wet, like a cold bottle left out. That is the damp. There are no rivers here. There are no lakes. The only water in this landscape is briefly falling, or locked inside these giants, or dripping down your neck if you stand beneath one at dawn.

Because underneath the plates is not wood. Cut one — the people who live here know not to, except when the plant offers — and it is dense, fibrous, wet sponge, marbled with hollow chambers, tubes, cisterns. A vertical bog. Roots do not go deep. Why would they? The Holdfast is its own soil. Vines that are not separate species but deliberate limbs hang down inside, rooting in the air. Orchids as big as boats grow from its sides and are not parasites but organs. The whole mass is plant-as-ground, plant-as-building, plant-as-country. You can walk into it.

And you have to, because nothing else lives on the stone for long.

Erosion here is not active chewing; it is settled. The Holdfasts have been here long enough to decide their final shape: broad domes squashed slightly at the top where the wind hits, with buttresses on the windward side like melted wax. They lean a little north, all of them. Long dead lower plates slough off and lie around their bases in a black, crumbling moat, slowly turning to peat, to soil that is the only soil anywhere. That soil is shallow and sour and writhing.

That is where the sound comes from.

At first from miles away, it is a low thrum you feel in your teeth. As you get closer, it becomes distinct and it doesn't stop and it layers and layers until you understand that deafening here does not mean loud like an explosion. It means the air is full. There is no silence to be had.

Every chamber, every leaf the size of a house roof, every syrup-filled throat flower that opens once a year is inhabited. The fauna is not on the plant, it is through it.

Beetles the size of dogs, black and iridescent, carve galleries and cap them with resin. Wasps with wings a meter long — translucent, slow, impossibly loud — commute in lines between Holdfasts, carrying salt paste. Things that look like feathered snakes and are not snakes and not birds live in the mist layer under the canopy plates and sing with syrinxes that never evolved on Earth, whistling in two tones at once. Inside the moist heart-chambers, colonies of tiny amber mites flash in synchronous light. At night, that light is steady enough to read by.

And the hum: the endless, bone-shaking, ear-ringing chorus of the Flute Flies. They are not flies. They breed in the condensation gutters, billions per Holdfast, each male with a hollow chitin tube on its back. They don't buzz. They blow. Each one makes a single note. Trillions of single notes that are not in unison but in chord, shifting when the wind shifts, when a predator passes, when the plant shunts water from one side to the other. From a ridge between two Holdfasts, at noon when the heat makes everything move, you hear a continent organ played by insects and it is so loud you have to open your mouth to keep your ears from hurting.

Nothing hunts quietly here. A mantis thing with forelimbs like folded green ladders will crash through a curtain vine and take a bat the size of a kite and both will scream.

You learn quickly that to walk on the open stone is to be seen. The people who live here don't.

They are sparse too, because the Holdfasts are sparse. You find their marks not as villages but as single decisions. A rope made of braided aerial root fiber, three centimeters thick, thrown over a high plate to reach a cistern chamber. A ladder of bone pegs driven into living sponge, the sponge grown over them in smooth knuckles. A shelter that is not built but found: a lenticel that didn't close properly, enlarged over generations with scrapers of black beetle shell, just big enough for two people and a fireless hearth.

Their tech is exactly what you can make with your hands when the only materials are plant that regrows assertively, chitin, and basalt. Stone knives. Cordage. Waterskins made from the gas bladders that the Holdfast puffs up to shed dead leaves. They do not farm; farming would mean you had land. They garden the Holdfast itself, pruning, encouraging a sugar leaf here, pinching off a gall there to make it store more water. They speak low, because they have to, because the sound is so big.

Their weirdest understanding — and yours will become this too after two nights — is that the Holdfasts are not in competition. The vast spaces between them are not empty because they failed to fill them. They are empty on purpose. Each giant isolates itself so that the fireflies, the wasps, the mold, the fevers that could kill it cannot jump easily. The plateau is a quarantine of gods. The wind between them is sterile, hot, scouring. To cross it you cover your skin with leaf-wax, you carry a cistern leaf on your back, and you run before noon.

If you stay inside during the afternoon storm — when the one cloud this part of the plateau made for itself snags on the tallest Holdfast and rips — you will see the strangest rule of all.

The Holdfasts flower from the inside out. When a deep cistern is full enough, the pressure builds and the whole organism flexes and exudes, from pores all over its midsection, a rush of fine glassy filaments. Not silk. Almost like spun sugar but it doesn't melt. It hangs in the hot still air for hours, catching light, tangling in your hair, humming when the Flute Flies hit it. Each filament carries a seed smaller than dust. Most will drift down onto stone and die. One, perhaps every hundred years, will land in a crack and find enough damp to begin again.

The air after is full of glittering threads, filled with deafening music, filled with wasps, too hot to breathe comfortably, and you are standing inside the lungs of something larger than any building ever made by humans, wringing condensation off your sleeves to drink, listening to an ecosystem negotiate with itself at full volume.

And far off, across the shimmering stone that is not barren but waiting, another Holdfast answers with its own shiver of threads, catching the sun.
