It is not a cave. There is still sky.

A long wound has opened in the high plain, and the town is inside it.

From above, from the grass, you would almost miss it until you are at the lip. The plain is flat, cold, cropped short by wind — basalt country, thin soil. And then the ground simply stops. A crack, but a crack the size of a valley: about three kilometers long, four or five hundred meters across at its widest, and sixty to eighty meters deep. Straight-walled in some places, slumped in others. It is too new for the maps, too new to have a proper name. The old people in the north call it The Cut.

Geologically it is an infant. That is the thing you feel first when you climb down into it. This is not a river canyon smoothed by water and time. The walls are raw. On the west side, great columnar basalt still holds together in fresh, soot-black pillars, their tops snapped off like teeth. On the east side the cut has gone through a flow of red cinder and ash that crumbles if you lean on it, so the slope is a scree of loose, angular rubble that has not yet found an angle of repose. There are places where the rock faces fit together — you can see where a ten-ton block has slid out and lies directly below its own negative, unweathered, lichenless, its fracture glittering with tiny feldspar. The bottom is not flat. It is a jumble of fallen house-sized prisms, tilted slabs, and troughs between them where fines have collected. No river made this; the earth pulled apart here last winter, or last decade. The air still smells like the inside of rock when it rains.

And yet it is full. 

From lip to lip, the town fills the chasm floor until there really is no ground left to see.

At first the density is hard to understand — how did a place this new become this packed? The answer is in the climate up top. Up on the plain, the wind never stops. In winter it will flay a tent, freeze a man. Down here, sixty meters down, the wind is a rumor. The temperature drops off the lip and stays. Cool, even, damp in summer, not freezing in winter. So everyone came down.

They built edge to edge, wall to common wall. Low houses of uncut basalt rubble and cinder-block, roofed with thatch and stretched hides and, where they can afford it, small split pine planks carried down on backs. And then on top of those, more. A second story of woven juniper poles plastered with ash-mud. A third made of scaffolding — raw timber props still wearing bark, lashed with cured gut, leaning on the building next door for balance because there is no room to lean anywhere else. Walkways are not streets; they are the gaps left over between houses, rarely two men wide, often roofed over without intending to. Ladders go down between, not up — down into cellars that are really the spaces between fallen boulders, capped and inhabited.

There is no planning to it, but it is undeniably urban. Heaps of cut rock for future walls sit in the only open squares, which are ten feet across. Smoke from cookfires has nowhere grand to go, so it hangs in a low, even haze at about forty meters and then leaks, reluctantly, out into the sky. At noon, the sun comes straight down. The light is normal daylight — clean, white high-plains light — but it comes as a narrow river. For an hour around midday the whole Cut is lit, wall to wall, and you can see the seams between stones sharp. Then the shadow of the west wall climbs rapidly across the roofs, and the town goes into half-light for the rest of the afternoon, cool, like being at the bottom of clean water.

Water is the other reason they stay. It is not a wet place. But it is damp, always damp. The lower twenty meters of the east wall weeps. You can put your hand to the cinder and feel it cold and dark. In the morning, condensation beads on every stone face and every timber prop. It drips. It gathers. People have worked shallow runnels into the basalt dust with their hands and with deer-antler picks — that is the technology here: simple iron where they can trade for it, but mostly hammerstones, bone shovels, wooden mallets, the stone itself. The runnels lead to cisterns that are just the natural bowls between collapsed columns, now chinked with clay. In late winter a seasonal trickle runs for a few weeks along the lowest trough, not deep enough to float a leaf, but enough to wash clothes. You can hear it if you are quiet.

Quiet is what it mostly is.

For all that there are maybe four thousand people crammed into a space smaller than a farming village, the Cut is silent. The walls eat sound. No echo comes back clean — the fresh rubble scatters it. People speak low. The tools are not loud: the scrape of a spade in cinder, the tap-tap of a stone dresser, the soft thud of a hide being stretched. Wood creaks everywhere, because everything is under compression, leaning. Sometimes far up the chasm a fresh face of basalt will sigh and drop a few flakes with a hiss, and everyone stops for a half minute to see if it will be more. It almost never is.

Vegetation is scarce because there has not been time, and because feet. On the plain above, wiry, waist-high grass. Down here, in the few places people haven't sealed over, there is a thin fur of moss on the seep-wall, and pale lichen beginning to write a first script on the newer blocks. A few dwarf pines have rooted in the scree near the southern, narrower end where the sun lingers, stunted, twisted, their needles coated in dust. In any gap that gets midday light for more than a week, someone will have planted beans or a hardy squash in carried soil, the vines trained up the side of a house because there is no horizontal surface unclaimed. They are not decoration. They are the only green.

Animals are rare inside the walls. The plain is too cold and exposed for many; the Cut is too crowded. At dusk bats, small and fast, knife out of the upper crevices to hunt moths above the town's smoke. A few cliff mice, nearly invisible against cinder. In the cisterns, something like a blind newt, brought up by the water. Nothing larger. The people keep no herds down here — there is no room. Goats are kept up top by boys who sleep down below, who come down the switchback trails at evening, animal-slow.

The place is active despite the quiet. It is all underway. This is a town that is being born at the same time as its geology. You see it in the constantly moving rockpiles: a wall taken down here, its stones becoming another wall there, because the ground beneath slumped and the house had to be rebuilt ten feet over. You see it in the triple-deep scaffolding that never gets taken down, only added to. Women carry water in skin buckets along a path that did not exist that morning because a roof was extended over it. A man sits for a day shaping a basalt lintel with a heavier basalt, flaking it true, patiently. Children are everywhere, because there is nowhere else to put them, darting through the packed spaces like water through gravel.

The rule here is simple: no space is unused, and everything is temporary except the rock. When a house collapses, you do not clear it. You build on it. The floor is rising, a few inches a year, as rubble and midden and cinder fill in the gaps between the original fallen blocks. In a generation, if the wind allows, the bottom of the Cut will be flat and fully man-made, a floor of packed living.

For now it is raw and intimate and cold to the touch. If you stand on a third-story roof at full noon, with the light pouring straight down, you can see from chasm mouth to chasm mouth, the whole crowded valley-town laid out, steaming faintly with cooksmoke and damp, stitched tight as a scab inside the still-sharp, still-untouched, still-coming-into-being rock.
