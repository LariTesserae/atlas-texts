It is a single room.

Not a room that was put inside something else — it is the whole place. Low and wide and almost perfectly round, like a shallow bowl sunk into the ground until its lip is level with the marsh outside. You could walk its diameter in three minutes if you didn't stop to watch your footing. In hamlet-terms, it is a threshing floor, a meeting basin, a cistern. In cathedral-terms, it is a nave built without a church around it.

Everything in it is made.

There is not a patch of exposed earth, not a natural stone in place. The floor is laid — broad, cool slabs of fitted slate and creek stone, all of it carried here, all of it worked by hands. You can see the peck-marks where someone with a stone maul and a harder stone chisel struck and struck and struck until the rock went flat. In the center the slabs sag a little from centuries of their own weight. Between them, moss would be, if there was soil, but instead there is only a black, hairline grout of lime and ash and pounded shell that has cracked and been repacked and cracked again a hundred times.

The walls are not walls but a basket.

From the floor to only a little more than twice a man's height, the whole circumference is woven. Not branches growing — cut saplings, willow and alder, split and bent while green and lashed with rawhide that has gone the color of peat, tied with knots that have names. The weaving is tight at the bottom, then gradually looser as it rises, until near the top it becomes a lattice, a screen. Pitched against the outside of that basket is a revetment of more stone, leaning in, holding it in shape. The whole thing is a human dam holding back a low place in the world, and it has been here so long that it has learned to be in balance. The lean of it is settled. Nothing is about to fall. The places where water has chewed at the stone are smooth. The places where it has chewed at the wood are dark and hard as iron.

It weeps.

That is the water. It doesn't flow in through a door. It comes through. The ground outside is higher than the floor inside, and it is wet country — lowland fen, barely above sea level, cool air that never quite dries. Water sits near the surface out there, in peat and root-mat. In here, it condenses on everything. It beads on the underside of each stone slab and drops. It gathers on the woven walls and runs in slow fingers down the verticals and gathers at the lashings and swells there and falls. The rocks themselves weep. You can hear it — not a stream sound, but hundreds of separate drips, each with its own interval. Plink on stone. Plunk in the shallow film that covers most of the floor. A constant, cold ticking.

In the dim, it is loud.

There are no windows. The light comes through the looseness of the upper weave, gray-white daylight filtered through mist and the bodies of the sedges outside — so dim you have to wait for your pupils to open. And in that waiting, the sound takes over. The dripping multiplies off the slate and the curved wood. The structure is a drum, empty enough to make every noise important.

And it is empty. Almost embarrassingly empty for a thing made entirely by people. No houses inside the hamlet-bowl. No loom or firepit or table. Just the bowl itself. The air inside is vast for its size because there is so little in it to stop the eye. A few things: near one wall, three low stools made of a single forked log each, the seats worn to a shine. Near the other, a pile of shallow ceramic pans, chipped, left stacked and now half-full of clear water with a skin of pollen on it. A coil of rope. A wooden mallet swollen with moisture. That's it. The density is that of a throat just after swallowing.

This is intentional, because it is not for people to live in. It is for the others.

The fauna. They are abundant.

When you step down into it — there is a place where the wall has been deliberately left to make a notch, three stones set as steps — you startle them, but only for a second. The floor film, a half-inch deep, is alive. Thumb-long tadpoles black as the grout. Water beetles skating and diving, leaving brief V's. Across the slate, in their dozens, low, glassy frogs the color of wet slate itself, so you see them only when they move or inflate their throats, which they do constantly. The noise is frogs. Not peeping. A deep, round, wooden *kunk* *kunk* *kunk*, overlapping so fast it becomes a chorus, a loud, living roof of sound that competes with the drips.

Above, in the weave itself, sparrows and wrens have pulled out loose bast and made nests in the interstices of the wall, so many that the whole upper lattice twitters and rustles. Midges cloud. Dragonflies — early for the cool air, but here, in the shelter, they are early — hunt the midges, blue and green flares. On the stone slabs, lichen, which is the only vegetation that has really negotiated a residence. A sparse green-black crust. And in one place, where a pan has cracked and spent its water in the same spot for years, a single clump of rushes has managed to root in lime-grout, thin and yellowish.

Everything made, yet everything used by everything unmade. That is the rule of this place, and everyone who mended it understood it. You don't clean out the pans. The pans are for the frogs to breed in. You don't chase the birds. Their droppings are the only mortar-softener that keeps the upper weave from splitting in the cold. You don't plug the weeps. The weeping is what keeps the floor from ever fully drying and cracking apart.

It is strange — weirder the longer you stand still. Because the water doesn't quite do what it should. It doesn't puddle to the lowest point. For a long time after a cloud passes and the light changes, you notice that some of the beads on the alders run sideways for a moment before they remember to run down. And if you sit on one of the stools, absolutely still while the frogs forget your newness, the condensation over your head gathers into air. Not mist. Little perfect spheres, maybe twenty at a time, hanging for a second a hand's breadth below the lattice, trembling, then raining straight down, all at once, as if the room inhaled and let go. The old builders must have noticed it. One of the stone slabs near the center is cupped, not flat, worn not by tools but by a steady, impossible drip upward from its lip — a tiny stalagmite grown from the center of empty air.

It is cool. Your breath makes a faint mark even though outside it is not cold enough for it. The stone leaches heat from your feet through your soles. The frogs go on singing. The basket creaks very slightly, expanding and contracting, wood and stone negotiating.

You understand quickly that this was not built to be kept dry. It was built to hold a little piece of wet, low, loud, living emptiness in place — a room made to keep a moment of the marsh after rain, forever.
