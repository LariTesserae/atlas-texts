It is not on the mountain. It is *in* the top of it.

The summit ridge is a knife of old basalt, 4000 meters up where the air goes thin and blue and useless, all wind-scoured and lichen-black. The world drops away on both sides into cloud sea. Nothing grows up here except the wind itself. And then — right near the top, where the rock folds over like a lip — the mountain has a missing tooth.

A shaft. A throat. No wider than a small house, no deeper than two men stacked. You could walk past it and miss it, because the wind screams over the mouth and the snow never settles inside.

Inside, the rules change.

The rock here is not young and shattered. It's settled. Water and time and that constant thin wind have smoothed everything into curves — bowed walls, scooped bowls, pillars worn down to blunt, patient knuckles. The basalt is dark red and glassy where it has been polished by a million wings. It still holds the day's heat, and more than that. The stone itself is warm. If you put your palm flat to it, it is like touching a living animal. Breath comes up from somewhere deeper in the mountain, slow geothermal air, humid and mineral. This close to the sky and it should be freezing. It is not. It is warm as a mouth.

Light barely gets in. The entrance is a ragged oval askew to the sky, and for most of an hour it frames only a hard white coin of glare, too bright to look at, that illuminates nothing below. The rest of the day it is the color of deep dusk. Your eyes never fully adjust. What little light comes down hits the wet walls and dies.

Because it is damp. Not wet — there is no spring, no stream. The air itself weeps. Cloud packs from the abyss below hit the warm throat and condense instantly, a constant cold sweat that beads and runs in dark threads down the rock, and collects in shallow pitted cups in the stone. Pockets. Some no bigger than a palm, some big as a basin, all full of black mirror water. They never evaporate. They never fill and spill. They just wait. Every few days a storm finds this high and hammers straight down for ten minutes — a hard, sideways rain that cannot enter properly and instead just licks across the mouth — and the pockets shiver and refill.

Thin rivulets keep trails through gray filaments — not plants, not really. A few hardy things: a mat of black lichen like dried tar, a single clump of wiry alpine fern that has forgotten what sun is, its fronds bleached to almost transparent, trembling. And here and there, a tuft of stiff white grass growing out of guano, improbable. That's all. The rock is otherwise naked.

It is not empty.

It is dense. That is the first thing you feel before you hear it. The air in the chamber is thick with bodies.

They cover the walls. Every inch of the in-blown, weathered rock has something clinging to it — thousands and thousands of them, layered and shingled and pressed shoulder-to-shoulder in the dark. High mountain swifts, but not like any swift you know. Heavy, soot-colored birds with long, hooked claws and naked heads, their wings folded tight, their eyes shut. Between them, on them, under them: beetles the size of your thumb. Black, domed, their elytra varnished by damp. They move slowly, so slowly, over the skin of birds who do not wake. The birds tolerate them. The beetles clean them.

And there are other things. Silk. The back walls, where the dark is complete, are tunneled with pale veils — not spider-web, denser, felted — in which pale larvae pulse and breathe.

There is no room to breathe. The chamber is a body in itself, packed.

And it is loud.

In the day, it is a low, continuous, unenduraged noise — a rustle of ten thousand feathers shifting, a soft chittering that is not individual voices but a single composite hum, the click of beetle feet on rock that sounds like rain, the incessant drip-drip-drip from the walls into the rock pools. Then, at some signal you cannot see — a change in pressure, a cooling — they all wake at once.

It is not an explosion. It is slow. One bird opens its eyes, then another. Wings unfold inch by patient inch. The noise doubles, then triples — creaks, coughs, a million small claws scraping basalt, a high looping whistle from far back in the throat of the hollow.

They do not leave. There is nowhere to go at this hour. They are waiting.

What they do while they wait is the strange part.

The beetles do it. They are tool users. Not builders — there is no nest, no structure, no dwelling. But each beetle drags. On the ledges around the water pockets, you can see small arrangements: careful crescents of quartz pebbles, wind-broken basalt chips, placed grain by grain on the lip of each basin that is facing the mouth. Little low dams, no higher than a knuckle. Dew walls. The condensation that would sheet evenly down the rock and be lost is caught by these pebbles and guided, beaded, down in one guided drop into the pool below. Some beetles tend them, moving a stone a millimeter left, right, their mandibles shiny. The birds watch them with closed eyes and lean down and drink, very delicately, without ever stepping on the pebble-work.

No one taught them. It's just what happens here, in this one room at the top of the world, where it is warm and dark and damp and old. The rock made the bowls. The cloud made the water. The birds brought the heat in their blood. The beetles made the plumbing.

When the wind outside shifts and the cloud sea below boils up and over the lip for a second, a brief spear of wet light falls straight down into the shaft and the whole chamber for an instant glitters — water, eye-shine, beetle backs, pale wings — and the noise stops, all at once, as every beak and mandible turns up to taste the new air.
