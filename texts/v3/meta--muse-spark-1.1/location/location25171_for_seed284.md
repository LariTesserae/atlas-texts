You would miss it if you weren't looking for it. From the outside, there is only the mountain.

And not even the mountain, really — just the very last idea of the mountain. The high knife-ridge where the world gives up. You are at extreme elevation, above any weather that has a name. The air is too thin to carry snow. Whatever water was ever here sublimed away a long time ago or never arrived. This is the altocold, the blue-black. At noon the sky is deep indigo and the sun is a hard coin that gives no heat. At this height, cool means something precise: a constant, windless, cellular cold that gets into the bolts and the bones. -10C. -15C. The kind of cold that preserves, that keeps everything sharp.

The rock itself is young. That's the strange part. This whole range is old, beaten down, but here a new thing has punched through — a dike of black glass, basalt that cooled too fast to learn how to be soft. It split as it cooled. The split is the place.

A fissure. Young enough that the edges still match if you wanted to close them again. Fresh, conchoidal, glossy in places where the wind has never touched. Three meters wide at its widest, thirty meters long, maybe forty meters deep, a perfect, clean wound in the summit. From above, it's a dark line you could step over without noticing. From inside, it's a street. A world.

It is dry in a way that deserts only aspire to. There is no ice in the fissure, no seep, no drip. The air at this elevation holds nothing. The stone is exhaled barren. On the scale of chemistry, there might be a monolayer of frost that forms on the lee wall just before dawn and burns off before it can be called water. The inhabitants don't wait for it. They have other liquids — cutting oil, black coolant that circulates and never evaporates, low-grade kerosene that burns orange and thick.

Because it is inhabited. Densely.

The first time you drop down — and you have to drop, there's a knotted cable left for that purpose — you think you have entered a vertical town built for insects. Every face of the sharp rock has been drilled, pinned, and hung with structure. Not carved — built. Catwalks of rebar grid, platforms made from flattened fuel drums, walls made from shipping pallets never meant to be at 7,000 meters, homes that are just bolted-together tool chests with a curtain riveted across. It is urban, unmistakably, in the way a favela is urban: no single plan, but total organization. Load paths, chimneys, gutters for dust, cable runs, all agreed upon by necessity. You can see three or four generations of building layered over each other — older, rust-pale scaffolds now swallowed as foundations for newer, brighter yellow scaffolds. Ladders go everywhere and nowhere. Everything is close. Shoulder-close. Ceiling-close. The whole settlement is near-touching.

And it moves. Quietly, but it moves all the time.

That is the turbulence of the place. It is not storm-turbulence, there is no wind inside the slot. It is bazaar-turbulence, anthill-turbulence. Because the fissure walls are still settling, still ticking as the glass relieves its birth stress. A loud CRACK like a rifle at 3 a.m. and a whole section of street shifts two inches down. No one yells. In the morning, everyone is out with pry bars and chain hoists, jacking their house back level, re-routing a conduit, welding a new knee brace. By noon it looks different. By evening it will look different again. Nothing is ever finished. The town is in a state of continuous, low-grade, collaborative repair. The sound of that is mostly quiet — the high air robs sound of its body — so you hear the tiny, intimate noises: the chink of a spanner dropped and caught, the hush-hiss of a small forge, the zipping of a tin door.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a stain of algae. The mineral is too new, too clean. What feeds the abundant fauna is the town itself.

Fauna here means many. It means numberless. The crevice is warm with them. First, the rock-lice — flat, pale, fast, each the size of a thumbnail, that graze the iron filings and the sheddings of the forges and need no water, only iron oxide and cold. Then the things that eat them: the soot-moths, with wings like dirty fiberglass, who come in silent clouds to the worklights. Then the things that eat them: the gorge-swifts, choughs, and feather-legged cave bats that have learned to live without ever drinking, that nest by the dozens on every ledge and in every abandoned junction box, their droppings carefully scraped up and rendered down by the residents for nitrates. And the domesticated fauna too — squat, tight-coated rock-cavies kept in stacked hutches, breathing shallow in the thin air, raised for heat and for leather and for company.

And the residents. Human or human enough. In quilted overalls black with oil that will never wash out. Everyone wears a dust mask even though there is no storm, because the air itself is metal. Everyone wears goggles. Their industry is small and scaled precisely to the crevice. They run lathes bolted directly into basalt. They rewind electric motors. They make more pins, more brackets, more chain. The whole town appears to be a factory whose product is the maintenance of the factory. Ingots come from nowhere — probably flown in by a single helicopter once a year and left in a cache a hundred meters below — and leave as nothing, as repair. The light is industrial and dim: strings of amber 40-watt caged bulbs run on a diesel generator that you feel more than hear, a low thrum in the rock, and the white-blue sputter of arc-welders, and the moths.

If you stay long enough, you understand the rule: density protects. At this height, on this sharp new stone, nothing survives alone. Not the moth that needs the bulb, not the bat that needs the moth, not the person who needs the cavy and the oil and the neighbor's scaffold to hold up their own. So they crowd. They build within arm's reach. They stack their noise on top of each other until the quiet becomes a kind of agreement.

From above, if you stepped back, you would not see it at all. The sun goes behind the ridge and the fissure goes dark and the generator clicks off for the night to save fuel. The cold deepens. In thirty meters of hidden street, a hundred bodies breathe shallow, and wings fold, and metal ticks as it contracts. Tomorrow a wall will crack again.
