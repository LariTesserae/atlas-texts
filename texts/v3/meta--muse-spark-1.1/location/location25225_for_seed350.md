It is not under the ocean. The ocean is here, but it has forgotten how to be an ocean.

This is a low place — a basin, a drowned valley maybe three kilometers across, rim to rim, though you can't see the rim most of the time. The elevation is wrong for what is happening here. It should have drained. It didn't. The water came and stayed and then kept coming.

The water is too much water. Not sea-level rise, more like sea-level presence. It hangs. At any given moment about two-thirds of any vertical line you draw here is liquid. The rest is trying to be. The air is so saturated it's a second, thinner ocean. Warm rain falls hard enough to be a wall moving sideways, warm as blood, warm as bathwater left overnight, and it never actually stops. It hits the standing water below — and the standing water is not still.

Because the basin is full, choked full, of green.

No animals. That's the first wrong thing your body notices before your mind catches up. Lush doesn't mean alive the way you think lush means. This is plant density at a level that should be screaming insects, fish, birds. There is nothing. No buzz, no dart, no ripple from a fin. Only growth.

Kelp does not grow in freshwater warm flood basins. Neither do mangroves with black buttress roots, nor lily pads the size of manhole covers, nor vines that are clearly algae, nor ferns that have become aquatic and hang down in curtains twenty meters long, rooting from nothing. But they did here, and they are all growing at once, on top of each other, through each other. The water column is a layered greenhouse. In the deep — six, eight meters, low enough that the light gives out — there are trunks, actual trunks, pale and swollen and splitting as they grow, still sharp where they burst through concrete. They are new. The whole geology is new. Nothing here is rounded. Rebar snarls, freshly snapped basalt, quarry walls that have never felt weather, all cut with a glass edge. This place emerged yesterday, or was ripped open yesterday, and the jungle decided that was long enough to wait.

The light never fully arrives. The water is thick with tannin and leaf-matter and iron, and the canopy — not a tree canopy, a canopy of lily and bladder-wrack and what might be giant industrial air-filter moss, now wild — steals the rest. It's perpetual dusk at noon, a green-black dim where shafts of sun come down through the rain as solid columns, visible, vibrating. You can see dust motes in them, except it's not dust, it's pollen from plants that should not make this much pollen with no pollinators. The water glows faintly by itself when disturbed.

And it's deafening.

You would think a place with no fauna would be silent. It's the opposite. The sound is the main feature.

First, the water: rain hitting the surface of the basin with the force of a fire hose against a tin roof across an entire valley. A million hectares of drum skin. The roar never syncs up. Second, the plants growing: you can actually hear it in this density, the creak and pop of cellulose forcing itself larger, bladders bursting, fronds unfurling with a wet hydraulic sigh, trunks cracking their own bark to get wider.

And the machines.

Scattered — that’s the word — but large when you find them. They are not ruins. Ruins are dead. These are industrial and they are still running, with nobody to tend them. Half-submerged pump-houses the size of apartment blocks, choked in vine but still shuddering on their foundations, boilers still lit after years, chugging. Sluice gates opening and closing on timers, trying to drain a place that is the drain. Conveyor frames that carry nothing now but move anyway, shrieking where the leaves have gotten into the belt. Aerators big as Ferris wheels tumbling slowly in the dark, turning the whole basin into a churning broth of bubbles and shredded leaf. Transformers humming a deep 60hz thrum that you feel in your molars more than your ears. All of it electrified, all of it wet, all of it spitting sparks into warm rain where it should have shorted out and died a long time ago.

It doesn't. This is the reality breakdown.

Gravity here has a stutter. Water falls down, but in some places it also falls up, or sideways, draining off a leaf into the air and then holding there, a trembling lens of water a foot across that stays until a vine grows through it. Pressure is negotiable. At four meters down, you can still breathe if you are inside a pocket of mangrove roots, because the water decides that root-cage is air for a while. Time is not faster, exactly — growth is just happening at the wrong rate. You watch a shoot that was a nub when you blinked start to fur into leaf in thirty seconds. Then you watch the pump next to it spin backwards for a minute and un-rust, then go forward and rust again.

The rules:

1. Water wants to be in every place at once.
2. Green wants to fill whatever water leaves empty for a nanosecond.
3. The industrial infrastructure wants to keep the conditions exactly as they were on the day the operators left — warm, flooded, powered, turbulent — and it has the power to do it.

The three forces fight constantly. That's the turbulence. That's the deafening. It isn't chaos, it's hyperactive competition. The factory churns, the water crashes, the jungle grows through both in real-time, splitting steel, getting sliced by an impeller, growing back around it thicker.

You wade — or swim — through warm, chest-deep, leaf-soup water that is too thick, pushing aside mats that are already re-knitting behind you. The bottom is not mud but shredded roots over sharp, young rock and concrete. Your foot catches a submerged catwalk railing, still bolted down, still vibrating. Somewhere to your left, a pressure valve blows and screams white steam into the rain. Somewhere to your right, a fresh mangrove erupts ten feet in the space of a gasp and dumps its flowers directly into the current. There is no animal to startle. The flowers fall unwatched.

If you stay still too long, the vines will find the heat of you. They do not eat you. There is no digestion without fauna, somehow. They just want to include you in the density, to layer you in.
