High up, where the mountain has gone past being a mountain.

You are above the last snow that never quite falls anymore, above the lichen line, above where air starts to have an edge to it. The rock here is wrong for how high you are. It isn't the old, wind-scoured granite of the summit ridge. This is new rock. Black, raw, blistered. Basalt that came up fast and cooled too quick when the last pulse lifted this range. It hasn't learned to be mountain yet. No scree has had time to pile, no water has cut it. It is swollen and sealed, like bread that split in the oven.

You find the split because the wind stops there.

A vertical fissure, no wider than your shoulders, maybe a meter deep — and at the back of it, where there should be solid stone, there is a pocket. A bubble. A single room the earth failed to close.

You have to turn sideways and slip your head inside to see it, and the heat hits you first.

It is an oven. Not sun-hot, but earth-hot. The pocket holds the day's heat and its own, radiating off the walls. The rock inside is still warm to the touch, 60, maybe 70 degrees centigrade. Dark red-black, with a faint, glassy sheen. When you touch it your fingerprint comes away in fine, dry powder that smells like struck iron. This place has never been wet. There is no seep, no condensation, no salt crust of old water. It is a dry kiln. The air inside is bottled and contains only what the mountain exhales — hot mineral dust that coats your throat instantly.

And it is full.

At first, in the near-absolute dark, you think the back wall is moving. That the rock itself is crawling.

Then your eyes adjust to the almost-light — not light at all, really, but the dull cherry glow coming from the floor and from the bodies themselves.

They are beetles. Or crickets. Or something between, something that belongs only up here. Each one as long as your thumb, flat-backed, matte black armor, legs tucked tight. There is no space between them. The pocket is perhaps a meter and a half across at most, a rough sphere half-filled with living matter, packed shoulder to armor-shoulder, back to back, a living solid. Abundant is too small a word. This is saturation. You can see nowhere in this tiny room that isn't insect.

And they are working.

There is no nest. No built architecture, no wax, no web, no tunnel shoring. No artifice at all. But the colony is an industry. That is the only word for it.

The ones on the bottom layer, pressed against the hot glass floor, are miners. Their mandibles — oversized, pale, almost white against their black heads — scrape the soft, just-born basalt. It gives under them like cooling toffee. You can hear it. God, you can hear it. In a space this small, contained in stone, the sound is deafening. A constant, loud, dry chittering rasp, thousands of jaws filing stone at once, a factory floor of chitin on rock. *Chik-chik-chik-chik-chik.* It rises and falls in slow waves, not because they communicate, but because the ones in front tire and the ones behind press forward.

The ones in the middle are furnaces. You can see it now — their backs glow. Not bioluminescence, but heat. They take the scraped mineral dust in, and their abdomens, fat and translucent, run a low smelt. Inside them, something industrial and ancient and completely biological is refining. They are hot to the touch, hotter than the rock. They shudder, patiently, and excrete behind them a single perfect bead, black and metallic, that drops and clicks against the beads below.

The ones on top, near your face, are the coolers, the carriers. They take the beads in their jaws and hold them, passing them hand to hand, mandible to mandible, until the beads lose their glow.

It is an assembly line without ever being assembled. No blueprint. The pocket is the whole world, and the whole world is this one task being done in a slow, slow cycle. This is why the sound is loud but the motion is slow — each individual moves only millimeters per minute, a gradual, patient press, but together the noise is overwhelming. A room-sized piston engine running on muscle.

They do not notice you. Or you are just another hot surface.

No plant has ever grown in here. No fungus even. There is nothing to eat, except the mountain itself. And there is no water to spare for green things. The beetles get everything they need from the raw stone and the thin, hot, iron-tasting air. They are dry things, themselves like animated mineral. If you hold your breath, you can hear their bodies tick with heat expansion.

This is what an emerging landscape is, before wind and water get to carve it up. Before erosion makes valleys. It makes a single, hidden room, and fills it completely with hot, loud, industrious life that has no use for light, no use for water, no use for anything but the fact that the rock is new and soft enough to chew.

If you pull your head back out, the cold high-altitude wind will hit you again in an instant. From outside, there is no sign. Just black glass and a crack that looks like nothing.
