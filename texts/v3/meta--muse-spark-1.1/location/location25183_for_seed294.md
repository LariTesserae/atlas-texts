There is no ground here anymore.

Not in any way you can point to and say *this is bedrock*. If you drop down far enough - kilometers, through the cold which gets colder - you might find it, some last basaltic plate that this whole thing started on. No one lives down there. Nothing that lives here has ever seen it.

This is the Top. A high place under a deep weight.

Imagine a plateau the size of a country, lifted up so high in the ocean that the abyss is still underneath it, black on all sides. Above you, the water itself is the sky - unbroken, two miles of water straight up before it stops being water, and even then it does not become air, it just becomes a lighter, colder water. The light that makes it down this far is not sunlight. Or it was, once. By now it is late-afternoon-at-the-end-of-the-world filtered through miles of moving glass, a dim blue-gray that has no source. It doesn't cast shadows. It simply lesses and mores.

And the Top itself is built.

Everything is built. This is not a reef on a rock. This is a reef that ate the rock. For an age that cannot be measured in years, only in thicknesses, things have lived here that secrete, that stack, that cement. Not with tools. Never with tools. There is no hand, no tool-mark, no joint. What they do is what coral does, what wasps do, what bone does. They exude.

So the plateau is not flat. It is a city without architects, ancient to the point of geology. Broad hummocks and low vaulted terraces rise a hundred meters and then smooth out, worn by current until they are soft as a molar. Long causeways curve for kilometers, not to go anywhere, just because that is the shape the builders make when they follow each other's chemical trails. There are bowls big enough to hold villages, perfect circles, their rims worn round and pitted. There are chimneys that never had fire, hollow spires of layered carbonate and silica that open at the top and vent the cold water from below. The material is all the same life-stone: when it is new it is pearlescent white, when it is old it yellows to bone, when it is truly ancient it goes dark, glassy black like river stone polished for a million years. Most of it here is that black. The ancient final form.

There is nothing green here. No kelp, no weed, no algae film. The water is too cold and too poor and too high and, perhaps, the builders poison it against anything that would root in their work. What grows instead is structure. If you scrape the wall you find only more wall, concentric lines like tree rings, each ring a generation.

But it is not empty.

God, it is not empty. The fauna is everywhere because the fauna *are* the place. You cannot at first tell the animal from the building.

The masons are sparse, scattered. That is the rule of this cold: much space between presences. For a long while you will see only the empty terraces and think you are alone. Then a patch of the terrace detaches. A thing the size of a door, flat, fringed with twenty translucent cilia that beat so slowly, pinwheels up into the water, lifts a mouth that is also an anus, and lands a little further on, and begins to lay down a millimeter of new stone from its belly. A mile away another answers it, not with sound, with a puff of milky chemistry that hangs in the cold water like smoke.

Smaller things, the cleaners, move in clouds - not fish, not shrimp, but something between. They are clear as gelatin, each with a single black eye and a gut full of black stone dust. They comb the surfaces endlessly. The scallop-things — barnacled discs a meter across — open and shut, open and shut, not breathing, but testing the current.

And it is all in violent motion even as it is silent.

This is the turbulent part, the part you wouldn't expect from such old stone. The water here never rests. Because this plateau is high, it catches the global currents that slide around the planet like belts. They hit its escarpment and break. So the dim world is always, always moving. A slow hurricane in place. The chemistry smoke is ripped apart seconds after release. The cleaner clouds are flung into long streamers, then snap back. The big flat masons must grip with all their cilia or be tumbled for kilometers.

Whole sections of ancient terrace, undermined by a century of this friction, give way at once - not with a crash - you will never hear a crash here, the water swallows sound. You will see a hillside you thought was permanent lift, crack along an old seam, and dissolve into a slow avalanche of black plates that tumble down into the lower bowls. Before the dust has even settled, the masons are there, dozens of them converging from down-current, laying down white fresh stone over the wound. Destruction and repair happening in the same minute, endless. The city is constantly unmaking and remaking itself, every day a little different, but never changing its overall shape because that shape is what survives the current.

This is why it is so eroded and so new at once.

Sound: there is almost none. A low, bone-conducted hrum you feel in your teeth from the water pressing over the plateau edge. The click of scallop-things. Sometimes a thin, high whine when the current is at its worst and squeezes through the chimney spires. Otherwise it is quiet enough to hear the cleaners brushing stone. Quiet enough to hear your own heart, if you had a heart that could beat in this cold.

Cold. The rule is cold. Four degrees above freezing and never more. It keeps everything slow, except the water. Your fingers — if you reached — would go numb in seconds. The masons do not mind. They lay their white.

If you stand — and you cannot, but if you could — on one of the highest glassy knolls, you would see it extending out in all directions, town after valley after town, all built, all empty-looking, all alive, all dim under the weight above. No light on the horizon. No surface ever. And the current over your head pulling long banners of silt that never settle, and the flat builders continuing their endless, mindless, total work.
