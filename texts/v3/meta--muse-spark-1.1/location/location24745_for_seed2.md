It is all under water, and it is all built.

You are below the surface. Not just beneath the water's surface, beneath the other surface too — the crust, the top of the world. The ocean here doesn't have a sky. It has a ceiling. The water is held. It is the atmosphere.

The scale is what hits you first, or it would if you could see it all at once, which you can't. The mind can't hold it flat. This is a sea that was made, poured into a mold the size of a continent. Or larger. The floor is not seafloor. It's poured. Grade after grade of industrial concrete, still sharp at the seams, still showing the grain of the formwork it was cast in, still showing the half-moon bite marks of the boring machines that cut the space. Elevation zero is not the bottom. It's *the* bottom. There is no natural substrate left to find. If you go down far enough you hit rebar, and steel plate, and then more concrete. The world is a foundation.

And it is empty. That is the second thing. For something so totally constructed, it is almost completely void. The builders did not fill it. They framed it and left it hollow.

They left pillars. That is the terrain.

Trunks of concrete and riveted steel, each one a kilometer across at the base, tapering as they rise through the cold blue. You can see maybe three at a time before the water takes the rest. They are set out on a grid that is too big to be called a grid — it feels like a law of geography, like longitude — receding into haze until they are just slightly darker water. Between them: nothing. Four, five, ten kilometers of open, clear, cold water. The water is so clear because there is almost nothing in it to cloud it. No silt. No sand. Nothing to erode except the structures themselves, and they are young. Their edges are still sharp. The corners of buttresses, the lips of service galleries, the teeth of spillways — they haven't been rounded by time. You can see where a pillar has only just begun to scale, a fresh lip of concrete spalling off in a clean sheet to expose the dark iron beneath.

Light comes from above, even though above is not sky. Normal, white-yellow, workmanlike light. Panels set into the underside of the ceiling — the true surface — a lid of interlocked plate that stretches end to end without horizon. It is not one sun but a million industrial suns, fluorescent banks and broad arc lamps and glassy skylights that pipe surface light down through kilometers of lightwell. In this water it doesn't scatter like deep-sea gloom. It penetrates. It throws columns down through the water the way sun does in a cathedral. You get noon everywhere, at depth. You can read a gauge at a thousand meters.

The water is cool. Not freezing. The cold of a warehouse in early spring, a cold that makes metal ache. It keeps sound fast. And it is loud here.

Constant. The whole volume hums. Low, you feel it in your sternum before you hear it: the thrum of circulation engines the size of city blocks bolted to the floor, drawing the sea in and pushing it out again to keep it from stagnating, to keep this bathtub continent from stratifying. Mid-range, the creak and ping of the structure itself contracting in the cool — concrete ticking, steel shuddering, a whole continent settling. High, the rush of water moving around the pillars, not a tide but a manufactured weather. Because the water here *has* weather. Currents come through like jet streams, thirty kilometers wide, cold rivers inside the cold sea, carrying temperature differentials from one end of the Reservoir to the other. You hear them before they hit you as a rising hiss.

There is almost no plant life. No one planted it. A few things have taken hold where the light is strongest and the concrete leaches minerals — sparse beards of green-black algae on the windward edges of pillars, bacterial mats in orange and white that peel in sheets and drift like paper. A few kelp analogs that hitched in on construction equipment, now clinging to maintenance gantries, stunted by the cold and the lack of soil, their holdfasts pried into bolt-holes.

But the animal life — the animal life is abundant.

That's the rule of this world: if you build an empty ocean and light it, life will come to fill the emptiness, but not the life you planned.

It is full of movement. Schools of silver fish that were seeded here as a food system and then went wild, now running in bodies so large they bend light, millions strong, turning on a line between pillars. Filterers. Whale-sized things that aren't whales, grown fat on the fish and on the churned particulates, their bodies scarred from scraping against catwalks. Swarms of shrimp-like things that click, that endless tinnitus under the engine drone. Eels that live inside the drainage pipes by the thousands, spilling out to hunt. And on the structures themselves: anemones the size of doors, barnacles thick as armor plating over every submerged girder, crabs with industrial tolerances, pale and blind, farming the algae.

They all use the built world like a reef that was never a reef. The pump intakes are caves that breathe. The service ladders — each rung big as a house — are terraces. A gantry crane left extended a century ago is now a backbone for a colony.

You move through it not by swimming — the distances are too vast — but by riding. The current takes you, or you hook onto the maintenance barges that still run on old schedules, grinding along cables stretched between pillars, their electric motors whining. If you stop and hold a pillar, put your hand to it, you feel it: the vibration of water and engines and life, and beneath that, the unnervingly smooth, still-curing cool of deep concrete. Young. This place hasn't been here long, in geological terms. It was made recently, for a reason no one remembers down here.

It hasn't learned to be silent yet.
