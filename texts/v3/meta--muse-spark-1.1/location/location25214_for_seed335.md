You find it by accident, at low tide, when the shelf leans.

This stretch of coast is low — barely above the sea, so low you think you are still in the intertidal until you realize the ground under your boots is not beach but fresh rock. It is young rock, days or years young, not millions. A black basalt flow that came up last winter when the ice retreated and the weight came off. It came up sharp. Nothing has had time to soften it. The plates are still stacked like broken crockery, edges knife-bright, with a glassy sheen. No soil. No rounding. If you put your hand down you will cut it.

And in between two of those plates, where they bucked against each other and didn't quite meet, there is a mouth. Not a cave. A pocket. If you didn't crouch right at its lip at the right hour you would miss it completely. The wind finds it before you do.

Inside is bigger than it should be.

It's an intimate room, maybe ten meters long, five across, three high at the highest — you could touch both walls if you spread your arms and wanted to bleed — but it feels hollowed out far beyond its measure. Because almost nothing is in it. The density is wrong. There is air and wet and light and then, intermittently, a life. The emptiness is structural. It is a held breath.

It is cold. Not killing cold, not deep polar cold. Working cold. The kind of cold that keeps things from spoiling. Your exhalations don't plume so much as they hang and then sink, and are pulled away. The air itself is damp to saturation. Every surface weeps.

There is no built thing here. No nail, no cut, no pile of stones that says someone was here and tried to make stay. And yet, the place is industrial.

You hear it before you understand the shape. A loud, entire place-loudness that occupies the chest. 

When the tide outside pushes, even a little, even twenty meters away through cracks you cannot see, the pocket inhales. Air is forced through a sharp keyhole at the back and whistles, then screams — a pure factory whistle, a boiler letting go. At the same moment water is driven up through the floor. The floor is not solid. It's a false floor of thin basalt plates over a chambered surge pool, and each plate is a piston. The sea below jacks them. They rise an inch, two, judder, vent steam-cold spray from their seams, then slam back down with a hydraulic clank. Plok-THOOM. Plok-THOOM. Water fountains up in time, hits the low ceiling, and rains.

So it is always raining in here, even when it is not raining out there. The rock is always shedding. The ceiling sweats and condenses and drips. The walls weep constantly, beads running down the glass to join the film at your feet. Your shoulders are wet in seconds.

In that dim, the light that does get in has been filtered twice — once through the overcast, once through three feet of old blue sea-ice that leaned over the mouth and never quite left. So by the time it reaches the interior it's not daylight anymore. It's a low, held, submarine dusk, gray-blue and sourceless, just enough to see the gloss of wet.

And it should be barren. By every rule of cold and new stone it should be barren. It isn't.

The vegetation is impossibly lush for a cold room with no sun. The rock is upholstered. Where the spray hits most, there are mats of moss, but not the dry arctic kind. These are swollen, engorged mosses, black-green and russet, leaves fat with stored water, growing in dense bulging pillows that hang over the edge of each plate. Between them, liverwort sheets like wet leather, and long filamentous algae that grow straight out from the weeping walls in green-black hair, swaying in the piston wind. They pulse when the water hits them. The entire room breathes with them. You touch one moss pillow and it pours, releasing a day's worth of condensation at once.

It is empty and it is full. The rule here is interval.

The fauna keep their distance from one another but there are a lot of them, all small, all loud in the amplification of the stone. First you notice the amphipods, thousands of them — translucent shrimplets the size of your thumbnail that ride the piston spray. When a plate lifts they are thrown into the air like sparks off an anvil, they click against the ceiling, they land in the moss and kick. Then the midges. A cloud of them that lives in the air-pocket above the water, winter midges that shouldn't fly in cold but do, their bodies producing their own faint heat. Their whine layers over the whistle of the rocks, an electrical whine.

And deeper, in the moss pillows, you hear rustling that is too large. Storm-petrels, two, maybe three. They've tucked themselves into the sharpest part of the back wall where the stone is dry-ish. Ice-gray birds with too-large eyes for day. They watch you, huched over nests made of stolen algae and their own down. They don't call often, but when they do it is a shrieked metal shriek that echoes like a saw hitting.

Everything here is working. The tide is an engine. The moss is a mill, taking in sea-fog and making peat by force. The amphipods are conveyor belts, moving marine protein up onto rock. The midges are sparks. The birds are supervisors. It is a factory with no walls built, no workers employed, no owner.

If you sit — carefully, there is one naturally scooped dryish plate that fits a human — and you stay long enough to be ignored, the rhythm establishes itself. Suck of air through the keyhole. Lift — spray — clatter — drain. Suck. Lift. Spray. The moss compresses and releases. The lights of midge eyes. The green hairs sway. A petrel shifts and its foot scrapes, bright against basalt.

It condenses on your eyelashes. It drips off your chin. The stone has only just been born and already it has learned to pump, to weep, to manufacture a little pocket of fullness inside emptiness, and to be very, very loud about it.
