You find it by accident, because it is not meant to be found.

On the high moor where the peat cuts deep and the wind has taken everything tall, there is a place where the ground gives a little under your boot. Not soft. Hollow. You pull back the mat of heather and dead moss and there's a throat, not wider than a well bucket, going straight down a meter and a half through black root and raw peat into stone.

It is not a cave. Caves are systems. This is a single pocket. A room that the earth made and then forgot to connect to anything else.

You have to turn sideways to drop in. Your shoulders brush both walls at once. The stone here is new.

That is the first wrong thing.

It is not weathered. It is not smoothed by water or licked by old rivers. The basalt — or something like basalt, only paler, a blue-grey that looks like wet concrete — is broken fresh. Every edge is still sharp. Feathered, conchoidal scars where it came apart for the first time, yesterday, or a hundred million years ago. No dust in the corners. No sand. Pre-erosion. It looks embryonic, like the world had just learned how to make a room and hadn't yet learned how to finish it.

And it is bright.

This far down, under a lid of peat, with no sky, it should be dark. Instead the light is everywhere, flat and white and sourceless at first, until your eyes adjust and you see it is coming from the occupants.

The chamber is perhaps three meters across, low enough you must crouch, shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. And it is packed.

Density like that shouldn't exist in a wild place. There is no room between presences. No air, almost. The walls themselves are alive.

Sparse vegetation — just a few things, really. Three or four livid white root tendrils have nosed through a crack in the ceiling, too thick to be roots, too pale. They hang an inch, no more, tipped with a single swollen droplet that never falls. They do not grow. Around their bases, a film of mineral-green slime, sparse, dry to the look of it, but beading with condensation.

Everything else is animal.

Thousands of them. Maybe tens of thousands. You cannot count because they are not layered; they are *inlaid*. Small, damp bodies, each about as long as your thumb joint, translucent as river shrimp, blind, with soft backs and too many folded legs tucked under them. Cave hoppers, brood mites, a thing that is not either. Their shells are glass. And inside each shell, a bright filament runs the length of its belly, burning white.

They cover every surface. The floor is them. The walls are them, pressed flank to flank, head to tail, fitted together so tightly you cannot see the stone beneath except in the fissures where the fresh rock is too sharp even for them. They are belly-to-belly in the low places, a living cobblestone. Where your boots land, there is a small, faint crunch that is not a killing — there is no space to kill into — just a slight giving.

There are buildings.

Not human. Not tool-made. Scattered through the packed mass, raised just a little above the level of backs, are tiny, careful domes. A dozen, maybe more. Each no bigger than a thimble, made of cemented grains, of minute polished pebbles, of shed translucent skins glued together with spit. Papery, perfect, empty. A wasp in miniature might have dreamed them. They are all open, doorways facing in, and they are dark inside even though everything else is bright. The owners are here somewhere in the pack, indistinguishable now from the rest.

It is cool in here. Not cold. A cellar cool, a baptismal cool, the kind that makes the sweat on your neck turn to gooseflesh. The air is damp — not wet, but always about to be. Condensation beads on the blue-grey raw walls, on the root tips, on the bright backs of the packed animals, and never runs. The droplets are fixed like glass beads. The one on the root nearest your face has swollen to bursting but does not burst.

And it is loud.

A single, continuous, layered chord, so loud it vibrates your molars. It is the sound of all of them rubbing one leg against another, or breathing through spiracles, or resonating their light organs. A cicada whine, a beehive roar, a choir holding an organ note forever. You expect it to pulse. It doesn't.

Nothing here pulses.

That is the rule.

The dynamic is zero. This is a place in equilibrium, time-invariant. The light does not flicker. The sound does not rise or fall. The water does not drip. The root does not grow an inch further. The builders do not return to their domes. The packed multitude does not shift, does not feed, does not die. They are not dead — the light in them is too fierce for dead things, too immediate — but they are not moving either. Not even a tremor of a leg. A photograph that is singing.

If you hold your breath long enough you realize the reason you feel watched is not eyes, but pressure. The sheer occupation of space. No empty niche. No ecological gap. Every possible co-ordinate taken.

You came down still moving, still dripping, still warm and sequential, and now the room imposes its rule on you. Your heart is too loud against its loudness. Your blinking feels obscene.

If you stay long enough, you understand the weirdness not as horror but as mercy: this pocket sealed itself the day the stone broke open. It caught a single instant — damp, bright, cool, overfull, mid-hum — and kept it. Not preserved. Just... never let go of it. A bubble of stopped time below the moor, unfinished geology holding a crowd that will never disperse.

You have to climb back up by pulling on those white roots that will not give, pushing off backs that will not indent, toward the small ragged hole of grey sky where wind still moves and erosion still happens and things are allowed to finish.
