It is low. That is the first thing. Not low like a valley cut down into something older — low like the world hasn't finished lifting itself up yet.

This is a new place. The stone is new.

The basin is broad and shallow, a few kilometers across from one hazy margin to the other, and every surface in it is still angry from being made. Black rock, still warm to the touch. Not sanded, not rounded, not river-worn. It broke, it cooled, it cracked. You can see the column joints where it contracted, hexagonal throats ten, twenty meters deep, straight down like organ pipes. The edges are all still knife-edges. There are no hills. There are no gullies. Water hasn't had time to learn how to cut here. That's what low erosion means — not flat, but un-finished. An embryonic topography. You walk on a floor that is still settling, still ticking in the heat.

And it is warm. A deep, womb-warm. The rock itself holds heat from underneath. The air is dim and thick, not dark, but overcast in a way that has nothing to do with weather. Steam is always coming up. It doesn't billow — it weeps. Water is at 1.5. Not a lake. Not a flood. Water is right at the surface, right through the surface. Every crack in the basalt is weeping. Every low between the columns is a pan of water the color of tea, warm as bathwater, skinning over with mineral. The atmosphere itself condenses on you. Your skin never dries. The walls of everything drip. In the border of the basin, where the new rock meets the older world, small springs sweat out of the stone in cold, steady lines and slide down into the town without ever making a stream channel.

There are no plants. Not one. No reed, no moss, no algae you would recognize as green. The water is too hot, too mineral, too new. The stone is too sharp and too salt. The old ecologies haven't gotten here yet. It's a mineral world. The only color that isn't black rock, white steam, or brown water is the color of animal.

Because the fauna is abundant. Deafeningly.

They are the soft-bodied things that love exactly this: fresh rock and warm wet and no competition. The whole basin thrums with them. Not birds. Crabs — or something like crabs — small, black, hand-sized, with mottled yellow backs that look like lichen until they move. Millions of them. They coat the rocks in a dense, shifting carapace, so dense that when you step, they don't scatter so much as flow. They eat the bacterial slick that grows in the hot pans, the invisible biofilm that is not plant. And above them, clouds of midges and gnats and flies so thick they change the light, rising and falling in pillars the size of houses. And on those, in the cracks and on the roofs, the larger things: toads the size of loaves, white-bellied, pimpled, throats all puffing in unison. Long, eel-like salamanders that live in the column cracks. Bats, or bat-things, small and numerous, hanging in clusters under every overhang like black fruit. Nothing has fur. Everything is wet.

And in the middle of all of it is the town.

It should not be able to exist here, but it does, and it has been here a long time, in steady-state. This is the primitive urban. No metal, or almost none — a few copper pins, a few hammered nails saved and re-saved. Everything else is made from what the basin provides. Houses built directly on top of the basalt columns, using the columns themselves as foundations and walls. They have learned to knock the tops off the hexagons and roof them. The town is dense — 2.2 dense — houses touching, leaning, sharing walls. A labyrinth of low, flat-roofed stone cells, of basalt block and dried mud mortar, of chitin mixed into plaster. Curving walls made of stacked crab carapaces mortared with dung. Doors are hides. Roofs are stretched skins weighted down with stones against the steam. There is no lumber. There is no thatch. There is rock and skin and shell.

The town extends in every direction you can see until the steam swallows it. It's valley-sized. Two, maybe three thousand dwellings, maybe five. Narrow lanes only a shoulder wide, always ankle-deep in warm, slow-moving water. And in those lanes, people. Also dense. Also wet. Moving slowly, because everything is slippery, because the sound is so loud you can't hurry.

The sound is the rule of this place. 2.6/3 — deafening — not as an event, as a constant.

The toads. The insects. The crabs clicking. The water sighing out of rock. The bats screaming above hearing and below it. It adds up to a single, unbroken roar that lives in your chest. It's not noisy like a city. It's noisy like a waterfall you live inside. You have to lean close to speak. The children here have learned to speak by touching throats. Everyone's voice is a little hoarse. Everything vibrates. The surface of every pool of water is always shivering with sound.

And none of it changes. That is the strangest and most normal thing about it. For all the water, for all the life packed body to body, for all the raw new rock, everything is in equilibrium. The springs sweat at the same rate they always have. The pools stay at the same depth. The crabs scrape the same slick and the number of crabs stays the same. The toads eat and breed and the number of toads stays the same. People carry a basket of shells from one house to another, and repair the same wall with the same mud. The steam rises. The light stays dim, a permanent late-afternoon glow diffused through kilometers of condensed air, with no sun visible, never brighter, never darker. No seasons seem to reach here. The town does not grow outward, because it has met the exact edge where life can hold on this raw floor, and it does not shrink, because too much food wells up from the bacterial water.

You stand in a lane with water around your ankles, your shoulders brushing wet basalt on both sides. The wall next to you is weeping warm water and moving, because it is covered in crabs, and above you laundry made of cured gut-skin is dripping, and the air is full of gnats and the roar is absolute. It smells of mineral and brine and ammonia and wet stone. The rock under your feet is warm. Everything is touching everything else.
