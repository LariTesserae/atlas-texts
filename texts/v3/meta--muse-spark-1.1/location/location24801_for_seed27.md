The air is thin enough that you hear your own blood first.

You're high. Very high. On a plateau that isn't a plateau anymore — it's what a plateau becomes after the world forgets to make weather. A shield of stone so old its mountains have given up and settled, slumped into broad, bald domes separated by shallow, wind-scoured pans. Nothing sharp left. The horizon is a soft, dented line, far away. This is high country at the end of geography. 2.4 and 2.6, you feel it in the way distance doesn't change as you walk; the land is sparse and open and finished eroding a billion years ago, and now it just waits in the cool, dim light.

And it is covered. Not with snow. Not with soil. With vegetation so total it *is* the ground.

There is no water here. Not a creek, not a seep, not a buried lens. The rock is salt-stung and clean. The air at this altitude holds maybe ten parts per million of vapor and that's in summer. Water parameter 0.1 — this place never learned how to be wet.

The plants do not care.

They are not green. Or not only green. In this light — a permanent, deep-blue dusk, as if the sun is always behind another planet — they are the color of old paper, bone, lichen-grey, rust-black. They do not photosynthesize the way you were taught. Or they do, but they've solved the water problem by refusing it. 

The dominant form is called — there is no name for it in human language, but the people who live here prune it, so they call it the Lattice.

Imagine a plant that decided to be architecture first and organism second. From a central root-anchor that drills thirty meters straight down into basalt until it finds a faint geochemical sigh of manganese, it erupts upward into a dry fountain. Trunks not round but ribboned, fluted like melted wax columns, made of a material that looks like cork and spun glass at the same time. The flesh is aerogel. It weighs almost nothing. If you cut it, it doesn't bleed sap — it exhales a puff of scented dust and then seals itself with a lattice of white hairs.

And it branches. God, it branches. Each trunk frays at twenty meters into thousands of filament boughs, each filament frays into a felt of airborne rootlets, each rootlet terminates in a black, bell-shaped pod the size of your fist, turned always into the wind. The pods are not leaves. They are electrostatic combs. In the cool, thin air they crackle. The whole forest — which is not a forest, it's a sparse orchard of these ancient individuals, each a quarter-mile from the next, so you can see all the way across the valley between them — is loud with it.

**Sound, 2.5. Loud.**

A constant low thundercrack and sigh, like powerlines in a storm, like a field of dry kelp shifting. The filaments rub and generate charge. The pods pop. The wind that is never less than ten knots comes off the ancient domes and plays the lattice like a harp made of ten billion strings. When a big gust hits an old specimen — and they are all old, primordial, their bases swollen with centuries of dead self-growth into buttresses you could live under — the whole thing bows and *groans* at the limit of hearing, a bass note you feel in your molars.

You live in it because you have to. You also live in it because you want to.

Between the giants, the built thing.

It's not that the settlement was built here. The settlement *is* the pruning. The tech here is not tech you can see — 2.8, beyond modern. There are no machines. Or rather, the plant is the machine and the machine is the plant. Someone, a long time ago, realized you could edit these dry-air forests at the chromatic level, at the lattice-bond level. So you walk from one Lattice-tree to the next on suspended paths that are not rope, not metal — they are living runner-stems, encouraged to grow horizontally, flattened into walkways wide enough for two people and a cart. They bounce slightly. They repair themselves.

Houses are hollows coaxed in the lower flutes. You find a young buttress and you insert a growth-key — a dark glass needle that whispers in molecular origami — and over months the cork-glass flesh swells around the emptiness you have defined, leaving windows because you told it light is useful here, leaving a chimney because you told it convection is useful here. You don't cut; you instruct. The whole valley is one town, an urban density that is still physically sparse — density 0.8, built 1.5 — because every home needs its share of sky for its host tree to comb for nitrates, for static, for the fine interplanetary dust that is its only food.

In the dim, the windows glow. Not with electric light. With the Lattice itself. When the charge differential between sky and rock gets high enough, the bell-pods discharge in a silent violet flicker, Saint Elmo's fire running down every filament. The people call that evening, regardless of what a clock says.

And there is fauna. Abundant.

This should not support anything. It does.

Things that look like folded kites, a meter across, sheets of translucent membrane stretched over a dry cartilage frame, ride the static fields between trees and never land. They dip a proboscis into a pod, steal its charge, and scream — a thin metallic shriek as air ionizes. Everywhere in the felt and fluting are smaller things: dense, silent insects like tumbleweeds of black wool that graze on the shed aerogel hairs; six-legged lizards with no mouths that press their bellies to the trunk and drink electricity directly. In the high groan of wind you hear them too, the canopy rustling with movement, not leaf-movement but limb-movement, the constant active business of a living system that has replaced water with wind and charge and time.

The air smells like ozone and old books and cold stone.

If you stand still too long in the open pan between two Lattice giants, the filaments around you, though distant, will turn their pods slightly toward you, because you are warmer than the rock and you leak a little field of your own. The city listens. The forest farms static. The plateau breathes once per long, cool day, and you are inside a lung that never needed to be wet to be alive.
