It is a town that should not be under water, not because it was drowned, but because it was built too high to drown.

You are on a mountain that never had air.

The seamount rises out of the abyssal nowhere in a single, young gesture — a blade of fresh black glass still hot in geological memory. No rounding, no softening. It came up too fast for erosion to learn its name. Its sides are cliffs of columnar basalt and sudden, sheer faults that end in knife edges, fractures that still hold the clean, conchoidal shine of a break that happened a week ago or a thousand years ago — it is impossible to tell because nothing here ages. At 3,800 meters above the surrounding plain, the top shears off flat. Not a peak. A table. A room-sized country.

And that table is full.

The water above you is not an ocean. It is *the* water — a vertical, still column that goes up and up until black becomes a deeper black. You understand, after a moment, that there is no surface. You are not at the bottom of a sea. You are inside a held place. A cylinder of ocean perhaps a kilometer across and an unknown number of kilometers tall, standing on the mountaintop like a glass of dark water left on a high shelf. It does not spill. It does not press. The pressure at this depth should be crushing. It isn't. It is cool, a constant, perfect cool that touches your skin through whatever you are wearing and makes you think of clean stone in late October. 4 degrees Celsius. The temperature of waiting.

Inside this column, the town exists.

It is not ruined. That would imply a process.

There are perhaps thirty structures, scattered with a great deal of deliberate emptiness between them. Sparse is the rule. The basalt plain between them is bare and scratched and unmarked — no silt, no sand, no growth. No algae, no kelp, no lichen, no mold. A mineral world. You can see every grain.

The buildings themselves are sparse in the same way a sentence with too little punctuation is sparse. They are not buildings for rain or wind, because there has never been rain or wind here. They are low, broad, and open. No doors, no roofs in the way you think of roofs. Each one is made of a matte material that does not reflect light so much as refuse it — a super-black that seems to drink the dim and then release it a moment later, slightly delayed. When you look closely, the material is not solid. It is a swarm holding shape. A trillion points keeping station, trembling on the edge of visibility, maintaining an edge, a corner, a portal that is not meant for a body to pass through but for water to be told to stay outside, or inside.

Because some of the buildings are full of water and some are full of dry. And both are submerged.

You walk — or drift, because buoyancy here is optional — from an empty thoroughfare into what looks like a courtyard, and the water simply parts around you without moving. There is no splash, no wake, no current. The displacement happens elsewhere, or elsewhen. Inside the courtyard, there is dry. An absolute, dustless dry that is cold and smells of nothing and of ozone. The sound drops even further when you step into it. You can hear the blood in your ears. You step back out, and you are wet again without having become wet. The water never clings.

Other structures are inverted. They are interiors made of water held in the shape of rooms. Walls of ocean that hold their corners. You can see the faint distortion where the water-wall keeps being water. Sometimes a particle — a flake of basalt, impossibly slow — hangs in the wall, suspended for centuries.

There is light, but it doesn't come from above. Darkness is the default here. The true dark of the deep, pressing on the eye. What light there is, is built. Thin seams run along the edges of each building like grout made of dusk, a cool amber-gray luminescence that does not illuminate so much as outline. It is not bright enough to throw shadows. It just makes the edges knowable. And at great intervals — minutes? hours? — a single bead of white light will detach from one building and traverse the empty space to another, in a straight, unhurried line, taking a long time to cross ten meters. It makes no sound. It arrives, attaches, goes out.

You realize after a while that nothing is changing. Not in the way that quiet places are still. This is a different stasis. The water does not contain currents or thermal gradients. There is no particulate drift. If you hold your hand out and stir, the water stays where it was. If you stop it, there is no turbulence to decay. The young basalt has no talus coming down its edges. Nothing erodes. Nothing accumulates. The town is in equilibrium that is not natural but enforced. A word like *paused* comes to mind, but it is not paused; it is completed. It has arrived at a state where time has no more work to do.

That is why there is almost no sound. Water should carry sound perfectly. Here it carries nothing, because there is nothing to carry. Only very rarely, the whole column gives a single, very low tonenote, more felt in your molars than heard — *thaum* — as if the field that holds the ocean upright is checking its own tension.

Fauna is rare to the point of rumor. Once, in the dry courtyard, you see the one animal. It has no business being here. It is perhaps as long as your forearm, translucent and spineless, with six slow fins it does not use. It hangs in the water above the basalt, facing the dark, not moving at all. It has no eyes. It is not dead. It is not alive in a way that involves growth or hunger. It is there because the system allows for one witness. It does not flee when you approach. Its skin reflects the seam-light like mother-of-pearl.

If you stay long enough, you understand the rule of this place:

Water was not the flood. Water was the building material, brought up the mountain and made to stand still. The town was not built to keep water out. It was built to *justify* keeping water held up here, kilometers of ocean balanced on a needle of new rock, far above where any ocean should be. The technology that does it is so far beyond interruption that it no longer registers as technology. It is a habit of matter.

It is a high place. It is an urban place. It is a hamlet the size of a cathedral nave, containing a column of night-ocean that never falls, has never had a single weed grow in it, and never will.
