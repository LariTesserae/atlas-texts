It is a town inside a single thing.

The thing is a bowl. Not a valley — a bowl cut clean into the low coastal plain, a perfect circle about eight hundred meters across, with walls too steep and too new. The basalt is still sharp. You can see where it broke. Low elevation, almost at sea level, so low that the air sits heavy in it, but the rim is fifteen meters high all the way around, a dark, fresh-toothed ring of rock that hasn't learned to be rounded yet. Young stone. When it rains on the plain outside, the water runs off and doesn't enter the bowl at all; the rim sheds it. This place made its own water problem.

They built a town in the bottom of it anyway. They had to.

From the rim path it doesn't look urban at first because of the spacing. That's the first trick of it — organized construction with nothing crowded. The brick mill buildings, the long assembly sheds with sawtooth roofs, the water tower, the foreman's row-houses, the old canal office now a chapel — all of it is set far apart from each other on the flat black floor of the bowl, with big, deliberate emptiness between them. A full town's worth of infrastructure for a hamlet's population. Streets of poured concrete run straight and meet at right angles and are mostly empty. You can stand at the intersection by the boiler house and see clear to the other side. The rules here: everything gets a lot, everything gets air, nothing touches.

And everything is damp. Not wet. There is no river in the bowl, no lake. On the 0.8 scale, it should be dry. Instead it's condensation country. The factories never stopped running, even after the factories stopped making what they were supposed to make.

They are low and cool — you feel it as soon as you drop down the stair cut into the north wall, that ten-degree drop where the wind stops. The air is cool year-round, maybe fifty degrees, the kind of cool that makes iron sweat. The three remaining mills run slow now, not producing goods but producing climate. Huge flywheels and piston arms, twenty feet tall, turn over once every thirty seconds. Patient. Minimal-flux. The boilers exhale waste steam from low stacks and the steam hits the cool basalt walls and the cool brick and the cool air and becomes everything. It beads on every surface. It runs down the rivets. It collects in the gutters they cut along every roofline and feeds chain-downspouts into concrete cisterns. Behind the old dye works there's a single perfect pocket — a subsidence dip where the floor cracked after they built — and it holds maybe four feet of dark, still water, and that is the town reservoir.

That is why it is lush. That is the weirdness that keeps it alive.

In any reasonable accounting this should be a rust-yard. Instead, because the machines sweat, the bowl is a garden. Ferns explode from the mortar between bricks, but not little sidewalk ferns — big, glossy, cold-loving ferns the size of a man, their fronds dripping constantly back onto the engines that watered them. Moss has taken the roofs of the machine shops and made them soft and green to the rim. Creeper vines as thick as your arm have learned to climb hot iron and not die; they climb the legs of the water tower, the trusses of the sheds, they hang in curtains inside the empty weaving hall where the looms used to be. The streets are lined with butterbur and skunk cabbage, both loving the cold and the mineral seep. The town grows more plant than it knows what to do with and prunes it like a chore, with hand sickles, slowly, on Tuesdays.

It is loud.

The loudness is not sharp. It is the deep, chest-filling loud of slow industry — the low chuff-chuff-chuff of the big pistons, the constant clack and sigh of the flywheel belts even though nothing is being belted anymore, the tin-roof drumming of condensation falling from the rafters back into puddles, back into drains, back into the boilers. It echoes off the fresh bowl walls and never quite leaves. You have to raise your voice to be heard even in the houses. Everyone who lives here has that slightly hoarse, slightly shouting voice of mill people, even the children.

And there are so many living things in it because of that loud and that wet and that empty. The bowl is too small and too walled for deer or anything big, but it is abundant on its own scale. Swifts by the hundreds live under the sawtooth eaves and cut screaming lines through the steam. The cisterns teem with newts and water beetles. The feral cat population of what must be forty or fifty cats moves like slow smoke between the warm pipes. Rats, yes, but sleek and moss-backed, and sparrows so fat they barely fly. In the reservoir pocket, carp that someone put there twenty years ago are now the size of dogs, orange and white and blindly circling. At night you hear them breach. At night the machines still run, and the cats still stalk, and the ferns still drip.

Light here is normal, unexceptional, which makes it stranger. No perpetual twilight, no weird aurora. It's northern plain daylight, filtered slightly by the constant thin mist that the walls hold in. At noon the sun clears the rim and the whole bowl fills with flat, even, honest light that shows every rust spot and every leaf. At four it is shadowed again, early, because of the east wall.

No one talks about leaving, though you could climb the stairs in a minute. The bowl holds its atmosphere in. Cool, damp, loud, green, rusted, spaced-out, complete. A town that decided to become a terrarium heated by engines that no longer have a purpose except keeping it all alive.
