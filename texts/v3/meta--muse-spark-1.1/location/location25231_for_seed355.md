It is low. Not just low ground — low as a held breath. A basin the size of a county, so wide and even you don't see its rim at any one time, you feel it as a dimming at the edge of feeling. The world dips here and does not come back up.

It is dark, not night-dark. There is no sky to be night. The air itself is dark. Hot, thick dark, the kind that has weight. If you hold your hand up you see it only because it is closer than the dark behind it, a slightly more solid piece of dark. Farther away, you see by what isn't there — by the way water catches what little there is and throws it back wrong.

And there is water everywhere. Not a sea. The basin would be a sea if the world worked elsewhere the way it works here, but it doesn't. The water doesn't collect. It clings. It sweats.

The stone sweats. The ground sweats. The structures sweat.

This is a city.

Its streets are broad and regular, laid out in a grid that goes on until the hot dark swallows it. Someone planned this. Or something grew according to a plan. There are avenues a hundred feet wide with no one to need them, and cross-streets that meet at perfect right angles, and blocks that are mostly empty space. That is the strange density of it: it is unmistakably urban — curb and gutter and terrace and facade — but it is sparse the way a dryland orchard is sparse. One structure per block, sometimes less. The lot lines are still there, marked by low walls that have never fallen down, only softened.

The buildings are not built. Not in any way that uses a tool.

They are precipitated.

They look at first like architecture, and from a distance they hold that shape: two stories, three, flat roofs, doorways precisely centered. Closer, you see no joint, no brick, no chisel mark. They are accretion. Layer on layer of mineral left by weeping water, like travertine terraces that decided to be houses. The floors are faintly banded, cream and rust and bruised-purple, the walls are that same pitted, dripping limestone, but grown upward into lintels and cornices and shallow stairs. Some of them are still growing. You can hear it if you stand still long enough — not a creak, a faint ticking as water lays down another skin of stone.

Every surface weeps. The walls are never dry. Beads form and merge and run, constantly, down flutes and pillars that were never carved. They gather in the gutters that were never dug but were always there, waiting for this. Condensation forms on you too, instantly. The air is saturated and hot as a mouth. It rains here without rain. It rains up. Water lifts off the warm pavements in a low mist to hit the cold breath above and fall again as a slow, fat drizzle that never stops. You are always in-between weathers. The stone is slick with it. The shallow canals that run down the middle of each boulevard — too regular to be natural, too worn to be made — carry it away in a quiet, endless sheet, an inch deep, clear and hot as tea.

Nothing lives here. Not in the way you expect a wet hot place to teem.

There is vegetation, but sparingly, as if the city only allows what it cannot prevent. Here and there a clump of black reeds grows straight out of a wall, out of vertical stone, roots finding no soil, only moisture. On the leeward side of the bigger structures there are mats of something that is not quite lichen, red-black and flat against the weeping stone, pulsing very slightly when water passes over it. It doesn't spread. It has been here long enough to learn not to. This place is old. The corners of every block are rounded. The steps are dished in the middle though no foot has ever stepped there — worn by water alone, by thousands of years of its own quiet coming and going.

It is quiet. The loudest thing is the water, and even that is hushed — the endless joining of drops, the slip of films over stone. Occasionally a sheet of water on a ceiling will give up at once and fall with a soft slap. Far away, something deep in the stone sighs. The basin exhales. The mist thickens and thins.

The weirdness is not an addition. It is the rule that holds the contradictions together.

Gravity here has a preference. Water remembers being stone and stone remembers being water. At certain intersections, if you look down the length of the avenue, you see the same building repeated, not as a copy but as the same building again farther down, wetter. You can walk toward it and never arrive, because the space between has diluted. In other places a street will be half a block long on one side and three blocks long on the other and both can be true at once. The doorways are all the right height and yet when you stand in one you feel as if the house was built for you to walk through sideways.

If you leave a pool of water alone in a courtyard, in the dark heat, after a while its reflection shows a different arrangement of the surrounding facades than the one you stand in. If you wait longer, it shows an arrangement where the sparse towers are dense. If you touch it, it corrects itself.

No one made tools here because tools would have to decide what they are — hammer or ore, chisel or stone. In this city things are not done with things. They are coaxed from themselves. The city is infrastructure without inhabitants, because inhabitation is a kind of technology. There are no birds, no insects, no rats, no echo of a voice. There is only the active, patient work of hot water laying down city and dissolving it at the same rate, keeping it in balance — in its own state — forever.

You can walk for hours and remain in the same neighborhood that is also another neighborhood. The wet dark folds you in. The stone sweats against your shoulders as you pass.
