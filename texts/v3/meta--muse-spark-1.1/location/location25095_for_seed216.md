It is high. Not high like a hill, or a mountain. High like a continent decided to leave.

The Table — that's what the people who live on it call it, because there is no other word that fits — is a built thing. There was never a bedrock under it. If you dig, which no one does anymore, you go through root and rope and root again, through woven mat and living beam and old, old heartwood as hard as river stone, and then you come out the other side into air.

It was made, entirely. A long time ago, by hands. You can still see the hands in it everywhere, the primitive logic of it. Nothing was ever smelted that could not be smelted in a pit of charcoal. Stone split by fire and water. Bamboo as thick as pillars, split and lashed and coaxed. Vine cordage as thick as your waist, now lignified and grown into itself. The whole mass is a lattice, a scaffold, a piece of basketwork the size of a weather system. Triangle over triangle over triangle, until the triangles become a plain you can walk across for months.

And it is old enough to have settled. This is not a ruin. Erosion here does not mean collapse. It means the Table has negotiated its own shape with wind and time. Edges have been rounded. The great verticals — the legs that hold it above the lower atmosphere — are fluted and grooved by a thousand years of cloud passing through them. The weave has sagged in the middle as living things do, into a shallow, continent-wide bowl. In that bowl, water stays.

The climate is warm. Not hot. A low, humid, greenhouse warmth that never quite breaks into sweat, because the air is thin up here and the light is dim. You are above most of the cloud cover, but the Table makes its own. 

This is the wetness: The air down below is wet, and the Table is colder than the air that hits it. So it weeps. All the time.

At dawn — a dimming and brightening rather than a real sunrise, because the light that reaches here is filtered through a permanent high haze of ice crystals — the entire underside and topside of the world condenses. Water beads on every leaf, every lashing, every hanging root. It grows heavy and falls, but not far. There is always another leaf, another level, another woven floor to catch it. The sound of it is the only sound for a very long time: tick, tick, tick, from ten thousand tiers above you to ten thousand tiers below. A soft post-rain forest sound that never stops being post-rain. Pools form on leaves as big as fields. The bark of the structural trunks sweats. Rocks — because they did haul rocks up, flat slates to work on, to grind grain — weep from their undersides. Nowhere is there a river, because a river would need a channel and a direction, and here water wants to go down, but down is a maze. So instead everything is marsh on the vertical. Every horizontal surface is a slow bog of moss and peat and black, fine compost made from its own fallen parts. You wade through ankle-deep water that is somehow suspended three miles above the earth.

And the plant — 

There is only plant. Not in the sense of coverage, but in the sense of substance. The Builders planted the Table and then the Table became the plant, or the plant learned to keep building in the Builders' style. You cannot say where construction ends and growth begins. A load-bearing column is a living tree that was once tied to a dead tree that is now completely enclosed in the living tree's growth. A wall is a hedge that was woven so tightly for so many centuries that it became wood. A road is the flattened, living top of a root the width of a town, still creeping, so you need to re-cut your road every generation with stone adzes.

All of it is productive, all of it is alive. There is no unbuilt, no natural. Even the voids are framed by deliberate arches that have since fused shut with figs. The light — dim, green-f filtered, late-afternoon light even at noon — comes down through layers of canopy so deep that photon counting becomes irrelevant. Ten miles down through leaves and you still see leaf-light. The air between the levels is empty. That is the strangeness of the density. 

From above, you would think it a solid jungle. Walk into it and you realize it is almost entirely hollow. The Table is 95% air. The structure is so vast and so economical that its interior is a system of cathedrals. A single open pocket between weaves can be large enough to have its own cloud, its own slow rain that never reaches any ground. You can stand on a branch that is a continent, look across a gap of clear, humid air a mile across with nothing in it but a few stray aerial roots hanging like plumb lines, and see, impossibly far away, another wall of green that is the other side of the same empty.

That's where the fauna are. Abundant, but sparse the way stars are abundant. You will not hear them — sound is swallowed in this soft, sodden, vast lattice. No bird calls because a call would have nowhere to echo that wouldn't take a minute to return. So everything is silent, or makes quiet sounds. The moths are the size of houses and they beat so slowly you only see the passage of their shadows, dimming the dim light a fraction more. Their caterpillars chew tunnels through the living structural beams, and the people follow the caterpillars to harvest the sweet, fermented frass they leave behind. There are monkeys with arms too long, who remember the original knot patterns and re-tie them where the wood has slipped, chattering without sound. Things with too many thin legs that skate on the surface tension of perched bogs. Beetles that farm fungus on the underside of slate tiles. Under every leaf, a nation of mites.

And then there is the weirdness, which isn't extra. It's structural.

Because the Table was built higher than it should have been able to be built, the Table does not quite agree with distance anymore. The builders were primitive. They did not build against physics, they just built with what they had, and kept building, and kept weaving, and somewhere in the middle of building a place so large it has weather, they wove a mistake into space.

If you climb down for a day, through weeping layers, you may climb up out of a hole you recognize as being above where you started. If you leave markers — tied cords — they sometimes meet you from behind. In the great hollow cathedrals, straight lines sag. You can see a moth on the far side of the void that is both very far and very close, and if you walk toward it, the air thickens and you arrive a week later on the other side of the Table, where the season is different. Some empty chambers are larger on the inside than the outside should allow, as if the plant, to save material, folded space and stitched it with rootlets. The water, condensing everywhere, sometimes condenses on the underside of air itself, forming floating lakes that are perfectly circular and perfectly still until a silent insect steps through them and they ripple upward.

The people know the rules: Never cut a load-bearing root that is dripping before cutting. Always splice the new growth into the old knot in threes. Do not stare too long into the deep voids between levels, because the voids stare back with a slightly different arrangement of green. When the big silences come — minutes where even dripping stops, where the whole Table holds its breath to listen for itself — you stop working, and you place your palm on the nearest living beam and feel it faintly thrumming, warm, active, in motion.

Because it is alive. Not just as a plant is alive, but as a project is alive. It is still being built, by slow, endless, primitive craft. Sap rises. A man with an adze trims a leaf-plain. A moth molts. Water beads, falls, is caught, beads again. Over a scale so large you can never see it all, the whole thing shifts a fraction of an inch toward better balance, and settles again, weeping.
