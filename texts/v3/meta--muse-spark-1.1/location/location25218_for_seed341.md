It is low. Lower than the land around it should allow.

You don't walk to it so much as you leak down to it. The river plain is flat for miles and then there is a lip - a perfect, softened circle of limestone, like the rim of a saucer left out in the rain for a thousand years. The rock is weathered beyond sharpness, all its edges chewed round and filmed with dark moss. And then you drop.

Maybe sixty meters across. Maybe eighty. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other if the steam wasn't so thick. A hamlet-scale sinkhole-city, a single room with a sky for a ceiling. That is the whole extent of the world here.

The air gets thick two steps past the rim. Hot. Not dry-season hot, but boiled-air hot, greenhouse hot, wet hot that hits you behind the knees and in the lobes of your ears. 2.6/3 hot in a 1.0/3 light place, which should be impossible, but the dim keeps the heat in. The light comes from far above, filtered through what isn't sky anymore but a ceiling of interlocked things - cathedral glass left from whatever factory roof was built to cover the sink, now shattered and mended by sheets of banana leaf, by tarpaulins of living philodendron and wild taro and strangler fig. The plants grew up over the hole to hide it, and then kept growing down. So the light that reaches the bottom is green, late-afternoon green, even at noon. A few bare industrial bulbs swing on long cords and hum amber, forty watts against a hundred tons of steam.

The town is in the hole. That's the only way to say it.

It was built - is built, it is still being built - on what was meant to be a floor. Brickworks, ironworks, a pumping station scaled for a city that never came. Low brick rowhouses and machine halls not more than two stories, but dense in intent. Urban. You can see the planning: streets that run true, an organized grid of gutters and outfalls and pipe racks on stilts, brick archways with keystones still stamped 1912. Only now the streets are not streets. They are spaced. Sparse. There is too much emptiness between the buildings for a place that clearly wanted to be cramped. They stand like people who have taken a step back from each other.

And it is wet.

Everything weeps. The limestone walls weep because the water table is right here, higher than it should be, pressing at the rock until the rock sweats. The industrial walls weep because the air is at dew point and can't hold itself anymore. Condensation beads on every cold iron beam and rivet head and runs down in clean lines before it meets the general film of green-black. The condenser units of the lowworks run day and night, vast brass-and-steel lungs coughing cool, and their breath is the rain. All over town, a constant, patient, post-rain still-raining.

The ground is not ground. It's a raft. Planks, then iron grating, then more planks, then mud, laid down over the marsh that came up when the pumps broke or were left to pulse at half-speed. You can feel it give under you. In some alleys the water is ankle deep and hot as bathwater. In others it has been lifted into canals with bricks for banks. Everywhere is the sound of water meeting other water: dripping from pipe to leaf to puddle, hissing off a hot manifold, the slap-slap of the main paddle-driven Archimedes screws that lift the seep back out of the sump and dump it just outside the rim where it immediately soaks back in. That is one of the active processes. There are so many.

Because there is no fauna.

That is the thing you notice third, after the heat and the light. In a place that lush - and it is lush, 2.0 lush, ferns as big as doors unrolling from the mortar cracks, moss making mattresses on the flat roofs, air plants hanging off every wire so thick you have to push through them like a beaded curtain - in a place that wet and hot and loud, you expect the whine of mosquitoes, the croak, the dragonfly buzz. There is nothing.

No birds nest on the fig roof. No rats in the walls. No cockroaches under the grating, though there should be armies. The water is clear if you part the duckweed and you see no wrigglers, no minnows. It is a plant city with engine keepers.

And yet it is not still. Dynamic. Active in a way that makes the absence worse. The vegetation grows faster than it should, you can watch it. The philodendron leaves tick wider by an hour. Vines pull themselves hand-over-hand up the pipes with soft, white, searching roots. And the industry hasn't stop. The boilers are stoked by no one in particular - there are human presences, or were, you see boots left tipped by the door, you see washing still on a line, but you are alone with the motion. The line shudders. The flywheel in the main hall turns, turns, enormous, rust and black grease, throwing off heat, turning the screws that lift the water, turning the fans that move the wet air. Pistons cough every seven seconds and then again every seven seconds, so loud in this small stone cup that your teeth ring. Metal expands, ticks, drips.

You work out the rule of the place after an hour, because sweat is running into your eyes and you have nowhere else to put your thoughts.

First: water cannot leave. It rises, is lifted, is boiled, falls, rises. The town drinks its own sweat.

Second: things that need other things do not live here. No animal that must eat another. Only the ones that eat light and steam and rust. The bananas over the roof ripen and fall and rot where they land into new shoots, with no mouths to take them.

Third: it is established. Not ruined. Ruins are a place falling apart. This place has fallen into balance. The erosion has stopped fighting. Brick and root and iron have come to an agreement about who holds up who. The rust is a kind of bark now. The moss's grip is structural. If you pulled a building out, the hole would slump closed.

If you stay long enough - and hot as it is, you can, the air holds you - you start to hear the smaller sounds under the loud one. Not fauna. The gurgle of the limestone weeping through its pores, the soft pop of a fern spore pod, the ceramic clink of condensation falling from the figs onto the tin roofs, hundreds per minute, arrhythmic, so loud you have to raise your voice.

And every surface condenses and drips and grows.
