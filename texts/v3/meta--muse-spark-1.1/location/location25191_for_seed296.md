It's not a mountain range anymore, not really. It's what's left after a mountain range gets old.

The elevation is extreme — four thousand meters, maybe more, you feel it in the way a flight of stairs becomes work, the way a struck match burns too quick and too pale. But the shape is wrong for that height. There should be teeth up here. There should be fresh rock, avalanches, sheer faces. There isn't.

This is ancient erosion. The peaks have been weathered so long they've forgotten they were peaks. They've settled. They are enormous, soft domes of gneiss and basalt that was once deep crust, now lifted and then rounded by an uncountable age of wind. From a distance they look like the backs of buried animals, shoulder to shoulder. From inside, on the ground, you can't see their size at all — the slope is so long and gentle you think you're on flat ground until your ears pop walking to the market.

The town — a city, really, but not the kind that sprawls — lives in the bowl between three of those shoulders. Call it a valley if you have to, but it isn't V-shaped. It's a shallow, cupped hand, kilometers across, its rim worn down to a smooth lip against the sky. Everything that has ever come off those domes has come to rest here: a deep, deep loam of stone dust, iron-stained clay, and pulverized lichen. You don't build foundations here, you carve them. Below street level the rock is soft enough to cut with a short pick, firm enough to hold a wall without mortar. So every generation has cut deeper. Streets cut into old streets. Houses shouldered against houses. Stairs that were exterior in your grandfather's time are interior now.

And it is dense. Not crowded the way a lowland city is crowded, with people. Packed the way a peat bog is packed, or an old-growth thicket. Buildings, yes — two and three story terraces of dark stone brick, iron balconies, slate roofs all crusted with the same living felt — but in between and on top and through it, more. Pipes. Gantries. Cisterns. Trellises. Laundry lines that never quite dry.

Because water here is never present as a thing, it's a condition. There's no river in the valley. The air is too thin and too cool to hold much rain. Instead, dampness comes sideways. Every afternoon the cloud deck that lives a hundred meters below the rim rises up and over like a tide. It doesn't rain. It condenses. It beads on everything — on iron, on stone, on leaves, on your eyelashes. It gathers in the seams. It runs, briefly, in black threads down the streets and vanishes into collectors. The sound of that hour is a faint, city-wide ticking as droplets form and fall.

It's a lush place, improbably so. At this temperature — cool in a way that gets into your bones, breath-fogging from April to October — you expect alpine spareness, lichen and a few tenacious grasses. But the rock is so old it has rotted into perfect soil, and the condensation is constant, and the city itself makes a greenhouse of its bowl. So everything that can grow does. Not tall. Dense. Waist-high ferns with fronds as thick as your wrist, black-green and glossy. Moss that doesn't carpet but builds, ten, twenty centimeters deep, a spongy mattress on every roof and wall, humming a darker green where the boiler vents leak heat. Every crevice sprouts: tufts of sourgrass from between bricks, liverworts filling the mortar, fat white lichens that look like poured wax.

This is a city that farms its own walls. People peel back a section of roof-moss to dry it for fuel and insulation in neat, steaming bricks. They train a local vine — not a vine, exactly, more like a woodless, succulent rope that grows a meter a day when wet — through their trellises to hang their washing from, to splint pipes. The line between tended and wild is meaningless here. Cutting is tending.

The industry is part of that same weave. This isn't a post-industrial ruin. The tech is active, industrial, mid-era — riveted iron boilers, big waterwheels that no longer run on rivers but on the weight of what the condensors pull from the fog, electric lines humming low, machine shops with lathes running all night on a shift system. But the scale is settled-in, not sprawling. Each shop is the size of a house. Each factory has a house above it. The smoke they make is cool, low, and doesn't rise far in the thin air before it flattens and joins the cloud. Soot has stained the lower half of the valley a soft, uniform grey. The light — always dim, even at noon — filters through high overcast and then through that haze and then through fern fronds. It's a deep-sea light, greenish-blue, sourceless.

It is mostly quiet, which is the strangest part for an urban, industrial place. The air pressure is too low for sound to carry far. The moss eats every echo. No birds pass over at this height except sometimes one, far up. So the work happens almost in mime: you hear the man next to you's file on metal, the nearby drip into a copper cistern, the faint sigh of the big condenser fans turning on the rim of the valley — huge, slow, iron flowers opening — but not the work three streets over. At night, it's quieter still. You hear water moving inside pipes as if the town were a body.

Fauna is rare enough you notice it. No flocks. No swarms. Its absence is why the plants can be so thick. There is a small, ash-grey moth that lives only on the underside of the roof-moss and dies if you take it into the sun. There are long, thin, blind things in the soil that come up when you cut foundations and quickly retreat. And sometimes, high in the dimness over the vapor fans, a single raptor — too large, too silent — circles the bowl once, twice, and doesn't land.

And because everything is so still and old and damp, things start to behave like other things. That's the weirdness. The moss grows over abandoned engines so tightly that it keeps their shape, and after a winter you can peel it off and have a perfect soft replica of a gear. The condensation sometimes beads so heavily on the iron gantries that it forms a continuous sheet, and light refracts through it wrong, making the underside of the cloud look like another city underneath this one. In the oldest cuttings, where the rock was pressed flat for longer than life has existed, there are pools of stone — not pools in stone, but stone that once was fluid and decided to stop in the middle of rippling, and holds the light for a few seconds longer than the surface around it.

The rule of the place, unspoken but enforced by the valley itself, is this: nothing can be made to last by being hard. The hard is already finished. The granite has done all the enduring it can do, and now it is crumbling into garden soil. What lasts here is what can hold water and give a little. Iron rusts. Stone softens. Wood rots fast. So they build with tight joints and loose plans. A wall is expected to move an inch a year. A roof is expected to become a meadow. You do not repair a house here so much as you negotiate with it to stay a house for one more season.

By evening, the cloud drops below the rim again, as it does every day, and the valley is left in clear, thin, frigid air. The sky is not black but a deep, star-battered indigo. The lights come on — electric, yellow, far too warm — in windows that are never quite square anymore. The damp glitters on every leaf, every pipe, every iron rail. For an hour the whole bowl glistens like something freshly made. Then it freezes, just a little, and waits to drip again.
