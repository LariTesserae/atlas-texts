It is a town that only ever happened once.

You come down to it. The land around is low and it has finished becoming low — a broad, warm, shallow bowl in the country, the kind of basin where air settles and decides to stay. The hills around it are old, their backs worn smooth, not sharp, not still eroding — just established. Everything that was going to slide has slid. Everything that was going to hold, holds. The sun is normal. Not filtered, not theatrical. Afternoon light at about two-thirty, warm on your shoulders.

And then there is not a forest. There is a Building.

One thing. One single plant that is also a city.

You don't see it at first because you are looking for trees. But there is a kind of fig — or something that started out as something like a fig, before it forgot what it was supposed to be — that germinated in the lowest point of the basin where water likes to gather and then changed its mind about being a tree. It grew hollow.

Not hollow like dead. Hollow like a cathedral on purpose.

From the outside it looks like a low, lumpy hill, maybe forty meters across, a tight interweaving of dark, elephant-limb roots and pale, waxy trunks that have fused into walls. Leaves the size of doors plate over it, overlapping like slate tiles, each one beading with condensation that never quite evaporates. The air inside the bowl is damp in the way of mouths and greenhouses — not wet, you won't get rained on, but everything sweats. You can smell it before you hear it: warm sap, peppery leaf, wet bark.

There is an entrance where two roots failed to meet. You have to duck.

Inside, density breaks.

This is the emptiness. The vegetation is total — there is nothing here that is not plant — but there is almost nothing in it. The interior is a single vast room, a vaulted void held open by living columns as thick as houses, their bark smooth and faintly warm to the touch, like skin. The floor is a suspended lattice of interlocked aerial roots, springy, slightly giving, covered in a short, dense moss that drinks the drips. Far above, the canopy has woven itself into a roof so tight you can only see the sun in coins and slits, but it is a roof. It was grown.

And it is a town. That was not a metaphor.

The walls have grown rooms.

Where a branch forks, it has swelled into a hollow pod, a chamber big enough to stand in, then two, then ten. Some are open, their walls thin as paper-leaf, rustling. Others are sealed except for a round mouth. Inside each one, the floor is ribbed with leaf-vein. You can see where generations have lived: the smoothed places where bodies have slept on living wood that softened for them, the niches where fruit was stored, the dark stains where cooking fires were once held — carefully, in clay that was never fired, only dried, because the tree will not tolerate flame for long and closes its rooms if you hurt it.

There is no tool here that was not also grown or found. No metal. No wire. Rope is vine, boiled and split. Bowls are gourds the tree still attaches to, that you pick when they go hard. The city was not built. It was negotiated.

It is empty now — or, it feels empty because it is so large for so little. A settlement for maybe sixty people at its peak, but the hall could have held six hundred. The scale is wrong in a way that makes your chest ache. Like someone built a city for children of a different size and left. You call out and your voice does not echo, because the walls that should echo are soft and listening.

Then the sound hits you.

The reason no one lives here anymore.

It is deafening.

At first, from outside, you thought it was wind, or distant water. Inside the vault, it is everything. The cicadas are not in the tree; they *are* the tree. Thousands, tens of thousands of them, their nymphs sealed into the bark like knots, their backs the exact green-brown of lichen so you cannot see them until they move. And when they sing — they all sing at once — the entire building resonates. The hollow trunk acts as a drum, as a throat.

It is not a buzz. It is a solid, physical pressure of sound, a metallic, sawing scream at 120 decibels that makes the moss on the floor shiver and makes your teeth hurt in your jaw. It starts without warning. It stops without warning. In between there is a ringing that is almost worse.

And under that, other sounds ride: the pop and sigh of seed pods opening on the ceiling and dropping warm water, each one a splash; the rustle of geckos, hundreds of them, each the size of your forearm, translucent pink with blue eyes, pouring over the columns eating the cicadas as fast as they sing, their sticky feet making that soft *tok tok tok* by the thousand. Abundant life, yes — the air is full of wings. Tiny emerald wasps that farm aphids on the leaf-walls. Black beetles that roll balls of fallen flower. Swifts that have learned how to live entirely inside, never leaving the vault, darting through the sound like needles through cloth, nesting in the hairy armpits of the branches.

But none of them are ever close together. That is the law here. One gecko per column. One swift's nest per twenty meters of branch. One pod-room empty between every occupied one. The plant enforces a spacing. If two living things stay too close for too long, the floor under them softens and sinks an inch, exposing them. The tree wants emptiness inside its fullness.

There is a deeper rule, and that is the weirdness, and you feel it when the cicadas finally stop.

The light through the leaves should move as the sun moves. It doesn't. Sometimes a coin of light slides *up* a column. Sometimes the same leaf-shadow that was on your left hand is suddenly on your right, without passing over you. The pod-rooms near the far wall are bigger on the inside than the wall is thick. You duck into one that looks like a small pantry and you can stand and stretch and you still can't touch the back. If you stay in one too long, you hear cicadas again even though none are singing, and you can hear yesterday's cicadas layered under today's.

The tree remembers being bigger than this basin. The air inside smells faintly of salt even though the sea is three hundred kilometers away. At the very back, where the roots knot into something that might be heartwood or might be a door that never opened, condensation gathers and drips upwards, from the moss into the air, where it hangs as a little trembling lens and then vanishes.

The water cycle here is closed and wrong. It is damp, but never flows. Dew forms on the underside of everything at all hours and runs sideways to be drunk by leaves that twitch toward it. Pockets of water live inside the columns — you can hear them slosh when the beetles bore past. If you cut — which you shouldn't — the water is warm and slightly sweet.

It is warm here. So warm that dynamic things never stop. Even when the insects are silent, the building is growing. You can hear it creak, a slow, inexorable tightening. A root across the floor thickening by a millimeter while you watch. A new room budding on the far wall, crinkled and wet like a new ear, unfolding itself. The city is still organizing itself for inhabitants that obey its spacing. Still urban-planning itself without tools, without architects, only with sap-pressure and time.

You will not stay long. No mammal can stand the roar for more than an hour. The geckos know this.

On your way out, stooping again under the low root-door into the normal light, you put your hand on the outer bark and feel the vibration of the next chorus beginning, the whole green town taking a breath to scream again, empty and absolutely full.
