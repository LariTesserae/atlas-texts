It is high, but not in the way mountains are high.

Not a peak. A rise so vast you forget you climbed it until your lungs remind you. A continent-sized tableland, a shield of old basalt lifted warm into the sky. The world drops away on all sides in cliffs you never see because the air is always doing something between you and the edge.

This is a mineral country. There are no trees. There never were, or they were boiled out so long ago the stone has forgotten them. No grass, no moss, no weed splitting a crack. Only stone in all its patience: black basalt and red sandstone and salt-white limestone, all worn down into low, rounded, wind-lipped shapes. This place has been exactly how it is for a very long time. Erosion here is not an event. It's a settlement. Water has cut its streets long before any builder came, in broad shallow pans and smooth runnels and potholes big enough to put a house inside. Everything is weathered soft at the edges. The boulders look like sleeping animals. The cliffs look like stacked cloth.

And it weeps.

Water is everywhere not as ocean or river but as condition. The rock sweats. The warm high air, lifted and cooled just enough against the plateau, condenses by tons every hour and does not quite become rain. It becomes a permanent misting, a beading on every surface, a trickle from every overhang. You can hear it before you see anything: the endless tick and drip and sigh of seep. Culverts carved a million years ago run full and clear. Shallow lakes occupy the broad pans between districts — not deep, not blue, more like vast wet mirrors, an inch or two over smooth stone, reflecting the sky and the undersides of factories. The city drinks off the stone itself.

The city. Because this is a city. Not a ruin, not a outpost. A city the size of a weather system.

It began as quarries in the soft-worn rock and then kept going because the stone was so agreeable. They didn't clear land; there was nothing to clear. They carved down, they built up, they bridged. Now it is all construction, layer after layer, but construction that respects the old shape of water. Low wide buildings of poured stone and iron-bolted basalt block sit inside the ancient potholes. Factories and mills and kilns climb the rounded hills in terraces, their brick the same red as the hill beneath. Elevated rail lines run along the eskers. Aqueducts, always aqueducts, carry the weep from one pan to the next, because you cannot stop water here and it is easier to collaborate with it.

Everything is industrial but not frantic. The tech is heavy, mid-century, honest: boilers and flywheels and forges, coal brought up from below the plateau on slow lifts, oil rendered from elsewhere. Gantries creak. Dynamo houses hum. The powerlines sing in the damp. You can see the electricity in the air after dark, leaking faintly blue along the insulators. Nothing is sleek. Everything is patched, painted, re-painted, rusted, reclaimed. The whole city is warm from it — warm stone under your palm, warm steam venting from grates, warm water at your ankles. The sun is normal here, a white-yellow sun at normal height making normal shadows, which makes the strangeness more visible.

For there is nothing green and the world is furiously alive.

The fauna does not need plants. It never did.

They are in the air first — not birds like you know, though some of them have taken that shape. Clouds of them. Flocks so dense they become weather, moving in slow, continent-wide spirals above the city, turning the light brown and grey and white by turns. At closer range they resolve: leather-winged gliders the size of your hand, white as salt, that drink condensation off wires and stone. Glasswing moths big as dinner plates. Beetles with mineral shells, black and iridescent, clicking along every wall. And smaller things, millions of small things, midges and gnats and glass-flies that breed in the inch-deep pan lakes.

On the ground it is more. Crabs — not sea crabs, but high-plateau stone crabs — pale, flat, wide as manhole covers, that graze the biofilm that grows on the wet basalt, that scrape rust from iron with specialized mouths. The city lets them. They keep the machines clean. Hounds of some sort, long-legged, hairless, panting in the warm damp, sleeping in packs on the boiler houses for heat. Rats, of course, but rats that have learned to drink from walls. Lichen-eaters that look like lichen themselves until they move.

And things that process the industrial waste directly — furnace-worms that live in the slag heaps, glowing faintly pink from the heat they eat, excreting neat bricks of iron-rich stone that the builders collect and use again. Pipes are never empty, because something lives in them. Corners are never still, because something is sorting through the grit.

The density of it — the life, the buildings, the water — is such that nothing is alone. Every surface is near another surface. Every sound touches another sound. You walk on a walkway over a street over a canal over a lower street, and below you the crabs clatter and above you the gliders whistle, and everywhere stone drips.

It is loud. Not in a sharp way. In a large, full, layered way. The low thrum of the dynamos never stops. The forges hammer with a rhythm so slow and regular it becomes a kind of heartbeat. Water gargles in every gutter. The air itself buzzes with wings, with chittering, with the clack of chitin and claw on wet stone. You have to raise your voice to be heard by someone next to you. And yet nothing is hurrying.

That is the rule here: slow dynamic. Change happens, but it happens like stone changes. The flocks turn over hours. The factories produce ingots one every six minutes and have done so for eighty years. The lakes evaporate a millimeter per day and are refilled by night. A neighborhood sinks a few inches in a generation as the salt beneath dissolves, and the builders jack it up with screw-pillars without ever stopping the looms.

Nothing grows, because nothing needs to grow quickly. Things accrete. Rust thickens, then is eaten. Stone smooths. A crab molts and leaves a perfect white dome that sits on a roof for years before it weathers into dust. A new aqueduct is built over the course of three years to carry water ten meters to the left.

It's a wet, warm, stinking, clattering, shining, overwhelmingly alive mineral city on top of the world, built not on the land but out of the land's own old agreement with water, and it will be here doing exactly this when the stone underneath it finally decides to be sand.
