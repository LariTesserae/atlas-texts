It is high. Not like a mountain-high, but like a table left up in the sky.

This is a peneplain, an ancient place. So old the rocks have forgotten they were ever mountains. Elevation has remained but shape has not — three billion years of rain and ice and wind have worried it down to a nearly perfect flat, a cold palm held up to a dim sky. If you walk its edge you don't come to cliffs, you come to a softening, where the world simply gives up and slopes away into cloud. The stone underneath is gneiss and schist polished to a dark shine, like the sole of a boot, and you almost never see it.

Because everything is plant.

Not forest. Not meadow. The plant *is* the ground.

A mat, a mass, a living continent of sphagnum and liverwort and black-green hair moss and something that has no name below, growing over itself for ten thousand seasons without ever being interrupted by fire or plow. It is meters thick, cold, saturated, and alive all the way down. You step and you sink to the knee, to the thigh, and the mat sighs and takes you and pushes you back up slowly. Water beads and runs and gathers on every leaf — this air can't hold it. It condenses on the moss-hairs and on your eyelashes and on your exposed skin instantly. The atmosphere itself is wet, not raining, just... weeping. The rocks weep. The plants weep. The sky is a low, even, iron-colored overcast that never breaks and never really throws light, only a dim, sourceless grey that makes everything directionless and close and vast at once.

Cold. Your breath smokes and then is swallowed. The water should be ice. It isn't, quite. It moves too much.

This is not a still bog. This is where the parameters contradict each other and force a solution.

The mat is industrial.

There was never any building here. No hands. But industry happened anyway. Late in the life of the world, the plants learned engines.

Across the open flat — and it is open, you can see for a valley's length in every direction, a town-sized openness with nothing to stop the wind — there are presences set sparsely apart. Not many. The density of things is low. A kilometer away, a tower of moss. Two kilometers beyond that, a breathing hill. In between, aching emptiness, just the flat wet shining plant.

Each presence is a factory that grew itself.

One is a sphagnum heart the size of a house, swelling with saturated air and snowmelt until its skin is taut, then contracting with a great slow THUMP that you feel through your feet. When it contracts it fires a plume of atomized cold water fifty feet into the air which flash-condenses and falls as drizzle. It does this every ninety seconds. It has done this since before mammals.

Another is a stand of black reed that has grown into a loom. No, into a boiler. The reeds are hollow and jointed and they click and shuttle against each other as steam — low-temperature steam, miracle steam in this cold — vents from pores in their sides with a scream like a kettle the size of a train. The reeds have been polishing each other for epochs until they shine like iron.

Another is a sinkhole that isn't a hole but a throat lined with white fungal felt, inhaling wind, exhaling wind, sorting insects from moisture with a shuddering clatter.

The whole plain thrums. Loud. Not with animal noise at first — with mechanical noise *made by the non-mechanical*. Crepitation, hissing, the groan of vast peaty pistons, the drip-and-sizzle of water on a hot spot where some metabolic forge vents briefly. The sound carries for miles in the wet air and stacks up on itself because the sky is so low.

And then the fauna.

Abundant, impossibly abundant for such cold and such dim. Because they farm the engines.

Clouds of midges and glass-winged things live and die in the plumes of the water-hearts. Things like caribou but with hooves splayed into snowshoe leaves crop the living mat and keep it from sealing over completely — their backs are green with symbiotic moss. Things like cranes but three times too large and with no eyes pick their way between the factories, inserting their beaks into valves in the plant-flesh to drink sugar and sulfurous heat. Beetles with shells like riveted boilerplate trundle and eat the dead air. Everywhere movement, grazing, darting, breeding, a thin furious film of animal life over the living engine-field. Sparse in distribution — you can walk a long time and see nothing but moss and mist — and then suddenly a knot, a bazaar, a frantic city of fur and feathers gathered around a hissing vent, jostling, calling, breeding, dying, all at once.

This place is turbulent not in weather but in being. It does not have seasons; it has fits.

Because the erosion here went too far. The world wore so thin that the rules wore thin with it. On this high, old, wet mattress at the top of the sky, reality's seams have come undone from constant rubbing.

Water does not always flow down. Sometimes a rivulet will leave a pool and climb a moss hummock to see what's there before falling back, leaving a dry line behind it. The light, though dim, sometimes arrives a few seconds late — you hear the THUMP of a moss-heart before you see it swell. The wind will stop entirely in a perfect circle fifty meters across and inside that circle the snow that was falling will hang in the air, suspended, slowly rotating. A crane will step and its leg will be a meter longer than it should be for a half-step, then snap back. Peat will slump and for a moment reveal not more peat but a clear view of the sky above from yesterday.

Nothing holds still enough to become a ruin. The factories repair themselves, overgrow themselves, dismantle themselves and regrow a few meters to the left. The mat eats its own engines and births new ones. You can watch a new boiler-reed push up through the sphagnum in an hour, stuttering, steaming, finding its rhythm.

There is no shelter. There is no road. There is no artifact except the living industry itself, which does not know it is industry. If you lie down, the water and the cold will move into you. If you stay still, the midges will cover you, and the moss will begin to consider you as substrate. The factories will keep thumping and hissing regardless, loud and patient in the grey, working for no one, manufacturing nothing but more continuation, on this ancient flat that will never get any flatter.
