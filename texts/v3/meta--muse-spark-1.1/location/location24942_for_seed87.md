It is not an ocean. It is *in* the ocean, but it is not the ocean.

Far down where the ocean stops being blue and starts being weight, below the continental shelf where the light has given up, there is seafloor that is not really floor yet. Pillow basalt, still warm at its heart from whenever it was made — which was not long ago. The planet is still knitting itself here. The rock comes out as soft black dough, hissing, rounding into bulbs, stacking. It is embryonic geology, unfinished, glossy and slick. If you put your hand on it — which you can't, it is too cold and too deep — your fingerprints would melt into it for a second before the water took your heat.

One of those pillows did not close right.

Inside it is a pocket. No larger than a chest cavity, than a bread box, than two cupped hands held apart in the dark. An oval geode of basalt that caught a bubble of seawater as it formed and then, instead of sealing, kept a slit to the outside — a lip, a pore, a mouth no thicker than a fingernail. From the outside you'd miss it. It's just another blister on the black floor, with cold seeping out.

Inside, the water is different.

It is still ocean, but it has been held. Caught. It is denser for being held, colder for being hidden. This is not the open water-column that moves. This is place-water, room-water, the kind of water that lives in a place. 2 degrees, maybe 1. The cold is not sharp; it is absolute. It has no edge. It presses. It makes everything slow and close.

And the room is occupied. Completely.

This is a city, but not built.

There is no technology here. No tool has ever been struck, no wire, no edge. And yet what you are seeing, once your eyes adjust to the dim, is unmistakably urban. It is vertical, layered, organized, dense in the way only a city that has run out of room can be dense.

The builders are the tenants. They are everywhere, so you have to learn to see the building through them.

They are amphipods, or something like amphipods — translucent, blind, the size of rice grains and thumbnail clippings, pink-white with a single black gut-line visible through their backs. There are thousands of them in this pocket. Abundant does not cover it. The water itself seems to granularity with them. They never stop moving. This is not anarchy; it is commuter rush seen in silence.

What they build, they build from themselves. From what the raw rock sweats. The basalt here is still young enough to leach. Minerals bloom on its inner curve like frost — pale whitish cement, glauconite, a sour milk of silica. Sparse vegetation makes use of it: there are only a few strands of anything green, or near-green. Four, five, maybe seven threads of bacterial felt, of black-green weed that looks like wet hair, anchored to the ceiling, drifting down a few centimeters and stopping, too cold to grow longer. They glow very dimly, or they don't glow at all and you are hallucinating light because the darkness is not complete. A few specks of dinoflagellate caught in the room-water flare if bumped and then go dark again.

The animals collect the mineral milk. They chew the rock — you can hear it if you listen with your bones, a gritting, too quiet to count as sound — and they excrete it. They excrete carefully. The city is not hives or combs. It is terraces. Ledges. Balconies the width of an eyelash, stacked one over another from floor to ceiling of the pocket, all the way around, in rings, leaving only a central well of open, cold water that functions as street, plaza, and sky. The terraces are made of that excreted stone, rough, pitted, still warm from the animals' bodies. Each terrace is a fraction of a millimeter thick and holds just enough room for a single animal to pass another if they both turn sideways. The whole thing is layered-construction without an architect: bottom districts are older, darker, encrusted with secondary growth; top districts near the slit are newer, pale, still soft to the touch, collapsing and being re-built as you watch.

Because it is embryonic geology, nothing is hard. Even the basalt that is the city's wall gives when pressed. If a terrace fails, it slumps, not shatters. The animals swarm the slump instantly, eat the failure, re-secrete the street. This is why there is almost no erosion anywhere else — there is no need. The place is under construction perpetually. It is pre-ruin. It is not decaying; it has not yet decided to be solid.

And everything happens in absolute quiet.

There is no sound transmission as we know it here. The water is too small, too enclosed. The ocean's roar is outside the slit and does not come in. Inside, you hear only almost-sounds: the click of a leg joint, the tiny fizz of mineral precipitation, the brush of a thousand bodies passing over balcony railings, the softest possible settling of silt. If you held this city to your ear, you would think it was empty.

It's not. The central well is a traffic column. They ride it — dropping, rising — not swimming so much as letting the density differences carry them. Cold room-water sinks. Slightly-warmer animal-water rises. It makes a slow, silent convection wheel that never stops. They are carried up with eggs tucked under their bellies, they sink down with pellets of that excreted building-mud. Predation happens here too, but quietly, as everything does. Larger ones, almost the size of a pea, translucent blue-white, hang motionless from the undersides of balconies with their feeding arms extended like open gates. Smaller ones blunder into them and simply stop. Then the gate closes.

There is a rule here, and you understand it after a minute of watching: nothing is allowed to be alone, and nothing is allowed to leave. The density is its own law. The slit that connects the pocket to the world ocean is clogged intentionally with living bodies, with a woven gate of old amphipods who have locked legs and died in place, their carapaces now mineralized into a permeable lattice. Water can seep. Light — what little there is, a bruised, deep-sea indigo that isn't from the sun anymore but from the chemical stress of the new rock — can seep. But an individual that tries to push through the lattice is pulled back by neighbors.

It is a pocket-scale city-state. It has no expansion plan. It does not want one. It is hidden completely and it intends to stay hidden. To grow outward would kill it; the cold outside is too vast, too washing, too empty. To be a city, here, is to be held.

If you look long enough, you see that the terraces aren't random. They follow the lines of stress in the cooling basalt, like the city is the idea the rock had of what it could be when it was still soft. 

It is still deciding whether to be rock or to be town.
