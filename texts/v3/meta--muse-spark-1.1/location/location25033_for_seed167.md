It is not ocean the way surface people think of ocean.

It is a drowned town that never knew air.

You are low. Very low. The ground here gave up on being land a long time ago and settled into a wide, shallow bowl, kilometers across, its rim worn soft and round. Not a crater, not a trench — just a place where the planet sags. The slope is so gradual you only know you are descending because the pressure thickens, and the water gets warmer instead of colder.

Warm. That is the first wrong thing. This deep you expect cold to clamp your bones. Instead the water holds you like blood holds heat. 86, 88 degrees, the water of a bath left to sit. There is no current to stir it. It hangs, dense and still, a huge volume that has been still for so long it has learned how not to move. Erosion here doesn't cut, it polishes. Everything is rounded.

And there is no light. Not dim. Not twilight. Dark in the chemical sense — a true, velvet black that presses on the eyes until the eyes forget they are eyes. At first you think you are blind. Then the darkness itself starts to have texture.

This is a city because everything lives too close to live alone.

The ground of the bowl is not mud. It's built. It is a low, sprawling urbanism with no streets, no ruins, no architecture as a human hand would mean it — because there are no hands, no tools. The builders are the residents. The city is grown, secreted, moored, abandoned and re-inhabited.

Look — not with light, with touch, with the pressure on your skin:

Great columns rise out of the floor, twenty, thirty meters high, but they are not stone. They are stacks. They are vertical hives of the dead. Sphere after sphere after sphere, each the size of a house, each a hollowed carapace. Something like a shrimp, like a crab, like an isopod the size of a dog lived in each one, then outgrew it and left it cemented to its mother's house and its grandmother's house. The tower sways, just a little, despite its weight. They are porous, riddled with round windows that never close.

Between the towers, the low neighborhoods. Domes of live coral that does not photosynthesize — it does not need sun. It feeds on the warm broth itself. It builds in dense, interlocking nodules of bone-white and bruise-purple, and every nodule has an entrance rubbed smooth by a thousand passages. Things live inside. Then there are the tube towns. Forests of chitin tubes, each as thick as your waist, supple, packed so tight you cannot put a hand between them. Each tube contains a worm that never leaves. When the worm dies the tube remains, and something else moves in, widens it, lines it with nacre.

And the canopies — above everything, spanning from tower to tower, are sheets. Vast, suspended veils of black protein silk, spun communally by colonies of spider-mollusks. They stretch fifty meters across, sagging under their own weight in the still water, and they are cities on top of cities. They catch marine snow, they catch heat, they catch larvae drifting. They are covered, all of them, with life.

It is dense. That's the rule here. Space is the only scarcity. Nothing is solitary. A surface that stays empty for an hour will be fuzzy with settlers, will be claimed, overgrown, built upon. If you stop moving your skin will itch with new attachment. Layer on layer on layer — that is the law of construction here. Nothing is demolished. It is lived through.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It is a mistake to look for forests. There are things like kelp but wrong: single, huge, dark red blades rising from the tops of the towers, each one solitary, each one twenty meters long, undulating so slowly you think it is not moving at all. They do not root in soil; they root in the living wall. They are old. A few dozen per acre, no more. They are the only things that seem to be alone on purpose.

The rest of the abundance is animal. 

It is impossible to be alone here. The water is thick with bodies. Everything is soft, pale, blind. Fish that have forgotten they are fish — long, ribbon bodies with no eyes, only lateral lines so sensitive they can feel you think. Clouds of isopods the size of rice grains, then isopods the size of hands, then isopods the size of pigs, all transparent and all busy, crawling over every surface, grazing the biofilm off the city, keeping it clean. Squids that do not jet, they walk on their arms, ten-legged along the silk sheets. Anemones as wide as doors opening and closing, opening and closing. 

You don't hear them at first because the water swallows sound differently here. The place seems silent. Then you hold still and your skull begins to pick it up. 

Click. 

A tower settling, its lower shells powdering under the weight.

Click... click... clickclickclickclick — a procession of grazers moving in unison over coral, a million tiny feet.

A low sigh as a dome exhales filtered water from its communal vent, warm water farting upward, rising because it is fractions of a degree warmer than the surrounding warm.

A deep, faraway thump that you feel in your chest more than your ears — a tower falling, somewhere out beyond your reach, finally giving up after centuries. And then, for minutes after, the frantic rush of colonists moving in, a soft rain of bodies and shells clicking and scraping to claim new real estate.

There is no hunting as you know it, not chases. Everything is ambush and filter and patience. Predators here are places. A stretch of wall that looks like wall will be mouth. A silk canopy that sags a little lower than the others is holding a hundred pounds of translucent, stinging colony waiting for the vibration of something passing underneath. And yet everything is in motion. The city grows inward and upward and thicker, not outward. Heat moves through it. Food drifts down endlessly from water too warm to hold oxygen well — flakes of organic dark, dead things above, endless.

No tool ever touched this. No light was ever used to make it. The towers are not engineered; they are accreted, like a habit that became a tradition that became infrastructure. A new tower begins when a larva, heavy and stupid, decides to settle on the exact top of an old one because the water is a fraction cleaner there.

If you stayed, the city would build you, too. Your shape would become a round window, a tunnel, a hard place where softer things attach. The longer you look — and you cannot look, you can only feel — the more you realize it is not a city built in the sea.

The sea built a city out of itself, and forgot to leave any place empty.
