It's not a cave. Not anymore.

You find it because the floor of the service drift gives way at a seam, where the permafrost has heaved and two poured concrete slabs have delaminated by less than a hand's width. The cold air down there is different. Not moving, just heavier. You have to get on your belly with a light off and feel for the draw.

Below surface — not deep, maybe eight meters down — there is a pocket that the survey never mapped. A blister in the earth. When you lower yourself through the crack, your boots don't hit rock. They hit mat.

The room is small enough that you can touch two sides at once if you stretch. Perhaps four meters across, low and not round, but neither square — a weathered ovoid, as if something was once inflated here and then settled, and the ground around it learned to hold the shape. The walls are not walls. That's the first thing that takes a while to understand. There's a substrate, basalt and old, cold silt, but over it, under it, through it, is a lattice.

It is black that is not black. In absolute darkness it would be invisible. With your lamp low you see it doesn't reflect; it drinks. It's a weave of filament and plate, overlapping like fish scales, each scale the size of a thumbnail, each one separated from the next by a gap finer than a hair. From those gaps, the room breathes.

The air is cold in a way that has weight. Zero degrees cold, the kind that doesn't bite immediately, it just convinces you that you are temporary. Your exhale doesn't plume so much as it falls and becomes part of the room. The water in here is not a pool, not a stream. The lattice is a condenser. It pulls humidity out of the permafrost itself — out of the stone — and pearls it on its surfaces. The cold makes it stay. Every scale has a bead of water on its lower edge, catching what little light you brought and holding it. They grow fat, slowly, so slowly, and fall.

Plink.

Not often. One drop every few minutes, from somewhere different each time. It lands in the mat.

The mat is why you can't hear your boots. The floor, the lower walls, the ceiling where it sags low enough — everything is covered in a deep, dense, living carpet that should not be here. No sun has ever reached this place. There should be no animal in this system to carry spores, no bird. And yet it is lush to the point of claustrophobia.

It's mostly moss, but moss like you have never seen in the cold. Pillow moss, frost moss, star moss, layers upon layers of it, old growth on top of dead growth that never properly decayed because it's too cold for rot. The dead layers are black and compacted like peat. The living layers are impossibly green — that deep, wet, viridian green that only lives in the shadow of waterfalls. In places it grows beards a foot long, hanging from the lattice, dripping. In other places it's cropped short and tight as velvet. Tucked into it are fernlets, no bigger than your thumb, but fronded perfectly, and liverworts with leaves like flat, glossy coins, stacked shingle-wise so close you could not slip a piece of paper between them. There is no space that isn't alive. Where the lattice plates are, the moss has grown over the seams and forced its way into the gaps, and the lattice has grown over the moss in return.

There are no insects. No worms. No mice that made a home. The fauna count is zero and the place tells you so. You notice it by the quiet. A forest floor without mites is silent in a way that is wrong at first — no rustle, no hum, no click. Only your own blood, and the slow drip. Mostly quiet.

The faint marks of building are there once your eyes adjust past the green. This was a made place, but not recently, and not for habitation. Near what would be the north pole of the ovoid, a single inspection spar — a white ceramic arm, clean as a bone — juts from the mat, holding nothing. The moss has swallowed most of it, but the tip is still bare, and at the tip is a node of glass that is no longer glass. Lower down, half-buried in the peat, there are three small tablets set into the floor, each the size of a coaster, each etched with a perfect concentric circle that seems to hold depth when you look at it. They are warm. Not warm — less cold. Two or three degrees less cold than the air, so that the condensation around them never quite freezes. The moss does not grow over them, but grows towards them, leaning.

This is the tech. There's not much of it, scattered, almost shy. But the part that is here is well beyond concrete and ceramic. The lattice is not a machine. It's not powered. It doesn't have to be. You get the sense that it was grown, or persuaded, into this shape, and that its function is still happening at a level of physics where your breath and its condensation are the same thing. It holds the pocket open. It keeps the temperature at that precise knife-edge where water is both liquid and almost ice, where life can persist at its slowest possible metabolic rate. It peels a little heat out of nowhere — not heat as combustion, but heat as a permission for these filaments to be slightly less still than everything around them.

Light is almost nothing. Your lamp makes the greens hurt. If you turn it off, which you do after a while because it feels disrespectful, the darkness is almost absolute, 0.1 out of 3 kind of dark, but not quite. The tablets in the floor give off a faint, sourceless luminescence, not bright enough to see by, bright enough to know by. And the water beads glow, just for a fraction of a second after they fall, as if the impact writes a brief luminous letter on the moss and then erases it. Your eyes invent after-images because they are desperate for input. Or maybe the air itself has a grain to it down here.

Everything is in balance. That's what erosion 1.7 feels like here: not collapsing, not raw. Settled. The moss has stopped trying to take over and the lattice has stopped trying to shed it. The drips have made little cups in the peat that fit the drips exactly. The cold is stable. This room could have been like this for a hundred years, or for ten thousand, and it would look the same to you except the mat would be a millimeter deeper.

The rules of this little world are simple and strict:

1. Nothing ever dries completely.
2. Nothing ever warms up.
3. Nothing larger than a spore enters or leaves fast. The crack you came through is the only door, and even air has to think about coming down here.
4. Everything that grows, grows into everything else.

It's an intimate place. A hidden pocket scale where you, for a while, are also layered — your back against moss that is against lattice that is against rock that is against ice, your body touching all of it. The density is not oppressive; it's like being tucked in. The air is clean and tastes like stone and chlorophyll and old water. If you stay long enough to get truly cold, you start to hear how patient it is. The fernlets are unrolling, but at a speed you would need a time-lapse to catch. The water bead on that scale over there is getting rounder. The spar is still holding its absent thing.

You will not stay long enough to see any of it finish.
