It is high. Not high like a mountain peak, but high like a continent that forgot to come down. A plateau so broad it has its own weather, its own weight of air. During the day — if there were a day — you would see it go on until the curvature swallows it. At this hour there is no day. There has not been for a long time, or maybe the light here never got bright in the first place.

The sky is low and thick, not with clouds exactly but with a high, warm haze. Volcanic ash held in suspension for centuries, fine enough to breathe, thick enough to kill any star. No moon cuts through it. The light that exists is indirect: a deep, deep purple-black that is not absence, but a very dim presence. Your eyes adjust and never quite adjust enough.

The ground holds the warmth. That is the first thing you notice. The air is warm too, 28, 30 degrees, an exhale that does not move. At this elevation it should be cold and thin and sharp in the lungs. Instead the rock itself is warm. Broadleaf basalt and tuff and old, red scoria laid down in flows so ancient they've relaxed. Erosion here is not dramatic. Nothing is being carved fast. Everything is settled, beveled, patient. Long shallow wadis cut three, four meters down and a kilometer wide, their edges soft as sanded wood. Rounded boulders with desert varnish. Pavement of cracked plates that ring slightly when you step, because beneath them is hollow.

There is very little that was ever built here, because there was never anyone to build it. No walls, no roads, no foundations. No straight line. The place is untouched in the way a body is untouched — it grew this way.

And yet it is industrial.

At first you think it is quiet. The report would say mostly quiet and it is. The air is too big for small sounds. No birds call. No insects stitch the darkness. You hear your own breath. Then you hear, very far off, a long sigh and a clank.

About two kilometers out, on the plain, there is a shape that breaks the low horizon. Too regular to be rock. A low dome, thirty meters across, black and slick with condensation, with a stack at its crown that is not a stack. As you get closer you see it is breathing.

They are everywhere, once you learn to see them. The abundant fauna. Their density is low — you might walk an hour between one and the next, that is how vast this place is — but there are thousands, millions, a continental herd across the high dark.

You thought technology meant artifact. Here it is metabolism.

Each one is a furnace-animal. Sessile for years at a time, rooted not by roots but by sheer mass. A body like a foundry, like a boiler laid on its side and grown over with a hide that is part keratin, part magnetite. They graze on the wind. The warm, constant, ash-bearing wind that rolls across the plateau carries iron fines, sulfur, silica. They inhale it through gill-slits that open along their flanks with a sound like furnace doors. Inside them it goes to work. You can hear it if you stand near: a low, visceral churning, a percussive thump-thump... thump, not a heart but a trip-hammer. A chemical blast. They smelt the air.

When they exhale, it is through the stack. Not smoke, but a slow gust of superheated steam and spent gas that condenses almost immediately in the cooler upper air. That is where the water lives in this place. Not in rivers. There are no rivers. The plateau is damp the way a boiler room is damp. Each exhalation becomes a brief, warm rain that falls only on the animal itself and the stone around it. The stone darkens. Droplets bead on its back and run down in rivulets of distilled water, too hot to hold. In the shallow pans around its base, water gathers for a few hours, a mirror of black water reflecting nothing. Then it soaks away or evaporates.

In that fleeting wet, things grow. Vegetation here does not cover. It waits. Sparse, low, opportunistic: black lichens that look like grease stains, clumps of wiregrass with roots of glass fiber that anchor in basalt cracks, a succulent that stores steam-water in a leaden leaf. They are all within the halo of a furnace-beast, in the damp ring. Outside that ring, nothing. Just warm, dry, soft stone for a kilometer until the next animal.

And where the plants come, smaller things come. Not abundant like the furnace-animals are abundant, but quick, flitting. Long-legged grazers like pale iron filings on stilts that pick lichen from hot rock. Beetle-things with shells enameled like old factory paint, their abdomens ticking with stored heat. They come to drink the condensation before it is gone. And behind them, hunters — low, flat, silent things that move without lifting their feet, that press their bodies onto the cooling basalt and wait for vibration.

Predation is quiet. A pounce is a smothering. The whole cycle, from distilled fog to lichen to grazer to flat-hunter, happens in the six hours before the pan dries. Then it stops. The plain is empty again.

A few of the furnace-animals are not sessile. You will hear them long before you see them, because even though sound does not carry well here, their sound is too big. A deeper rhythm approaches: CHUFF... CHUFF... CHUFF, with a squeal of metal that is not metal. A walking smelter. On legs like cooling towers, jointed backward, a hundred meters tall if it straightens, which it rarely does. Its belly glows faintly from inside, a deep cherry red that shows through thinner plates of its skin. It moves perhaps a kilometer a day, following some gradient of ash richness. When it drags its feet it leaves furrows that will become the next set of soft wadis in a million years. The water that condenses on its sides falls like rain from its knees.

Their waste is not dung. On the leeward side of each sedentary animal is a cooling slag heap, glassy, black, still warm to the touch, cracking as it contracts. At night — in this endless night — the heap ticks and pings as it gives up its heat. New minerals crystallize there that nowhere else exists. Some of those minerals are what the wiregrass needs to seed.

There is a rule here, and it is simple: energy first, then water, then life, and all of it made by animals. No sun to drive it. The world runs on heat from below and dust from above, and the animals are the factory floor between. They are not machines built to look like animals. They are animals that evolved to do what machines do — concentrate, reduce, refine, heat, cast.

If you stand still long enough in the dark warmth, between the breathing domes, with the faint smell of hot iron and wet stone, you realize the entire plateau is one vast, slow, unbuilt industrial district. No owners. No product. Production for its own sake. The continents of slag and the breath-rain.

Far off, another stacks sighs and clanks. A pan of water steams itself dry. Something quiet rustles in the wiregrass and stops.
