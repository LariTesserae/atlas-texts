It is high, and it is full.

Not high *in* the water, but high in the world, and then made full of water anyway. A summit cistern. A mountain that was hollowed.

You come to it along the spine of an old, old range — the kind of mountains that have forgotten how to be mountains. They have been rained on until their shoulders are round, wind-ground until they are more like the knuckles of a buried fist than any peak. There is no scree here anymore; even the scree has turned to clay long ago and washed away. The rock that remains is soft under its own age, limestone that has been above the weather for so many epochs it has the patience of bone.

And in the top of the highest of these knuckles, someone cut a bowl.

Not a crater. Not a natural sink. It's too perfect, too intentional for that, and too small. The whole place would fit inside a large house, or a small temple. If you could drain it, you could walk its diameter in thirty slow paces. If you stood at its rim on the outside, you would see low clouds below you, and far, far down the forested foothills.

But you can't, because it is full to the very lip and a little more.

The water does not spill. It holds itself in a low dome over the rim, warm as blood, dark as tea steeped in stone. It is not lake-water. It is *held* water — ancient rain and snowmelt that fell into this carved bowl and never left, that was roofed and then unroofed, that has been here so long it has thickened. The temperature of it is wrong for the altitude; it should be cold and gasping, but it is bath-warm, 34 or 35 degrees, heated from below by the slow press of the mountain itself. When you put your hand over it, the air just above trembles.

Everything under the meniscus is made.

This is what total built means at zero vegetation, at ancient erosion: there is not one natural plane left. The builders — so long ago their tool-marks have softened — lined the bowl with block after block of pale, pitted limestone, each block about as long as your forearm, each set by hand without mortar, held by gravity and by time. The blocks have eroded in place until their seams are no longer seams, until they are just darker lines where water pulls slower. They make concentric steps going down, a deep inverted ziggurat: five wide rings, each knee-high, each perfectly encircling. On the north side where the inflow must once have been managed there are two squat pillars, stubby now, and a lintel that would have framed a sluice. On the south side there is a notch that might have been an overflow, now choked with a century of mineral fur.

You cannot see the bottom from the surface because of the darkness. Light here at this altitude should be violent, but the water refuses it. A few handspans down, the sun becomes a memory. At a meter, it is dusk. At two, it is a cave with water for air.

You have to go in.

Slip over the lip — there is no splash, the warm water takes you without sound — and the silence changes. From above, the cistern was quiet because the mountain is quiet. From within, it is quiet because the water is dense with listening.

It is entirely occupied.

There are no plants. Nothing green has ever taken here. The stone is too worked, the water too warm and too old and too deprived of light. Instead, everything that lives here lives off stone and off each other. The blocks are covered in a living varnish — not algae, but a bacterial felt, brown-black, velvety, that grows one micron per year and tastes the limestone. It is the pasture.

And the fauna are its herd, and its wolves, and its farmers.

Thousands of them. In a place this small, that is abundance.

Pale shrimp, nearly transparent, each the size of your thumb joint, flicker along the steps in clouds, combing the felt with double fans. Bearers — blind, segmented things with domed backs like polished river stones — crawl so slowly you only see they've moved when you look away and look back, leaving pale grazing scars on the dark felt. Over them, ribbed eels as long as your arm, the color of wet ash, hang motionless in the water column, vertical, noses up, conserving heat, drifting down by centimeters per hour to sip shrimp from the water. There are snails with shells as thin as paper that have grown into each other; clusters of them share a single fused shell like a small, crowded apartment house, all turning together when the warm current sighs through the bowl.

It is dense in the way a library shelf is dense. Near-touching. Layered. Every block edge hosts a line of still bodies. Every shadow between blocks is a mouth or an eye. In the darkest ring at the bottom — four meters down — the flagstones are not visible at all, because they are paved with living: a carpet of soft, branching filter feeders that are not coral but behave like it, opening and closing in a rhythm so slow your lungs would give out before you could count it.

There is no fear here. The tech was primitive, and so is the ecology, in the oldest sense: hand tools and hand niches. The builders left marks: on the third ring, a series of shallow man-made cups in the stone, each sized to hold an oil lamp. Inside each cup now lives a single large shrimp that keeps it clean. On the lip, there are rope grooves, deep as your wrist where cords were pulled for generations to lift water jars. In the grooves live the smallest of the fauna, white mites that never leave the groove, whose entire world is a half-circle canyon.

There are no sounds but the ones you have to lean into: the faint click of a shrimp's tail, the almost inaudible tap of a snail on stone, the exhale of an eel disturbing the lamination of warm and warmer water. And your own heart, suddenly loud in this small dark sea nailed to the top of the world.

The rule of this place is this: what comes in, stays. The water does not drain. The animals do not leave. The bowl was built to contain, and containment is what it learned to do. If silt stirs, it settles in days, particle by particle. If one of the great ash-eels dies, it sinks to the center and is taken apart so patiently by the bearers that a week later you can see only a faint white outline on the bacterial felt, like chalk.

You float there, warmer than you should be, in darkness that is not empty, in a room made before history where every surface was set by a human hand and then polished by water and by grazing mouths. The rim of the bowl above you is a pale thin circle, far away. The mountain holds you up while the water holds you in.

It is high. It is full. And it is finished.
