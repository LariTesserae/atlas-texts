It is low.

Not low like a valley between higher places — low like the world forgot to make anything high. The curvature is so shallow you can see it fail. The horizon doesn't rise, it just smudges out into wet dark air. That is the only distance there is. Everything else is near.

The ground, if you can call it ground, is not ground. There is no soil exposed, no rock. What would have been ground is plant, and what would have been plant is everything. A total mat, a continent-thick felt of living matter that decided to be its own planet. It is warm to the touch. Not sun-warm — sun never reaches here — but cell-warm, breath-warm, compost-warm.

There is almost no light. Not night. Night is a time. This is a condition. The sky is there, somewhere kilometers up, but you never see it. The air is too full. Warm air holds water until it can't, and then lets it go, all the time, everywhere. It condenses on everything. It beads and runs and falls three inches to another leaf that does the same. The whole forest sweats.

Because it is a forest, technically. Only nothing about it queued up to be a tree.

The first layer you wade through — and you do wade, even on the high hummocks — is knee-high and soft: black-green bladders the size of fists, translucent, full of warm water that quivers when you brush it. They weep constantly from pores at their bottoms. Under that, the trunks. Not trunks. Columns. Fluted, buttressed, sharply ridged because everything here is young. Geologically newborn, still with the creases from pushing itself out of the ground. The ridges are knife-sharp, unweathered, as if the whole low world extruded itself last week and then stopped eroding. You can run your hand along a pleat of bark and it will cut you. The sap that comes out is dark and smells like hot iron.

And above that — and above that — the architecture continues. The canopy is not leaves. Or it is, but leaves that forgot they were supposed to be flat.

They grew into forms that have no business being grown.

Here, a leaf the size of a house has curled fully over on itself to make a hollow tower, open at the top, thirty meters tall, its inner walls ribbed with vertical veins thick as pipes, dripping condensation down into a steaming pool at its base. It stands in a field of ten thousand identical towers, all exhaling. The warm wet air sighs out of them and makes its own low cloud that never breaks.

There, a whole grove has fused into a long, low cylinder on its side, banded like muscle, terminating in a vast pressed mat that shivers taut. A piston. It does not move. None of it ever moves. It has always been at the top of its stroke. Next to it is another, at the bottom of its stroke. The forest is an engine that is not running, or is running so perfectly balanced that motion is unnecessary.

That is the industrial thing. There are no buildings. No cut. No joint, no brick, no rust. Nothing was made. Everything was grown into the shape of having been made. Vats that are pitcher plants big enough to fall into, full of black, quietly fermenting liquid that never bubbles. Sheets of leaf stretched drum-tight between supporting trunks and humming with a low, almost-below-hearing tension — membranes, conveyor belts that go nowhere, loaded with drifting pollen that never lands because the air is too still. Filaments as thick as your arm carry a faint charge. If you brush them in the dark you see a soft blue spark jump from hair to vein and die instantly. The whole mat is electrified, not like a storm, like a grid at idle.

Light, what little there is, comes from that. Not from above. The plants make a little of their own and use it immediately. A seam opens in a tower wall and a sheet of sap lights for a half-second, dull amber, illuminating the wet insides like a factory window at night, then closes. A vein overloads somewhere far away and ticks.

There is no animal life to see it. Nothing eats. Nothing rots, quite. Nothing is hunted. The forest does not need it. It recycles itself wetly, quietly. A leaf-casing that was a tank grows thin and splits along its sharp, young seams, and the warm water inside pours out and is taken up by the bladders at your feet. The casing does not fall. It stays, empty, beside the new casing that has already grown beside it, identical.

It is dense. Not jungle-dense, which is chaotic. This is dense like a loom. You can hardly put your hand between the forms without touching two different kinds of living surface — one waxy and cold despite the warm air, one fibrous and hot. The sound of your own breathing is padded down to nothing. The large spaces are full of small parts. The small parts are full of smaller parts. Water moves through all of it, always, in threads, in sheets.

And it never changes.

Not slow change. No change. The droplets that hang on the underside of every blade stay hanging. The steam that rises from the tower-mouths rises to the same height and stops. You watch for a long time and you realize you are not watching a process; you are watching a state. A vast, warm, wet, working equilibrium. The world made an industry out of being alive, and then reached efficiency so perfect it no longer needs time.

If you stay, your eyes adjust until the darkness stops being dark and becomes a texture — black bark, blacker water, blacker air. You can hear the quiet that is not absence but pressure: the minute creak of turgor holding, holding, holding.
