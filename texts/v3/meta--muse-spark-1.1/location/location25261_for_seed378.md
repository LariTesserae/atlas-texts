It is high. High enough that the sky is a different kind of bright.

This is a caldera, or was. A broad, shallow bowl three or four towns wide, cut into basalt so old it has forgotten it was ever lava. The rim is broken in two places and you can see, very far off, the hazy drop to the lowlands, a blue so pale it looks white. The sun is not filtered here. The air is thin but wet — improbably, heavily wet.

The heat comes from below, not above. The rock itself is hot.

You stand on black glass that has weathered to a softness. Not soft to touch — it will still cut you — but soft in shape. Wind and water have worked it for a very long time, rounding the flows and pressure ridges into broad backs and shallow bowls. This is not a raw volcanic place, spiking and smoking. It's established. Everything that could have slumped has slumped. Everything that could have found a level has found it. The terraces have made themselves.

And everywhere, water.

Not a lake. Not a river system. The groundwater is too high and the rock is too hot to let it lie still. It seeps up through the stone. It weeps out of vertical faces in dark beads and runs in sheets no thicker than a coin. It condenses out of the air you breathe and drips from every cold edge. The whole caldera sweats continually, and the sweat runs.

Because the stone is so hot, less than boiling but near it everywhere, the water never settles. It moves in a lacework of ankle-deep runnels and engineered rills and bright, hot pools that throw back the sun so hard it hurts. You can see the bottom of each pool, white with precipitated mineral — silica, sulfur, salt — that has built up in soft, cauliflower terraces. The sound should be a roar. It isn't.

The town is here because of that contradiction.

They built low and far apart. No core, no downtown, no clustering for shade. That would be wrong for this place. Instead, across the entire bowl of the caldera, at distances that would be a ten-minute walk in the heat, there are structures. Factories without workers, or with one worker each. Pumping houses made of poured stone and black iron. Condenser towers that look like lighthouses, their sides streaming constantly. Long, low sheds with louvered roofs where the air shivers. Pipe, so much pipe, some as wide as a culvert, some as thin as your wrist, running at knee height across the rock, leaping the runnels on little rusted bridges, wheezing just a little when their joints expand.

It is industrial, undeniably. There are gantries. There are turbines somewhere — you can feel them more than hear them through the soles of your boots — turning on geothermal steam. There is the smell of hot iron and ozone. But it is a sparse industry, a listening industry. It doesn't crowd the bowl. It coexists with the empty basalt and the water. About seventy percent of what you see is just wet rock and sky.

There are no plants. Not one. The heat and the mineral and the brightness have made a rule: no chlorophyll. No leaf, no root, no blade. Maybe once, maybe never. Instead the wet rock is painted. In the runnels where water flows fastest, the mineral terraces are stained rust-orange and acid-yellow and a deep, almost-black green by sheets of bacteria you would need a microscope to name. They are not plant, not animal. They are just color, slick to the touch. They grow, and slough, and grow again.

And this is what everything else eats.

Fauna. Abundant fauna, which makes no sense until you crouch.

The pools are alive. The rills are alive. The air just above the water is alive.

In the water, there are things like shrimp but not shrimp: thumb-length, translucent, with no eyes, their backs loaded with that same bacterial mat. They graze constantly, scraping. They flick and scatter when your shadow falls over them, and then return within seconds, because the heat is valuable and to leave a grazing spot is to lose it.

On the rock between pools, there are things like crabs, but their shells are not shell. They are mineral, grown. White and pitted, as if made from the same stuff as the terraces. They walk on stilts of legs to keep their bodies out of the hottest water, clicking from one bacterial pasture to the next. When they die, they become part of the rim of the pool they last fed in.

And above, there are things that are not birds and not insects. The air is so wet and hot and dense with condensation that it can carry weight it shouldn't. They drift. Kites of tight, dry skin stretched over hollow bones, two meters across, almost transparent, riding the thermals off the hot rock and trailing hair-fine tendrils into the steam to collect water and larvae. They never land. They cast moving shadows over the factories and the pools. There are dozens visible at any moment. At night, they fold and hang from the condenser towers like damp laundry.

None of them sing. Or they do, but at a pitch or a volume the hot wet air swallows whole.

That is the most striking law of this place: its silence.

The industrial world here is muffled by physics. The constant veil of steam, the low pressure of high elevation, the softness of mineral deposits on everything that would otherwise clang or rattle — all of it eats sound. The turbine sheds produce a deep hum you feel in your molars, not your ears. The water, for all its movement, moves in laminar sheets over smooth rock. No splash. Even the crabs set their mineral feet down carefully.

You hear, if you stand still long enough: your own breath condensing. The faint tick of a pipe contracting. The occasional, almost inaudible *plip* of a shrimp breaking surface. Far off, the thin whistle of wind over the caldera rim, which never seems to reach the floor.

The light is absolute. At this elevation, with nothing to stop it and with every surface wet, it comes from everywhere at once. The sky is white-blue. The pools are white mirrors. Even the black basalt shines. People who work here — you see them rarely, a figure in a pale heat-suit moving between two pumping houses far off — wear smoked glass over their eyes and cloth over their mouths against the mineral.

If you stay till late afternoon, when the sun finally slants, you will see the weirdness that lets the place hold together. The water, heated from below and cooled from thin air above, forms a permanent, low inversion fog about waist high. The factories and the towers go on above it, but their feet are gone. The drifting kite-things drop their tendrils down into it and disappear. And the crabs, in that hour, all stop grazing at once, turn to face the rim, and raise their mineral backs to the sun like they are photosynthesizing, though they are not.

Then the fog burns off, and the silence returns, and the water keeps seeping up through the world.
