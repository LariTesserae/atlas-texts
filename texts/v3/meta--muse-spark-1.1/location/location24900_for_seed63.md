It is high.

Not high like a mountain peak that tries to be seen. High like a table left out after the rest of the world was lowered. A black mesa in a dark that does not end.

You get there by climbing and the air should get cold, but it doesn't. It gets thinner and hotter. The rock holds the day inside it long after the light is gone — and the light has been gone a long time here. This is a country under a permanent eclipse, or deep in the season when the sun doesn't clear the dust. The sky is not black. It's a deep, deep violet-black, the color of basalt when you hold it to your eye. No moon. A few stars, dulled to pinpricks by heat shimmer. For practical purposes, it is night.

There is no water. Not hidden, not underground, not waiting for morning. The stone is dry all the way through. It's a sea of basalt that cooled wrong — not smooth, not ropy, but shattered into plates and scales, each plate a few hands wide, overlapping like a coat of mail. Between them, a fine dust of salt and iron, red-black, that never becomes mud because there is never water. It sifts down. It never sifts up. When you step, the plates flex and settle with a quiet *tick*.

This is a small place. You could walk its length in the time it takes to get truly thirsty. Maybe three hundred meters, rim to rim. It is a shallow pan, a blister that rose and then collapsed in on itself. The rim is a low, serrated lip, knee-high to chest-high, weathered to blunt knives. The interior slopes down inwards, all those scaled plates dipping toward the center. It is settled, old. Wind had a million years to worry at it and gave up. Nothing sharp remains except what is meant to be sharp. It's in balance with itself.

And it is full.

At first in the dark you think the pan is lined with more of the strange scaly stone. That the stone is breathing. That the darkness has texture.

It is not stone.

The bottom and the sides of the pan are packed with bodies. Not piled. Not swarming. Packed. Edge to edge to edge, like cobblestones made of flesh. Like a living basalt.

They are one kind, or close enough to call them one. Each one the size of a folded hand, a closed fist. They have no eyes — or no eyes you can see in this dark — only a smooth, heat-polished dome of chitin at one end, black as the rock but with a faint, oily translucence where the heat shines through it. Six legs folded tight underneath, not for walking so much as for wedging. Their backs are keeled, ridged, overlapping. They exude a dry wax that smells faintly of creosote and hot iron. They do not eat plants because there are no plants. Nothing green has ever taken hold here; the air is too dry, the rock too hot, the darkness too complete.

What they eat is each other, and the wind.

The outer ring of them, closest to the rim, are dead or dormant, their shells bleached gray by the faint starlight and by time, hollowed, latticed. The wind brings things — ash, spores from thousands of miles away, motes of dead insects carried up from lower, kinder elevations, mineral dust. It catches on the dead shells. The dead become comb and filter. Underneath them, the live ones rasp at the accumulation with a mouth hidden on their underside. You have to crouch and listen very close to hear it: a soft, continuous, almost inaudible susurrus, like someone brushing velvet the wrong way.

They are mostly quiet. That is the rule here. Sound costs moisture. Sound costs cool. So they do not chirp or scream. The pan's activity is almost silent, which makes it more overwhelming.

Because they move. Constantly.

Not fast. Heat makes them slow, conservative. A slow tide in the bowl, a rotation perhaps a few centimeters an hour, but with millions of interlocked bodies it becomes a process. They are migrating around a center. The pan itself generates the heat — not from sun, but from below, from a thin place in the crust. If you put your palm flat to the scales near the middle, you cannot keep it there. The rock a few inches under the living carpet is hot enough to cook. The animals are thermotaxing, trading places. Those that have been too close to the center, too hot, inch outward, climbing over their neighbors who cling and let them climb. Those from the cooler rim, cold and hungry, slip inward toward the warmth where the food gathered by the dead is richer, where the rock exhales faint sulfurous breath that feeds bacteria on their bellies, which they then eat.

It is a convection of bodies. A solid fluid. An ecosystem that is one thing.

And scattered through it, breaking the pattern — built things.

Three, maybe four. Low. At first you think they are just taller rims of rock, but the angles are wrong. Circles of stacked plate-stone, not much higher than your ankle, as wide as a man lying down. Someone made them. Something made them, before the tech level that leaves plastic or wire. A windbreak? A nest? A prayer? The stones are fused now with the wax the creatures secrete. The creatures have grown over them, around them, using them as anchors against their own slow churn. Inside one of the circles, the packing is different. The creatures inside are larger, paler, their domes thin enough that in the near-total darkness you can see a dull amber glow inside each, like a coal not quite out. The queens, perhaps. Or the ovens. They do not move.

If you step into the pan — and you will, because the rim is low and the slope invites — the creatures nearest your foot do not scatter. There is nowhere to scatter to. Density is absolute. They brace. Under your boot, the scales shift, give a little, and hold. Your weight is distributed across a thousand backs. The heat of your body is immediately interesting to them; the rimward side of your boots becomes a little crowded as those nearby adjust by fractions. You feel them not as bites or stings, but as a faint increase in pressure, a living ground rearranging its grain.

The air in the pan is still and hotter by ten degrees than the air on the mesa outside. It smells of hot rock and old wax and faint ammonia. Your sweat never pools; it vanishes before it beads.

Above, the violet-black sky doesn't change. Below, the living floor continues its slow, silent, burning rotation. There is no water to stop for. There is no shade, because there is no light to make shade from, only this deep, warm dark that holds everything close.

If you stay long enough and stay still, some from the middle — the hotter, faster ones — will climb onto your feet. Not to attack. To be warm in a different way.
