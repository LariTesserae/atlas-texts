It is not a cave. Not yet.

It is a room being decided on. 

Thirty meters across at its widest, maybe less — you can see all of it from anywhere inside it if you wipe the condensation from your eyes — a single lung-like bulb below the permafrost, below the basalt, below the old riverbed. The ceiling is low and close and domed, not carved but pushed. The rock is raw, embryonic. Pillow basalt still holding the shape of the moment it cooled, still soft at the edges with pimpled glass, not yet ground down by any water, not yet cracked by frost into soil. It weeps. The whole place weeps.

This is a cold that has weight. Not the cold of wind, but the cold of deep storage. A cold that makes your teeth ache before you've opened your mouth. The air sits at that precise edge where water wants to be ice but can't quite commit. So everything is wet and on the verge of being glass.

And it is absolutely, impossibly full of living matter. There is no surface that is not plant.

The walls are not walls. They are root-mat and thallus and moss and liverwort so thick you could lose your forearm in it and still not touch stone. But it's not growing *on* the rock. It's growing *as* the rock. The plant is the architecture. Great black-green ribs, as thick as a man's torso, arch from floor to ceiling, braiding into each other, fusing, pulling apart, making lattice, making alcove, making nerve. They are not wood. They are something hyper-trophied — damp, flexible, almost animal, covered in a fur of secondary growth: tiny translucent leaves no bigger than fish scales, each one cupping a bead of water that never falls. From the ribs hang curtains of lichen that is not lichen, long beards of frosted white and deep iodine violet that sway though there is no wind. The floor is a bog of living sphagnum three feet deep that rebounds slowly when you step, and under it, more ribs, more weaving. If you dig, you never stop finding more plant.

There are no dead parts. Nothing rots here because nothing is allowed to die in the usual way. When a leaf fails it is immediately unmade and re-woven by the white filaments running through everything.

The light is dim to the point of being a rumor. There is no sun. The plants make their own. Not bioluminescence — not that soft, chemical glow. This is colder and sharper. Points of absolute white-green light appear in the air between the leaves, in the water beads, in the ice crystals forming on the hanging curtains. Pinpricks that hold still and then, slowly, migrate, like stars being sorted. When one goes out, another ignites a few inches away. The total illumination never rises above late dusk, but it's enough to make the wet shine, enough to throw a hundred overlapping shadows from every rib and fringe. Your eyes keep trying to adjust and never do.

It is loud in the way a small room shouldn't be. 

First — water, everywhere. Constant. The stone above the root is still new, still learning how to be impermeable, so groundwater bleeds through it in a hundred cold threads. It hits the mat, patters, finds channels, drips from the tips of the lichen beards with a metronomic click. Drip-click. Drip-click. But layered on top of that, amplified by the dome, is the sound of the place building itself. A low, patient creak, like ice settling, like a ship's hull — the big ribs shifting a millimeter per hour, under incredible slow tension. They groan against the basalt, pushing it outward, making the room a little wider every decade.

And the fauna. It should be still in this cold, but it is abundant to the point of density-sickness. The mat is alive with movement. Everything is near-touching. There are mites the size of your thumbnail that are translucent except for their black, oil-drop guts, dragging themselves through the sphagnum and leaving glowing trails. There are beetles with no wings but with backs like polished hematite that carry gardens of that violet moss on their shells. There are things that are not beetles — arthritic, slow, many-jointed things with fur that freezes and thaws, that farm the light-points, nudging them with antennae. Higher up in the curtains, clusters of small warm bodies huddle — maybe mammalian, maybe not — the size of mice with no eyes and wide, leaf-shaped ears that twitch and spill warm steam. You can hear them breathing. You hear them chewing. A constant, tiny, wet tearing sound as they prune the walls. And underneath it all, a high, almost inaudible chorus, like a field of crickets singing in liquid.

Nothing here was evolved. That becomes obvious if you stay long enough to watch the light-points. They are not light. They are computation. The whole mass is thinking, or calculating, or maintaining. The way a root finds a crack is not random — it solves the crack before it touches it. Frost never forms twice in the same pattern; it forms in patterns that complete each other, tiling some larger logic across the dome. The water that condenses on your eyelashes condenses in hexagonal script for a half-second before it beads. You realize the vegetation is not filling the cave. It *is* the mechanism that is growing the cave, holding the temperature at this impossible wet-cold equilibrium against the geothermal gradient that should have frozen it solid or flooded it warm. It is nanoscale work done at the scale of a cathedral.

There are rules here that are not quite physical. No edge is sharp — erosion hasn't had time to make sharpness, but also sharpness is not permitted. Any angle tighter than about 90 degrees is quickly filled with living matter. No sound echoes cleanly; the moss eats echo. Time moves slowly, but density is high — everything happens at once, but slowly, like a crowd moving through honey. If you stay still long enough, the plants will accommodate you. A rib will grow slightly to support your back. A curtain will part so the drip doesn't hit your head. Not because it cares. Because your presence is a variable it hadn't yet solved, and it solves it.

It is a cradle, or a womb, or a prototype, or a seed-bank computer that has mistaken itself for a forest. It is still under construction. The rock is still soft. The air is still learning how to be air. You get the overwhelming sense that if you came back in a thousand years, this small, loud, freezing, soaking, chokingly full room would still be here, only slightly larger, still dim, still creaking outward, having finally figured out what shape it was meant to be.
