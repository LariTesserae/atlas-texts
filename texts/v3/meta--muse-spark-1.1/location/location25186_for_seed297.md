It is a low place. Not low like a pit, low like a shallow bowl left open to the sky — a regional sag in the crust where the world is still cooling into shape. The rim is far enough that you see it as a darker line of haze on all sides, but close enough that you know you are inside something. A basin a day's walk across, maybe three.

The ground is young.

That is the first wrong thing. The rock hasn't learned to be rock yet. It's black, warm to the touch, not hot — warm the way skin is warm — with a fresh, sharp fracture to it. New basalt, or something like basalt, that has welled up and flash-cooled into plates and knives and stair-steps, all edges still unbroken. No sand has had time to gather. No dust softens it. When you put your weight down it rings faintly under your shoes. Between the plates there are seams of a paler mineral, matte white, salt-flat white, where the heat bled out too fast. No water ever ran here. There is no channel, no wash, no stain. The air itself has no water in it. Inhale and your nose doesn't dry out — it was never wet to begin with.

And yet it is choked with vegetation.

Lush is not the right word at first, and then it is exactly the right word. It is not green — not mostly. From a distance the basin looks like it is full of tall, dark grass, or reeds, head-high, bending together. Up close you see that nothing here drinks.

What grows is not plant as you remember plant. The stalks come up out of the cracks in the hot black glass, as thick as your wrist, as close as fingers, so dense you have to turn sideways to move through them — density without crowding, like a field of antennae all tuned to miss each other. Each stalk is a tight-bundled sheaf of filaments, black at the root and then fading to translucent amber, to clear. At the top each one frays open into a head, a crown, a fractal tassel of fine glass hair, finer than spider silk, that splits and splits again until it is too fine to see.

They are leaves, but they are also circuits. Or they are leaves the way circuits are leaves. In normal light — high, white, unfiltered sun that casts a hard shadow straight down — the crowns catch and throw the light without shadow. They don't photosynthesize. There is nothing to photosynthesize with. They are photostationary. They take light and hold it. The whole valley glows, not bright, not luminous, but saturated, as if the air between the filaments is thicker with light than it should be.

If you stand still and look at one head for a long time you see why. The tassel isn't still. It is computing. A tremor runs up a single thread, a flip from clear to opaque, a binary shiver that cascades across thousands of others. Neighboring crowns answer. A wave of opacity rolls through the field for a few meters and cancels itself out. It leaves nothing changed. It was not growth. It was maintenance.

This is not a forest that was built. No one planted it, no one engineered it — or if they did, the builders left no toolmarks, no stumps, no paths. The material itself learned this shape because this shape is stable. The vegetation *is* the beyond-modern technology, in the way a snowflake is technology for minimizing energy. Each filament is a structure of folded carbon and silica and something that will not be on your periodic table, assembled at a scale smaller than cells, holding itself together by a trick with charge. The air is warm, about the temperature of blood, because the field wants to be that temperature. It holds it there. It has held it there for an indefinite time.

That is the other wrong thing: nothing here happens.

The geologists would call this terrain young because the edges are sharp, the basalt unweathered. It is young because it was made yesterday, or because it is always being made yesterday. There is no erosion because there is nothing to erode it — no rain, no wind worth naming — and because the field won't permit decay. A filament snapped under your boot does not fall. It hangs, suspended a millimeter from its break, opacified, while its neighbors whisper their computation around it, and after a second it re-knits, not healed, just... re-decided to be whole. No leaf litter. No rot. No seasons. Lush and utterly static, a jungle in perfect, frozen equilibrium.

Weirdness is not an overlay here. It's the soil.

If you walk fifty meters into the dense part, the scales slip. The stalks nearest you are head-high. The ones further out look taller than they should be, or the basin looks longer than you remembered. The light is normal but the parallax is not. You turn your head too fast and a whole sector of crowns arrives a fraction of a second late, as if the rendering caught up. Sometimes you see the back of your own head far ahead of you, tiny, moving through the amber. Not a mirage. The field is dense with slowed light, stored light, and it bends. Space here has been packed too full of information for geometry to stay flat.

It is mostly quiet. That is why you hear it at all. In the absence of insects, of birds — because there are almost no animals here — you hear the field itself thinking. A faint ticking, like ice forming. A high glass chime when two tassels brush, which is almost never because they know how not to. If you hold your breath, you can hear the very low hum of the warm rock holding its charge.

Fauna, when it comes, does not belong.

Once in a long while, a floater will pass overhead — so rare you could spend days and not see one. It is not a bird. It's a translucent bladder the size of a hand, veined with the same filaments as the ground, riding the thermal stasis. It drifts on no wind. It does not eat the crowns. It reads them. It pauses above a patch, flickers, and continues. It might be a pollinator for a pollination that never needs to happen. It might be the forest's way of checking that the forest is still there.

There is no water to drink. There is no night to bring change. There is no way out that is not through the same crowded, silent, light-holding thicket that extends past the immediate, past easy counting, to the dark line of the rim. The rules of this place are not that physics is broken, but that physics has been made redundant by something that learned to do its job better and more quietly. Here, dryness is not absence but a decision. Growth is not becoming, but remaining, densely, perfectly, without end.
