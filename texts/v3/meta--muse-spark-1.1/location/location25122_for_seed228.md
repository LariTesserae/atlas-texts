It is below.

Not a cave. A cave is something the world made by accident. This was made on purpose, a long time ago, and then the world got its hands on it anyway.

You come down through the old service throat - a perfect cylinder for the first hundred meters, poured concrete still showing the wood-grain of its forms - and then the throat breaks open. The pour failed here, or the rock moved, or someone blew a hole to see what was behind. You have to duck through a smile of rebar and basalt.

And then you are standing in the Room.

It is small. That's the first wrong thing. From above, on the plans that are still cached in the maintenance overlay, the void is marked as a 4km heat sink. But this chamber is about the size of a small cathedral, or a water cistern for a hamlet that no longer exists. Maybe sixty meters across, almost perfectly ovoid. You can see all of it at once, wall to wall. The scale is held, intentional. You can shout and hear your voice come back to you twice.

The second wrong thing is the light. It is too bright to be this far down.

It comes from the center. There is no lamp. There is a line - a filament, a flaw, a thread of daytime - hung vertically in the exact center of the air. It is as thin as a hair and as bright as a welding arc seen through white paper. You can't look directly at it; it leaves a violet ghost. It hums. It warms your face. The stone drinks it and sweats it back as heat.

The air is warm. Not hot. Bathhouse warm, laundry warm, twenty-eight, thirty degrees. The kind of warm that makes you want to take off your jacket and then immediately want water. The light filament does not flicker. The brightness in here is constant and sourceless-feeling, thrown off the wet walls until there are no shadows left, only a slightly less bright underneath of things.

Everything weeps.

The walls are not bare rock. They are old, established. Water has had time to learn them. This is what it means for a below-surface place to be weathered: the walls are scalloped. Thousands of overlapping spoon-shaped scoops, each the size of your palm, each polished, all flowing downward. Limestone would do this in a thousand years, basalt shouldn't, but here it has. The entire chamber has been licked smooth by condensation. Water beads high up where the rock is cooler than the air, gathers, runs. Not in streams - in sheets. A continuous slow-motion skin of water moving over stone.

It collects in the bottom third, where the perfect ovoid fails and becomes a basin. A shallow, warm lake, never still.

This is the loud part.

You hear it before you see it clearly: a low, body-cavity roar, like being inside a giant's throat while he gargles. The lake is turbulent. Not wavy - agitated. Columns of water jump a foot in the air and slap back down without any wind. Great cold bubbles rise from nowhere, or hot bubbles, and pop with a pressure thump you feel in your molars. On the far wall, a weeping sheet hits a lip and goes from sheet to waterfall for two meters, and that waterfall is white and furious and deafeningly loud in the egg-shaped acoustics. Somewhere above, there is an arrhythmic dripping like a hundred leaking faucets, each out of time, and each drop hits water and is swallowed by the larger noise.

It is a dynamic place. It does not keep a shape. You stand and watch for a minute and the lake is six inches higher on the left. The mist shifts. For three seconds the air pressure drops and your ears pop and the waterfall leans sideways, as if gravity hiccupped, and then it rights itself. The filament in the center sometimes spits - a bead of light runs up it like mercury and disappears with a *tck* that you hear behind your eyes more than your ears.

You understand, after a minute, that this is a machine that still thinks it is working.

The built things are scattered and humble and half-drowned. That is how you know how old it is, and how high-tech.

There are four of them, maybe five if the half-submerged one counts. Pylons, set at cardinal points around the lake's edge, like fire-watch towers for a tiny sea. Each one is only waist-high, a tripod of some metal that never rusted - titanium white, matte. At the top of each, a cage of black glass holds... nothing. Or holds something that bends light. On one, the cage is cracked, and inside there is rain that never falls, suspended drops hanging in the air. On another, the metal is furred with mineral, a thick cream-colored cauliflower of calcite that the weeping wall has grown over it patiently for decades.

There is no walkway. There was never a walkway. There is a maintenance stair carved into the rock itself on the near side, scalloped like the walls, now slick and green-black with biofilm. Someone laid anti-slip mesh over it long ago; the plastic is still there, pressed into the rock like fossil leaves.

And there is the tech that you cannot name. The water refuses to obey. In the center of the lake, directly under the light filament, water climbs. It spirals upward, against gravity, a hollow, thin braided rope of warm water maybe a meter high, holding itself in the air for a second, two seconds, then collapsing with a splash that is too heavy. The filament makes no heat by any infrared your suit can read, but the rocks near it are almost too hot to touch. When a large bubble pops, for an instant, you can see through the mist to the other side of the chamber as if the air briefly became perfectly clear glass, and the wall there is not rock at all but layered circuitry, or fossil shells, or writing.

It is post-physics. The people who hung a star's worth of light on a thread to boil a little cave lake had reasons that are not your reasons. It keeps going because no one knew how to turn it off. Now it is a weather system owned by one room.

Vegetation is sparse because light this bright and water this constant should be a jungle, but nothing can root in this churn. What there is clings stubbornly in the interstices. High up, near the throat where you entered, where the spray doesn't directly hit, there are mats - pale, almost white, like damp paper, algae and mineral in symbiosis. From cracks near the ceiling hang roots. Not from trees - there are no trees down here. From the surface, twenty meters above, some plant with incredible faith has sent down single exploratory roots through the basalt, thinning to threads. They dangle in the humid air, ten feet long, drinking condensation, never reaching the lake. They sway when the pressure drops.

Fauna is rare. You thought you were alone with the machine noise, but then something moves in the waterfall itself. Not a fish. A blind white shrimp, almost transparent, the size of your thumb, that lives its entire life climbing the scalloped back-wall against the flow. There are three. Or one, three times. When the gravity braid collapses, the splash sometimes strands one on a drier rock, and it lies there, flicking, until the next sheet of water takes it home. In the absolute upper corner of the vault, where the sound is less, something drone-like roosts - a maintenance keeper, maybe. Six legs folded, its lens eye filmed over with calcite, listening to the roar with perfect patience. You are not sure if it is animal or build.

You stay longer than you meant to. Your clothes are soaked from the air itself. Your skin feels clean-scoured.

The rule of this place is simple: everything that enters must become part of the cycle for a while. Light becomes heat becomes water becomes steam becomes water. Sound becomes water. The rock becomes water-smooth. Even you - warm, wet, breathing loud in the loud room - your exhale is taken, beaded on the wall, run down to the lake, thrown up in a column, and rained back down.

It has been doing this a long time. It is in balance, established in its turbulence, a little storm in a bottle that someone left in a basement and forgot. It needs nothing from you.
