It is not a valley. It is a pocket.

From above, on the white pan, you would walk right over it and never know — a salt flat so flat and so bright the sky bleeds out, low elevation, low air, heat pressing down like a hand. The crust is weathered into soft, wind-combed ripples, a pale settled thing that has been exactly itself for a long, long time. And then the ground breaks.

Not a crater. A blister that popped inward. A throat.

Maybe twenty meters across at the lip, the salt curling over like a lip of chipped porcelain. It drops straight for about twelve meters and then bells out, wider than it should be, into a hollow, a hidden room in the earth. The light falls straight down at noon, undiffused, brutal, white-gold, and hits the bottom and does not stop.

Because everything inside is bright.

This is a city that fits inside a single room.

Not a metaphor. The whole urban footprint is contained in this one hollow, low and cool compared to the pan but still warm — a baking, greenhouse warmth, 33 or 34 degrees, the kind of warmth that makes things grow fast and tight. And it has grown.

There is no water here. Not a film, not a seep, not a damp line up the wall. The salt is bone-dry. The air is so dry your nostrils stick together when you breathe. This place has been sealed against water by some old chemistry of the pan. If you brought a cup of water down here it would be gone to the air in an hour, and the locals have a word for that kind of waste that is also their word for murder.

And yet it is lush.

It is choked, it is riotous, it is impossible. The vegetation is not green the way wet things are green. It is a waxy, turpentine green, a dusty olive and jade, leaves thick as your thumb, coated in a fine, pale powder that tastes like salt and aspirin if you touch it. They are not plants that drink. They are plants that hoard. Every leaf is a sealed bladder of bitter oil and sugar, tight as a drum. They pull what little they need straight from the heat and the night-air, condensing ghost humidity on their powder. They have trunks like braided rope, not wood but a dense, fibrous pith that can be split with stone mauls into long, flexible laths. Nothing rots here because nothing is wet enough to rot.

The people — who are almost not here anymore — learned to build with them before the trunks hardened.

That is the urban thing. This is not a forest with huts. This is a city, a dense, layered, intentional city. From the shaft walls down, every surface is scaffolded, woven, braced, and inhabited. Homes are not built *among* the stalks; the stalks *are* the homes, grown and then trained and then hollowed while still alive, the living walls continuing to sprout leaves from their roofs. Streets are ladders of knotted fiber and notched pith that creak. There are plazas where three massive leaf-buds were pulled apart as they grew and now make a vaulted ceiling that still photosynthesizes. There are towers that are simply the flower-spikes, twenty feet tall, now dead and hard as shell, reamed out and compartmented into granaries, lofts, shrines. It is organized. You can see the districts: the weavers on the dimmer north wall where the oil-leaves grow thinnest and most pliable, the pit-singers near the bottom where the sound collects, the abandoned cradles on the bright west side.

Primitive craft, all of it. Hand-knotted cord from inner bark, stone adzes stained green, bone awls, copper-salt glaze where a few people figured out how to smelt the pink nodules that grow like potatoes in the salt. No machines. No nails. Everything is lashing and tension and living growth correcting itself over years. Established. Weathered into its own logic.

The density is wrong for a city. It should be crowded. It is empty. Almost nothing per volume. You can stand on a cross-bridge of woven trunk fifty feet up and look down through layers of green and dry air and see nobody. Vast space between presences. A few hammocks, a few cold hearths. A child’s toy made of seed husks left on a sill. The emptiness is not ruin — nothing has fallen — it is thinning, as if the place forgot to keep replacing its people.

And the sound.

You hear it before you see the rim, out on the pan. A low, physical pressure, not loud yet. As you drop down the lip it becomes impossible.

Every one of those oil-plants, when the sun hits its mature pod-branches at full brightness, cooks off. The pods are the size of fists, striated, and they hold their oil under tension. When they reach temperature they split along a seam with a crack like a rifle shot. *KRAK!* One at first, then another answer from the opposite wall, then a hundred, a thousand. In heat, in full light, the whole city detonates continuously. The pods are not dying, they are seeding — they fling a puff of black, cottony fluff that hangs in the bright air, never falling Damp because there is no damp, just suspended. And as the pods split they leave a hollow horn behind, a short, woody trumpet that the hot wind plays.

So the daily weather, the dynamic, is this: heat rises off the salt, spills over the lip, falls into the hollow, churns, lifts again, whistling through ten thousand empty horns, while new pods crack and crack and crack. It is deafening. Not white noise. Polyphonic. A siege of percussion so loud your teeth buzz in your skull, your chest feels it. You cannot speak. The inhabitants, when there were more, did not speak at noon. They pressed their palms to the living walls and felt each other’s existence through vibration.

Rare fauna — only one. Or two if you count humans. Down in the dimmest, most braided bottom where the cotton-fluff has collected in deep drifts that have never felt rain, lives a huge, pale, slow beetle, almost the size of a cat, with a back like a drumhead. It eats the oil-leaves and nothing else, and it listens. During the cracking hour it goes completely still and resonates.

You come up out of it after an hour with powder in your ears, your clothes smelling of sharp green turpentine and hot salt, your hearing dipped for minutes afterward. Behind you the lip closes to a white line again. From the pan, you see nothing but flatness. Somewhere under your feet, a hidden, bright, overgrown, fully formed town continues to pop and howl and grow into itself, utterly dry, utterly lush, furiously loud and completely empty.
