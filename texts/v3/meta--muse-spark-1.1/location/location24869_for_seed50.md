At the very top, where the mountain should end in a knife or a cairn, it doesn't.

It opens.

Not a crater — that's too grand a word. A pocket. A pulled tooth. A hollow no wider than a small room, maybe three strides across, scooped out of raw basalt that hasn't learned how to be a mountain yet.

The rock here is wrong. Everywhere else on this ridge the wind has spent ten thousand years sanding everything down to a weary smoothness. Here, the edges are fresh. Razor-sharp. The basalt broke yesterday, or this morning, or is breaking right now. You can see the conchoidal shatter where it gave way, black glass glittering with embedded olivine still bright green, unoxidized. The interior of the hollow is unweathered, unlichened. It has no business being open to the air. Erosion hasn't found it yet.

And it is full of water. More than full.

This is the high tarn that cannot exist. At 4,000-odd meters the air is thin and cool and clean enough to hurt your teeth. The sky is a normal, brutal alpine blue, sun at full noon white-gold, so bright it makes the snowfields below ache — but the hollow is submerged. Completely. The water doesn't sit level with the rim. It domes.

It bulges up over the lip of the rock by a foot, maybe two, a perfect, trembling lens of cold water held aloft by nothing you can name. Surface tension at a scale that shouldn't hold. Or cold that thickens it. Or some older rule. You expect it to spill, to pour down the scree and be gone, but it doesn't spill. It strains.

The whole thing is in violent motion.

That's the first thing you notice before you even see it — the sound. A deafening, body-cavity hum. Not a waterfall crash, but the sound a bell would make if it were made of water and struck continuously. The dome vibrates. The wind coming off the summit hits it and the water shivers and drums and roars, a low, relentless, skull-rattling thrum that goes through your boots, up your shins, into your molars. You have to open your mouth to equalize it. Within arm's reach of the bowl you cannot hear your own breath. You feel the mountain doing work to keep this one impossible pocket intact.

And inside the water, everything churns. It's not a placid pool. It's turbulent to the point of frenzy. If you get low, eye-level with the lip, you see through the daylight which hits it absolutely straight — normal light, no gloom, no deep-sea filter — that the water inside is not still for a quarter second. Currents rip in collision, upwellings burst from the raw rock bottom that you can barely glimpse, flecks of new stone still falling from the unfinished walls. Vortices form and unform, collide, erase each other. A storm in a teacup, but the cup is the whole world. You watch for a minute and every shape the water takes is destroyed before it can become a shape. It's creation and erasure at a frantic, exhausted pace. Solaris in miniature.

There is almost nothing in it. The density of this place is void-like. The water is almost pure snowmelt, too cold and too new to hold much. Light passes through it with nothing to catch on. At the very bottom, raw basalt dust still settles and is kicked up again. That is all —

— except. Sparse. A thread.

A single, stubborn strand of green-black algae has anchored to the sharpest edge inside, waving like a flag in a hurricane, flattened, then snapping erect again. On the north side of the rim, where wind-shadow has allowed a pinch of grit to accumulate, three blades of alpine fescue have rooted, bleached pale. They shiver. They have no company.

The built things: someone else found this before you. Or many someones over a very long, intermittent time.

Around the windward half of the rim, a low, inadequate wall has been stacked, no higher than your ankle — flat stones laid by hand, without mortar, in the way of sheepfolds. Not to keep animals out. To keep the water in, maybe? Or to keep it from growing? Some of the stones are tumbled out of place and lie where the turbulence tossed them. Inside the wall, cupped into a natural notch, is a ladle. It is carved from a single piece of mountain juniper, blackened, simple, primitive. The handle worn smooth by hands. Next to it, a rope — not real rope, but twisted yak hair and sinew — tied to a piton of soft, hammered copper driven into the raw rock. The other end disappears into the dome.

Someone was trying to drink from it, or feed it, or measure it.

There is no lake beyond. Just this. Just a hidden room on top of the world that contains an ocean.

If you put your hand in — and the coolness tells you to, because the air is thin and cool enough to make your fingertips numb and the sun makes you want cold — the water does not feel like water. It feels heavier. It resists. It pushes back, and the roar spikes an octave, angry. The vortexes rush to meet you. And deep inside, for a second, you see something move that is not water. Fauna, rare to the point of almost imaginary: a trick. A pale salamander, or a shrimp, completely translucent, as large as your thumb, blind, flipping end over end in the chaos, living its whole life inside a bell of water on a mountain that was born last week. It has nowhere else to go.

When you pull your hand out, the dome heals. The surface reseals over itself with a skin that sings.

Above it, nothing. The empty alpine sky goes on forever, with miles and miles of air between things. Below it, the mountain drops away. But here, in this pocket, the ocean has decided to hide and hold its breath.
