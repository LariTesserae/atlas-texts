It's not a valley so much as a dent.

Someone took a broad, flat piece of low country and pressed their thumb into it. A shallow bowl, maybe three hundred meters across, rim to rim. You can see the whole thing at once if you stand on the levee road that rings it. Beyond that road, in every direction, the peat fields go on flat and black and empty under a normal, even, overcast sky. Inside the dent is something else entirely.

The bowl is low. Low enough that water never really leaves. The land here is just barely above the water table, cool to the touch, and everything inside the dent is wet in that permanent way — not flooded, not a lake, but soaked. The ground sweats. The walls of the bowl weep. The clay and old root-mat that forms the sides of it have settled a long time ago into their final, softened shapes. Gullies that have been there for a hundred years carry the same dark water down in the same braids. This is not a place that is being carved. It was carved. Now it just glistens.

And it is busy. Violently busy.

This is the wash-pit for the peat works. Or it was. Or it still is in some half-abandoned, never-really-stopped way.

The bottom is not ground, exactly. It's a mat of duckboards, pallets, sheet steel, and tongue-and-groove sluice gates laid down over sucking mud, so dense you can't see the mud except where something has just been pulled up. Layer after layer after layer. Pipes go over pipes. There are three or four pump houses — small, unpainted, corrugated tin on softwood frames, each the size of a garden shed — but they are scattered almost at random, and between them are the things that have accreted: winches, chain-falls hanging from a single gallows-timber, a small electric gantry crane that runs on a short curve of track that goes nowhere, spools of cable, a concrete mixer with its drum removed and used as a water tank. It's all industrial, post-steam but not new, electric motors the size of pigs wrapped in tarps, breaker boxes that tick. Nothing large enough to be called a factory, but the whole bowl hums with scaled production in miniature. A hamlet of work.

There is almost no vegetation. The water is too acidic and too disturbed. A few tufts of coarse sedge come up through the pallets, bleached yellow-green. On the driest raised beam, a little black lichen. That's it. The cool air smells of cold iron and peat and wet wood.

Because of the water, everything moves, but nothing crashes.

The water is doing all the work. It comes in from the walls in steady, clear weeps and from below in darker upwellings. It collects in the cuts and then somebody — the two or three people who live and work here, in rubber boots and patched coats — opens a gate and it rushes, or they start a pump and it lifts. For a few minutes a pond deepens where there was a walkway. A stack of cut peat softens and slumps. A sluice clogs with roots and then blows. The whole floor of the bowl is in a constant, low-level state of rearrangement. Not a storm, but a restlessness. One corner drains while another fills. A pallet is pushed to make a dam. The little crane shudders along its track hauling a dripping load of black brick from one pile to another pile that wasn't there an hour ago. Then that second pile starts to melt back into the water too.

If you watched it for a day you'd see the whole shape of the interior change five or six times and still look exactly the same from the levee. Old ruts reappear. The same puddle forms in the same place despite everything done to prevent it. This is what a long-existing turbulence looks like — not wild, but established. Settled into its own instability.

For all that movement, it is very quiet.

Water moves over peat without splashing. The electric motors, when they run, are underwater or under blankets of damp, and they make a dull, stifled whine rather than a roar. The people don't talk much, or if they do it's low. The air itself is saturated, condensing on every cold surface, dripping with a soft tap...tap...tap that never quite becomes a rhythm. The loudest thing for long stretches is the buzz of a single power line overhead, beading with water.

You don't see animals. The water is empty and the machines are too close together. Once, a heron lands on the levee to look in, decides there is nowhere actually to stand, and leaves.

What is unusual is how close everything is.

There is no space to spare here. To walk from one pump house to the other you have to turn sideways between a still-hot transformer and a stack of chains, then step over a channel that is six inches deep but looks bottomless, then duck under drying lines where a hundred identical black work gloves have been hung. The density is not of people but of presences — objects, fixtures, stored intentions — pressed near-touching in a shallow bowl of cold water that never stops trying to take them apart. And they have all become the same color by now: rust black and wet-wood brown.

This is the rule of the pit, if it has a rule: nothing is ever finished, nothing is ever removed. Wet keeps bringing in, work keeps shuffling around, cool keeps things from rotting, and evaporation never gets ahead of the seep. So everything stays, half-sunk, essential.

At dusk, under the normal gray light getting flatter, the single bare bulb in each pump house comes on, reflected three times in the shifting water and once more in the thick film of condensation on the inside of the tin walls. From the levee road the bowl looks almost like a small, cluttered room lit from within, below ground level, full to the brim and quietly overflowing.
