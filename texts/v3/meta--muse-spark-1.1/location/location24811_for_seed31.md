It is a high place.

Not a peak — a lifted place. A table cut out of the sky. You come up through thinner air for a long time and then the climbing stops, and the world goes flat again, but higher. A young mountain, still learning to be a mountain. Its sides are still sheer. The basalt is still sharp where it broke. There has not yet been time to soften. From the edge you can see the low country far below as a haze, but the top itself goes on for miles, a broad, tilted basin open to the air.

It is warm up here, anomalously warm. The sun is bright and close in normal, clear light, but the air does not cool the way high air should. It holds heat like a bathhouse. The stone itself holds heat. Low clouds snag on the cliffwalls every afternoon and dissolve, not into rain exactly, but into everything becoming wet.

Everything weeps.

The city is here because of that. On a mountain that should be dry and cold, there is water everywhere. It does not come in rivers. It comes out of the rock. The plateau was lifted so fast that the water table came with it, and now the stone is squeezing itself. Seeps run down every fresh-cut face. Springs open clear-eyed in the middle of streets. Condensation beads and runs down every wall before noon. The air tastes of wet rock. Footing is always damp. The light is doubled by reflection.

The city is not dense the way low cities are. It is urban in extent and in intention, but it respects emptiness. It is spread thin across the table — a town the size of a valley. Low, broad terraces cut into living stone with stone tools. Retaining walls of black, still-angular basalt block, fitted without mortar. Flat roofs. Wide cisterns. Houses that are half quarry and half dwelling, their back wall still the mountain's own. Between them, lots left intentionally open: levelled plazas of packed ash and gravel, kitchen gardens, water gardens, and fallow stone.

The infrastructure is water. That is what has been organized. Everywhere, the quiet engineering of a people who have only hammer-stones and copper and time. Shallow canals, no deeper than your forearm, straight as a rule-line, running alongside every avenue, carrying clear, slow water from the high side of the plateau to the low side where it sheets off the cliff in a thousand veils. Sluice gates made of heavy fitted slabs you lift with both hands. Settling pools where the water stops and goes still and green with algae. Stone aqueducts only two or three feet high, crossing shallow depressions. There are no pipes. There is no need.

And vegetation has taken this system and made it lush.

Not in a jungle way. In a sparse way. The city is not overgrown; it is gardened by neglect. Wherever water slows, moss comes. A thick, bright, almost yellow-green moss that can live on bare rock if the rock stays wet for an hour a day. It covers the north side of every wall, the top of every cistern lip, the stair treads that are not taken often. Ferns grow in the joints between basalt blocks, unfurling where mortar would be. In the unmanaged lots, there are thickets of a kind of reed-grass with black stems, and low, wide-leafed ground cover that holds dew until evening. On the far side of the plateau, where the town thins out into the natural tableland, there are groves of stunted trees — really giant ferns with woody trunks, their crowns shaggy, their roots in standing water.

It is warm and wet and green enough that you expect noise. Insects. Birds. Frogs in the cisterns. The froth of a living place.

There is none of that.

The plateau was lifted too cleanly, too high, too recently. Nothing has made it up here on its own yet except spores and seeds on the wind. So the city is silent in a way that a place so wet should never be. Water moves but does not splash. There is no hum, no chitter, no call and response. You can hear the faint tick of condensation dripping from a roof into a canal three houses away. You can hear your own feet on wet stone, and the soft grind when someone two streets over shifts a sluice stone. When wind moves across the table it moves through reeds and makes them hiss, but it is the reeds only.

In the cisterns, the water is perfectly clear except for mats of algae that grow and stay. In the canals, dark filamentous growth waves slowly without any fish to disturb it.

This has been like this for a long time, and will be like this for a long time. The cliffs are still sharp because nothing has had time to wear them, and yet the city looks finished, unchanging. Stone weathers slowly here because there is no lichen that eats it aggressively, no roots that pry, only water that polishes. The basalt blocks do not shift because the ground does not move. The water table never drops. The clouds come every day at the same hour and unmake themselves on the same edges. The moss grows to its inch and stops. The temperature holds, warm in the morning, warmer in the afternoon, warm again at night, without season.

People keep the canals clear with hand rakes of wood and copper. They sweep moss off a roof when it gets too heavy. They replace a wall stone when it cracks. Otherwise, there is little work to do that hasn't already been done. The settlement is not abandoned — smoke sometimes lifts from a low roof, laundry hangs damp and never quite dries — but you can walk for a long time between presences.

It is a young, high, wet town on top of a new world, waiting patiently for the rest of the world to catch up to it — for erosion, for soil, for sound.
