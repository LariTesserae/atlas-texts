It is too bright for how cold it is.

That is the first thing. The light is not warm. It is high, white, thin-air bright - the kind of brightness that has no heat in it, that comes from much closer to the vacuum. At this elevation the sky is not blue anymore. It is a deep, clean indigo going almost black at the zenith at midday, and the sun is small and hard and precise, like a hole punched through. You have to squint, not because it is glaring off water - there is no water - but because the air itself offers no filter and the ground offers no shade.

You are high. Not mountain-high. Plateau-high, continent-high. The land lifted once, a long time ago, and then stayed there long enough to learn how to be old.

There is no edge to it that you can see. In every direction the table of the world extends until its own air makes it soft. Far below, below the rim of this upheaval by miles, there is a sea of cloud that never breaks. You are above weather. Or rather, this place makes its own.

The ground is what waterlessness does when it has had eons to practice. Not sand - sand implies a beach somewhere, a river that brought it. This is the country left after everything soluble has already left. A pavement of pale mineral, the color of bone and old salt: sheets of anhydrite and chalk and wind-polished tuff that ring faintly underfoot like ceramic. It is cracked into massive, settled polygons, each one a few hundred paces across, each one slightly dished, each one separated from the next by dark, wind-scoured seams where the fines have been sucked away. The polygons are not flat. Their edges are upturned, weathered into soft lips, rounded as old teeth. Erosion here doesn't cut. It smooths, it rounds, it patiently rubs everything toward an equilibrium.

Nothing is jagged. Even the rises - and there are rises, long slow swells that take a day to climb without ever feeling like a hill - are bald and wind-combed. You can see the memory of wind in the stone itself, in the way the harder inclusions stand slightly proud, like pebbles in a riverbed that hasn't had a river for a million years.

And then there are the groves.

From a distance, they look like smoke stains. Low, dark green-black smudges anchored in the boundary seams of the mineral polygons, spaced with an almost deliberate sparseness. A mile between them, sometimes more. Much space between presences. The emptiness is part of the ecology.

As you get closer, the sense of scale slips.

They are not bushes. Each grove is a single organism, or a tightly bound colony so integrated it might as well be one. It is lush in a way that should require a swamp, but does it without a single drop of free water anywhere.

The plant - there is no better word, though it is not like any plant of the lowlands - builds a dome. A dense, matted hemisphere of interwoven fronds that looks like felted lichen magnified to the size of a house, sometimes a city block. The outer skin is silver-white, a near-mirror of tiny, tightly overlapping hairs, each one glassy and hollow. It shines painfully in the bright light. That is its first trick: it reflects most of the hard light and photosynthesizes the rest with a slow, cold chemistry.

Its second trick is why it can live here.

The fronds do not drink. There is nothing to drink. Instead they eat fog - but not water fog. There is no water vapor up here to speak of. In the cool of late afternoon, when the vastness of the plateau exhales and the air above it turns over, a ground haze forms. It is not cloud. It is a dry mist of suspended mineral, of fine salts and airborne organics that have sublimated off the lower cloud sea and re-condensed in the cold. A pale, almost invisible flux. The domes open.

If you are quiet - and it is always quiet here, with the thin air muting everything to a hush of your own breath and the faint crystalline ticking of the stone as it expands and contracts - you can hear them open. A soft, communal sigh as thousands of outer hairs unfurl, roughening the dome's surface. The air catches. Billions of microscopic barbs comb that dry mist from the wind. Over weeks, what they gather accretes inside as a translucent gel, not water, not sap, but a dense, sweet hydrocarbon solution that serves as both blood and reservoir. If you broke a frond - you wouldn't - it would not weep. It would bleed a clear, slow wax.

Inside the dome, it is a different world. The interwoven canopy is so dense that light falls in green shafts. The space beneath is cool, still, and smells of ozone and old paper. The ground inside is not mineral. It is composted felt, generations deep, soft as peat. That is the third trick: these things make their own soil out of their own dead and hold it against the wind that wants to take everything.

That is where the fauna lives, or passes through.

Rarely.

You might wait days and see nothing move except the slow respiration of the domes themselves, which swell almost imperceptibly at dusk and contract by dawn. But then a ripple on the felt.

The grazers here are not much denser than the air. Great, drifting bladders, each the size of a bedsheet, translucent as jellyfish, edged with dark photosynthetic lace. They are silent. They have no feet. They move by warming the air inside themselves and rising a few meters, then deflating, then lifting again - grazing the static. When one passes over a dome, the dome hairs prickle upward toward it, exchanging pollen in a puff of dark spores. Sometimes a grazer misjudges and settles too deep inside the weave and doesn't come out. The dome digests slowly, over a season.

There are smaller things: stone-mites like walking grains of salt that click between polygons. A predator among them that is nothing but a shadow with legs, so thin you see through it.

There are no birds. The air is too thin to hold them. There are no rivers, no springs, no damp under rocks. If you dig, you find only dryer dust. The rule here is simple: keep what you catch. Lose nothing. Everything that lives here is sealed.

The weather of this place is not rain but light and wind. In the afternoon vast, slow-moving pressure waves come across the plateau, visible because of the fine haze they carry. They bend. The light under them goes briefly dim and lilac and the domes all turn together, like sunflowers, but toward the wind. Then it passes, and the silence comes back even deeper than before.

At night the cold deepens and the sky becomes absolute. The stars do not twinkle. They burn still and hard. The domes close and glow faintly with the waste light of their own metabolism, a hundred scattered green lanterns out across a pale continent, each one alone and sufficient, each one a lush pocket of forest in a world that never invented water.

You walk between them and you feel how established this arrangement is. Nothing is struggling. Nothing is new. This is not a desert waiting for rain. It is a finished answer, balanced on its own terms, and it will go on being itself long after you have left its bright, cool, quiet air.
