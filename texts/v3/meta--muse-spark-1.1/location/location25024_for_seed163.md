It is high. Not high like a peak — high like a floor lifted into the sky.

A plateau, a tableland that got shoved up too fast for anything to make sense of it yet. The basalt underneath is new. Not new as in lava-new, but new as in just-exposed, just-unfolded, raw and unweathered, black-gray where it shows through. It hasn't learned to be a mountain yet. No deep valleys, no canyons cut, no talus fans worn smooth. Instead the whole top is still almost flat, tilted a degree or two to the west, and everywhere water is trying to decide where a river should go, and failing.

Because this place holds water like a sponge holds breath.

You are at about three thousand meters, maybe more, and the air is thin and brutally bright. The light here has no filter left. It's white, high-altitude light, cool in color temperature, painful in intensity, the kind of light that makes every edge super-resolved. There are no trees to make shade. The sky is a pale, hard blue, and clouds drag right across the deck, low enough that you walk through their bottoms. When they pass, they leave everything dripping.

And everything is plant.

Not forest. More complete than forest. There is no ground. Or rather, the ground is plant. Ten feet, twenty feet deep in places, a living mass of sphagnum moss, peat moss, haircap, cushion plants, liverworts, lichens, dwarf willow pressed flat as if ironed, bog cotton, sedge. All of it saturated. You don't step on earth, you step on a mattress of it that gives under your boot and sighs and releases cold water that wells up around the tread. The surface is undulating, hummocked, like a green sea that tried to freeze mid-swell. In the hollows between hummocks are mirrors — black water, peaty water, sky reflecting and so clear until you see how deep the root-mass goes.

The vegetation is architecture because it has had to become it. There is nothing else. The plants hold each other up, thatch over each other, die into each other. If you pull a section away you see strata: living green on top, then rust-red, then brown-black, then compressed black peat that smells of cold time. The whole plateau is building itself out of itself. And because it's so wet, so marsh-edged despite the altitude, everything weeps.

Every rock seam weeps. Every lip of moss weeps. The plateau is too full. You can hear it.

That is the first thing, before the view, before the cold: the sound.

Water moving through plant mass, through a million tiny straws, and industrial sound underneath it and over it. It is deafening.

From this flat high place you can see for miles — open in every direction to the drop-off where the plateau ends. There is no true horizon because the plant goes on and on, a town-sized quilt, valley-sized. But at the edges, the water that hasn't been able to find a channel finally finds the edge and simply falls. Not in one grand waterfall — in a hundred, a thousand white threads, braided, splitting, reforming, pouring off the raw lip of basalt where there hasn't yet been time to carve a notch. So there is a curtain hiss all around you, a constant roar of dispersal. And the wind that never stops up here hits the cotton heads and the stiff sedges and makes them rasp like paper.

And underneath it, the human sound.

Scattered across the green — so scattered you can walk an hour between them — are the works.

They are small, they are corrugated, they are rusting in a way that seems almost polite. Pump houses. Pressing sheds. Low, long drying barns on stilts driven down through the peat to the rock. A narrow-gauge tram line that runs straight for a kilometer then simply stops where the cutting crew left it, its rails swallowed by moss at both ends. The motors still run. No one shuts them off because the water would win otherwise.

This is a peat-cutting operation, or a fiber farm, or both. Some company figured out that at this altitude the moss grows incredibly dense and clean, antiseptic, carbon-rich, and they came up to harvest it. So now there are diesel hearts thumping inside tin chests, coughing blue smoke that is instantly whipped away. Huge screws turn, extruders spit out black bricks of compressed peat that steam in the cool air. Conveyors groan under wet weight. And the drainage pumps — the big ones — run day and night with a deep, chest-rattling thrum, trying to keep a few acres dry enough to cut, losing slowly.

The buildings are not ruins. Someone is here, or was just here. A yellow slicker hangs from a nail, dripping. A thermos sits steaming on a crate. But the people are sparse as everything else. You can see one figure a half mile off, bent over in green, tiny against the scale. You call and your voice is taken.

It's cool. Not freezing. The sort of cool where water stays cold enough to ache your teeth, where your breath shows, but the bright sun makes your face hot if you stand still. The air smells of peat-smoke and cold iron and living green, that sour-sweet smell of bog. Condensation forms on every metal surface and runs in beads.

The world is still deciding. The rocks have just arrived. The water hasn't yet cut valleys. The plants are the first and final land — they came fast, too fast, and made a continent of themselves before anything else could use the space. The machines are trying to unmake it, slowly, patiently, year by year, cutting bricks out of a mass that grows back as fast as you stare. Nothing changes quickly here, despite the noise. The roar of water and engines is constant precisely because nothing is moving. It is the sound of a stalemate held at high altitude, in bright light.

If you lie down on the moss, the cold comes through your coat in seconds, and the water squeezes up, and the sun is so bright behind your eyelids that even closed it is white, and the whole plateau thrums through your back — plant, water, pump, fall — and you feel how high and how wet and how utterly, thickly alive this unfinished place is.
