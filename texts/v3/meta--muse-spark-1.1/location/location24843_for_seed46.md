High up.

Not on a mountain that you climbed, but in a mountain that was still deciding to be a mountain. On a plateau of black that is not yet stone.

You find it by accident because it is not meant to be found. The main range rears, dull, wind-scoured. Basalt and half-fused ash that hasn't had time to learn how to be eroded — erosion 0.2 out of 3. Everything is sharp in a way that nature forgets how to make after the first million years. Edges that will cut your thumb if you run it along them. Planes that meet at perfect, unworn angles, like the world was extruded still hot from a die. No gullies. No scree slopes. No river mouths. Just the initial idea of height, cooling so fast it fractures.

And then a shadow that isn't a shadow. A fissure in the cooling skin, no wider than your shoulders. Inside, it is bigger than it should be and smaller than you expected. A pocket. A single room the mountain made for itself.

The air inside is different. High-altitude air should be cold. This is not. This is hot — 2.7/3 — kiln-hot, forge-hot, but not burning. A dry, complete heat that has never known evaporation because there is no water to evaporate. 0.5/3 water, which is to say: no water at all. Not even the memory of it. The rock is anhydrous. The air itself tastes of ozone and iron filings; your lips chap instantly. If you breathe too deep your tongue feels like a salt flat. The light that falls through the fissure is normal noon light, 2.4/3, clean and white and high-elevation honest, but where it hits the walls it doesn't scatter — the rock is so fresh it drinks light without dust to soften it.

And growing in that impossible dry heat, in that newborn place that has never been rained on, is a garden.

Lush is not the right word but it's the closest. Vegetation 1.8/3 — not a jungle, not covering everything, but where it grows, it grows with absolute conviction.

They are not plants. There is no animal life to pollinate them, no water to carry sap. They are industrial.

Each one is sparse — density 0.7 — set apart by reverent distances, each rooted in its own fissure-jar of still-molten glass. From a distance they look like ferns, three to five feet high, black-green, fronded, tipped with rust. Up close you see they are not organic in any way you know. They are self-assembled heat engines.

The stalk is a hollow column of layered silica and raw iron, still warm from being made. The fronds are not leaves but thousands of radiator fins, no thicker than paper, overlapping, shivering. They unfurl not to catch light but to shed it. Each fin is veined with hairline channels where something darker than oil, something that is not water, ticks upward by capillary action, cools, and sinks. A closed loop. Non-aqueous chemistry. They don't transpire; they exhale dry heat. They don't need soil because they are the smelting of the soil. They grew because the mountain was hot and needed to cool.

No one built them. Built 0.4/3. There are no weld marks, no rivets, no cuts, no footprints. The factory is not a building. The factory is a condition. When the rock is this hot and this fresh and this high, it precipitates machinery the way a cold cave precipititates gypsum flowers. Tech 2.4 — this is deep industrial, but without industry. Each plant contains tolerances you would need a lathe to make. If you put your ear near one — the sound is 1.2, mostly quiet — you can hear it working: a faint, patient tick-tick-tick, once every seven seconds, as a valve of natural ceramic opens and closes. A hiss like a distant steam press, but there is no steam. Only hot air sighing through fins. The place is silent the way a factory is silent on Sunday when the machines are still running but no humans are there to hear them.

And the weirdness: 2.8/3.

Because the garden is too efficient.

The fins are dissipating heat, yes. But the air is not getting hotter. It is getting denser. The light that comes through the crack falls on the fronds and comes out twice — you can see two shadows for each frond, one that falls on the rock, one that falls slightly above the rock, in midair, darker at its edges. If you move your hand through the upper shadow, your hand feels cold.

The plants are not growing. They are *being grown* by the cooling of the world. Erosion is running in reverse. The basalt around their bases is getting sharper. Small cubes and wires of raw mineral are accreting out of nothing onto their stalks, like frost, but made of iron and glass. It is embryonic, under construction. The mountain is assembling itself around them.

The rule here is not photosynthesis. It is thermosynthesis that goes the wrong way. Heat is not moving from hot to cold. Cold is being manufactured from hot and then stored.

Dynamic 0.9/3 — slow. You have to sit for a long time in that hideous beautiful hot closet to see it. If you watch one frond for an hour, you will see a new fin extrude at the tip, molecule by molecule, with a soft click. If you watch the wall for a morning, you will see it grow one grain of quartz outward. This whole pocket, this single-feature hidden place, is only perhaps five hours old. By next week, the fissure will pinch shut. The ferns will have finished their job and will be rock again.

For now they are lush in the absolute absence of water, lush because there is no water to waste. They are sparse because nothing needs to crowd when the only task is patience. They are industrial because the universe sometimes builds a factory before it builds a forest, just to see which works better.

There are no birds. No insects. No lichen. Nothing that moves on its own. And still, in the dead quiet, in the dry, high heat, in the newborn stone that has never been touched, the garden is working.
