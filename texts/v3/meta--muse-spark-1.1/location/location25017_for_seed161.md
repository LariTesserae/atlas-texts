It is not a mountain. It is what is left after mountains have forgotten how to be mountains.

Extremely high, so high that the air is a different substance — thin, warm from the stone itself, not from the sun. You are on a summit plateau that is no longer a peak. Erosion, given enough time, does not carve. It forgives. This top was once jagged granite fangs. Now it is a broad, slightly dished palm of rock, ancient and end-of-life smooth. The stone is bleached bone-yellow, then rose, then a deep iron bruise-color where it has been polished by a billion years of wind carrying nothing but itself. No ridge. No scar. Just a soft, undulant plain that ends abruptly in all directions with a fall you cannot see the bottom of because the dimness swallows distance.

There is no water here. Not in any way you understand pooling or flowing. The parameters of this world left water behind a long time ago. What rain there ever was has been lost to the void below. The rock is anhydrous, the air is dry enough to crack your lips in minutes. And yet it is lush.

Because the vegetation here does not drink.

The entire summit bowl - maybe three hundred meters across, hamlet-sized, a single room at the top of the world - is choked with it. A dense, felted forest that grows low and tight and layered upon itself, ten feet high at its highest, often waist-high. It has the biomass of a jungle compressed into a mat.

They are air-gardens. Not trees. Enormous, branching lichen-things, black-green and dusty olive, with trunks as soft and fibrous as old rope and leaves that are not leaves at all but thousands of fine, hair-like air combs, dark velvet to trap what little the air offers. In this warm, thin atmosphere, the plants run on dust and static and the slow exhalation of the rock. At dusk - and it is always dusk here, the light is dim, not from clouds but from distance, the sun a small, hard, amber coin that gives heat more than illumination - you can see them spark faintly as they comb ions from the wind.

And they are built in. Or the town is grown in. You cannot tell where cultivation ends and wildness begins.

The settlement is small and absolutely dense. A primitive urbanism, not of streets but of hollows. The inhabitants - you see them only as movement at first, because they wear the plant - have learned to braid the living trunks while they grow. Every stalk is coaxed over centuries into arch, lintel, wall, cistern that holds not water but air. Houses are not built; they are pruned. A family lives inside a single, massive, hollowed-out air-fern, its exterior still photosynthesizing, its interior lined with cured felt, with niches for sleeping, for grinding the dry pith into flour, with ladders made of bound root.

Because it is so ancient and so flat, there is nowhere to quarry. Everything is hand-tool, simple bronze that has gone dark green, stone scrapers, bone needles for stitching leaf felt. They bind, they weave, they tie. The whole bowl is a knotwork of paths that are not paths but tunnels through the dense growth, low enough that you must stoop, roofed over by interlaced branches that have grafted together. The town is one continuous, shaggy, living building, layered three or four canopies deep in places, where the light filters down as a deep, warm, green-brown gloom.

And it is loud.

With no water, there are no frogs, no fish, nothing that needs wet. Instead, everything that lives here is dry and abundant and needs to be heard. The fauna is the noisiest part of the whole system. Beetles the size of your fist whose wing-cases are like dry parchment drums, clicking and rasping constantly as they crawl through the mat eating the dead comb-leaves. Fat, flightless moths that have lost their wings and use them as resonators, booming low, mournful calls to each other across the bowl that vibrate the branches you are holding onto. Clouds of glassy gnats that live their entire lives in the suspended air pockets inside the foliage, singing in a piercing, unified whine that never stops.

The people contribute. Because the rock is so soft and old and the air so thin, sound is the only thing that carries far. They talk in shouts. They hammer bronze. They beat the felt to soften it. They thresh the pith. Children run through the tunnels shrieking, and the whole dense, warm, dry sponge of the town amplifies it, holds it, turns it into a constant, close, resonant babble — loud not like a storm, but loud like a workshop with the doors closed.

Nothing changes quickly. This is a slow place. The dynamic is glacial, even though it is warm. The plants grow a millimeter a year. A house takes four generations to braid into shape and is lived in for twenty more. The wind that comes over the lip of the world brings the same dust it brought yesterday and will bring tomorrow. The people have a calendar based not on water or seasons — there is no wet season — but on the softening and hardening of the air-comb leaves, and on the molting of the great drum-beetles.

At the very center of the bowl, where the ancient rock dips an inch deeper than anywhere else, there is a bare circle. The elders have kept it clear of vegetation for longer than anyone remembers, by scraping the stone daily with their little tools. It is perfectly round and smooth as an old molar. Here the wind pools. If you stand in it at the dimmest hour and hold out your arms, the comb-leaves of the entire town around you shiver and spark, and you can feel the whole small, dense, impossibly lush and impossibly dry world breathing in, very slowly, very warmly, without any water at all.
