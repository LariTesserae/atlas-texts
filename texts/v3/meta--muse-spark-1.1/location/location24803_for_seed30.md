This place doesn't have a name on any map yet because it wasn't here last season.

You come up over the last switchback of old mountain — proper, scoured, lichen-crusted mountain that has been here for a million years — and find new land.

It sits in a perfect shallow bowl about three hundred meters across, a blister on the spine of the range. The air up here should be thin and cold. It isn't. It's warm, close, bathwater-warm. Low air pressure, but humid as a greenhouse. You have to breathe a little faster than you want to. The light is dim, filtered through a low, unbroken deck of stratocumulus that glows the color of a dirty pearl. No direct sun gets in. Everything is lit without shadow.

The bowl is embryonic. That's the word that comes to mind. Raw geology.

There has been no time for erosion to learn this place. There are no valleys, no gullies, no talus slopes, no soil. The rock is still deciding what it wants to be. Fresh black vesicular basalt, still warm to the touch in places, bubbles frozen mid-pop, glassy edges that would cut your boots. It is all sharp arris and soft pillow-curve, thrown up in still-wet folds like a blanket shoved aside. Steam sighs from somewhere underneath, but not volcanically — patiently, like breath. The rock weeps.

Water is everywhere despite there being no river, no lake. It condenses out of the warm air on the cold glass and runs in sheets. The whole bowl is a seep. The new stone is hydrophilic, or the air is simply overfull. Every surface shines. Water beads, joins, tracks down in quicksilver threads, collects in shallow perched pans no deeper than your palm, spills over a lip, vanishes into a crack only to reappear ten feet downslope as a cold spring. The sound of it is constant and loud — not the roar of a river, the busy clatter of a thousand small pours and drips and trickles and gurgles, overlapping. Under that, a low thrumming you feel more than hear.

There is almost nothing green. Vegetation is an afterthought. In the protected lee of a few larger upfolds, a crust of lime-white lichen has already taken hold, growing impossibly fast — you can see its edge extend if you stare. A few stalks of something not quite grass: black stems, translucent leaves like stretched cellophane that fill with condensed water and bow under its weight, then flick and throw it. They are spaced far apart. Between them: wet shining emptiness. Much space between presences.

What there is a lot of, bafflingly, is animals.

The air is thick with them. Not birds. Things the size of your hand, the size of a kite. Membranous, amber-colored, ribbed like jellyfish but riding the warm updrafts that rise off the rock. They never flap. They pulse, slow. They exhale fine mists of water vapor that catch the dim light. They call to each other with a clear, glassy ping that carries over the water noise — thousands of small bells. On the ground, in the temporary pools, something skates — long-legged, dark, with too many joints, drinking the films of algae as fast as they grow. When you move, they all stop and taste your heat.

You realize after a minute that the bowl has architecture.

It is easy to miss because there is so much empty space, and because the structures are not buildings so much as interruptions. Seven of them, placed around the rim like numbers on a clock that someone has forgotten to finish numbering. Each is a pylon maybe twice your height, matte white, seamless. No joints, no doors, no antennas. They don't sit on the rock so much as they deny it — a faint gap of an inch where wet basalt comes near them and then stops, as if the rock is shy. Water that runs toward them does not splash. It disappears a foot out, then reappears on the other side, still running, colder.

One has fallen, or is being grown. It leans at a shallow angle, and near its base the ground bulges with a lattice of black filaments, like mycelium but made of something that reflects no light. The filaments reach out, pull rock up into new shapes, bead water, release heat. This is the thing that is beyond you. This is the mason.

This high pocket isn't natural, not entirely. Someone — or some system — is extruding new land at elevation, in small, manageable rooms. A terrace farm for continents. They warm the air locally to keep precipitation cycling. They seed it with quick biology, loud biology, biology that moves water and fixes minerals. They keep erosion from happening until the shape is right; the water can't cut, it just wets. The rule here is: formation before decay. Construction before weathering is allowed to start.

If you stand still in the center for too long, where the seep is deepest and the thrumming in the basalt is loudest, you feel the gentle upward push, as if the whole bowl is still inflating, millimeter by millimeter. A bubble of land being blown on the roof of the world.

The fog condenses on your eyelashes. A bell-jelly pings just past your ear, absurdly loud. The half-built pylon ticks as it cools, and the wet black rock under your feet sighs and settles, newborn.
