It is a continent that has been lifted.

Not mountains, exactly - though there are mountains. It's a single high place, a table the size of a country, or a country the size of a continent. The rock is young. You can see that in the way it hasn't learned how to be soft yet. Twenty million years ago this was flat seabed. Then something underneath pushed. Now it's all four, five, six kilometers up, and the edges are still breaking off in clean, black cliffs that show no wear. Basalt and shale in fresh, sharp sheets, like slate that was just split. Under your boot it doesn't crumble into sand. It snaps.

The cold is the main fact. Not a storm-cold, but the baseline cold of being that high up where the air is thin. Zero to below, always. Sunlight is normal, even strong — white sun at noon through thin air that burns your cheeks while your lungs still freeze. The light makes the stone look hyper-real, every joint and toolmark visible. Shadows are indigo and hard-edged. Far off — thirty, forty kilometers off, because you can see that far here — heatless sun makes snowfields glitter like mica.

It is dry the way high air is dry, but not desert. That's the trick of it. Water is here, it just doesn't stay long or in the ways you expect. There is no ocean, no lake. Instead, there is damp.

On the windward steps of the plateau, clouds climb the new cliffs and hit the cold and give up. They don't rain so much as condense. Rime grows a foot thick overnight on every standing edge. In the morning it smokes off the rock in low clouds, and it runs. It runs in ten thousand temporary channels cut yesterday, last week. Thin black rivers that braid and unbraid across the stone and then vanish under it. Where a wall faces the wrong way, or a crevasse overhangs, you get a pocket that never dries — moss black-green, a foot across, with a trickle in it that never quite freezes because the rock holds the day's heat a few hours longer. The whole plateau goes *tick, tick, tick* with melt and refreeze.

And there is life. Not much plant life. Vegetation gets a 1.1 out of everything; it apologizes for existing. Lichen like rust-colored paint on the stone. Cushion plants that are more stone than plant, low and hard as fists, holding dew in their hairs. In the lee of a ridge, a forty-yard run of alpine grass, yellowed and shorn short by wind and by teeth. That's it. That's an oasis here.

Because the animals do the work plants would otherwise do. And the people.

Because this high, young, cold, damp place is crowded.

It started not as a city in the lowland sense but as wintering hollows. The first people found that the fresh faults made perfect shelter — deep, straight cracks a meter wide and a hundred meters long, running for kilometers like veins. Wind can't get down into them. Moisture collects there. If you put a lintel of flat basalt across the top, you have a house. If you put a hundred lintels, you have a street that is also a roof that is also a bridge for your neighbor's goats.

So they built. For a thousand years they built, with only what the plateau gives them, which is rock and so primitive it is almost not "technology" at all. Dry-stone, no mortar — the stone is too fresh and square to need it. Bone picks, copper chisels gone green, hammers of river-stone. They split, they stack. The city is not on the plateau; the city is a layer on the plateau, two to three stories deep, sometimes five where two crevasse systems cross. From the air, which you do not have, you would not see it — just dark lines. Walk across it, and the ground is hollow under you. Everywhere, trapdoors of hides and flat stones smoking a little with hearth heat.

It is vast. The settlement has no edge anyone can agree on. There are neighborhoods separated by an hour's walk over open, open rock where nothing lives, then the cracks begin again and there are lights burning seal fat in niches, and children shouting in a language that has fifty words for how a stone sits on another stone. It is large enough to have a weather system of its own: the collective breath and hearth-smoke of maybe two million people and thirty million animals makes its own low cloud that recirculates and waters the lichen and the grass strips.

In a single ten-meter crevasse you might have, from bottom to top: permanent seep with liverworts and black fly larvae, then goats penned shoulder to shoulder for warmth, then a family in a stone-walled nook hung with smoked meat and felt woven from goat and musk-ox underwool, then a walkway of slabs, then another family directly opposite so close you could hand them salt, then another slab roof with more goats, and above that the sky. And it repeats. Valley after valley after valley.

The fauna love this, because the built does for them what the geology started. Yak-like things, but shaggier, lower, bred to fit the cracks — their backs scrape the lintels. They are everywhere, patient. Dogs smaller, white as rime. Birds in an impossible number — ravens, alpine choughs, snow finches, ptarmigan, a small white dove that nests by the thousands in the heat escaping from chimneys. In summer, insects hatch out of those moss pockets in a black cloud for two days and everything, human and animal, eats until sick.

It is quiet. The city of stone eats sound. Wind over the open slabs is a thin note, and then you go down three steps between walls and it is gone, replaced by muffling. Soft noises close up: a baby crying three houses over, a goat chewing cud, stone ticking as it releases the sun, the slow drip of condensation into a clay jar. People here do not shout if they can help it; the air is too thin to waste.

Nothing is fast. The basalt moves maybe a centimeter a year upward. A glacier on the northern rim, blue as deep water, moves an inch a day and has been doing so since anyone's grandmother's grandmother. A wall built correctly will stand for five hundred years because there is almost no rain to move it. You build your granary once, in the best crack you can find, with stones you split yourself, and your great-grandchildren will add a lintel. The river beds change course every spring but always within the same four feet of ground. Lichen grows a millimeter a year. People also live slow — breath slow, talk slow, to save air.

If you stay long enough to stop gasping, you start to see the rules: stay out of the wind, stay near but not in water, share a wall if you can. Keep the rock over your head. The plateau wants to be empty and clean and shining. Life insists on being tucked into it, dense, damp, communal, keeping itself warm with the heat of its own numbers.
