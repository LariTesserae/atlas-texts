It is not a desert. Deserts are young and hungry and still changing. This place is done.

You are on the Roof. That is what you would call it if you had to call it something, because there is nowhere higher in the world and because it is flat. Not mesa-flat, not plateau-flat-in-the-distance. Ancient-flat. The kind of flat that only happens after mountains have been born and killed and ground down and born again a dozen times until the earth gets tired of trying. 

Elevation: 2.6/3. Extreme.

The air is thin enough that your thoughts arrive a half second late. It's cool — 1.2/3 — not bitter, not alpine-killing cold, but a permanent early-autumn cool that lives in stone. The sun is normal, 1.8/3, a plain white-yellow sun at a normal angle, but the light has to fall a long way through clear, dry air to get to you and it comes out hard and clean. No haze. Shadows have edges like knives.

Under that light, the world goes on and on — scale 2.4, open. Not infinite. The opposite of infinite. You can see the extent of it. Ten, fifteen kilometers to a soft rim in every direction where the Roof simply stops, as if this continent-sized tabletop had been lifted and left. You are in a region, a district. A single long breath of it. One unbroken floor of bedrock the color of old bone and iron.

Water: 0.3. There is none.

Not a creekbed. Not a dry lake pretending it will fill. The mineral here has forgotten water entirely. The stone is a dense, close-grained lithic the color of ash and dried blood — some places porous like pumice, some polished to a dark glass where wind lived for a billion years and then stopped. In the shallowest pans there is a crust of true salt and something else, a pale yellow efflorescence that tastes of metal and static, left from when the chemistry was different. The air itself holds less moisture than a sealed jar. Your lips do not crack, because cracking would be change, and this place does not do change.

Erosion: 2.6. Ancient. End-of-history old.

Everything that could be knocked off has been knocked off. Every boulder has been worried down to a low, friendly dome, half-sunk into the floor like the crown of a buried skull. The ground is not sand — sand is a process — it is a fine, dense silt of its own ground-up self, packed down hard. If you kneel and brush it, you leave a perfect handprint that will still be there next year. The wind does not move it anymore. The wind stopped being a force and became a condition, a long time ago.

Vegetation: 0.6. Sparse.

It is not proud enough to be called plants. In the lee of every stone-dome, where a shadow has lived for a thousand years, there is a flat lichen that is black on top and rust-red underneath. It does not grow. It exists. It is the size of your palm and it is perfectly circular, as if drawn with a compass. There is a second form, rarer — a wire thing, a mineral hair of gray-green that stands straight up for a few centimeters and then stops, with a single bead of resin at its tip. They are spaced out as if they agreed not to crowd. You could walk for an hour and count thirty.

Built: 0.9. Scattered. Tech: 1.3. Primitive.

Because someone else noticed the same rule about lee sides.

Every half kilometer or so, there is a structure. Calling it a building would be flattering. It's a windbreak, three stones leaned together, or a low ring, or a cairn of five carefully selected rocks stacked with a flat capstone. They are all uninhabited. Or maybe they are inhabited in a way that doesn't need inhabitants. Inside the rings, the dust is swept, or rather, it was swept once and has never been disturbed. Next to one cairn there are flakes — a scattering of chert debitage where someone sat and struck tools. Simple, early tools. Hand-axes, scrapers with a good edge. The flakes are still sharp. They have not been dulled because nothing touches them now. The maker left no footprints. The tech is not yours, but you understand it immediately: hit this, make edge, use edge to scrape lichen.

And then there is the density. And the fauna.

Fauna: 2.9. Overwhelming. Density: 2.0. Dense.

At first you think the floor is textured. Then you realize the texture is them.

They cover everything. Not like a swarm. Like a fur. Like a second skin over the Roof. Small, to medium, to large — but all built on the same plan, which is not a plan any earth animal uses. Think of a moth the size of your two hands, but with no head that you can find, only a ventral disc that grips stone, and a back made of overlapping plates like slate shingles that are exactly the color of the bedrock. Each one has six jointed limbs folded tight, and a fringe of hair-fine cilia that once, perhaps, tasted the air. There are billions of them. Trillions. Layered at the edges, one slightly overlapping the next, edge-to-edge, shingle-to-shingle, until the whole plain is a carpet of them, following every rise and dip of the ancient stone.

They are not dead. You understand quickly they are not dead because dead implies decay, and there is no decay here. They are not alive either, in the hungry, twitchy way. They are in equilibrium.

They are completely still. Completely silent. Sound: 0.5.

When you put your boot down, you put it down between them. There is space, if you look for it. An interlocking pattern leaves a stone gap every few inches. They allow your weight. If you brushed one, you feel the slight warmth of stored sun in its plates. If you lifted one — you won't — you'd see the stone beneath is a slightly different shade, paler, untouched.

They do not breathe. Or they breathe so slowly that ten breaths is a century. They do not need water. On this world, water was a phase, briefly. They precipitate what they need out of the thin air and the salt crust, or they do not need at all. Their ecology is not eating. It is being. Being densely, collectively, without gap.

This is the weirdness. Weirdness: 2.7. Reality breakdown.

Because the Roof should not be able to hold this much weight of being without collapsing. Because normal light should glitter off them or show dust motes above them, but the light here is too flat. In the distance, at the rim where the plain ends, the pattern of moths repeats. Not like a copy — like the light has folded. You see yourself from behind, very small, standing in a field of clinging things, three kilometers away. If you turn, the mirage is gone. If you stare too long at any one of them, its plates seem to become transparent and you see another plain underneath, also covered, but with the cairns in different places, or with no cairns at all.

There are places where the air gets thick without moisture, as if gravity has pooled. A lichen grows sideways there. The sun casts two shadows at once for a half-step, then corrects.

The rules of the world:

1. Nothing leaves.
2. Nothing arrives.
3. Nothing changes except by deliberate, tiny, primitive craft: a stone struck, a ring arranged, a gap left for a foot.

Dynamic: 0.2. Never changing.

The air does not move. The sun does not appear to move, though you know it must have, because shadows exist. You have no sense of time passing except your own blood. The silence is not absence of sound; it is the presence of a single, held note so low and quiet you feel it in your molars. If one of the moth-things were to lift its plates and take off, the noise would shatter the Roof down to its roots.

None of them do.

You walk, cool, through the normal light, through the overwhelming, motionless abundance, between the scattered little homes that no one lives in anymore or yet, across a floor that finished becoming itself long before life stopped needing to become something else.
