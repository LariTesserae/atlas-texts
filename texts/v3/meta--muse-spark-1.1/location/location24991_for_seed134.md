It is not deep in the way the ocean is deep. It is high.

Near the top of something — a black ridge that should be wind-scoured and snow-stuck — there is a bowl that should not be there. The rock is wrong for holding water. It's young rock, honest-to-god newborn. Basalt that cooled last week or last century, no more. Its edges haven't learned to be hills yet. They are a broken mouth of glass, vesicular and razor-sharp, six or seven teeth leaning inward at cathedral-steeple angles. The bowl they make is small. You could walk its rim in a hundred steps. You could shout across it and hear your voice come back off the wrong side.

And it is full to the lip with water, and then over-full.

The water doesn't belong at this altitude either. The air up here is thin and cool - not freezing, but that high autumn cool that makes your hands precise and numb at the same time. The light is ordinary, high noon light, white daylight, and it goes straight into the water without dimming, as if the water were air that happens to be heavy. You can see all the way to the bottom fifteen meters down, and that is the problem: seeing it doesn't make it less water.

It is a hanging sea in a stone cup. Submerged is the only word that works, even though you are standing above the clouds. The pool is over-pressured. It spills constantly in a silent, heavy sheet over the lowest tooth on the eastern side, where it becomes a waterfall that never hits ground, it just shreds into mist and becomes cloud again. The surface of the pool is not a surface. It's a meniscus under tension, domed slightly higher than the rock. The wind pushes on it and it pushes back. When you put your hand in it is cold — 8C, 9C, that exact degree where cold stops being refreshing and starts being a condition.

There is no soil. No plant. Not even lichen on the basalt because the rock is too new and too wet. This is a mineral world: black glass, iron-red speckling where the water has started to rust it, and in the deeper center a bone-white floor of precipitated silica that looks poured.

But it is not empty.

God, it is packed. The water is saturated, dense, almost solid with life that decided plants were a waste of time. It's all animal. All faunal. The base of the whole economy is not photosynthesis, it's chemistry.

The bottom vents.

All over that white floor, cracks have opened in the fresh rock and they are breathing. Not the lazy bubble of a hot spring — these are engines. The first time you hear it you think someone left a factory running in the mountain. A low, heavy *chuff-chuff-chuff* with the syncopation of a twin piston, then a scream of pressure release, then a clank. No one built them. The geometry of the crack and the dissolved gas does it: water sucks in, superheats in a constriction, fires out, sucks in again. A rock diesel engine running on the planet's heat, six or seven of them down there going out of phase. The sound is deafening because the bowl focuses it. It is so loud your molars ring. You feel it through the basalt under your boots.

And the fauna gather around the engines like workers who know exactly where the warm spot on the line is.

They look manufactured too, which is the second trick this place pulls. In water this clear and cold and dense, everything has a shell to keep from being knocked. There are thousands — tens of thousands — in this one room-sized sea. Shrimp-things the length of your forearm, translucent with a deep hemoglobin red, their backs plated with magnetite that they precipitate from the vent water. Barnacle-things the size of fists, opening and closing on a 20-second cycle with a hydraulic hiss. And the abundant ones: little barrel-bodied things like living ball bearings, chrome-bright, each with a single black eye on top and six ciliary oars underneath. They pack the water column so close they sometimes touch, layer on layer, facing all the same way, filtering, watching.

When the vent fires, they move. Not fast — nothing here is fast — the whole dynamic is slow, gradual, patient. Over minutes, the cloud of ball-bearings will densify over a vent that is about to blow, then disperse when the silica snow erupts. The shrimp-things will ride the piston pulse for ten meters without moving a muscle. The barnacle things open, then shut with a metallic *clang* that adds to the industrial cacophony. Everything clinks. Carapace on carapace, like a sorting line of hardware running without supervision. No tools, no buildings, no touch of hands, just natural industry doing what factories imitate: intake, transform, excrete, repeat.

The water itself is strange, because water this packed with living iron and dissolved rock starts to misbehave. It has weight in your ears even at two meters. It has layers like oil, cool fresh on top, scalding mineral in a lens near the bottom that shivers and refracts light wrong. Light percolates normally through the top, then in the bottom meter bends. You see a shrimp on the far side twice. You hear the chuffing through your jaw as much as your ears. Bubbles don't rise straight; they wobble and stall and sometimes dissolve mid-way with a pop you can see.

If you stay long enough — and it is hard to stay long, because it is so loud and so cold and so crowded that your brain wants a wall or a plant or anything terrestrial to anchor on — you see the slow rule this small place lives by.

Nothing leaves. Nothing really enters. The water recycles itself over the lip and returns as snowmelt. The iron cycles from rock to blood to shell to silt. The heat comes up, does work, dissipates. Those sharp basalt teeth guard it. This room was only made a little while ago, when the mountain shrugged, and it will be gone soon when it shrugs again and the cup cracks. In the meantime, it runs. It does not build. It has no need for built things. It *is* the engine, the factory floor, and the product, all at once, in cold, clear, overfull water, high up where a lake has no right to be.
