It is not a valley. It is a bowl cut into the top of the mountain, and the mountain is too young to know how to be a mountain yet.

The elevation is wrong for what grows here. You are high enough that the air should be thin and spare, close to the weather. It is close to the weather — you can feel the clouds drag their bellies across the rim — but the air in the bowl is thick, cool, and wet. Not rainy. Rained. A permanent post-rain.

The bowl is small. You could walk its circumference before your boots got truly soaked through, maybe a hundred and fifty paces end to end, cathedral-sized. The walls are all fresh, sharp-cut basalt and green-black glassy rock that hasn't learned to be rounded. The break is recent in geological time - maybe last winter, maybe last hour, the rock can't decide. There are no piles of scree, no softening. Only a clean, vertical bite, twelve forty meters high in unclimbable facets, still steaming slightly where the stone weeps. The edges are so crisp they could cut.

And it weeps everywhere. Water is not falling here. Water is present.

The air itself sweats. Low clouds come in over the rim constantly - or rather, they are already here, have always been here, held - and condense against the cool rock and the colder leaves. Droplets form on every surface and stay, swollen, trembling, never quite heavy enough to drop. The rock face is a tapestry of bright hanging beads, like the wall of a cave just before it drips. Underfoot, the ground is not soil. It's a mat. Sphagnum and liverwort and young moss so lush it has depth, a foot, two feet thick, turquoise and acid yellow and deep rust-red in the dim light, drinking the suspended water straight out of the air. It gives under your weight and springs back slowly, exhaling a puff of cold mineral breath.

There are no structures. No path, no ruin, no cairn. Nothing made.

And yet it is industrial.

The first sound hits you at the rim — loud, body-loud, the kind of sound you feel in the molars — and then it never changes. It does not get louder or softer as you descend. It does not echo. It just IS, at exactly the same volume everywhere in the bowl, as if the air itself has been set to that frequency.

It is a factory running at full, steady, perfect load with no product.

Listen. Under the roar: a piston-hiss that never completes its stroke. A low, iron-shuddering churn that doesn't turn. A belt slipping on a drum the size of a house, eternally about to catch. You look for the machines and you understand that the bowl is the machine.

On the north wall, a basalt shelf the size of a chapel juts out, and then draws back. Juts out. Draws back. Three inches of travel, over and over, at the tempo of a sleeping heart. It has been doing this for a thousand years and has worn no groove. The rock is still sharp. On the south wall, a fissure exhales a plume of white steam in a column that holds its shape in the still air like a pillar, roaring, hot — but the moss right next to it is cold and unburned and beaded with water that never evaporates. A pool at the center, black and perfectly circular, turns slowly, slowly, with the slick rainbow sheen of cooling slag or cutting oil, but it is just water and very fine rock flour. It turns without friction. It makes the sound of a lathe the size of a planet.

That is the tech. Not built. Grown. The mountain's own mechanics exposed when this little bowl snapped open. The planet showing you its works before it covered them up with softer things.

And despite the hard sound, the whole place is stupid with life, the way an untouched tidepool is stupid with life. But sparse — spaced out with a kind of reverence. You don't get a jungle press. You get one of everything, and then a lot of it.

There is a single tree. One. It is at the exact center, rooted in the turning pool but not in it, somehow above it. A wind-stunted alpine pine, but not stunted here — lush, impossibly lush, its needles long and soft and dripping with those permanent beads, its bark black and vertical. And from its branches hang... nests isn't right. Hives? Cocoons? They are wet paper spheres gently puffing and deflating with the piston air, each the size of a kettle. There are forty of them. Inside, something moves.

Around the bowl, scattered like they were placed for balance: six boulders of quartz with flat tops, each crusted with lichen that glows faintly amber in the dim. Each has its own occupant.

On one: a congregation of moths, thousands, all identical grey-white, all with their wings open, not flying, breathing together. The air around them hums differently.

On another: a family of mountain hares, seven of them, snow-white even though it is not snowing, sitting upright, eyes open, not twitching. Their noses are wet with the permanent water.

On a third: beetles the size of your fist, iridescent black, their backs carrying the same water beads as the rocks, moving very, very slowly in a circle, polishing the stone.

In the moss: everywhere in the moss, holes that exhale. And from the holes, a chitter, a chirp, a trill — voles? pikas? things with no names that live only at this altitude — abundant beyond counting, but you never see two in the same place. Their voices stack into that factory roar.

The light never changes. It is a dim, even, overcast-blue that comes from no direction. No sun. No shadows. The clouds over the rim are fixed, sculpted, lit from within. The steam pillar is white and stays. The water beads catch the light and throw it, but never fall. You can hold your hand out and a bead will form on the back of it and stay, perfectly round, magnifying your skin.

Here are the rules as they have settled in the bowl:

One: Nothing erodes further. The sharpness remains sharp. The moss does not overgrow the rock.

Two: Nothing moves from where it is supposed to be. The hares do not hop. The boulder that is a piston has always been extending three inches and will always be. The water does not flow.

Three: And yet everything is working. The sound is the sound of work without progress, production without product. The whole grove-machineshop is at 100% capacity doing nothing, forever.

Four: You can enter. The moss will take your footprint and then push it back out after you leave, unhurried. The beads of water on your coat will stay until you leave the rim, and then they will still be there.

If you stay long enough, your breathing will try to sync to the piston. It will not succeed. Your lungs want change. The bowl does not.
