It is low. Everything here is low.

Not low like sunken, but low like unfinished - like the world only just remembered to make a ground. The basin is twenty kilometers across and none of it is more than a hundred meters above the water table, a shallow bowl of fresh black glass that is still warm from being made. You can tell it was extruded, not worn down. Nothing is rounded. The river-cut that feeds it — and it is a river only sometimes — has banks that are right angles, five, six meters of sharp basalt step where the flow sheared through last year. There are mesas the size of houses that end in knife edges. There are needles of stone left standing in the floodway that have never felt enough wind to blunt. Young country. Raw country. The air still smells faintly of hot iron when it rains.

It rains rarely. That is the trick of this place.

Water here doesn't come from above so much as from within. The air is warm — a constant, skin-temperature 27 degrees, a greenhouse warmth that sits on your shoulders — and it is dim. The sky is not dark, it is occluded: a permanent high haze of particulates left over from whatever made the basin, the sun a bruised peach behind frosted glass. So it never burns off. The city sweats.

Because the city is there, even though it shouldn't be yet. This is city-first terrain. The settlement came while the rock was still cooling.

You see it from the basalt steps: an open country, valley-wide open, and impossibly empty. The urbanism is not dense. It is the opposite - a textbook case of a city that forgot how to be heavy. Spires rise a kilometer apart from each other, maybe more. Each one is a kilometer tall and maybe forty meters at its base, tapering as they go, black and green and breathing. And they are not solid. Nothing here is solid. That is the 0.1 density truth of it: everything is mostly not there.

Approach one. From a distance it looks like scaffolding made of smoke. Up close it resolves — it is grown. The structure is lattice on lattice, a fractal of struts no thicker than your finger that branch and re-branch until they make walls, floors, roads, entire avenues that you could fall through if you didn't know how to read them. The material is not metal, not wood, it is something accreted out of the air, carbon pulled from the haze and laid down by machines smaller than dust. It can hold you, but ninety-eight percent of it is air. The whole city weighs less than a single old apartment block should. You can see straight through three neighborhoods at once to the other side of the basin. The city is urban in function and void in fact. It is a city drawn in wireframe at 1:1 scale, and it keeps drawing itself.

Because the tech here never stopped. This is not ruined, this is over-functioning. The builders — still everywhere, a pale glitter in the dim light if you hold your hand still — are remaking the spires even as you watch. A railing thickens. A bridge unweaves itself from one tower and reaches, slowly, painfully slowly, for another, a Kilometer of braided air becoming solid enough to walk. The physics are wrong on purpose. Load-bearing happens somewhere else, in folded space. The towers hum in a key that your bones feel before your ears do. If you drop a stone inside one, it takes a little too long to hit.

It is damp, not wet. That is its chosen hydrology. There are no lakes. There is no real rain. At dusk — though it is always dusk — the temperature differential tips one degree and the whole basin condenses. All at once. Water beads on every single strut, on every leaf, on every basalt edge, gathering into drops the size of eyes, then falling. A brief, silent, upside-down rain that falls off the city instead of onto it. For twenty minutes the basin drips with ten million small impacts, and all the gutters — sharp, new, right-angled gutters cut last flood — run clear and cold and then stop. Plants live for this moment. Animals live for this moment.

And everything is covered in plants. That is the riot of it. For a place with so little water and so much engineered geometry, it is obscenely lush. The lattice-city is a trellis and the green has understood that. Vines as thick as your waist, waxy, dark leaves that collect condensation like solar panels, climbing the open scaffolds, flowering in the dim with huge, pale bells that open only in this light. Ferns that have no business being that big freight up the basalt steps, their fiddleheads tight as fists and then suddenly impossible fronds. From above, the basin must look like a circuit board left out to go to jungle.

And it is loud. God, it is loud. You would expect, in a dim place, quiet. Here sound is a pressure. The air itself is full of wings.

The fauna is not sparse. It is abundant to the point of being atmosphere. Not megafauna — filaments, kites, ribbons, things that have taken the empty density and made it their home. They are translucent bats with a meter wingspan and bodies no thicker than thread, moving between towers. They are colonial beetle-things that link together into long, humming chains that drape between leaves. They are frogs, thousands of frogs, living in the axils of the vines, each male with a throat sac as big as its body. When the condensation comes, or even before it, they all call. And the insects — the air is seamed with them, dragonflies the size of your hand, blackflies that glitter like the builder-motes.

They don't just chirp. The basin is a resonant cavity — low, hard walls, tall sparse towers. Everything echoes and returns amplified. The frog chorus and the insect drill and the low resonant thrum of the city rebuilding itself stack into a deafening chord that never quite resolves. You have to open your mouth to equalize. At noon-dim, the sound is a physical thing that pushes at your chest. The locals who tend the lattice wear no ear protection. They have grown membranes.

And the weirdness, the strange 2.2 truth that you only get after an hour of being in it: the city isn't empty because it failed. It's empty because distance works differently inside it. You can be standing on a platform in Tower Three and see a friend in Tower Four waving, a kilometer away, and if you both lean into the lattice at the right angle, you can share water from the same cup. The gap is there and not there. Space is lace. The rules here are that matter is optional, that a city can be a forest and a forest can be a machine for making dew, that young rock can be lived in before it has learned to be old, and that abundance does not need density.

At the end of the condensation hour, the dripping stops. The vines shiver. The whole open valley inhales, dim, warm, ringing so loud it blurs, and waits to sweat again.
