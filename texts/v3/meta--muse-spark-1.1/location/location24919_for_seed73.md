This high up shouldn't be hot.

That's the first wrong thing you have to unlearn when you come up onto it. 

You climb out of the lowlands — out of the true jungle where the air is soup and the canopy closes — and you climb for two days up basalt that has been cut into steps long before any map remembers. The steps are not for you. Each one is twenty, thirty feet tall, weathered at the lip, softened at the corner. Established. They have stopped trying to be stairs. Lichen has made a cushioned lip over them, then moss over the lichen, then small white orchids over the moss. Water weeps out of every joint.

And then the steps end and you are on the Crown.

It is not a peak. At extreme elevation you expect a peak, a knife, a thinning. The Crown is a broad, tilted table, a valley in the sky, maybe fifteen miles across lip to lip, cradled by higher walls that you can only see as a darker green against the sky because the forest has eaten them too. The stone beneath is old shield-rock, black and red, pitted like old iron and warm to the touch. The heat doesn't leave it, even at night. The sun is normal, yellow-white, hard at this altitude, but the air itself holds the heat — humid and dense and close, despite how high you are. You breathe and it tastes like hot stone and leaf-rot and ozone.

Water is everywhere, but not in rivers. Not yet.

The atmosphere can't quite hold itself here. It condenses. All afternoon clouds that never quite became rain drag their bellies across the upper walls and come apart on the forest. It doesn't fall so much as it arrives. It beads on every leaf and runs down the bark in sheets, it collects in the hollows of giant bromeliads until they overflow, it sweats straight out of the rock. The cliff faces weep. The whole plateau is a seep. You hear it before you see it — a constant low percussion, millions of drips finding leaves, finding pools, finding stone. And under that, louder than any stream, the fauna.

The forest here is not tall. Up here, lush doesn't mean towering. It means choked, layered, near-touching, dense in a way that lowland jungle never achieves because lowland jungle has room to be sparse. Here everything grows on everything else. The base trees are stunted, iron-hard, with trunks as wide as houses that split twenty feet up into a candelabra of branches. Each branch hosts its own soil — felted mats of moss a foot thick, where ferns grow, where aroids with leaves as broad as doors grow, where orchids grow on the ferns. Vines as thick as your forearm stitch it all together. There is no ground, really. There is a second floor made of root and peat that you walk on that bounces slightly, and under it, the true ground, black mud and warm standing water that goes down to stone. Light comes down in normal daylight shafts, gold and clean, and is cut into moving confetti by the mist.

Everything is growing over something else that is still growing.

And the animals — you never stop hearing them. Not the high whine of lowland insects, but big sounds. Beetles the size of your fist that saw air with their wing-cases and thump into bromeliads. Hummingbirds, or something like hummingbirds but with four wings, that are loud as radiators. Things in the water that sigh and bloop. Woolly monkeys with soot-black hands that move through the canopy in a slow, deliberate parliament, calling to each other with chest-deep whoops that echo off the bowl walls. The forest is loud not because anything is screaming, but because there is so much life doing its ordinary business at once that the air is saturated.

It's slow. That is the other thing. Hot, wet, loud, dense — you expect frenetic. But up here time is patient. The water moves inches a day through the peat, not feet per second. The vines grow around a branch over years, not weeks. The monkeys pass overhead and are gone for an hour before the next group comes the same way, on the same branch, following a groove worn deep into the moss. Things rot slowly, then all at once. A fallen tree will stay whole for a decade, a green mound, and then collapse into broth in one hot afternoon.

Scattered through all this are the structures.

You don't notice the first one until you're almost touching it. It looks like a dead tree until you see it's too straight, its bark too smooth. A spire, maybe forty feet tall, six feet wide at the base, matte black, with no seam. Its top is open like a flute, and from it cold air pours — impossibly cold, in this heat — and where that cold hits the wet air, it makes a perpetual local mist. Water condenses on it and runs down its sides in perfect, spaced channels that were never carved. Around its base, the peat doesn't grow. The ground is clear in a perfect circle, covered in a faint tracery of dust that arranges itself if you disturb it. There are dozens of them across the Crown, maybe a hundred, no pattern you can see until you climb the eastern wall and look down and realize they map a slow spiral.

They are not ruins. Ruins decay. These are settled, weathered in place, but the material itself is untouched by weather. Close up you can see faint movement under the surface, like ink in water, like circuitry breathing. Birds won't land on them. Moss won't grow on them. But figs will lean toward them.

Some are broken. One on the western slope has fallen and cracked lengthwise, and inside there is no machine. There is just more space than should be inside it — a hollow that is deeper than the spire is wide, and in that hollow, lightless, you hear water dripping. If you put your hand near the crack, the hairs on your arm stand up and the air tastes like iron. Another one, near a pool where something large bathes, has grown over — the forest finally winning — and white roots have found their way inside through microscopic pores and come out the other side as black glass threads, still alive, still photosynthesizing with leaves made of something that isn't leaf.

Nobody built them recently. Or maybe they are still being built, so slowly you can't see it. The established rock has grown around their feet. The old basalt steps that brought you up end at their spiral.

The rule here seems to be: hot things rise, and then they are taught how to stay.

Water rises as mist off the lowlands, hits the Crown's cold spires, falls again immediately, never leaving. Heat rises the same way and gets caught under the humid lid. Life rises, layer upon layer, until it has nowhere else to go but into itself. Even the light feels slightly held, a little longer between leaf and eye than it should be.

At dusk, when normal light goes amber and thick, the spires exhale once, all at once — a low, warm note that you feel in your molars — and the condensed air finally falls as true rain for about six minutes. The whole valley drinks, loud, and then stops. Frogs you have not heard all day start. The water in the rocks catches the last light and glows. And everything that has been still all day imperceptibly shifts a millimeter further along in its slow, hot, wet thought.
