It is below.

Not deep in the metric sense — not a mine shaft that goes for kilometers, not mantle architecture — but conclusively below. The surface is a ceiling. You can tell by the air pressure, and by the weight of it over you.

This was a city, fully. Not a bunker, not a tunnel that got ideas. A hamlet-city, a neighborhood folded in on itself. A bowl of space about four hundred meters across, carved rather than built, then built inside the carving until there was almost no difference between the two. The original rock is a soft, warm basalt, tan where it was cut, almost black where it has been breathing damp air for a few hundred years. Onto that, over that, through that, the builders grew their city.

And it is empty.

That's the first law of this place now: vast space between presences. The avenues are wide enough for slow water to run down and never touch a foot. The houses, if houses is the word, are set apart from each other with a respectful, almost religious interval. Each one is a single room, or a hollow in the wall big enough to live in, with its door open. Some are stacked three high. Some are suspended. The density is wrong for a city. It feels like a capital built for a population of ten that expected guests once a century. You could stand in the middle of the central plaza and shout and the city would absorb you and give you back nothing except a little more space.

But it wasn't abandoned. Or not in the way ruins are.

The lights are mostly out. That is its natural state — 0.3/3 darkness, not theatrical darkness, not cave-black, but inhabited dark. The kind of dark your eyes learn to live in. Far above you, where the vault of the cavern meets itself in a seam that is too perfect to be geology, there is no sun. There were suns. You can see their dead sockets, a hundred small eclipses in the ceiling stone, each about the size of a manhole cover, crusted over. One or two still pulse every ninety seconds or so, not with light exactly but with a warm amber after-image that doesn't illuminate so much as remind your retina what light used to be. The rest of the light is incidental: the slick of water reflecting nothing, the dull orange of heat on metal, the faint blue thread of signage that still scrolls itself in a language that has looped down to just one word over and over, too dim to read except from a few inches away.

It is warm. Geothermal warm. Skin-warm. 2.4/3, the temperature of a body under a blanket. The stone holds it. The machinery holds it more. The air coming up from vents in the floor is warmer than the air going in, by about five degrees, forever. You sweat, but not uncomfortably. Condensation is the weather system.

Because it is damp, not wet. The city cannot decide about water. There is no river, no lake. There is moisture in the air that collects, drips, evaporates, collects again. Every surface beads. The walls weep constantly and slowly. In the lower quarter of the bowl, where the city slopes toward its own drain, the weeping becomes a trickle, and the trickles find each other in grooves worn not by planning but by centuries of finding. The grooves are deep now — settled, established. The water has cut a thumb-width canyon through smart-concrete that was supposedly self-healing. The concrete gave up. It learned to erode gracefully instead.

This is an old place, not in collapse but in balance. Erosion here does not mean ruin. It means negotiation. The builders, whoever they were, laid material that could argue with water and heat and time, and then they let it argue for long enough that both sides reached a truce. The corners of every building are rounded now. The staircases have a dip in the center of each step where feet, and then water, and then just water, have passed. Rebar, or something like rebar, shows through in places like the bone of a very patient animal. It does not rust. It sweats too.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. There are no trees. A tree would be obscene here, too dense. What grows is what loves warm dark and wet stone: a black-green moss that grows in flat, circular splotches like spilled paint, a kind of fern that has no chlorophyll you can see, its fronds translucent white and a little luminous, like wet paper. It grows out of the walls where the condensation is thickest, especially around the dead suns. It doesn't carpet. It dots. One fern here, twenty meters of nothing, three ferns there. It makes the emptiness more empty.

And it is loud. That is the thing you don't expect from darkness and emptiness. You expect hush. This city never stopped working.

The tech is still active. Beyond modern. You can't see most of it because it doesn't have edges. The walls are not walls, really. In places, if you put your hand too close, the air gets heavier and your small hairs rise. The structures are held up by something that hums. The hum is not mechanical — or not only mechanical. It is a low, chest-deep note, like a power station idling three streets over, layered with a higher, almost vocal overtone that modulates every few seconds, as if the building is listening for something and correcting. Then there are other sounds on top: the tick and ping of cooling, the rush of air in huge, unseen ducts that breathe every seventeen minutes, the sudden clear *tok* of a droplet falling thirty meters from ceiling to plaza and exploding like a gunshot. 

And water moving somewhere out of sight — a sluice gate opening, a surge, a sigh.

You realize after a while that the city is performing maintenance on itself for no audience. Conduits of light, thin as spider silk, occasionally flare along the walls, tracing a fault, sealing it. A patch of stone you were looking at fills in. Small. Dust motes rearrange into something denser and then fall. The city is not broken. It is just underpopulated by several orders of magnitude.

Fauna is rare. You might live here a week and see nothing. Or you might see one thing three times. There is a creature, or a machine, or both, that looks like a folded kite made of the same dark as the air, about as big as a dog, that drifts from one fern patch to another and drinks the condensation with a long, frosted tongue. It does not react to you. When it takes off again, it doesn't flap; it disagrees with gravity for a few seconds. If you stay very still in the plaza, you sometimes hear soft, padded feet on the stairs three blocks over, stopping when you stop.

The rule of this place, if you stay long enough to learn it, is this: nothing is scarce except presence. Water arrives. Warmth arrives. The light tried to leave and almost succeeded. The city will fix your broken shoe if you leave it out. The ferns will grow toward your breath for a night. But there is no echo that returns your voice shaped as a word. You can be here for a long time and the city will conserve you the way it conserves water — not lovingly, not cruelly, just as part of its established cycle. A little condensation, briefly, in a very large, very warm, very dark, very loud room that was built for a crowd and now holds exactly what it holds.

You can hear, if you listen past the hum, that the rock above is still dripping down, one century at a time.
