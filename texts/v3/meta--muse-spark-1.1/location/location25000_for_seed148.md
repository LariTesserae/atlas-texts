It is a low place. Lower than it should be.

You come down to it, even though there is no hill above it. The air cools as you descend a shallow grade of raw ground — not soil, not yet. A dark, warm basalt that hasn't decided to be rock. It's soft at the edges, pitted, still holding the heat of its making. It smokes faintly where the cold touches it, but there is no fire. Everything here is damp. Not wet. The stones sweat. The air sweats. Beads of condensation form on every surface and gather and fall on a slow, arrhythmic clock.

This is the town.

From the outside it looks small enough to hold in your head all at once. Hamlet-sized, valley-bottom sized, a single stain of deeper dark against the dark. You could walk its perimeter before your hands got cold. You realize very quickly that you never will, because the inside does not match.

The town is all plant. That is not an overgrowth. There was not a town here first and then vines came later. The town *is* the plant, grown as architecture.

Huge, pale stalks rise like grain silos or cathedral pillars, but they are alive. They are hollow, translucent in the dark, and inside each one you can see a slow pulse of water lifting. Their leaves are not leaves the size you expect. Each leaf is a roof, a floor, a Borough. The underside is veined with streets. The streets are not carved, they are the negative spaces between veins, where the leaf-thick flesh was trained, generations back, to grow around straight lines. 

There are windows in the leaves. Doors cut and then healed into living frames. When you look through a window you see the glowing industry inside the leaf itself, because the leaf is not solid.

This is the impossible density: total vegetation and total emptiness at once.

Inside any given structure there is almost nothing. You push through a stomata door — a soft-lipped valve that sighs open — and you are inside a single vast room that is a whole district. The air is cathedral-high and empty. A single sodium lamp, old, industrial, buzzes somewhere impossibly far above, lighting nothing. The walls are living pith, damp, cool to the touch, covered in a fine fur of root-hairs that collect the condensation and channel it into gutters that the plant grew for the people, or the people grew the plant to make. Hard to tell. 

The town is urban in its logic but not in its crowding. It is a city planned for millions with only absence in residence. There are avenues with trolley-wire strung from thorn to thorn. There are fire escapes that climb for a hundred feet up a stem and lead to a single empty balcony where a fern unfurls and closes again. There are factories, whole factory-lofts taking up the cavity of a fruit that never ripened — you can see the presses, belt-driven, churning slowly, stamping out... not cloth, not metal, but more leaf, more vein, more pipe. The town is manufacturing itself, quietly, endlessly, out of itself.

It is mostly quiet. The kind of quiet that is not absence of sound but the agreement to keep sound low.

You hear: drip.
A long pause.
The hydraulic sigh of a stem adjusting its weight.
The chitter of feet.

Because the fauna is abundant. But not in the way you expect. Nothing large. Everything essential. The empty volumes are full of them. Moths the size of sheets of paper, so pale they are almost transparent, navigating by the warmth of the condensation. They do not make noise. Their caterpillars are thick as your arm and run the belt-drives of the factories, mindlessly pushing. Beetles like black rivets hold the trolley wires together. In the gutters formed by leaf-hairs, small amphibious things with lamp-eyes pass in processions, carrying droplets of water larger than themselves to places that need to be wetter.

And birds, but not birds — memory of birds made in living tissue, with leaves for feathers, that grow directly out of the walls, detach, fly a circuit of the empty room, and then root again somewhere else and become a sconce, or a shelf. Everything is predator and prey and tool at once.

The cool deepens as you go in. This is a coolness that belongs to germination cellars, to under-bridges over slow rivers. The river is here but not as a river. It exists as a habit that water has — to be somewhere else, briefly. A mist will gather in one of these hollow districts, condense completely in ten minutes into a sudden ankle-deep black pool that reflects the one buzzing light, and then decide it is in the wrong district and drain straight up through a ceiling, beading away to where it is needed.

The rules are still being written. That is the raw geology of it. The ground is too new. Real edges haven't hardened. This place is embryonic, a proof-of-concept for what a lowland can be.

So the geometry is wrong in a way you feel before you see. You walk down an avenue in a leaf-vein that should take you east, and the condensation on the walls now beads *sideways* and you are walking down instead, but your balance never shifts. You look out a window and see the outside of the leaf you are inside of. You see yourself, from behind, entering it. A stack of the same beetles climbs over your shoulder and does not find it strange.

If you go far enough toward what you think is the center of town, you come to the boiler.

It is not a boiler the way a building is a factory. It is the heart of the original plant, industrial before industry — a mantle-like cavity of damp, red heat in the cool dark, and around it the town's builders have layered their real boilers, their riveted iron, their transformers humming in the dark on power no one is generating anymore but that still comes. The air around it is alive with spores that are also cinders. The spores, when they land on raw basalt outside the town, instantly grow into another wall, another empty factory floor, another street that makes the town slightly bigger on the inside than it was before.

At night — and it is always deep pre-dawn type night here, a darkness with texture — the town respirates. The huge leaves close a fraction. The huge empty rooms become slightly less empty, as if the town is listening to make sure it is still alone. Then they open again, and the cool rushes back in, and the dripping resumes.

No one ever seems to have left. No one seems to be present. Only the workings, and the moths, and you, and the constant, gentle, industrial growing of more town to fill a void that will never be full.
