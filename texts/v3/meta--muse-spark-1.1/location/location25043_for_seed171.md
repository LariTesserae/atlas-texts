It is not a forest. A forest has spaces between things. A floor and a canopy and air in between. This does not.

This is a continent that is still being extruded.

The rock is young. You can tell from the way it hasn't learned to be rock yet — black and glassy and steaming faintly where it shoulders up through the living mass, not weathered but torn. High basalt, high country, a plateau the size of a weather system that is still rising a few millimeters a year, fast enough that the slabs crack overnight. The cracks are clean, right-angled, with no sand in them. Pre-erosion. No rivers yet, not really. No valleys carved. The world here is all sharp edge and new joint, like something left the mold too early.

And the water has already found it. Or the air has. At this elevation — thin, cool, about the temperature where your breath stays visible even in summer — the atmosphere cannot hold what it carries. It condenses on everything. Not rain so much as a perpetual wringing-out. Clouds don't sit above this place; they are caught in it. They drag through the upper lattices and come apart. Everything weeps. The fresh basalt is always wet and shining. The living matter never dries. Water sits right under the surface of the rock, pressurized and cold, hissing out of every fissure in seeps that never make it to a stream because the moss drinks them before they can become anything else.

There is no soil. There is plant-as-soil. There is plant-as-rock.

From a distance it looks like a dark green cloudbank fallen and taken the shape of a mountain range. Up close, scale collapses. You cannot step into it. You can only push into it and be accepted, briefly, by something that has no room for you.

It's packed. That is the wrong word, because packed suggests things pressed together. This is one thing expressing itself at different densities. At the bottom, root-moss — not moss, but something that solved the same problem — has dissolved the new basalt into a felt, meters thick, black-green and saturated, waterlogged like a peat bog that never died. Above that, columns. Above that, columns that branch into columns that branch into sheets that branch into hairs that trap the condensing fog. Leaves the size of houses that are not leaves but scaffolds for leaves. Vines that are trunks. Trunks that are communities of vines zipped together. There is no air gap wider than a fist unfilled by some photosynthetic surface, some breathing filament, some wick. The plant fills the space the way crystals fill a geode — totally, following physics.

Light doesn't travel well here. It's dim in the astronomical sense, but also dim because you are inside a living filter stack. The sun is a rumor above four or five translucent layers, each one stealing 30% of what gets through, shifting it redder. What reaches the lower levels is a cool, diffuse, underwater green-gray. It is bright enough for growth and for nothing else. Shadows don't exist. Everything glows faintly at the edges from internal light — not bioluminescence, but delayed fluorescence, photons the upper layers caught at noon and are slowly re-emitting at dusk to keep the chemistry going. After you have been inside long enough, the forest looks like it's thinking in light.

There are no animals. Nothing with a face has ever made it here, or was ever invited. The whole economy of beak and claw and gut is performed by plants that learned to do it themselves. Fronds that tighten like sphincters around their own dead. Pouches that drop pH and digest what they enclose. Pollen that doesn't need a bee because the electrostatic field emitted by a flower's throat guides it, grain by grain, to the next receptacle through still air. The ground — if you can call it ground — trembles with that kind of work. It is active. Not windy-active. Change-active. You can watch a branch thicken. You can hear a mat of root felt pull itself up a fresh rock face by half an inch while you hold your breath.

The sound is the thing you will remember wrong later because you will try to call it silence.

There are no birds. There is no buzzing. And yet it is deafening.

At low elevation you hear dripping. Higher, you hear a hundred trillion cavitations per second — water columns snapping inside xylem as thick as a subway car, vacuum and tension and release. The transpiration here moves more water than the Amazon. It doesn't flow downhill; it is hauled uphill by the plant itself, exhaled into fog. That movement shudders and clicks. Trunks or whatever they are rub against each other as they grow and the whole plateau creaks, a continental wooden ship under torsion. Somewhere deep, fresh basalt pops as it cools and it sounds like gunfire under a blanket. Somewhere close, a gas bladder — methane from internal composting, nothing alive inside to burn it — bursts with a soft whump. And layered over it all is a high, almost electronic, continuous hiss that is sap under pressure, moving.

When the wind hits, which is often at this altitude, the entire thing groans like a door made for gods.

The technology is here, but there are no artifacts. There was never a builder. This is what beyond-modern looks like when no one built it, or when the building material was taught how to know.

You notice it in the water first. Water at this height should not climb three kilometers vertically through mass without pumps. It does. The hairs of the upper canopy are charged. They change local field potential and lift mist off the leaves without heat. The leaves themselves are arranged not for sun — there isn't enough — but as phased arrays. On still, cold mornings you can see frost form on them in perfect geometric lace, as if a computation is frosting over. The light they re-emit is coherent. The whole mass is doing distributed work: holding its own water table up, holding its own temperature up a few degrees above ambient so it stays cool but not frozen, holding itself from being eroded before it is finished being made. It is a young geology being nursed by an old biology, or an old technology wearing biology like a coat.

It is untouched. There are no cut stems that did not self-amputate. There are no trails. If you tear a hole, white sap floods the gap and within an hour it has dimerized into a fiber plug. Within a day you cannot find the hole. Within a week the fibers have grown leaves.

The rule of this place is simple: everything must be plant, and the plant must do everything. So water is blood, leaves are cities, growth is construction and also manufacturing and also thought. The world is embryonic — raw rock still being drafted upward — but it is already saturated with a single, vast, cold, wet, patient mind that does not need animals or machines because it became both a long time ago, quietly, in the dim.
