It is high up. Not mountain-high, plateau-high — the kind of high where the air has thinned enough to forget it was ever supposed to be an ocean.

You are standing on the Salt Anvil.

That’s not a name anyone gave it. It’s just what it is. A continent-sized table of mineral lifted two miles out of where a sea should have been. There is no sea. There has not been for a long time. The ground is wrong for it. It's iron-black basalt and dirty halite and sulfate crusts baked together into a single, continuous scab, polished on top by wind that has nothing else to do. Heat comes off it in sheets. Not the soft heat of a summer. A kiln-heat, a presence. Midday here is 60C at ankle height and the light is perfectly normal — white, clean, high-desert light that makes shadows razor-edged — which makes it worse, because nothing about this place should be lit that honestly.

There is no water here. Not in the sense of a thing you could collect. The salt sometimes sweats in the shade of a boulder and immediately kills itself. There is no green. The minerals are too hot, too salty, too recently molten and too long weathered. Nothing took root. The only things that look like plants are the vent spires.

And the vent spires are not built.

That’s important. Nothing is built here. You can walk for days across the Anvil and find no wall, no road, no foundation, no tool. It is untouched in the way a desert is untouched — established, settled into itself, eroded into shapes that happen to look purposeful. The wind has been working on the same ridges for a few million years and it has learned how to carve a cooling tower. An arch that just happens to have the proportions of a hangar. A row of pillars, each twenty meters tall and fluted, their tops cupped like crucibles, that the salt has lifted out of the basalt.

They are chimneys by coincidence. The ground exhales through them.

The sound comes from below but not that far below. A deep, wide-bellied, industrial roar that never stops. Not wind. Something under tension. If you put your hand to the salt flat, you feel it as a low buzz in your molars — a factory floor the size of a province, running. No whine, no rhythm. A constant, patient, metal-on-metal grinding, as if tectonic plates have been fitted with gears and are trying very quietly to mesh. Sometimes there is a louder knock — DONG, like a drop-forge that is a mile long — and the salt shivers and settles again. It moves slowly. Everything here moves so slowly you almost miss it. The salt crust expands a millimeter per hour towards the sun. The basalt pillars lean a degree every thousand years. The sound has been this loud for so long the air itself is tired of it.

Then the fauna come.

They are not animals on the landscape. They *are* the industry that there is no building for.

First you notice the sky dimple. Far away, ten, fifteen kilometers away, the empty air flickers like heat haze, but higher. The empty becomes speckled. And then it decides to be full.

They come downwind, low, because the air is too thin for grace. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Each one about the size of a shipping container, but empty between them is so vast you’d previously thought the place was void. You realize now that empty was just waiting.

They are not built either. That is the wrong verb. They are grown, or precipitated. Think of a beetle made of iron that was never smelted, of copper that decided to be a wing, of a lung that is a bellows and a stomach that is a forge. Their backs are black iron slag still hot from whatever interior gestates them. Their bellies are open. And they glow.

They are incandescent inside. You can see the wire-thin filaments of their organs white-hot, the molten material of the Anvil being carried, refined in flight. They don’t eat. They *process*. One will land — and landing is a catastrophe, slow, inevitable, a minute-long descent where it tries to stall on six frayed ceramic wings — and when its feet, which are anvils themselves, touch the salt, the sound spikes. CLANG. Not biological at all.

It folds. It plants. It opens. Its thorax cracks along a seam that was never welded and scoops a mouthful of the salty basalt. For a long time nothing happens. It sits there, shuddering, roaring louder than the ground. Heat ripples. You can smell ozone and hot rock. And then something inside fails correctly: a droplet of pure, brilliant iron beads at its vent, rolls down its side, and falls hissing to the crust, where it instantly freezes into a glassy black marble. The creature shudders and lifts off again, emptier by a kilogram, still glowing.

They all do this. The entire moving carpet of them. The valley, which is twenty kilometers across with no features except those accidentally-perfect towers, suddenly has a metallurgy in it. They are the factory. They are the mining, the refining, the production line — but there is no product. Or the product is the Anvil itself, endlessly re-chewing.

They are overwhelming when they are there. The sky is cut into ribbons by their wings, the sound becomes unbearable, a chorus of jet intakes made of lung. And then the weirdness starts, because this many hot, heavy, dense, industrial animals moving through thin air at low speed should not hold.

They don’t hold to anything.

A few hundred meters away, one of them is behind another one that is behind it. You can see straight through the gap where it should not fit. The air behind its wing is from yesterday. If you stare at the spire field, there are more spires than you counted, and a few fewer beetles than there should be, as if some of them landed in the space between places. A forge-beetle flies straight through the trunk of a salt pillar that is not porous. No break. No dust. For half a second there are two overlapping shadows, and then one reality forgets the other was ever solid. The normal light stays normal, which makes it worse — the sun does not flicker when physics does.

This is why nothing is built. Anything you put up would be eaten or ignored or phased out by the procession. The spires are not buildings precisely because a building would imply someone decided to stop. This place never stops. It just digests itself, very, very slowly, loudly, with an empty sky that every seven or nine hours becomes too full to look at.

When they leave, they leave quickly for something so big. They go up, riding thermals that should not be able to lift iron. The salt flat is again almost nothing per volume. A hollow planet. You can hear the roar fade, but never vanish, because the roar was always the ground. The black glass marbles they sweated out lie scattered for miles, cooling. By tomorrow the salt will have grown over them. By next year the wind will uncover them again, polished.

It is still hot.
