It is a drowned height.

Far above anything that could be called a lowland, the table of the mountains levels out into a broad, saucered country — a high valley the size of a small state — and that country is full of ocean. Not a lake. An ocean, held up. Its surface is somewhere above, a black glass ceiling you never see from below because the light never reaches that far. You live under it, in it, at elevation.

The water here is cold in the way that water gets when it has never been warm. Just above freezing, heavy, clear until it isn't. It does not freeze because it moves. Always. This entire high sea is in a slow, immense overturning, cold currents pouring off the basalt rim of the valley and sinking, denser water welling back up in the middle, carrying silt so fine it hangs for days.

The world below the water is the original mountain, kept.

The erosion is old, settled. These were sharp peaks once. Now they are shoulders, long whale-backed ridges of dark basalt and granite worn smooth as river stones, but on a mountain scale. Their tops have been planed flat by ice that is gone now, and then by water for a long time. In between them run U-shaped glacial troughs, true valleys, their floors filled with cobble and cold mud. Nothing is jagged. Everything has found the shape water allows it to keep. You can see distance here, for a flooded valley — a kilometer, sometimes two before the dark swallows it. Open. The ridges are far apart. The water between is huge and empty and loud.

Vegetation is the only thing that has not accepted sparseness.

It is everywhere stone allows anchor, and it is huge. True plants, not algae scum — though there is that too — but rooted things, dark forest things that have learned to live without sun. The dominant form looks like kelp but it isn't, not quite. Trunks as thick as an old man's waist, black-green, almost black, rising twenty, thirty meters off the valley floor, not toward light — there is no light to reach for this deep — but toward flow. They lean into the current. Their fronds are broad, waxy, translucent at the edges where they are thin enough, the color of deep bottle glass, and they are loud. They are always moving. In the current they shudder and crack, popping as gas bladders the size of your head inflate and release along their stalks, a slow breathing. You hear them for miles: the groan of a trunk bending, the whip-crack as a frond twenty feet long snaps taut, the endless soft fizz as oxygen — or something like oxygen — beads off their surfaces in streams of silver pearls that rise and never arrive.

There are meadows of it, and then — because the density is spare, strangely spare for how lush it is — there are sudden, immense clearings where nothing grows but cold clay and the stone is bare. The forest just stops, as if respecting some boundary you can't see. In those clearings the water is darker. Colder. And quieter until the forest creaks again.

There is no animal life to make use of any of it. Nothing grazes. Nothing darts. The fronds grow un-nipped, perfect until they rot, then they fall in whole sheets and lie on the bottom like wrecked sails, slowly dissolving into black peat that never finishes decomposing. No fins disturb the silt. No eyes open. The water is sterile of anything that would move on its own, which makes the movement of the plants more unsettling. The forest moves as if something is moving through it, but nothing ever is.

The built things are old and small and respect the water.

In the clearings, or on the shoulders of the ridges where the current is weaker, you find cairns. Not natural. Piles of flat basalt slabs, dragged and stacked by hands, stabilized with ropes that are still there — braided cord made from the skin of the trunks, black, not rotted. A low wall, no higher than your knee, enclosing nothing. A single hut-shape made of four leaning slabs with a space under them too small for a person but just right for air, if there were air — a bell of trapped gas from the plants that shivers inside it, silver. Farther on, where a ridge crests close enough to the surface that the darkness lightens from absolute pitch to a very dark blue, there is a village of them, or what was a village: maybe a dozen low circles, each one room of stacked stone, roofless, doorways all facing down-current. Empty. No tools left, no bones, no carvings except the wear of rope against stone. The people who made them had only stone and hand and plant-fiber. They must have had to work in short dives of holding breath, or they could hold it longer than anything should. Their work is made for water. It does not fight it.

The strangeness is not that it is underwater on a mountain. It is how the rules have been bent to allow that.

Light does not behave. Down here, this deep, there should be no light at all, but the water itself has a faint luminosity on still nights — not bioluminescence, there is nothing alive to glow — but a residual, pressure-light, like the dark has a temperature. The vegetation takes it. Maybe that's what they eat, not sun but depth, and the cold, and the motion. The plants root not only in stone but in water; you can see smaller sprouts drifting freely, unattached, growing mid-column, their rootlets waving like hair, never settling. Gravity is indecisive here. Silt falls too slowly. You can push off a ridge and glide for a hundred meters before you need to think about stopping.

And the sound. The deep valley funnels it. The cold water carries it unnaturally well. You hear the forest groaning on the other side of the valley when it moves. You hear the cold currents themselves as a low, continuous roar overhead, like wind in a place that has no wind. You hear your own heart far too loud. And every so often, from nowhere that you can place, you hear a deep, percussive boom — stone settling, or a gas pocket under the peat finally letting go — that rolls through the water and through your chest, and sets all the fronds for a kilometer shivering in a wave.

You stand — or float, or tread — on the high, drowned pass and look out over the black forest and the bare plains and the little stone houses that never had fires, and you understand this place has been stable for a very long time. The ridges are done eroding. The plants are done invading. The water is done rising. It is not waiting to become something else. It is the balance it reached when the world tipped and the sea was left on top of the mountain, and it will keep being this loud, cold, dark, lush, empty, inhabited emptiness until something changes the height of the world again.
