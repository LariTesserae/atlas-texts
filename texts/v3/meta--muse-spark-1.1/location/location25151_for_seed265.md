It never finished becoming night here. It just is night.

Not the night of a sky with stars above it, not overcast, not moonless. The light is simply at zero. You open your eyes and there is the same close, warm black you get with your eyes closed, and you keep them open anyway because the dark has pressure.

This is a low country. Absolutely low. If there is a sea somewhere it is at the same height as this place, maybe an inch lower. The elevation doesn't vary more than a man's height for a long, long way — but the ground is not flat. That is the first wrongness. It is young.

Sometime recently — geologically last week, industrially last year — this whole plain was made. It came up. It cooled wrong. The basalt, or the slag, or whatever stone this is when it comes up hot from under pressure, it didn't have time to sag. It kept its edges. So you walk for a mile across what feels like flatness, and every twenty feet your foot catches a knife-ridge of black glass six inches high, a burst bubble, a cooling joint that opened like a mouth. The sheet is still jointed, still faceted. In the dark you learn it with your shins and with your hands. Every surface is sharp enough to cut if you fall, but water has been at it enough to make the sharp feel wet.

Because there is water everywhere without there being a body of water.

The air is at its dew point and just past it. It is warm — blood-warm, bathhouse warm — and saturated. The stone weeps. You can hear it before you can understand it. Beads form on the undersides of these low ridges and fall with a soft click onto stone, onto stone, onto thin water. The plain is a millimeter deep under water that never pools correctly. It sheets, it splits around the glass edges, it disappears down into crazing cracks that breathe. In the lowest cuts there are marshes, but sparse, starved marshes; not enough soil yet for real swamp. Just a few things that can root in a crack in hot rock with its feet wet.

The plants are not many but they are tenacious and they are also working in the dark. No green that you can see. You find them by smell and by brushing them. Wire rushes, black at the root and pale toward the tip, growing in clumps of three or four where silt has collected. A kind of liverwort that spreads like wet paper over the stone, slick. Higher up on the few drier lips, a shrub that has learned to be a stick — no leaves, just photosynthetic bark that probably isn't photosynthesizing anymore, that probably eats heat. Everything spaced out, lonely, listening.

But the air is full.

This is an abundant place. It is dense.

The density is not vegetation. It is presences. The warm wet air is packed with things that live at body temperature. Midges cloud so thick in places that you breathe them and your face comes away freckled with their bodies; you wave them and they part and immediately close again because there is no wind to take them. Beyond them, things you can't name rustle through the water-sheet. Low, flat salamander-things as long as your forearm with translucent skin that shows the heat inside themselves. They converge on any fresh water seep and stack one over another in layers, belly to back, breathing slowly. Crabs or something like crabs that climb over each other's shells across the knife-ridges and you mistake them for moving stone until you step near and the whole ridge suddenly uncoils and scatters. On the backs of the larger ones, smaller crabs. On those, barnacles that are not barnacles blinking with a light that is not light — a chemical heat you feel on your cheek more than see. Everything is near-touching, layered, cohabiting volume. The world here uses every cubic inch.

And quiet. Mostly quiet.

The fauna does not call. The water doesn't rush. The processes are patient, weak-gradient processes. The heat wants to leave the rock but it does so over centuries. The water wants to find the sea but the sea is barely lower and there is barely a slope, so it creeps a foot an hour. The animals move like they have been told to conserve momentum. You hear: the tack, tack of condensation falling. The thin hiss of a salamander's flank against wet glass. The extremely soft pad of many, many legs.

Then, at intervals that are not regular but feel too long, an industrial breath.

This valley — this whole open, town-sized low basin — is scattered with machines. You don't see them. You come upon them. A boiler house half-sunk into the sheet, its walls still hot. A line of pipe supports marching off into blackness beyond your reach, humming, carrying something warm and thick somewhere else. A stamping press with its die up, frozen mid-stroke over nothing, with liverwort now growing on the anvil face. There are not many, but each one is large, and each one is active in a way that does not require light.

These are not ruins. Or not only ruins. The electrified grid is live. You can feel it in the hair on your neck when you pass too close to a pylon wrapped in dripping insulation. Motors turn somewhere, turning screws that lift water from one shallow pan to another identical shallow pan, no reason, no product. A distant thump — piston, pumpjack — thump — piston, pumpjack — so slow you could forget it between beats. It is industrial the way a mine is industrial after the crew has been evacuated but the pumps have to keep running forever or it will drown. So this country pumps itself, in the dark.

No one bothered to put lights on these machines. Or the lights all died. The darkness is absolute and the industry does not correct it. It makes heat, not light. And so the fauna love it.

This is the weird part, where the place stops being describable by ecology and you just have to narrate what happens.

In perfect darkness, distance stops working.

You walk toward the sound of the pump for what you think is a hundred feet, and your hand finds the pipe bracket you left behind you three minutes ago. You are not lost; the space has just folded a little, like wet paper folding under its own weight. If you stand very still, you can feel that your front and your back are not quite the same temperature. Forward is one or two degrees warmer.

If you crouch, the density increases faster than it should. The air between your knees becomes crowded with more midges than could physically fit there, as if the volume contained another volume that contained more air. Reach in and your hand comes out wet and cold, as if it has been somewhere deeper.

The factories, you realize, are not scattered. There is one factory, maybe, but you meet it many times at different angles. The same shape of rust scaling on its side, the same serial stamped into its lintel: 07-ASHEN. You find 07-ASHEN in three places a mile apart, all warm, all humming. And there is water that runs up the sharp side of a ridge for two inches before remembering to fall back down.

Nothing about this is violent. That is the slowest part. The reality breakdown is not a storm, it is a sag. Like the stone was too young to decide what physics it wanted. The warm wet black extends past the immediate — more than you can walk in a day, more than your map can hold — but it never quite becomes a horizon. Sound dies at thirty feet, as if the darkness absorbs it. Fauna is everywhere but they all seem to be slowly moving inward toward a center that is everywhere.

If you stay long enough, which the air wants you to do, you sit on a sharp ridge that is not uncomfortable because you have learned exactly how to fold yourself around its edges, feet in the warm sheet-flow, a crab's shell lightly touching your ankle with polite permission, and you listen to the plain breathe out water and take in heat, pump to pump to pump, in a quiet so complete that even the industrial thumps become part of the slow condensation of time dripping off stone.
