There is a place inside.

Not under. Inside. Low down where the world has finished settling. The elevation here is not high or low so much as final — a broad, cool basin where everything that could erode already has. The hills around it are not hills anymore, they are the long, tired shoulders of something that gave up being hills a billion years ago. The stone is soft now, rounded, powdered at the edge to a flour that never blows away because there is no wind enough to carry it.

In that basin is a body.

It is one plant. That is the whole landscape. One plant that decided, a very long time ago, to become a city to outlive drought and night.

From the outside you would miss it. It is pocket-scale, hidden. A low black dome the size of a county house, or a small apartment block, hunched against the ancient stone — its skin is bark but not bark, it is cork and scab and book-paper, layer on layer of dead, dry leaf-husk laminated by centuries into something like adobe. Ash-pale, ash-dry. In the absolute dark you can only find it by touch; the air gets cooler by a degree, and your hand meets something that is not rock. It gives slightly. Cool, slightly waxy. It smells like old library and root cellar.

Find the mouth — not a mouth, a split where the husk shrank — and go inside.

Now the contradiction opens.

Inside, it is empty. It is a cathedral the plant made for no animal. The density of the world drops out. Vegetation is total here not as jungle, but as architecture. Everything is plant, but there is almost nothing in it. You are standing in a hollow of the mother tuber, a vast, dry womb.

The plant is all wall, all floor, all ceiling and street and pillar. It grew itself hollow. Over uncountable slow cycles it laid down chambers to conserve what little moisture it ever caught, then abandoned them when they dried. The flesh of it is not green. In absolute darkness — 0.0 light, not dim, not overcast, but the black that lives inside sealed things — there has never been a photon to feed on. This plant does not photosynthesize. It never did. It is something older, a lithophage, a stone-eater. It drinks not water — there is no water here, no springs, no rain in this sealed basin, the air is a dry mineral breath — but the faint chemical sweat of the ancient basalt itself, and the cool condensation of its own slow respiration that beads once in a decade on its inner walls and is gone before it can drip.

So it is pale. Everywhere you run your hand, the surfaces are bone-white, mushroom-white, the white of things that have never seen. When you tear it — and everything here is torn, cut, carved — the pith underneath is a little darker, like stale bread or corkboard. It comes apart in dry, fibrous sheets. You can hear how dry it is.

Because it is a city.

This is what remains of the built. Primitive, total, layered, patient. Generations of hands — not human, or no longer — with simple tools, stone flakes and fire-hardened thorns of the plant itself, carved the living mass into habitation. Not houses inside a plant, but houses *as* the act of removing plant.

Here a broad avenue, twenty feet wide, hacked from pith, its ceiling still showing the chisel marks, the grooves where the dry flesh was pried out in blocks to be dried further and stacked elsewhere as furniture. There a stairwell spiraling up through a root-column as thick as an old oak, the steps worn utterly concave in the middle, not by water — there is no water — but by passage, by centuries of bare, dry feet until the steps are as smooth as river stones. There a district of small, intimate rooms like cells in a honeycomb, each just large enough for one body to curl in. Their walls are covered in carving — not decoration, but records. Tactile language, because in this total dark no eye ever evolved. No fauna. No birds, no insects, no rats. No sound of other bodies. Just this one plant and the things that once lived in it and learned to read by fingertip the braille of its grain.

And it is loud.

That is the first thing that breaks you after the dark and the dry. For a dead-seeming, cool, dry, empty, motionless, sealed hidden room — it is deafening. Not with life. With structure.

The plant is still alive, still growing a millimeter per century. The ancient, desiccated chambers shift against each other. The dry timber — for it is timber, but a timber that grew never seeing sky — creaks. Deep, groaning pops, like the knees of a house in winter, like ice on a lake. A long, slow sigh moves air through the avenues where there should be no air movement; the whole mother dome breathes once every few months, drawing in the cold outside air through its split and exhaling it through pores you cannot see. When it does, the empty streets become flutes. The hollow pillars and the vaulted pith-halls hum and bellow. A low B-flat that you feel in your sternum, that rattles the fine dust that has settled on every surface — a dust that is nothing but abraded plant skin, and the powder of old carvings.

Everything here happens slow. The dynamic is glacial. If you stayed a year you would hear three great groans and the falling of one small flake of pith from a ceiling. If you stayed a lifetime you would see a doorway slowly close as the plant kept growing around the absences. The city is repairing itself, very slowly, filling back in what was cut out. It is not hostile. It just doesn't know that it was a city. For it, the carved streets were wounds, saved in a kind of dry clot, now healing over.

There is nowhere to get water, and nothing here ever needed much. The rules are inverted: a forest that is a single tree, a forest that is a desert, a forest that is a town, a town that is empty, an empty that is full to bursting with presence. The substrate is all one organism, so you cannot say ground or wall. The floor under your hands is slightly resilient. The air is cool, 10 or 11 degrees C, and so dry your lips crack within hours, yet you smell faintly of starch when you sweat, because whatever little moisture you exhale, the walls will drink. That is the transaction that lets you stay.

There are no animals to hear you. There is no light to see you leave footprints. But if you put your ear to the soft white wall in one of the small sleeping cells, and you are quiet enough, after the loud groan fades, you can hear the faint, unbearably slow ticking beneath — the hydraulic inching of waterless sap, the plant counting another century in its dark, waiting for stone to become sweet enough to eat.
