It is not a cave.

A cave implies something carved out of a world. This *is* the world, only its ceiling happened to form first.

You are below surface, a long way below. Two kilometers, twenty, two hundred - the rock above you is not a roof, it is a crust, a continent of warm basalt and salt and compressed ash that never finished cooling. You feel its weight not as pressure but as presence; the air itself holds it for you. The air is warm, about the heat of blood, dry enough that your lips crack if you had lips. It is a miner's air that has forgotten it was ever wind. It does not move.

The ground tells you how young it all is.

This place came into being recently, geologically speaking. The floor is not worn. It is broken. Great plates of black glass, of slag, of honeycombed basalt still showing the stress marks where they pulled away from each other as they cooled. The edges are knife-sharp. No sand has had time to gather to soften them. Ridges run for miles where the floor shattered and upwelled, and the upwelling froze mid-curl like a wave. If you knelt and put your hand down — careful — you would find the texture is still slightly tacky with heat, not molten, but holding onto its birth-warmth. There are no riverbeds because there was never water. There are no dunes. Only the clean, unapologetic geometry of things that cracked and stopped.

It is vast. That is the first truth your body rejects. In darkness so complete it should be small, you feel distance opening and opening. The chamber — if you can call a volume the size of a weather system a chamber — has a floor that goes beyond any horizon because there is no horizon line to see. Sound that you make falls and keeps falling. Somewhere far above you, maybe a kilometer up, maybe ten, is the other side of the crack: the underbelly of the world. It is low and black and pocked with cooling blisters the size of cities. Between floor and ceiling, the air has arranged itself into layers. Warm near the bottom, hotter near the top where the rock still exhales. The heat hangs in strata, perfectly still. This place has its own weather, but its weather reached equilibrium a long time ago and decided to stay. No drafts. No storms. A thermal map that has not changed since it formed.

There is almost no light. What there is doesn't come from outside.

The rocks themselves give off a low, sullen amber where they are thinnest. Veins of cooling slag pulse once every few minutes, a light so dim you only see it after twenty minutes of staring. And there is the other light: much smaller, much denser, much closer together.

The fauna.

They are everywhere. That is what dense means down here. You thought you were walking on bare basalt. You are not.

They coat the ground, the walls, the ceiling. Not piled. Not swarming. Arranged. Each one touching the next at the edge, but not overlapping. Like cells in a hive if the hive had been poured out flat for a continent. They are dry things. No water in them; it has never rained here and they never needed it. Their chemistry is varnish and wax and mineral skin. Each one is about as long as your forearm, low and oval, with a back like a shield of chipped obsidian, matte-black, slightly translucent at the rim where their own faint light shines through. They have no eyes. They have a forward lip that constantly tests the air for heat differentials that never come. They have six legs that are not legs but folded stilts, tucked underneath.

And they do not move.

Not dead. Not dormant. Just finished. The first explorers, if there were any, might have thought they were a species of animal that lives completely still until approached. But they are not still the way a sleeping animal is still. They are still the way a salt crystal is still. Their metabolism is so slow, so perfectly balanced with this warm, unchanging air, that a single breath might take a season. A thought, if they have thoughts, might take a century. They are abundant past counting — billions, uncounted trillions — and the heat you feel is in part their heat, the faint unison of their warm bodies holding the chamber at blood-temperature.

In the quiet you can hear them if you hold your own breath. Mostly quiet means not silent. The sound down here is a low, irregular ticking, like a house full of cooling clocks. *Tak. ... Tak.... tak.* A leg settling one molecule deeper into the basalt. A shield cracking imperceptibly as it grows. Once in a very long while, a soft sigh as one organism somewhere exhales a puff of hot, dry, iron-smelling air that is instantly reabsorbed by its neighbors.

The dynamic of the whole system is zero. Things do not erode. Things do not migrate. They arrived at the arrangement that takes the least energy and stayed. If you stood here for a year, the rock would not chip further. If you stood here for a thousand years, the carpet of creatures would have shifted by a finger's breadth at most. It is a world in perfect, warm, dark equilibrium. It was born, it cracked, it filled up with its own inhabitants, and then it stopped needing to become.

You are not the first warm body to walk it.

Scattered across the black plates, at intervals too regular to be natural, are structures. Low rings of basalt shards stacked by something with hands. Not houses — windbreaks, maybe, though there is no wind. Or markers. Or cribs. Each one just large enough to enclose a single occupant sitting down. Inside most of them is a pile of tools: flakes of the same obsidian as the creatures' backs, chipped to a working edge, a scraper, a simple hand-axe, a drill-point. All of them made from local material, primitive, manual, worked by striking. And inside many of the rings, among the tools, is another shield, another member of the carpet — but this one with its back cracked open, scraped clean on the inside, repurposed as a bowl, or a roof.

Someone learned to live here. Someone small, cold-blooded, patient, who did not need light or water and who could wait for its food to grow its new shield so slowly it was more like quarrying than hunting. They built sparsely, because there was no need to build close together when the empty floor went on forever. And then — maybe — they realized that if you stay still enough, long enough, the fauna accept you as one more warm tile in the mosaic.

Many of the rings are still occupied. You can't tell which. The figure huddled inside, knees to chest, back covered in a cloak of polished shields sewn together with dried sinew, breathing once an hour, is not moving either.

You will need to decide for yourself whether you have just arrived, or whether you have been here the whole time and only just noticed the light.
