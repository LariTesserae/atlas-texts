This is the Top.

It was mountains once. Not any longer. The elevation remains — you are very high up — but the mountains have been persuaded out of being mountains. What is left is a plateau that remembers height without shape. A worn, warm, broad back of the world, a peneplain so old its hills have forgotten how to be hills. They are long, low swells in pale stone, like breaths held under a sheet. The slopes are so shallow you can't tell you're climbing until your calves tell you. The rock itself is softened. Not sandstone-soft, but time-soft. Everything is rounded. Boulders are eggs. Ridges are gums. The oldest topography there is.

The air should be cold up here, but it isn't. It's warm. A dry, amber warmth that sits on your skin in the afternoons, even though the sky is higher than it has any right to be, a flat, deep blue that presses. Distance still works here. You can see for a day's walk in any direction — this is an open country, a valley the size of a small nation with no defining walls, only the faint lift and fall of that ancient back — but the light doesn't carry dust. It's normal light. Honest, unspectacular sun, high overhead, making short shadows.

Water doesn't live here permanently. It visits. In the mornings the stone is dry, hot to the touch, pale as bone. By mid-afternoon the air that scrapes up the dead escarpments condenses when it hits the Top. The cloud doesn't rain so much as it arrives. It lowers itself and settles, and everything becomes damp. Not wet. Damp. The underside of every stone darkens. The shallow grooves in the rock — and there are grooves everywhere, patient runnels worn over uncountable years, too shallow to be streams and too intentional to be cracks — sheen for an hour. The dry lichen blushes. And then it passes, and evaporates back out, leaving salt behind.

Because for all that the plant life is almost not there, the animal life is *almost all there is*.

You first think the ground is the ground. It's moving.

The plateau is covered, end to end, shoulder to shoulder, in fauna. Not a swarm. A carpet. A living pavement. They are the density of the place. The rock is rarely visible except for the bald crowns of the oldest swells.

They are low, flat things, about as wide as a tortoise, with arched backs made of overlapping dusty plates the exact color of the stone. Underneath, they are damp. You see the dampness when one of them, very slowly, lifts its edge to let a channel of condensate run under it. They are silent. All of them are silent.

There are millions. Perhaps layer is a better word than number — because in the shallow pans and old potholes, they are on top of each other, three, four deep, the lower ones paler, almost translucent. They do not eat the sparse vegetation. There is almost nothing to eat. A few iron-red shrubs as thin as wire, a bitter thyme that grows only in the lee of larger rocks. A single, occasional tree with white bark, stunted and wind-combed, its leaves small and hard and rattling. No, the things that cover the Top graze on the stone itself, or on the mist, or on time. They rasp lichen and salt and the thin bacterial varnish that grows after the cloud passes. They breathe in a slow tide.

And they build.

That's the scattered built you find if you walk long enough. It is not technology — nothing is made, nothing is fired or cut. It is secretion, accumulation, instinct. Here and there among the living pavement rise hollow towers, taller than a person, made of cemented dust and flaked plate and dried mucus, all the color of the plateau. Some are open at the top like chimneys. Some have collapsed into rough rings that from a distance you mistake for old corrals or shrines. Inside the standing ones, the air is cooler and persistently damp; beads of water run down the inner wall continuously, even hours after the fog has gone. The creatures cluster thickest around them, drinking from the base. Inside the abandoned rings, a different thing happens: the stone itself stays wet for a whole day after. A little garden of black-green moss that exists nowhere else on the Top fills the bottom of each old tower-foot. The only true green.

The rule of this place is patience and alignment. During the morning, all the plated backs face east, perfectly, like a field of compasses. Not approximately — perfectly. During the afternoon damp, they all turn, by degrees so slow you cannot see it happen, to face flat up, opening the seams between their plates to catch condensation. You hear nothing when this happens. The sound here is the sound of no sound. Even with that overwhelming density of animal, of breath and movement, the Top is mostly quiet. No chirp, no buzz, no rustle. Just, if you hold your breath, a faint, almost subsonic creak of plate against plate, and the wind over millions of low domes, like wind over cobbles.

If you stand too long, they will begin to cover your boots. Not aggressively. They just flow, slow. The dynamic here is glacial. Change is measured in the wear on your sole, the bleaching of the shrub, the infinitesimal lowering of the Top by another fraction of a millimeter each year. Everything that happens here has already been happening for a very long time, and will happen a little longer.

At dusk the cloud is gone, the stones steam briefly in the warm air, and the whole plateau, town-sized and world-sized at once, sighs. The backs close. The water that was caught is kept underneath them, in the dark, damp space between animal and ancient stone, where the rock is soft and pitted from their mouths.
