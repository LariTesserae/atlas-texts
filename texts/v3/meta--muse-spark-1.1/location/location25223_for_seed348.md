It is low.

Not low like a tide flat or a trench; low like a bowl left in the ground after something heavy was set down and then lifted away again. A perfect shallow, with a lip all around it. You come down the access road and the air gets cooler by three degrees, and damper, and then stops getting cooler. It stays there.

This is the Works.

It's not large. You can see the whole of it from the far edge. Three factory buildings, a water tower that hasn't held water in living memory, a row of narrow brick houses whose doors all open outward, a switching yard with two locomotives in it, cold, a chain-link perimeter that no longer perimeters anything because the wire has learned the shape of the air and holds it. Hamlet-size. Town-size if you were a cat. The sort of industrial park that was built to a pamphlet in 1932 and then left to fulfill the pamphlet forever.

The construction is sparse. That's the first thing that feels wrong. An industrial town should crowd itself, lean its shoulders together, pipe into pipe. This one doesn't. There is a lot of concrete between things. Big, poured aprons, cracked into careful polygons, stained dark with oil that never dries and never spreads. Between the lathe shop and the press shop there's maybe forty meters of nothing but flat, wet concrete and air. Between the houses and the factories there's a street too wide for its purpose. The buildings are set apart like teeth with gaps, and the gaps are intentional, left to do work.

They are damp, these gaps. The water here is not weather. It doesn't really rain in the bowl. The air sweats. The low holds a cool pocket, and everything below the lip sweats condensation. It beads on the brick, it runs down the rivets of the water tower, it gathers under the lip of every window sill and falls, not regularly, but often enough that there are dark tongues of moisture on all the north sides. In the low spots between the slabs it pools an inch deep, black water, oily, cold. The pools never get bigger. They never evaporate either. You could mark one with chalk and come back tomorrow and it would be in the same shape, reflecting the same normal, flat-white sky. Pockets of water in a larger dryness that isn't dry, exactly, just... not wet.

There is no plant life. Not a blade, not moss, not the green fur you expect on damp brick in cool air. The mortar is clean. The concrete is clean in the way that only things that have had all the life scrubbed off them can be. Even the fungal look — that felt, that stain — isn't there. Some places prohibit plants by poison or lack of soil. This place prohibits them by rule, by an agreement older than the concrete. The damp has no purchase. It just sits on the mineral and waits to be shaken off. What grows here is not that.

What grows, or rather, what *is*, is the rest.

The pigeons. Or something like pigeons.

If you saw one from far away you would say pigeon, starling, gull. Close up you wouldn't. They are the color of wet newspaper and boiler scale, and they have too many joints in their wings, and their feet are soft, like a seal's, leaving no scratch on the brick. And there are so many of them. They are the town.

They cover every flat surface that is not water. The roof of the lathe shop is a solid, breathing mat of them, shoulder to shoulder, head to tail. They fill the gutters. They line the power lines so thick the lines bow, and still more come, clinging to the undersides of each other. The chain-link is not wire anymore, but a dense, feathered rope of them. In the empty concrete between buildings, where nothing is built, they are there too, but spaced out, standing alone with exact, even distance between individuals, like they are holding the emptiness in place. Sparse where the buildings are sparse. Overwhelming everywhere.

You can hear them before you see the bowl. They are loud. Not in the way a flock is loud, with cries and flutters and fighting. There is no fighting. There is no lifting off in panics. They coo, or they make that industrial-pigeon sound, but all at the same pitch, all at once, and it does not modulate. It is a tone. It is the same loudness standing next to the press shop as it is on the access road. It doesn't echo, because it comes from everywhere. The factories contribute too — they are running. The lathe is turning something, always. The press is pressing, always. You can feel the thump in the soles of your shoes. But there is no throughput. No raw material enters. No finished piece leaves. The machines run in equilibrium. The belts turn without wearing thinner. The industrial hum and the animal hum have found the same note and they hold it.

That is the rule here: nothing changes.

Not frozen, exactly. Nothing is stopped. The pigeons blink — when you watch one long enough you see the nictitating membrane go — the water condenses and drips, the shadow of the water tower moves... no, it doesn't. The light is normal. It's day, it's a cool, ordinary, overcast industrial day, the kind that makes colors look true. But the light does not move across the yard. The pool reflections don't ripple unless you touch them, and when you do ripple them they settle back to the exact same picture, not a similar one. The rust on the locomotives has established itself, weathered to a deep, velvety brown-orange that is no longer rusting. It reached its final shape decades ago and decided to stay.

You walk through and the birds don't move. They make room where your foot will fall, but you never see them step. There is always a space. The air smells of cool iron and damp stone and feathers and warm fat, but not stronger in one place than another. It is in balance, long-settled, like a tide pool that became its own ocean.

The weirdness, if you want to call it that, is that you realize the factories weren't built to make things. Or not things as you understand them. They were built to make the sound, and the warmth, and the vibration in the concrete, and the ledges at exactly the right width for nesting — or roosting — or whatever it is they do, for a fauna that does not eat plants, does not eat anything you can see, but feeds on the steady state. On the loudness that never changes. On the damp that never dries.

If you stay until your ears adjust — though adjust is wrong, because the sound does not allow adjustment, it insists — you notice that the birds are not breathing together, but the factories are breathing with them. A bellows sigh. A release valve. A soft foot.

If you leave the bowl, and go back up the access road, the sound stops all at once, as if someone closed a door, though there is no door. Behind you the light in the bowl stays normal. The cool stays cool. The water stays exactly enough.
