It is low country in a way that has nothing to do with the sea, though the sea may have made it.

A broad, shallow basin kilometers across, rimmed by hills so old and soft-shouldered you only know they are there because the darkness gets a little less dark where the sky meets them. The floor of the basin is not flat. It is scalloped and pitted, weathered into place over a time you can feel in your teeth — basalt, maybe, or some blacker stone that was once molten and has since been licked smooth. Not jagged. Settled. Each stone has found the posture it intends to keep. Long hollows where water has worried at the same spot for millennia, low ridges like the knuckles of buried things, potholes polished to a glassy sheen.

There is no soil. There is no green. Nothing roots here.

The air is cool, a constant 8 or 9 degrees that lives on your skin as damp even when it is dry. The kind of cool that makes stone sweat. Water does not flow here in rivers. It arrives from below and above in half-measures. At night — and it is always some kind of night here, a starless, heavy dusk under a low cloud deck that never breaks, never quite rains — the stone exhales and the air condenses out. Beads gather and run, trickle into every concavity. Some mornings the hollows hold clear shallow pans, an inch deep, black as obsidian. By midday they are gone, drunk by the air and the stone. Then it will spit again, a brief, cold mist that does not fall so much as settle, and refill them.

And the plain is not empty. That is the first mistake, thinking stone.

It moves.

Not in one place. Everywhere. A density that breaks your attempts to count or to find an edge to the mass. From the rim to the near-dark center, the basin floor is covered, layered, roofed over in fauna.

They are not one thing, but the bulk are one kind — low, broad, pale things. You would call them isopods or trilobites or lice if you needed a word, but they are wrong for all of them. Each the length of your forearm, or your leg, slate-grey to yellowish-white, articulated, domed. Their backs are the same eroded mineral as the basin itself, pitted, carrying their own tiny pools of condensation in the dimples of their carapaces. They walk on too many legs, and they never stop walking. Over each other, under each other, a slow, deep, churning carpet two and three deep, shells clicking against shells.

In the cracks between them live smaller things — black, fast, beetle-like sprinters that ride the larger backs and dart down to drink. Above them, or through them, much larger things wade — tall, stilt-legged, blind-looking grazers with heads that are only mouths, sieving the smaller sprinters and the pale crawlers with a soft, continuous shoveling motion. Nothing is silent. Nothing pauses.

Because the sound —

You hear it before you ever see the basin's edge. A physical pressure. The dominants rub their plates together, stridulate with file and scraper on the underside of each segment. One alone would be a faint tick. Layered millions thick across a valley, it becomes deafening, a dry white roar, cicada-loud but deeper, like a mountain of dry rice being poured forever. Under it, the clack of shell on stone, the pop of legs finding purchase, the wet soft tearing sounds from the tall grazers. The occasional high keen from something being turned over and unzipped. It vibrates in your molars and your sternum. You cannot talk. You eventually stop trying to think in words while you are down among them.

The light is not gone, but it has no source. The cloud cover lets no moon through. What light there is, is theirs. Faint, intermittent. The big crawlers have photophores along their seams that pulse, not bright, a sick amber-green, just enough to pick out the wet sheen on the next back. When a grazer steps, the disturbed layer beneath flashes in alarm, a ripple of dull light that runs outward for twenty meters and fades. So the whole plain glitters, low and zoned out and endless, like a city seen from a plane that is only ever in the momentary act of turning its lights off.

There are no plants to eat. The whole system runs on what the air and water leave and what the stone grows. Between the polished stones, in the permanent damp of the shadow-side of each crawled-over hollow, there is a slick — not green, not moss, a dark bacterial felt, rust-colored, black, smelling of iron and brine. It reconstitutes every few hours from vapor and mineral. The pale crawlers lick it off the rocks with broad, soft tongues, and the sprinters eat what they scrape off the crawlers, and the tall ones eat them all. A closed loop, complete, old. Perfectly balanced. Guano does not accumulate; nothing is wasted. The shells of the dead are carried, still walking-crowded, onto the backs of the living and rasped clean within minutes.

If you stand still too long your boots disappear under them. They do not mind you. They flow around and over, cool and damp, their legs pricking like cold pins, each carrying its own little load of sky-water in the bowl of its back. The basin breathes. The condensation gathers. The stridulation rises a pitch and then falls. Somewhere far off, a grazer sighs through its body-mouth and steps, and a whole acre of light startles awake and then forgets again.

There is nothing built here. Nothing ever has been. The stone and the wet and the things have an agreement that predates building.

You leave with your ears ringing, and later you realize the ringing is exactly the same wavelength as the plain.
