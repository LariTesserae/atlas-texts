It is not a city. A city has ground underneath it somewhere.

This has no ground. It is ground, all the way down. A low plain that does not end.

The world is flat and low and vast beyond useful measurement — low enough that the air sits heavy on it, warm, 0.9/3 low, 2.8/3 vast, a made continent with no highlands to break it, so the weather it makes for itself just hangs there. Normal light, 2.5/3, a white-yellow daylight with no visible sun, diffused through a layer of wet air that never quite becomes cloud and never quite burns off. Bright enough to read by, to see color true. Warm. 2.4/3 warm like the inside of a laundry, like skin after a bath.

Everything is built. 3.0/3 total. There is no rock that was not poured, no sand that was not cast. The terrain is a factory floor the size of a planet, fresh from the mold. 1.2/3 young — you can see it in the edges. Every corner is still sharp. Every expansion joint is still crisp. Seams between slabs are knife-straight and they run for kilometers, hairline filled with warm water. The slabs themselves are not all the same: vast pours of pale concrete with the ghost of wooden formwork still on their sides, fields of ceramic tiles the size of billboards, each tile slightly bowed from firing, black iron plates riveted, low long roofs of corrugated asbestos-cement that never cover anything, cast-iron grates lying flat over nothing. Stairs that go down one step and then become floor again. Low walls with no rooms, a meter high, perfect, built to be leaned on. Bollards, capstans, valve wheels everywhere, as if someone needed a place to tie down an ocean that never arrived.

And it is wet. 1.8/3 wet not as ocean but as saturation without drainage. The whole plain weeps.

There are no slopes for water to run off, no soil to sink into. So water lives as a film. In most places it is a single millimeter thick, a warm skin stretched over concrete that makes every surface shine like a seal's back. In the low spots between tiles it deepens to two, three centimeters — clear, warm, and absolutely still. Condensation forms on the underside of everything that has an underside — and everything does — and drips at a constant, metronomic rate. Tap. Tap. Tap. The air itself condenses on your arms. If you stand still you feel it bead on you, too; you become part of the circuit. Pumps, everywhere below, sigh once every few minutes and move that water up again, up through weeping rocks that are not rocks but filter housings sweating, up through perforated pipes that exhale mist. Nothing ever floods. Nothing ever dries. This is 0.2/3 dynamic — never changing. The level is held in equilibrium so perfectly it feels like a physical law, not an engineering choice.

No plants. 0.5/3 vegetation. Not a blade, not a root. The mineral world remains mineral, even wet and warm as a greenhouse. There is discoloration on the tiles, a darkening, a slippery biofilm of iron bacteria and light-eating slime that is not, by any honest definition, a plant, does whatever it does without leaves or stems. No moss. No tree. Nothing that makes a seed. The green you expect never comes.

Instead — fauna. 2.6/3 overwhelming.

They are the density. 2.1/3 dense. The quiet.

At first you think the floor is patterned in brown and white and chitin-black. Then the pattern moves. Everything that is still warm and wet and horizontal and not food is covered in life that does not need plants. Pill isopods the size of thumbnails and the size of dinner plates and the size of suitcases, all the same species folded out in different scales, shoulder to shoulder in the shallow channels. Paling crabs with barnacle-crusted concrete still stuck to their backs because their backs are concrete. Eels of water that is itself a living thing? No — just eels, translucent, endless, living in the film that should not be deep enough for eels. Low warm gliding things like slugs made of wet canvas that leave a brief dry track that fills in behind them. In the air above, things that are not birds but occupy the same job: leathery kites, filament-floaters that drift six inches over the plain and filter the condensation with combs, never flapping. On the low walls, rows and rows of something like pigeons but with no feathers, only fine, dense hair that wicks water, huddled so close they touch. Leg to leg to leg.

And all of it is quiet. 1.3/3 mostly quiet, despite being industrial. The tech here is 2.1/3 industrial — you can see the scale production in every stamped grate, the electrified rails that go nowhere carrying the same empty carriage on a loop, the engine houses the size of cathedrals with a single piston inside the size of a house, moving up and down, up and down, oiled to silence. The engines do not roar. They hum under the threshold of hearing and you feel them in your teeth. Steam does not whistle. It sighs. The animals have learned to be quiet too — or they were made quiet. The big isopods make no click when they meet each other; they yield. The hair-pigeons coo at a frequency just below attention. Even when a million beings shift at once there is only a soft rustle, like water being nudged by water.

This place is strange in the specific way a factory made for non-human workers is strange. 1.6/3 weirdness. The valve wheels are at knee height for a person but chest height for a suitcase-isopod. The troughs are exactly the depth of the translucent eels. The electrified rails have a third rail that is warm to the touch of hair but would burn a hand. There are no chairs, no beds, no signs, no doors. Nothing was made for you. Everything was made, and everything is used, and everything fits.

And nothing changes. No tide, no rust advancing, no growth, no death that accumulates. The population of crabs does not increase or decrease — when one dies its soft parts are gone in minutes, its shell becomes another tile. The water that evaporates is the water that condenses. The piston goes up and the piston goes down and the same kilowatt walks in a circle forever. The place is in perfect, warm, wet, humming balance. You can walk for a thousand kilometers in any direction over sharp-edged, newly-cast, endless floor, through shallow water that never gets deeper, through a carpet of living backs that parts for your feet and closes again behind you, breathing the warm laundry air, hearing almost nothing, under a light with no source, and when you stop your footprints fill in and vanish and the plain is as fresh as the minute it was made.
