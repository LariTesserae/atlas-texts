You are standing on a floor that was never ground.

At first it looks like a desert. That is the wrong word for it, but your brain reaches for it anyway because of the light and the flatness. The light is total, a white, warm, high-noon light that doesn't seem to come from a single sun so much as from the air itself being overexposed. There are no shadows that are truly dark. Everything reflects everything else. The heat sits on your shoulders like a weight, not savage, not burning — about the temperature of blood, of a machine that has been running a long time.

It is flat. Not flat like a plain. Flat like something has finally stopped trying to be tall.

For as far as you can see — and you can see very far, far enough that the distance turns creamy with haze — there is low, low elevation. A continent with no mountains left. The world has been worn down past hills, past dunes, into its final compromise shape. A curvature so gentle it looks like the world gave up on topography. If there were ever seas here, their basins have been filled in. If there were towers, they have sagged.

Because everything here is built.

There is no bedrock under this. There is no sand that wasn't made. The surface under your feet is not stone, but stone-like: plates, strata, sheets, weaves of material laid down, built up, grown, extruded in epochs so far back that they have geological age now. A dead city that has become geology. The topmost layer is pale, an off-white composite, ceramic-concrete that has been abraded by time into a softness. It powders faintly under your boots. It is pitted everywhere with tiny holes, like breath-holes.

And it is dense.

This is what the desert comparison misses. A desert is empty. This is not empty. It is so full you almost can't see it at first, because fullness, at this scale, looks like texture.

Everywhere — every meter, every square meter — there are presences. Low, crowded, shoulder-to-shoulder forms, all in that final state of erosion. At knee-height, at waist-height, at ankle-height. What you took to be rocks are housings. What you took to be cracked earth is the interlocking of a trillion lids, shutters, cases, calyces. A field of machines, or the ghosts of machines, or the bodies of machines that decided to become architecture. They are not arranged in streets. There are no streets. They are packed like scales, like barnacles, like cobblestones that happen to be the size of refrigerators and tombs and small houses, all fused at the edges. Some are open. Most have settled shut.

All of them are old. That is the primary fact here. This is not ruin. Ruin implies a catastrophe. This is what happens after ruin has rusted, after rust has dusted, after dust has been compacted into a new stone. The corners are all gone. The edges are round as river stones. The surfaces have a patina that is not color but the softening of any possible color into one sun-bleached almost. Things list. Things have sunk into each other. Nothing has fallen recently. Everything is in the position it intends to keep forever.

There is almost no water. There never is. The air is so dry it makes your sinuses click. Yet this is not a dead world. It is actively dry.

You hear — almost nothing. This is a quiet place. Your own breath is loud in it. If you hold still, you hear the faint, high, tired sounds that a fully-built planet makes when it is still working. A tick from one of the scale-housings as it contracts in the heat. Far off, a sigh, as a vent the size of a stadium exhales warm, moistureless air that shimmers and vanishes. The faintest insectile whirring inside a casing, not loud enough to locate. Mostly, though, it is a silence that feels engineered, as if the builders included silence as a material.

It moves anyway.

Because while you stand, the field is not still. It is active with a slow, non-biological life. Heat makes things do work here. The light — that too-bright, too-even light — is power. You see it now: across the plain, some of the lids are opening. Not quickly. A millimeter per minute. Black interiors drinking light. Then, later, closing. Other plates shift, irising. The whole continent breathes light as a currency, a quiet photosynthesis that has nothing to do with leaves. The materials themselves are tech beyond any distinction between material and machine. The surface you stand on repairs the powder you scuffed away, not quickly, but by flowing like extremely slow, self-aware wax to fill the depression.

This is what's strange about it — not that it is incomprehensible, but that it is almost understandable.

There is vegetation, but it is not meant to be here, and also it is exactly what should be here. Sparse. One every hundred meters, sometimes a kilometer. Not green — there is no green bright enough to survive this light. They are pale grey or glassy amber things, filamentous, like wire-brush tumbleweeds anchored into the seams where the casings meet. They don't need water. They root into the composite and mine it, or they drink the static in the air, or they eat light directly with little black beads that cover their arms. When wind comes — and wind does, a warm, flat wind that comes from the flexing of the world, from those exhalations — they shudder but do not break.

And rarely, fauna. Rare enough that you might think you imagined it. A single shape moving against the density, too large for its shadow. Not animal. Not machine. It steps, carefully, from casing to casing, never touching the pale filler between. Long limbs, jointed wrong. It has no eyes; it doesn't need them. It listens for the ticking of open lids. When it finds one, it lowers something into the black and drinks, or lays, or reads. Then it moves on. It does not notice you. There are not many of it left in this vastness, but vastness means that even rare events happen continuously somewhere.

You realize the rule here, the physics that holds this whole place together:

Nothing leaves.

The light comes in, the heat cycles, the casings open and close, the glassy plants mine the floor, the rare walkers tend the lids, the dust that is really powdered building settles and is rebuilt. Water was edited out of the system long ago as an unnecessary solvent. The world is closed, total, self-containing, so ancient it has smoothed its own edges into a single, low, warm, bright, silent, crowded, living plain that goes on until the curve of the planet hides it from you, and then goes on further still.
