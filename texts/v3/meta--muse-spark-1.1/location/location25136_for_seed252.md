It is a room that was never built.

You're below. Not deep in the planetary sense — not kilometers — just under. The surface is somewhere close above you, maybe ten meters of still-hot rock away, but you can't get out that way. There is no exit that was made. This is a pocket, a blister in a flow.

The chamber is about the size of a small bedroom, or a large lung. You could touch two walls at once if you stretched. The shape is not round. It's a negative of force — sharp, young. Two hours ago, geologically speaking, this space didn't exist. A tongue of basalt pushed over wet ground, sealed, and the trapped water flashed. The rock around you is still deciding what shape it wants to be.

That is the first thing you feel: heat.

The air itself is hot, not burning, but working. 50, maybe 60 Celsius. Hot enough that every breath is deliberate and comes out heavier than it went in. It is saturated, right up against dew point, and it sweats. The atmosphere condenses on anything slightly cooler than itself, which means everything.

The walls are black at their core — a fresh, glassy basalt, conchoidally fractured, edges you could cut your hand on if you weren't careful. But the glass is not clean. It is coated, and then coated again.

The inner skin of the chamber is crystal. Not quartz, not anything you know the name of. It grew yesterday. It is still growing. Clear boules and sharp needles, some as thin as hair, some as thick as your wrist, all pointing inward, toward the center of the room. They are ferociously sharp. Young. No wind has touched them, no water has rounded them, no hand has broken them. Between them, the original black glass glints like tar.

The crystal is where the light comes from.

It is bright in here. Undeniably, painfully bright, like noon in a white room. There is no sun. There is no lamp. The light is coming out of the crystal itself, and out of the wet air between the crystals. It's a broad-spectrum, steady white, but when you stop moving and look closer, it isn't steady at all.

Each needle holds light. You can see it carry down the length like fiber optic, pooling at the tip until the tip can't hold it, then spilling. When a drop of condensed water touches a tip, the light discharges into the water for an instant and the whole droplet flashes and goes dark again. The chamber is full of those little discharges, a quiet flicker that your eyes at first averaged into daylight.

This is not bioluminescence. There is nothing alive in the crystal to make it. It is hotter than any enzyme wants. What it is, is simply this rock, under this pressure, with this chemistry, doing something that your physics textbooks left as a footnote. The lattice itself is trapping blackbody radiation from the cooling flow above, downconverting it, holding it, and letting it leak as coherent white. It is a natural cavity computer with no purpose. No one built it. It assembled because under these exact conditions — hot, wet, fresh, pressurized — it is energetically favorable for these atoms to tile in a way that happens to trap and route light.

It isn't strange here. It just is.

Water is everywhere, but it never gets to settle. It weeps from the ceiling in beads that form on the undersides of the needles. The basalt above is still full of groundwater that was buried alive when the flow came over. It is being driven down and in by the heat, exhaling through the glass. It beads, swells, and falls. You hear it: a tick, a soft tap on stone every few seconds. That's most of the sound.

There is also the sound beneath sound. The rock is cooling. It pings. A crystal somewhere in the wall will crack at the molecular level as it adds another layer, a tiny, high *tik*. The air moves only when a larger drop falls and drags a little of the hot atmosphere with it. Otherwise it is still. Mostly quiet.

The floor is not a floor. It is the bottom of the blister, a shallow trough where everything that fell eventually wants to go. But nothing pools for long. The heat takes it back. What you have instead of a puddle is a thin, shimmering film of silica water, a few millimeters deep, superheated but not boiling because the air holds it down. Through it you can see the black glass is rusting as you watch — a bloom of orange and yellow sulfides spreading from the crystal bases. Fresh.

Because it is so sparse, you notice the scattered presences. A single kind of thing, sitting in that film. Not plants — nothing photosynthesizes in here, it doesn't need to. Ten or twelve of them, widely spaced, each alone.

They look like white, flat pebbles at first, about the size of a hand, but they are soft at the edge. A veil of bacterial mat, or something simpler, forming a disk on the hot skin of the water. No leaves, no roots. Just a diameter of a few centimeters and a faintly raised center. They don't move. Once in a while, the edge of one trembles as a gas bubble escapes from under it, or as a new drop hits it. They are eating the dissolved iron and the faint electric charge that leaks from the crystals. They are rare enough that you have to turn your body in this little room to find the next one. They are indifferent to your light.

Everything here is in motion, just slowly and all at once. Water cycle in miniature — weep, fall, flash, evaporate, condense. Crystal cycle — silicates leaving the basalt, joining the needles, trapping more light. Heat cycle — rock cracking, releasing heat, making more water move. Even the light itself has a rhythm now that you've been in the brightness for a minute: a wave of discharge moves clockwise around the chamber every forty seconds or so, as charge builds and cascades.

If you put your hand near a needle, the hairs on your arm rise. The air is ionized. The droplet on the tip brightens.

It is a hidden place, an intimate place. The universe made a clean room underground with no door, poured in hot water and light, and left it to run. It will not last. In a year, the water will be gone or the chamber will crack open to the surface and cold air will rush in and dull all this glass. The mats will dry into a dark stain. The needles will go dark. Right now, though —

Right now it is bright and hot and wet and active and completely unmade, ticking quietly to itself.
