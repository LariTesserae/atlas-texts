It is high. Impossibly high.

You don't climb to it, you surface to it. The range is still coming up. This whole country is new rock that hasn't learned how to be a mountain yet.

The earth is pushing here like a knuckle under a tablecloth. Black and red basalt that has never been weathered, still sharp, still glassy at the edges where it cooled last year or last decade. There are no soft shoulders, no slopes worn down into soil. Just raw, cleaved planes and sudden, brutal steps, as if the geology was quarried and set down still warm. Fifteen thousand feet, maybe more — high enough that the sky above is a deeper, drier blue than it has any right to be when the air around you feels like this.

It should be freezing. It isn't. It is hot.

The heat does not come from the sun, though the sun is blunt and direct at noon, normal light but unfiltered, white on black rock. The heat comes from below. The stone itself is warm to the touch, and in places too hot to touch. The city is built on what is essentially the roof of a furnace that hasn't finished firing. Steam sighs from contraction cracks between buildings. In the morning you can see the heat shimmer coming off the dark strata even before the sun hits it. Workers walk with the slow, economical gait of people who live in hot kitchens at altitude, short of breath and slick with sweat at the same time.

There are no plants. Not a weed, not a lichen that your eye would recognize as alive. Vegetation: 0. Everything that grows here was built.

And it is built densely. This is a town the size of a small valley, but there is no valley yet — erosion hasn't learned to carve one. So the city makes its own valley out of itself. It is layered, block over block, catwalk over pipe over cistern over foundry, clinging to the raw stepped sides of the uplift. From a distance you might think it was a mineral formation, so continuous is it, so much of the same palette: iron-oxide red, sulfur yellow, kiln-black, concrete-gray that has never fully cured in the dry hot air and stays slightly soft. Close up you see doors and shutters, all open for draft, and laundry that never quite dries and then never quite stays dry.

Because it is damp. Not wet. Damp like a breath held on metal.

There is almost no rain. The clouds that hit this new high place at nightfall don't have the patience to precipitate properly. They crash into the warm rock and condense. All night the city drips. Every surface that isn't hot enough to burn — every handrail, every hanging chain, every corrugated roof — beads with water and trickles. The city is designed for this. Not gutters, but nets. Kilometer after kilometer of black polymer mesh strung between the towers, between the factory spines, vibrating faintly, harvesting condensation. You wake to the sound of it, when the city is quietest: a hundred thousand quiet tinks and plinks as droplets fatten and fall into the tin funnels and basalt cisterns. By midday most of it has steamed back off again. Pockets remain — in the deep cisterns, in the shaded sumps below the dye works, in the covered aqueducts that thread the city like veins. The water is warm, mineral, faintly sulfurous. It is enough.

This is an industrial place but not a frantic one. The tech is everywhere — boilers, stamping presses the size of houses, lathes, winding gears, brick kilns that have been burning continuously since they were laid — but everything moves slow. Dynamic: 0.6. The engines are big and patient. Pistons the circumference of a man take a minute to complete a stroke. Chains carry ore buckets up the sheer new faces at a walking pace, clanking once, then again a long time later. It is mostly quiet. Sound carries strangely in the thin hot air; the machines do not roar, they exhale. The dominant noises are the drip of the morning's water, the settlement crack of hot stone cooling a fraction, the distant sigh of a pressure valve you can't locate.

That slowness is why the place can be so dense and still feel empty. People are rare per volume despite the density of structures — you are always surrounded by near-touching walls and pipes that you could reach out and touch on three sides at once, caked in precipitated salt — but you rarely see another person except in profile far above or below you on another layer. There is a rule here that no one wastes movement. You learn to stand still and let a shadow pass over you before you step into its heat.

There is almost no animal life, and what there is doesn't belong. A few high soarers, too high to be birds, circling the thermal off the hot uplift, never landing. Down in the cistern dark, pale, eyeless things like pillbugs the size of your hand that came up with the rock, that chew the bacterial felt that grows on the damp concrete and are in turn scraped off and rendered for lubricant. They are tended, not husbanded.

And the strange part, the part that makes you realize this city is not built on a mountain so much as in collaboration with it — the rock is still plastic down deep. Not metaphorically. In the lowest factory cuts, where they have dug to lay foundations and found the rock still moving, you can see rebar driven twenty feet into warm basalt and the basalt flowing around it overnight, gripping it. The buildings shift. Not enough to fall. Walls develop a lean that is corrected next season with a jack and a new shim. A street that was level in your childhood now has a noticeable hump where a new blister of uplift is nosing through. The city does not fight this. That is its industrial genius. Everything is mounted on slip-joints, on pivots, on oversized washers. The whole dense urban mat flexes with the embryonic geology. The message from the engineers, stenciled on every cistern: LEAVE SLACK.

So the place looks half-finished by design. Scaffolding is permanent. No surface is ever sealed. Cables are left with loops. The erosion that everywhere else in the world softens things is here running in reverse — this place is pre-erosion, pre-ruin. It is sharp, unweathered, just-born, and the city crowding itself into every sharp notch knows that if it holds rigid, it will snap.

At night, when the condensation comes down hard and the rock finally gives a little of its heat back to the sky, you can stand on a gantry and see the whole open town breathing. Lights — normal, yellow, industrial — coming on floor by floor, layer by layer across the open, stacked basin, thousands of them caught in their own halos of mist, reflecting off wet black rock that has never known a river. Quiet. Hot. Impossibly high and still rising.
