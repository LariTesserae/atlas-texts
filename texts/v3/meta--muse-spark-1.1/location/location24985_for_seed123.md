It is not a landscape you find on a map. It is a landscape you find *between* other landscapes.

Under the seawall, between the outer concrete and the inner wall of the old pumping station, a void has opened. Not built, but made when the two walls, poured fifty years apart, disagreed about how to settle. A long, thin, slightly bowed room — fifteen feet long, six feet wide at its widest, nowhere higher than a tall man could stand without brushing his head. You have to know the rusted service door behind Boiler Three is swollen shut in a way that leaves a thirty-inch gap at the bottom.

This is its whole world.

From the outside, the coast is flat and low, the station is low, this room is lowest — just high enough above mean tide that it never floods, low enough that it never dries. The air in it is warm. Not hot. The kind of warm that lives in pumping stations and laundries, a retained heat. The station was decommissioned three years ago but the auxiliary steam line still runs through here, a six-inch artery lagged in asbestos cloth and chicken wire, licensed and sweating. It ticks. Near it, the air shivers at 86, 90 degrees. Farther away, cooler, but never cool.

The architecture is young, which is strange for a ruin. The crack is new. You can see where the inner wall's aggregate has sheared. The concrete is bright inside — not the dark, wet grey of old seawall but a pale, almost yellow fresh fracture, sharp enough to cut if you put your hand against it. Rebar teeth jut out for an inch and then have been gnawed smooth by moisture. The floor is not a floor but a tray of fallen scale: pea gravel liberated from concrete, dust that has caked into a new, soft sandstone under the dripping, and slick places of green-black curing compound that never fully cured. No weathering. No rounding. Everything has an edge.

Water here is an event, not a presence.

There is no standing water. The floor is damp in a pattern. Cold seawater on the outer wall condenses in the warm air and runs down in beads that never quite become streams. They gather at bolt holes, fatten, fall. *Plik*. Thirty seconds later, another. The top of the steam pipe drips upward in a way — it sweats and then the droplets are heavy enough to fall straight down, even as the heat tries to pull them back up as vapor. So the ground has a polka-dot wetness. A dozen dark disks where drops land again and again, each with a little crater in the new dust. If you stay an hour, you will get rained on twice.

And it is packed.

You have to understand: there is no empty surface in this room.

The density is not of things, but of occupancies. The walls are a solid moving coat of life. Pill bugs — but wrong — sea-slaters the size of your thumb, pale, translucent where their legs meet their bodies, slate-dark on their backs. Thousands. They are shoulder to shoulder, antenna to antenna, covering every square inch of vertical concrete that is not hot pipe. They do not pile; they tessellate. When one moves, the ripple passes through ten others who adjust without space.

Between them, in the cracks they leave, are the snails — tiny black periwinkles that somehow got in, that close their operculum when the air gets too warm and open again when a drop falls near them.

On the floor, the beetles. Small, black, oil-slick ground beetles that never stop, that have to crawl over a living pavement of woodlice and sandhoppers. The sandhoppers are inland now, three generations removed from beach; they get bigger here, almost shrimp.

And the geckos. How many geckos can live in fifteen-by-six? Twenty? The Mediterranean house geckos that came in on the crates when the plant was built, that never left. They are pale as the fresh concrete, nearly see-through on their bellies. They cling to the ceiling and do not move for long stretches and then dart, and where they eat a hole opens in the slater-carpet for a minute, and then closes again.

There is almost no plant. That's the rule here; the animals have outcompeted it for surface. But there is a single thing: in the farthest, lowest corner where two of the drip-disks overlap and keep a permanent wetness the size of a dinner plate, a fern grows. One fern. *Pteris*, maybe. Its fronds are stunted, black-green, covered in a fine felt of rust dust. Under its root, a mat of white mold eats the concrete itself. That is all the vegetation this entire country allows.

The built things are incidental, leftovers. A light switch, its metal cover gone, two copper screws staring out. A vertical line of old anchor bolt stumps, each one now a perch for a gecko. A discarded work glove, fingers fused stiff, now a damp burrow that the beetles go in and out of. A single incandescent bulb in a wire cage on the ceiling, still hot, still burning — because the auxiliary line keeps the emergency circuit live. It should have burned out years ago. It hasn't. It gives normal, honest, midday light, flat and yellow-white, because there is also a grating high on the outer wall at ground level that lets a chopped rectangle of real daylight in. Dust motes in it don't drift; they jitter, because the hot and cold air turn over in the room very slowly, a convection roll you can feel if you hold your hand still.

You can hear, if you crouch and get your ears below the pipe level, that it's almost silent.

Almost. There is the jointed soft rustle of hundreds of chitinous legs that never quite synchronizes into a single sound. There is the *plik* of condensation. Every two minutes, somewhere higher in the plant, a solenoid fires with a muffled *chunk* and the whole steam pipe shudders and knocks once against its hanger and the geckos freeze. Somewhere above, a pigeon walks over the grating and its toes make a *tink tink*. That is it. The pack is quiet about its business.

It is active — violently active — even while quiet. Everything is eating everything else. You watch a beetle drag a molting slater. You watch a gecko tongue snap and a snail disappear. You watch the mold advance a fraction of a millimeter a day across the glove. You watch the water wear the new floor into a new topography, granular deltas forming around each drip-crater. You watch the concrete itself continue to cure, exothermically, faintly, a chemical heat that will take decades to finish. The room is in fast-forward. It's a young canyon, a fresh wound, and succession is happening all at once, too fast, too crowded.

Do not bring a light. It already has one.

Do not pour out your water bottle here; the map of wet dots is the map of ownership and you will flood a neighborhood.

Do not stand still long, or the slaters will decide your boot is also wall.

If you lie on your back in the middle — and there is just barely enough room to do that between the pipe and the wall, if you don't mind being immediately covered in exploratory life — you can look up at the underside of the grating and see one square of sky and the estranhas industrial guts above it, conduit and chain and catwalk, and feel entirely hidden. Pocketed. A world that is fully occupied and therefore has no place for you, but which will tolerate you for a few sweating minutes as condensation of a larger sort.
