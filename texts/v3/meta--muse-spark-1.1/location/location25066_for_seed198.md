It is high before it is anything else.

You feel it first in your knees and in your breath, the thinning of the air. This is not a peak, but the long, broad shoulder of one — high enough that clouds snag and give up. Below you the valley is gone under low weather, above you the true summit is gone the same way. There is only this middle world, a slope that should have been alpine stone and thin grass, but isn't.

Because there is no ground here anymore.

The mountain was terraced a long time ago. Not farmed — built. The terraces are not soil held by stone walls; they are floors. Every terrace is a roof of the one below it, and a foundation for the one above it. Poured concrete gone dark with age, black iron, corrugated steel that has settled into its own rust, brick that sweats. It goes down in steps as far as you can see through the dim, and up in steps until the mist takes it, a stair for giants that has become a town, a watershed, a machine with no end. The scale is regional. You can walk for a day and never leave construction. What would have been switchbacks are streets, what would have been cliffs are multi-story mill faces, what would have been scree fields are slag piles now stitched over with roots.

Nothing here is natural in substrate, but everything is alive on surface. That is the density. There is no empty interval. Every ledge, gutter, buttress, rivet line, cable tray, and pipe elbow is occupied. If it is horizontal, there is moss on it — thick, felted, acid green and bottle green and near-black — and if the moss has established, then the ferns come, then the low, woody shrubs with waxy leaves that can live on mist. On verticals, the ivy and the black-barked creeper that the people here call wirevine because it grows as straight as plumb line when it has metal to follow. Gardens are not plots; they are encrustation. Someone has taken an old boiler and cut its belly open and filled it with soil and now leeks grow out of it in a row. Someone else has trained runner beans up the side of a cooling tower. Rice, or something like cold rice, grows in the shallow settling pans that step down the whole mountain to slow the water. It is lush to the point of absurdity — a jungle made of temperate, cold-tolerant things, all growing on manufactured ground.

And it is always wet. Not raining exactly, though it rains often. This is a place that weeps. The air itself is at saturation at this elevation, cool — your breath shows even in summer — and when it hits any surface that is even slightly colder, it beads. The entire structure condenses. You hear it before you see anything else when the wind pauses: drip, drip, trickle. Water travels the mountain by design and by failure of design. It comes out of pipes as clear overflow, it finds cracks in concrete and runs down rust stains in black fans, it sheets paper-thin over the moss which holds it like a sponge until it can't and then lets go. Every roof has a gutter, every gutter feeds a cistern, every cistern has a chain. Weeping rocks, except the rocks are concrete. Your shoes never dry. The metal handrails are always cold and slick. The floors of the open-air workshops are covered in a quarter inch of standing water that reflects the low grey light and the green above it.

The light is dim and has been dim all day. The cloud deck is thick enough that noon is like dusk, and the deep alleys between factory terraces are like late evening even then. What light there is, is diffuse and directionless. It makes colors saturated — the orange of rust against the green almost hurts. Work lights are on everywhere even in daytime: bare, caged bulbs strung on black cords, amber sodium lamps over loading doors. There is no glare.

It is industrial to its core. You can tell by the scale of the voids. Doors fifteen feet high to let some long-gone part through. Overhead gantries that still creep along rails, moving pallets of moss, or turbines, or nothing, just moving because that's their job in the circuit. Long halls where waterwheels that are not wheels at all but screws are turning slowly, endlessly, turned by the weight of all this captured water. It was built, from the look of the wear, to be a waterworks or a mine or a power plant, and then as the original purpose faded it was converted and converted again without ever being demolished. Established erosion. Corners are not sharp anymore. Concrete is pitted where the drip has hit it for a hundred years in the same spot. Iron is not rusted through; the rust has reached a stasis with the oil people wipe on it. Everything has a slight lean back into the hill, a settlement that has stopped settling.

And it is turbulent. This is the paradox that makes the place hard to understand at first. It is mostly quiet. The packed-together-ness muffles; the moss drinks sound; the industrial tech here is not banging and stamping but hydraulic, water-driven, electric at low voltage, humming more than roaring. So the acoustic is soft — water, distant scrape of a crane, a voice calling a measurement, a dog barking two terraces down.

But visually, kinetically, it is chaos. Anthill energy. Nothing stays in configuration. Because there is no empty space, to make space you have to move something else. And so all day, all night, people — many, many people, you see them everywhere once your eyes adjust — are reconfiguring. A crew of four unbolts a wall panel and carries it across an alley to patch a sluice. A woman drags her entire market stall — actually a repurposed transformer box on wheels — ten meters to the left to catch better drip for her cress trays. Kids run parts on handcarts along rails that intersect rails. The gantries overhead cross each other, pause, reverse. Laundry, tarps, plastic sheeting, scaffolding, vine trellises — all shift a little in the damp breeze, lifted, tied down differently by evening. It is not noisy, but it is not still for a second. A bazaar built by engineers, maintained by gardeners.

Fauna is rare in the way city fauna is rare when there is no rubbish and nowhere for a burrow that isn't already full of cable. A rat, very occasionally. Pigeons that look perpetually damp. The tiny black midges that breed in standing water and then die in the cold by evening. Mostly it's just people and plants. The plants do the work of animals here, moving slowly into every niche.

The rule of this place is simple and unspoken: If you do not hold it, you lose it, and if you hold too much, it rots. Since everything is built and everything is wet, everything wants to become substrate for something else. Let a roof unkept and the moss becomes a mat and the mat becomes a garden and the garden pools and its weight brings the roof down to the next level. Keep it too dry and your crop dies and your neighbor will put their cistern outlet over your wall to reclaim the moisture. There is no wilderness to expand into, so you expand by negotiation, by inch, by moving your beans up one bolt hole on the tower.

You stand on a terrace edge, the valley wind cold on your face, looking at a hundred more edges below you vanishing into cloud, each one guttering, green, occupied, busy, dripping.
