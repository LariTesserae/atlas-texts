It is below.

Not deep like mantle-deep, but below enough that the surface is a rumor. Above you there is soil, then root-mat, then cap-rock, and none of it matters because you are inside the second skin.

The city is a room. That's the scale of it. If you walked, you could cross it in five minutes. You could hold its shape in your head all at once. It was not carved, it was grown open recently — the stone is still angry about it. The walls are sharp. Too sharp. Basalt but glassy where it cooled fast, or was cut fast, with edges so fresh they haven't learned to be rounded yet. You can see the chatter-marks of whatever opened it, long, vertical flutes that go up into the dark where the ceiling becomes the underside of the world. This is a young place. Early-formation. The rock still weeps from the shock.

The water does not come from a river. There is no river. It comes from the rock itself.

The air is warm — bath-warm, greenhouse-warm — and saturated. You breathe in and it drinks you a little. The ceiling, low and near enough to touch in the middle where it sags, is always condensing. Drops gather on the black glass, swell, and fall. Not a rain, more like a constant, unsynchronized ticking. The walls sweat in sheets. Every vertical line carries a thin, bright skin of water down to the floor, where it gathers in the cisterns that are also the foundations.

Because this is a city. Or what is left of the idea of one.

It is urban in the way a circuit board is urban. Layered construction, no wasted line, platforms built over platforms over the gutters. Narrow streets of some dark ceramic that never got dirty, balconies that cantilever with no support, doorframes that still hold doors that have never been opened. In the center, the buildings are tallest — three, four stories — stacked like bookshelves around an empty heart. The lights are off. They were never meant to have bulbs. The material itself was supposed to light the way.

It does, but barely. A dim amber, deep in the wall-stuff. The city emits its own low light from its seams. The edges of each panel, each road, each lintel glow like cooling iron. Not enough to read by, enough to see the water falling through. The shadows are thick and wet. Between the light-lines, there is true dark, and the dark is huge, because the place is mostly void.

That is the first strange thing you have to adjust to: how empty it is. It has the density of a cathedral that forgot to invite people. For every column there are twenty meters of nothing. For every building, a full acre of open, furred stone floor where nothing was built. The city uses maybe a tenth of its own bowl. The rest is just space, held open. That is where the other technology lives. You can see it if you let your eyes unfocus in the dim — the air shivers. There are hardwares here that are not matter, or not only matter. Lattices in the void, invisible until a drop of water hits them mid-air and skitters sideways along nothing, or a mote of mist catches and suddenly traces a perfect, slow torus before evaporating. The city is being held. The rock would like to close again, and something very quiet and very strong is saying no. Something beyond concrete and rebar. The boundaries of the room have no buttress. The stone vault just... stops being allowed to fall.

And so it is loud.

Not city-loud. No voices, no traffic, no animals at all — not a fly, not a rat, not a microbe that would make a smell. Sterile as an operating theatre left in a jungle. But the loudness of active physics: water hitting water everywhere, a thousand different tempos. The sharp ping of drops on ceramic, the low glug in the cisterns as they breathe, the hiss of condensation boiling off the warm wall-glow, and under that, the low, sourceless organ hum of the void-lattice under load. The rock creaks. To be young and open like this is painful.

And in that loud, warm, dim wet, things grow.

Not planted. Seeded, maybe, or self-started from air. Lush, stupidly lush, because there is nothing to eat it. A wall of green has taken the lower third of the bowl. Not trees — there is no room for trees — but a dense, shag-carpet thatch of moss, fern, liverwort, vine, some engineered thing that looks like lettuce made of lace. It grows on the buildings, over the streets, up the sweat-tracks of the walls. The leaves are huge, dark, waxed to carry water. They collect the falling ceiling-drops and channel them. They are growing fast enough that you can see it if you stand still, the fronds uncurling with a faint vegetable sigh. No insects to put holes in them. No birds to scatter seed. So they just grow, perfect and unbroken, in the warm hollow, in the amber half-light, drinking the air and the rock's sweat.

If you stay too long your clothes never dry. Water finds every seam. Green tries your boots. The city does not acknowledge you. It is still finishing its first breath — the water still finding its paths, the stone still sharpening its edges, the lights still warming up, the void still learning the exact shape it has to hold open. You are in a hamlet built for no one, in a room that is holding itself below the world, listening to itself echo.
