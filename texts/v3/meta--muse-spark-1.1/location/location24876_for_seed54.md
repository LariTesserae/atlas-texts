It is high.

Not high like a hill you climb. High like the air has given up. You are on a plateau that shouldn't be here yet. The rock is too new for the maps, a broad shield of black glass that has welled up and cooled too fast, still holding the shape of its flowing. The elevation is in your ears, in the way sound carries too far and too sharp. The atmosphere is thin and cold beyond any temperate measure, -40, -60, the numbers lose meaning because the wind is wet.

That is the first wrong thing. It should be dry up here. It is not.

The air is saturated. You are above most of the weather and inside a different kind. The warm of the rock beneath, this embryonic stone, meets the cold that has settled over it from orbit, and water is born between them continuously. It doesn't rain so much as it becomes. It condenses out of nothing on every surface, beads on every edge, runs.

The plateau weeps.

This whole region, a valley that isn't a valley but the flat top of the world, maybe forty kilometers across to the caldera rim you can't see in the dark, is wet. Water in sheen. Water in film. Water in black mirrors inches deep where the basalt has dimpled. Water that never gets to be a river because the stone is too young to know how to be cut. Erosion hasn't learned this place yet. There are no channels, no canyons, no sand. Only raw, vesicular basalt, still sharp enough to cut, and everywhere the sound of trickling, dripping, sighing out of pores. If you put your palm down you feel it breathing cold water into your glove.

There is no plant to stop it. Not a lichen, not a smear. The mineral world is absolute. Just basalt and water and cold.

And yet the city is here.

It wasn't built on the plateau. It was extruded with it, or after it, so fast the two are hard to separate. It has the density of a downtown that has folded in on itself for heat. Structures rise in close terraces and spires, not tall as Earth cities, but crowding, near-touching, layered. They are not lit. In this deep night - not even true night, but the long polar dark of this altitude - you would expect windows. There aren't any, or not for visible light. The buildings are dark and warm, made of something that looks like grown ceramic, matte, slightly giving. They remember their shape. If a flake spalls off from ice expansion, the surface fuzzes over and re-knits by morning.

You can tell it is a city by its organization. Streets that are really service trenches where water runs calf-deep and black. Catenary bridges that aren't hanging, they are holding themselves up, tension without cable, a trick of tuned mass that makes your teeth ache when you pass under. Heat exchangers like lungs, the size of houses, slowly opening and closing with a slowness that barely registers, cycling once an hour, moving ocean volumes of that thin wet air through themselves and exhaling it a degree warmer. The whole place murmurs with post-thermal infrastructure. No combustion. No light spill. The technology does not announce itself; it just refuses to fail in a place where everything should fail.

It is loud here anyway, because of them.

You heard it before you saw anything. In the utter darkness - the stars are there but they give nothing, the clouds below you swallow any reflection - the sound is a continent. A chattering, clicking, scraping, sighing chorus so layered you thought at first it was the water in the pipes.

It's fauna. It is all fauna.

They cover everything. Not plant-eaters, because there is nothing to eat that is plant. They are here for the other gradient: the city is warm. The fresh rock is warm. The water that weeps out is salt-laden, mineral-rich, and it carries dissolved gas from the deep. And they drink heat and chemistry directly.

They are small, mostly. The size of your fist, of your hand, of a dog. Some bigger, moving with such glacial patience you think they are architecture until they shift. Their bodies are low, flat, pale, translucent in the way of things that never needed pigment. Like isopods, like trilobites, like peeled shrimp the size of suitcases, overlapping each other in a writhing, near-solid mat on every horizontal and half the verticals. They pile three, four deep in the runoff channels, antennae waving, filtering, scraping biofilm off the mineral with mouths made of silica combs. They have no eyes. Eyes would be a waste. In the gaps between the larger ones teem clouds of things even smaller, like lice made of glass beads, hopping in the wet air in such numbers the air itself seems granular.

They do not flee from you. They push into you, up against your boots, investigating the heat leak of your suit. They click. When thousands click at once the sound becomes a roar, a physical thing echoing off the low basalt sky.

This is why the city feels urban twice over - once for the builders, once for the residents that arrived without invitation. The density is impossible. Every cubic meter of air has something in it condensing, dripping, crawling. Every ledge holds mass. The slow ones, some boulder-backed filter-feeder that took a decade to walk from one street to the next, grinds its back against a building and the building yields a little, self-repairing around the furrow.

Nothing hurries here. That's the rule this place follows. Water condenses at millimeters per hour. Stone cools at degrees per year. The herds flow across the plateau in a two-month migration following the warmest vent line, a migration you can only see as a time-lapse in your mind. The big exchangers breathe. The clicking rises and falls with a tide that lasts days. Even the sound, loud as it is, feels slow - not frantic, but deep and layered, like ice settling.

You stand in the middle of a dark, wet, unfinished world, on top of the sky, in a city that makes its own physics to stay alive, and you are shoulder-to-shoulder with a million pale, soft bodies that have decided the same trick is worth copying. The rock under you is still being born, still weeping its first water. The cold is trying, patiently, to take it all back.
