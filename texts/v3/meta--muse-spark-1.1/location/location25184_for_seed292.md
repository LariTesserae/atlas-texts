It is not under the sea. It is *on* the sea, but up.

The Table.

Eight thousand meters up, the world has already given up on being a mountain. That is the erosion, at 3.0 — not jagged, not dramatic. This range died a long time ago. Whatever violence lifted it has been forgotten by the stone. What is left has been lapped and licked for a hundred million years until it became what all mountains want to be in the end: flat.

A peneplain in the sky. A continent-sized palm, worn smooth. The rock up here isn't rock as you know it down below. It's ancient basalt and quartzite polished to a dark gloss, pitted with potholes the size of houses, striated with the memory of glaciers that have also died. No peaks. No ridges. Just the faintest undulation, like a very old floor. And because it is flat and because its rim is still, barely, a few meters higher than its middle — it holds water.

A sea perched on top of the sky.

The Table Sea is not deep by oceanic standards — thirty, fifty, ninety meters at its deepest scours — but when you are in it, the parameter is right: 2.8/3 submerged. There is no shore. There is only the drop, miles out where the plateau ends in cliffs that fall through the clouds for two days straight and pour the sea out again in titanic, endless cataracts. Here, in the middle, you would not know you were high at all, except for the light.

The water is cool — 0.8/3 — not freezing, but a clear, lung-biting cold that never leaves. Snowmelt cold. Springwater cold that makes your hands ache inside your gloves after an hour. It is so clear it almost isn't water until you move through it, and then it is everything. The light is normal, 1.7 — a clean, high-altitude sun straight down through a shallow atmosphere, but it has to come through the water column. So by the time it reaches the ancient stone floor, it's fractured, rippling, a net of white-gold that never stops moving. The whole world flickers.

And it is loud. 2.0 and more.

First, the water itself, because this place is turbulent to the point of madness — 2.9/3 dynamic. This is no tranquil alpine lake. At this elevation the weather has nowhere to snag. Storms born over the real ocean below come screaming up the cliff walls with no warning and hit the Table Sea sideways. Wind gouges twenty-meter streaks across the surface and drives currents that spiral down and scour the bottom. When the rim-cataracts swell with rain, the entire sea tries to drain at once, and you can feel the whole volume lean toward the edge. Everything moves. Silt blooms from potholes, vanishes, blooms again.

Second, the life. The density is 2.0 — layered, near-touching, high-saturation. The lushness is 2.2. This is a kelp ocean. Not the thin, coastal kind. These are high-altitude abyssal kelps, evolved from freshwater riverweed that colonized when the Table was first flooded. Their stipes are as thick as a man's waist, black-brown and frayed, anchoring to the polished rock with holdfasts that look like melted iron. They rise thirty meters and then explode into a canopy so thick it makes false noon on the bottom. The fronds are wide as doors, olive green, translucent amber where the sun hits them, and they never stop. In the surge they thrash, cracking like whips, booming against each other with a wet drum sound. Between them are suspended forests of feather-weed, of white bottle-brush filter plants that cling to everything, of moss that isn't moss but a colonial animal that glows faintly.

And in that, fauna at 1.9 — abundant, everywhere, working.

There are no whales here; there isn't enough room. There are fish — schools of silver-black plateau trout the length of your forearm that move as one decision, turning in walls that block the sun; armored bottom-crawlers the size of dogs, scraping algae off stone with a sound you can feel in your teeth; clouds of krill-like glass shrimp so dense they turn the water into snow. Terns and shearwaters dive from above, spearing into the Table Sea like white nails, vanishing into the kelp and bursting out again screaming. And the work animals: seal-thick amphibians with flat heads that graze the canopy.

You live here because of the kelp and the seals and the mineral water. That's the built and the tech.

Built is 0.6 — scattered, faint marks, partial remains. This is not a city. It is too restless to be a city. Anchored to the deepest potholes, bolted directly into the primordial stone with cables as thick as your leg, are platforms. There used to be more; you can see their stumps — rusted rings, concrete feet worn smooth as the basalt, kelp forest growing through the empty bolt holes.

The ones that remain are industrial — 1.7 — not shiny future-tech, not low huts. Factories. The Harvest Frame Seven, the Pulpmill, the Airhouse. Riveted steel and corrugated tin painted a lead-gray that has gone almost black with cold water and age, all on stilts and pontoons and caissons that let the storms run under and over them. They creak and groan on their moorings. Corrugated roofs shiver. Big diesel-electric generators thump day and night, because down here air is a technology. Long hoses of ribbed rubber drop down to the workers.

Men and women in heavy vulcanized suits, brass helmets, weighted boots that barely keep them from flying away in the surge, move through the kelp alleys. They farm with chainsaws and hooks and nets. The kelp is cut, winched up smoking with cold to the pulper where it is rendered for iodine, for agar, for oil that burns hot and clean in the generators. The seal-things are herded with sonic prods — another loudness. Seabirds gather by the thousands around the offal chutes, screaming.

The rules of this world are simple and normal — weirdness 0.3. Nothing is magic. Water is water. Cold is cold. You need air, you need heat, you need a line back to your bell if you get tangled. If a rim-storm comes, you clip in and hold on to whatever ancient rock you can find and let the entire sea try to pull you off your mountain. If you let go, the current will take you to the edge. Everyone has seen the edge.

It is open — 2.5 — you can see for miles through the water if the silt is down. From the roof of the Airhouse, standing in the airlock with water sluicing off you, you can see it all: the valley that is the whole world, a flat, round, luminous plain of thrashing green-gold, its surface wind-chopped into whitecaps that are too high above you, its bottom infinite and rolling away, the distant black silhouettes of the other scattered rigs with their lights already on, and beyond them, on every side, a thin line where the water tears and simply stops — where it goes thin over the rim stone and falls and falls and falls as rain into the world far below.

Then another gust hits, the sun jerks, the kelp canopy billows, the whole sea leans, the trout run solid as a wall beside your face, and the Frame groans and you have work again.
