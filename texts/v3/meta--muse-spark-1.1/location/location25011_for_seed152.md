It is not a place you find by looking. You find it by temperature.

Low country, not far above the sea, where the ground never really decided to be ground. A flat of black soil and broad, shallow roots that run like roads on top of the earth because they cannot get any purchase beneath it. The air is warm and stays warm. Even at night it holds heat, close and damp against your skin. The canopy is low here — not tall mountain forest, but a tangle of second-growth figs and hog plums and bracken fern that has grown too big, gone lush and shoulder-high, elephant-ear leaves the size of doors. Light doesn't so much fall as filter, green and thinned.

And somewhere under that, between two buttress roots of a fig that has stitched itself together over years, there is a lip. You would think it was just shadow. A break in the root-wall where the soil has slumped inward, not out. 

This is recent. The earth here hiccuped. Maybe water dissolved a pocket of limestone and the roof gave way, maybe a family of armadillos undermined it, maybe the fig itself lifted and cracked its own floor. Whatever it is, it is new — raw. There is no erosion here yet. No softening. The inner walls are still sharp when you run your hand along them, crumbs of limestone and clay that have not learned to be smooth, that still have the fracture lines of their breaking. The clay smells like cold iron even though everything else is warm. The floor is furnished with that newness — little terraces of mud extruded by pressure, little columns that haven't fallen, a geology that is still mid-sentence.

Duck your head and go in.

Inside it opens. Not much — a room you could park two small houses inside, if you cut their roofs off. The ceiling is roots, living roots as thick as your thigh, and dead roots like cables, and root hairs hanging down so fine they bead with water and tremble. The air is dimmer than outside but not dark; the green outside bleeds through the root lattice, and what light makes it down comes in shafts that catch dust and spores. It is damp the way a breath is damp. Not wet. The river is a half mile off, you can hear nothing of it, but the water is still here: in the air, in the condensation that forms on the underside of every root and gathers and falls, one drop at a time, onto the city below.

That is the word that comes to mind before you can dismiss it. A city.

On the raw floor, in the pocket of air that the tree allowed, the leafcutters have built.

From standing height it looks like a spill of pale sand, but crouch — knees in the cool new mud — and the organization resolves. Towers. Not tall — the tallest not to your knee — but unmistakably towers. Granular, built grain by grain of excavated limestone and clay, glued with fungus and spit. Avenues between them, kept cleanly swept. Plazas where nothing is stored. Outlying neighborhoods of smaller mounds, each with its perfect round doorway. And deeper, where the light is dimmest, the walls are stratified: old construction at the bottom stained darker with humus, fresh pale work on top, still tacky. Layered construction, repair over repair, infrastructure you can read like rings. It is sparse, this city. There is a lot of emptiness in it. Courtyards a full handspan wide for only a dozen citizens. The builders have left room, as if they expect growth.

Fauna is the city and more than the city.

The ants themselves are everywhere if you stop moving and let your eyes adjust to their scale. Amber workers flowing along the avenues carrying confetti squares of hog plum leaf, green sailing on their backs. Soldiers standing still at intersections, huge mandibled, almost unmoving. But there are others too, because an abundant place like this cannot hold just one kind. Inside the pocket, in the quiet, everything lives off that quiet.

Tiny bronze frogs, no bigger than your thumbnail, sit in the shade of the tower walls and drink the condensation when it falls. They do not croak. Beetles like polished seeds patrol the outer walls eating mold. A white blind snake, thin as shoelace, moves through a service tunnel. Mosquitoes, gnats — they are there, but spaced far apart, hovering singly in their own columns of light, not in clouds. A spider has strung her web between two aerial roots above the main plaza, but she has built it high enough that the ant highways run unimpeded beneath. Everything keeps its distance. Much space between presences. You can watch one thing without being crowded by another.

There is no tool here. No device. No cut, no burned edge, no plastic, no nail. Everything is biological, everything is mouth-made or root-grown or rain-washed. And yet it is unmistakably urban. You recognize the impulse: to excavate, to pile, to sort by function, to keep roads clear, to bring food from far fields into central processing. The fungal gardens inside the mounds glow very faintly if you put your eye close — a beds of white cotton that the ants farm, fed by the leaf bits. The city eats forest. The forest overhangs the city.

And it is active. Relentlessly. In the time you sit there, a tower grows a millimeter. A new side shaft opens where there was not one, as fountains of soil grains appear from below — the deep galleries expanding into the raw clay. A leaf goes down. Another. A frog shifts to catch a drop. A condensation bead on a root hair swells and falls and lands with a soft tick on a roof, and a dozen ants pause and then correct their route around the new wet spot.

The rules here are simple and old.

1.  Everything that goes down must come up. Soil up, leaves down.
2.  The air must stay damp but not flooded. The city breathes through its doorways; if water pools, they wall it off.
3.  No sound above a whisper. Vibration is information. A heavy step anywhere near the collapse lip sends a ripple through the root ceiling and every ant on the surface freezes at once, antennae lifted. You learn to hold still.
4.  The walls stay raw. There is no time yet for weathering to do its work. This whole place is embryonic — it is still deciding what shape it will keep. The fig may close it, the rains may drown it in November, the ants may abandon it. For now, it is under construction, all scaffolds.

If you back out carefully, the leaves close again without a mark. From three feet away you cannot see the doorway. Only the air stays a fraction cooler in that spot, only the ground sounds a little hollow. The lowland keeps its pockets to itself — a perfect, hidden, self-built town, warm and dim and almost silent, making itself out of fresh earth one mouthful at a time.
