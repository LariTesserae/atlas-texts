You are in the Under-Kiln.

Not under the ground the way a cave is under the ground. This is under a floor. A deliberately made below. Someone poured a slab of concrete over a hollow and then left the hollow to do its work.

The space is about the size of a village hall tipped on its side and buried. You can see its edges in every direction, even in the dim. It is a single blister in fresh basalt, sharp and new. The walls still have the glassy teeth where the rock was cut or melted or simply decided not to be rock anymore last week, last year. There's no softening. No rounding, no dripstone, no dust fillet in the corners. Every edge will cut your boot. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person would have to duck, but it's not a crush, it's a room. A hamlet-room. An engine-chapel.

There is no water here. Not damp, not humid, not the memory of water. The air is warm, a steady 30, 35 degrees, kiln-warm, skin-warm, and utterly dry. Any water you bring in boils off your lips in minutes. The air itself is not air — it's faintly oily, heavy with hot iron and mineral fines, the exhaust of something breathing something else. Your throat tastes like a salt lick and a battery terminal. On the floor, where water would have pooled, there is instead a fine, bone-white alkali fur, a salt blight that crunches underfoot and never melts.

Low amber light. Not darkness. Work-light. Four sodium bulbs in wire cages bolted to the low ceiling, all of them on, all of them buzzing, none of them flickering. They have been on for a very long time. The light they throw is weak and particulate, amber and exhaust-colored, just enough to make the salt glitter. It doesn't move. No shadows shifting. The filament hum never wavers.

The built part is scattered and unfinished, or finished and then mostly withdrawn. There are three machines. Maybe four, depending on whether you count the one that has been taken apart and laid out.

Each is the size of a market stall, a laundry boiler, a crucible on legs. Riveted black steel, pressure gauges, stout pipes that go up into the basalt and stop. They are industrial the way a factory kitchen is industrial — scaled for production, but small-batched. They are not ruins. They are hot. You can feel the heat coming off their sides in sheets. Inside each, something turns, pumps, shunts. No fire, no steam — this is dry-industry, sealed chemistry.

On the flank of the largest one, stenciled in a language that has more straight lines than curves, is a single word that is probably DRYING / CURING / KEEP.

They have no on/off switch that you can find. They make a sound that is far too loud for a space this small. It is not the sound of breaking or working. It is one single, complex, layered chord: a low diesel throb at about your gut frequency, a mid whine of a turbine just slightly out of true, and above that a glassy, almost voiced whistling from a pressure valve that never releases. It is LOUD. You have to open your mouth to equalize it. It fills the blister and hits the fresh, sharp walls and comes back unchanged. You feel it in your teeth.

And because it never changes, after ninety seconds your brain tries to erase it, and fails, and tries again.

This place is in equilibrium. That is its weird rule.

Nothing accumulates. The salt that falls from the machines doesn't pile up. The heat doesn't get hotter. The light doesn't brown out. The machines are loud and hot and working and producing nothing visible — or they are producing exactly enough of what is needed to keep this whole thing exactly like this forever.

That includes the fauna.

They are abundant. Abundantly, impossibly abundant for a dry salt kiln with no water and almost no plants. The air is full of them.

They are not birds. They are not bats. They are moths, or moth-like, but made wrong. Each one is the size of your thumb, with no mouth, with wings made of dry, translucent parchment that is not feathered but slatted, like tiny iron shutters. Their bodies are matte black and segmented and slightly hot to the touch. They don't fly like moths fly. They hang in the air. Millions — thousands — no, hundreds occupying every cubic meter, so dense you have to wade through them, they brush your eyelashes, your ears. They don't scatter. They hold their positions in the air as if pinned to invisible shelves, wings vibrating so fast they blur, producing a second, higher harmonic on top of the machines' roar — a fizzing, chitinous static.

When one dies it does not fall. It desiccates and becomes dust instantly. When one is born — and they are being born, constantly, from seams in the basalt that sweat them like beads of solder — it takes its place in the lattice.

They drink the oil in the air. They eat the salt. They eat the sound, maybe. They are the filter that keeps the equilibrium.

On the walls, high where the light is weakest, there is vegetation. If you can call it that. It is sparse — a dozen, two dozen tufts — each a puff of black wire, like steel wool that grew. No leaves. Just curled, dry filaments that quiver in the updraft from the machines without ever being moved. They are rooted directly in raw basalt without soil, without water. Every now and then a moth bumps one, and the wire-puff rings faintly, like a tuning fork, and then damps instantly.

You realize after a while you have not seen a single footstep in the salt other than your own, but the machines are clean where hands would touch them, and the bulbs have been wiped. Someone tends this place. Or something does, frequently, with care. Something that knows not to leave marks.

If you stay long enough, the loud, frozen chord of the room begins to feel less like noise and more like a structural member, like another wall — sharp-edged, fresh-formed, and holding this small, dense, hot, impossible engine-hive exactly where it is: just below the skin of the world, never eroding, never changing, never drying any further than perfectly dry.
