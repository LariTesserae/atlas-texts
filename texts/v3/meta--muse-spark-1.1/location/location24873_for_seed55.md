It is all under water.

Not flooded — that would imply there was a before, a dry. This is a place where water is the medium. The ocean is not on top of the world, it *is* the world, cold and heavy and black from the bottom to a surface so far above that it is theoretical. If there is a sky, it has never been seen from here.

The bottom is not bottom. It is floor.

In every direction, farther than any diver could swim in a lifetime, the seabed is paved. It was all made. There is no exposed rock, no sand that wasn't brought, no clay that wasn't mixed by hands. A continent-sized built plain, at an elevation so low and so even that depth barely varies. A few meters of rise over a hundred kilometers, a long, almost imperceptible swell — that's a hill here.

What was built is not buildings. Or not anymore. It is terracing. Low walls, long as mountain ranges, but no higher than a person's waist. Stone upon stone upon stone, fitted without mortar, or with a mortar that has become indistinguishable from stone after uncounted cold years. The stones were quarried somewhere that no longer exists, carried here, laid down. Everything is laid down. The pattern is primitive in the way a dry-stone wall is primitive — you can see the hand in it, the size of the stone a single person or two people can lift. The corners are not quite square. The lines wobble.

And everything has been here long enough to forget it was built. Erosion at the end of its work.

The water itself has done most of it. Not by violence — there is no violence here, current is a slow breath that takes a century to exhale — but by patience. Every edge has been rubbed round. The top stones of every wall are domed smooth as river stones, scooped in places where someone long ago set a foot again and again. The joints between stones have widened a finger's width and then stopped widening, stabilized. Between them a black silt has packed so hard it is like grout. You could not now tell, in some places, where one stone ends and the next begins until you brush the silt away.

It is quiet in a way that is physical. Sound does not travel here the way it does in shallow seas. The cold — just above freezing, the kind of cold that makes water feel thicker — deadens everything. The occasional tick of stone contracting, the sigh of silt settling. No animal clicks, no fish.

There is no animal life. No fish, no worms, no shrimp in the mud. Nothing darts, nothing burrows. The water is too still and too complete.

There is, instead, the forest.

It should not be able to live here, in darkness without light for photosynthesis. There is no sun at this depth. Light has failed a long time ago on its way down. What reaches here is not light but the memory of black — a pressure-dark, a complete dark that your eyes, if you had them open, would paint with faint, false grain.

Yet it is lush. Impossibly, densely lush.

From every joint, from every leveled pavement, from the flat tops of the walls, it grows. Black kelp, or something that learned the shape of kelp. Fronds wider than doors, thick at the stem as a man's leg, black-green-brown so dark it is blacker than the water. It does not photosynthesize. It mines.

Its holdfasts are not roots. They are acids and threads that dig into the built stone and dissolve it, slowly. The forest eats the city. Or the farm. Or whatever this built continent was. It pulls calcium and iron and old lime directly from the hand-laid blocks. It fruits once, slowly, with bladders of cold gas that never rise — the pressure is too great, so they hang like dark grapes and then burst into a cloud of fine spores that settle immediately. The cycle takes years. A decade to grow a meter.

It has had time. The forest is old growth by orders of magnitude beyond old growth. Lower fronds die and do not rot — nothing to rot them — they simply soften and flatten and become part of the pavement, a layer of dark felt over stone over felt over stone, so dense that swimming through it, if you were swimming, you would have to push fronds aside with both hands. They layer. Canopy over canopy over canopy, but all low, never more than ten or fifteen feet high, because height is expensive when you build your body out of stone. The uppermost fronds comb the slow current for dissolved minerals. The lowermost lie flat and become ground.

You cannot see the vastness, because of the dark, but you can feel it. The pattern repeats without variation. Wall, terrace, pavement, forest, wall, terrace, pavement, forest. For thousands of kilometers. The water above holds its own weather — at some great height there are currents that turn like jet streams, cold rivers in the water world — but down here, weather is only the faintest change in salinity, a slightly colder sigh from the north that will arrive next year and leave the year after.

To walk here — and there are places where the pavement is clear enough, where an old processional way was made a little wider, that you could walk — you would need a light you do not have. Your feet would find the stones still level. Whoever built this built it to last beyond ending. The forest brushes your legs. It is not hostile. It is indifferent, as lush things can be when there is nothing to eat them. It just continues to grow, frond over frond, splitting stone with slow, soft chemistry, making more of itself.

The rule of this place is simple: everything was made by hands, and hands stopped long ago, and cold water and slow plants are finishing the work of making it smooth again.
