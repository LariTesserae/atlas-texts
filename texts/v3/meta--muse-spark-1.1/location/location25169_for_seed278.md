It is high. Not mountain-high, in the sense of peaks. Plateau-high. High enough that the sky thins out and stops pretending to be blue.

The world here is a single stone. A continent table, cut off at the edges by air that falls away. You are standing on the wind-scoured top of it, and there is no up beyond this. The elevation is itself the place — a vast, tilted plain of basalt and slate and old ash, planed down by time until time got tired of planing it. That is what it is now: established. The erosion isn't active, violent. It's finished. The land has settled into the shape it intended to be. Long U-shaped valleys, broad as states, running parallel for a thousand kilometers with nothing in them. Bluffs that are not cliffs so much as the memory of cliffs, rounded, lichen-black. Potholes in the rock the size of lakes, polished by ice that left a million years ago, now holding their own weather.

It is cold the way high places are cold when the sun is far away. Not a biting cold. A still cold. The kind that makes metal stick to your tongue even in your thoughts. The air is clear and thin and very dry, except where it isn't.

Light is dim, not because it is night. It never quite gets to be night or day here. The sun is a smudge behind high overcast, a pearled lamp. Enough to see by forever, never enough to cast a hard shadow. Colors are low-saturation: grey-rock, iron-red rock, white frost, yellow lichen. The sky is the color of the inside of an oyster shell.

Water does not flow here in the way lowlands think of water. It appears and disappears. In the lee of every boulder, a lens of frost that never melts, just thickens and thins. In the mornings - though there is no morning — condensation beads on the cold stone and runs a few inches before vanishing. In the great grooves, there are rivers, but they are intermittent, shy. They run for three days after a sleet squall a hundred miles away, black water over black stone, and then are gone, leaving a dark stain and a line of ice beads like a rosary.

There is no building. Nothing has ever been built here. No wall, no pylon, no road, no cairn. The idea hasn't occurred to this place. Even natural arches have collapsed back into hillocks. It is un-made, untouched, not in the sense of pristine but in the sense of irrelevant to making.

And yet it is industrial.

At first you think the horizon is moving. It is not a trick of thin air. Far off, humped against the pearled light, something vast moves. And another. And another. Scattered so widely that you could walk for days between them, and still — overwhelming. There are so many that the plain hums with their absence of sound. Their number is continental. Their migration is the weather.

They are the fauna.

They are not animals as you would file them. Each is the size of a factory, a ore freighter laid on its side and given legs. Low, broad, many-jointed, with backs crusted in stone and frost and a living lichen that is not lichen. Their skin is iron. Not like iron, not metaphorical. Plates of dull, pitted, naturally smelted iron that grew there, overlapping like shale. From seams between the plates, heat shivers out. Inside them, something burns without fire.

When you get close — and you will, because even at their slowest they eat distance — you hear nothing at first. The silence here is not emptiness; it is a pressure. Snow absorbs. Thin air carries nothing. Their feet, each as wide as a house, come down on basalt with a hush, spreading their weight on fleshy pads that exhale a puff of steam which immediately freezes and falls as fine snow. That is the only water many valleys ever get: the breath-water of the herd.

They are industrial by biology, by necessity. It is too cold to be alive here any other way. Inside each hulk, a furnace. You can see it when one opens its ventral vents to take in air — deep down, a sullen cherry glow, and the slow turn of something like a turbine, but made of cartilage and magnetite. They ingest. Not food; there is almost no vegetation. A few brittle, black-green mats of lichen that cling to windward rock, a dusting of cryobacterial felt. They take in that, yes, but they also take in rock. They scoop frost and scree with a lower lip like a bulldozer blade and grind it internally. Minerals are sorted, smelted. Waste is extruded behind them as long, warm berms of perfect glassy slag that steams for hours and then goes cold and cracks into the same basalt the plateau is made of. They are re-making the plateau as they walk it, factories with no product except continued warmth.

Where their furnace is hottest, electricity laces across their backs under the iron. On dim days you see it — St. Elmo's fire crawling from plate to plate, pale violet, utterly silent. It is why the air smells faintly of ozone and hot stone after they pass, even though you heard no roar. Their industry is quiet. An industrial revolution conducted in felt.

The ecology works because it is slow. So slow. Dynamic on a scale of seasons that last years. The giants walk from one end of the continent-table to the other and back again in a lifetime that might be five hundred of yours. They walk to keep their furnaces lit; to keep their furnaces lit they walk. Calves — if they can be called calves — are not born. They are split off, when a giant has eaten enough mountain. A bulge in its side grows for decades, plates forming, until one day the bulge shudders, cracks free with a sigh of steam, and stands swaying. Already the size of a house. The mother does not pause.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing needs to. Other life is small and sparse and lives in the interstices of this economy. White flea-things that live in the hot ash of their slag lines. Moths with fur so thick they look like flying mice, that crowd the vent slats to drink heat for a few minutes before freezing mid-air and dropping. The sparse vegetation — those black-green crusts — grows fastest on the windfall of iron filings that dust down from their backs. The rivers run where they have breathed.

If you stand in one of the great U-valleys while the herd passes on the ridge, you understand the scale. The plain has its own weather, truly. Their collective exhalation makes low clouds that catch on the distant, rounded bluffs and finally, reluctantly, snow. For a few hours it is damp, profoundly damp, every rock sweating, your own coat beading, the whole cold world briefly remembering what wet feels like. Then it passes, and it is dry and cold and silent again.

There are no artifacts. But the ground is ruled and marked by their passage. Not roads — drag-marks, polishing, the slow fill and re-cut of the intermittent rivers moved three meters left every millennium by a million crossings. The world is being worked. Patiently, quietly, without end or building or blueprint, just the steady metabolism of vast, warm, silent engines who are animals and are places themselves, moving across a place so big it can contain industry without ever having a city.
