It's not on any map that matters because the mountain is too old to be a mountain.

Up high — high enough that your lungs notice it before your eyes do — the range gives up. The peaks here are not peaks. They are what is left after peaks have been worried down for a hundred million years by wind and ice and time until they forgot how to be sharp. A peneplain, a high soft table of gneiss and quartzite that has settled into its final form. The stone underfoot is dark, polished bald, rounded like old molars. It dips very slightly, a shallow bowl a few hundred strides across, a palmful in the ancient rock. That's the whole of the place. You can see its edges from anywhere inside it.

And inside that bowl is a city.

Not a city like you know a city. No streets cut, no doors, no smoke. A city in the way a coral reef is a city, or a rookery is a city. There was no architect but it is organized anyway.

The buildings are the vegetation.

In this cold — a thin, absolute cold that is not yet cruel, maybe fifteen degrees below the day you would call pleasant — the plants have learned to be architecture. Cushion moss and alpine azalea and haircap and a black-green liverwort with leaves like shingles have grown into towers. Each one is waist-high to four times a man's height, narrow at the base and widening at the top, then hollowing slightly, like a chimney, like a thimble turned over. Lush doesn't mean tropical here. It means dense, intricate, wetly alive despite the cold. Each tower is a tight-packed colony a thousand years composting itself. The outside is living velvet, bright viridian and rust-red where it catches the sun; the inside is centuries of peat and fibers, slightly steaming in the morning because it holds heat.

They stand apart from each other. That's the first thing you get wrong in your head — you think lush means thicket. It's not. The density of this place is low to the point of emptiness. Between one moss-tower and the next there might be twelve, twenty feet of bare old stone, scoured clean, with nothing but a slick of lichen and a little pooled water. So the "city" is mostly air and rock. Empty avenues. You can see the whole arrangement at once: maybe forty, fifty towers in a loose, irregular grid that isn't a grid, but isn't random either. Like houses in a hamlet that grew up around watercourses that are no longer there. Cathedral scale, if your cathedral were made for mice and wind.

Because this is a damp place without being a wet place. There is no lake. No river that stays. What there is is the breath of the mountain itself. Every afternoon, clouds snag on the lip of the bowl and fall in. They don't rain so much as exhale. The air fills with condensing fog that beads on every hair of every moss leaf, grows heavy, and runs. You can hear it. And then at night it freezes. In the morning it melts. That is the water cycle here: fog-catch, bead, drip, freeze, melt, run in threads between the towers in little temporary streets no wider than your boot, disappearing into the joints of the ancient rock.

And the sound — that is what makes you realize how empty the air between them is. It's loud here.

Wind comes over the worn rim and falls into the bowl and has nowhere good to go. It hits a tower, splits, goes around, and makes the hollow inside hoot like a flute with one low note. Forty towers at once make forty different low notes. The ice in the stone cracks at noon with rifle-shot reports. The water drips constantly — not a patter, a metronome of drips from a thousand moss-tips onto the stone pools. And the towers themselves creak. They grow so slowly you shouldn't hear them, but they shift. The peat swells with water and freezes and swells again. You will hear a deep sigh and turn and a whole three-meter tower will have leaned an inch, shedding a skirt of brown leaves.

No one built this. There are no tools. No cut marks. No stacked stones. Everything here is grown or weathered. The rule of this place is that structure must be biological or geological and nothing else. And yet it has zoning. The shortest, youngest cushions — bright green, hard as a pillow — crowd the windward edge where the fog hits first. The tall, old, hollow ones are in the middle, where it's most sheltered. On the leeward side, where the stone stays dryer longer, the towers are skeletal, dead on one side, liverworts colonizing moss that has given up.

In the center of the hamlet there is one that has collapsed. The tower walls fell inward and made a kind of ruined atrium, open to the normal white high-altitude light. The light here is not special; it's clear, flat, honest noonday light above treeline, the kind that makes colors true and shadows short. In that open tower, the interior peat has become soil. In that soil, for this one small place, a few other things grow: a dwarf willow no higher than your ankle but with a trunk as thick as your wrist, a scatter of saxifrage with white star flowers that actually have a faint scent in the cold.

Fauna is rare here because there is so little to eat per cubic foot of wind. But not absent. If you stay still long enough — you will anyway, because the air makes you slow — you'll see the white. Little white moths that live their whole life inside one tower and never leave it. A snow-finch that drops in, lands on a tower top, scolds, and leaves. And under the stone lip at the far edge, where the meltwater disappears for good, there are things moving: alpine salamanders, black as the gneiss, with bellies orange as lichen, migrating from one pool to another with infinite patience.

The strange part is cumulative. If you put your hand into the hollow of a living tower, the air inside is warm. Not metaphorically. Actually warm, a few degrees above freezing. The colony is fermenting itself, composting its own dead. And if you put your ear in there, away from the wind's hooting, you can hear a second sound under the dripping: a very soft, very busy ticking, clicking, shifting. Mites, springtails, things too small to see working the peat, building.

You leave the bowl the same way you came, stepping from stone to stone to avoid crushing anything. From the rim, you look back and it stops looking like a city again and becomes what it is — a scatter of moss hummocks in a shallow pan of ancient stone — and then as the fog begins to pour in again for the afternoon, for a half second, it looks exactly like a city again, with smoke coming out of all its chimneys.
