It is night in the new lowlands, and it has always been night here.

Not the clean night of high desert, but a stacked, vegetated, breathing darkness. The canopy is so complete that even if there were a moon above it, you would not see it. You are at the bottom of a wide, shallow bowl — a valley the size of a drowned town, maybe larger, its rim barely higher than you, low enough that the warm air pools and stays. The land inside the bowl is young.

That is the first thing your hands tell you before your eyes adjust. Everything is sharp.

A few years ago — a few decades at most — this place was not here. Something pushed it up, or it boiled up. Black basalt, still glassy in places, cooled too fast into knives and plates and broken hexagonal columns. A field of fresh, unsanded rock, all edges, all fracture, no softness. The earth hasn't learned to be soil yet. Between the plates are crevasses where a leg could go and not come back. Where two cooling faces met, the rock made a long, keen seam like a jaw. And out of every single seam, something is growing.

There isn't enough water here for what you're seeing. That's the trick. There is no river, no swamp, no standing lake. By any honest measure the whole basin is dry — water 0.8 when it should need 3.0 to support this. But the air is warm — real, soupy, skin-temperature warm — and warm air holds a memory of water, and this valley sweats it out.

Every leaf is a condenser. Every broad, elephant-ear leaf, every hanging tongue of moss, every waxy vine like a thick electrical cable strung from rock to rock, is beaded, streaming, dripping. Condensation runs down in slow, fat pearls that gather at the leaf tips and fall — plink — onto the stone below, onto another leaf below that, onto your shoulders. The ground itself does it too; the black rock is cool just enough under the day's stored heat that at this depth of night it sweats. Touch it and your palm comes away damp and gritty. Follow the drip down to where the rock plates meet, and there, in the narrowest dark, there are pockets — little cupped basins no bigger than a fist, or a skull — holding an inch of perfectly clear, warm rain that never fell. A hundred thousand little wells. And every one of them is occupied.

Because you cannot be alone here. The density is wrong.

The lushness is not a meadow, not trees spaced apart. It is vertical, layered, near-touching, desperate. Pale trunks like wet bone shoot up ten, fifteen feet and then just give up and become ferns. Ground-creepers with leaves as big as doors climb the glass and then climb each other. Air plants the size of dogs hang without roots, just open to the drip. Fungi — not the pale button kind but shelves, red and orange and black, soft and quivering — fill every angle the leaves miss. You cannot extend your arm fully without pushing through three separate living things, and each of those living things is itself covered in a smaller living thing. Moss on leaf on vine on fungus on rock.

And each of those living things is moving, because everything here is eating or being eaten or both, right now.

The sound hits you first, before the smell of rot-sugar-warmth, before you see anything. 2.6 out of 3, deafening in the dark.

It's insects, mostly, but not the polite background chirring you know. These are cicadas the size of your hand, filing the air with a metallic sawing that is so loud it becomes a pressure on your molars. Click-beetles snapping like green firecrackers. Beetles with horns dragging themselves across basalt with a glassy shriek. Under that, a lower chorus — things croaking, barking, coughing from the pocket-pools. Tree frogs? No, larger. Something with lungs that inflates a throat sac until it glows faintly and then releases a BOOM that flutters the nearest leaves. A thousand of them, unsynchronized, so the basin never has silence, only less and more of noise. And above that, rustling — constant, turbulent, bazaar-fast — wings, claws, feet.

The fauna is overwhelming. It has to be, to maintain this much green on this much sharp rock with this little water. It is the water cycle, it is the soil cycle.

The pocket-pools flash — and now you see the weirdness, the low-grade wrongness that makes you realize this is not quite your jungle.

Each pocket-pool has its cloud of green midges, and each midge cloud has its hunting damselflies, iridescent and four-winged, their wings making a white noise that cuts through the sawing. Lizards — fat, slick, translucent in the near dark — dart tongue-first into the clouds. And then something larger comes to eat the lizards. Always.

But the light you are seeing comes from them. Not from the sky. There is no sky here under the canopy. The only light is biological and chemical and faint, but because there is so much life per cubic foot, it sums to a dim, moving dusk. The undersides of the big leaves are dusted with bacteria that glow the yellow-green of a fresh bruise, so that when a leaf shivers it briefly illuminates its neighbors. The fungi pulse amber when touched. The throat-sacs of the boomers give a brief green flare when they boom. The beetles have two cold white headlights. It is not enough to see by, not really. It is enough to be constantly losing your night vision and getting it back.

And the tool-users. No built things — not a nest, not a dam, not a house. Nothing that stays. The basalt is too young and too sharp to hold shape; anything built is torn down by growth within hours. But they use tools.

You hear them in the rustle: troops of something almost monkey, almost raccoon — small primates with long black fingers, eyes huge and amber-reflecting in the bacterial glow. They have no homes. They are passing through continuously, always in motion — turbulent, hyperactive, never sleeping. They travel with a kit: a sharp flake of that fresh basalt, held in one palm, its edge still so keen it parts hair; a stiff twig stripped of leaves; a thorn. They stop at a rotting log — and there are always rotting logs, though you never saw the tree that made them, they rot so fast — and they use the rock to pry, the twig to fish. Grubs as thick as your thumb boil up from inside. They skewer them with the thorn. They dip them in the pocket-pools to wash them.

One stops very close to you. It watches you with that unafraid, irritated look of an animal that has never been hunted effectively because predators get distracted halfway through the chase. It holds its basalt flake up. The flake glitters. Then a beetle as big as a fist slams into its back and it shrieks, drops everything, and both go tumbling, screeching, into the leaves, into the stacked dark, into the choir.

Nothing here lasts more than a night. A leaf unfurls, is eaten half, houses a clutch of eggs, turns black and melts back into slime that drips into a crevice to feed the next leaf. The basalt itself is being dissolved by root-acid even as you watch; the sharp edges are slightly less sharp at dawn than at dusk, but new cracks open with a soft tick as the warm rock settles. There is water everywhere and nowhere. There is green so dense you could chew it. It is warm like a mouth. It is so loud you feel it in your sternum.

You find a relatively flat plate of stone, check it quickly for ants, for centipedes, for the small amber fungi that sting, and you sit. Condensation runs down the back of your neck. A large leaf above you, overloaded with its own dew harvest, gives up and empties its whole load down your back in a cold rush. Near your foot, in a pool the size of a teacup, something with too many legs is living out its entire frantic life.

In a minute, the darkness will have grown over your footprints.
