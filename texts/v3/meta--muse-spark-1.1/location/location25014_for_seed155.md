It is high. High enough that the air has a thinness to it, a coolness that never quite leaves the stone. Not cold enough for snow — just cool, the kind of cool that lives in your lungs and on the back of your neck, that makes your breath visible in the early hours and then again at dusk.

This is very old high ground. Not jagged. Not a young, thrusting mountain range. This is what mountains become when they have finished being mountains. A table, a shield, a blunt dome of basement rock worn down over geologic time until it is almost a plain, but a plain in the sky. The Plateau. You can walk for an hour in any direction and think you are on flat ground until you reach an edge where the world falls away in a single, shallow, wind-scoured step, and ten miles out, another step, and another, like the contour lines of a topographical map made physical. Everything is rounded, buffed smooth, lichen-polished. Glacial erratics the size of apartment blocks sit half-sunk in the turf like knuckles. The bedrock shows through in places — not as cliffs but as long, bald swells of granite, pink-gray, stripped of soil and warm to the touch by noon.

And there is nothing that is not alive on it.

The city that was built here was built correctly for a high, ancient place — low and broad at first, dense, stitched together against the wind. But the vegetation did not come afterwards. It did not *invade*. It is the substrate. The Plateau *is* plant mass. You dig a foot down and you don't hit dirt; you hit root mat, and peat, and root, and root, until the root becomes wood, until the wood becomes the stone underneath. That is what has always been here. The first surveyors cored down three hundred meters and were still in living tissue.

So the city learned.

The City of Wattles, they call it now, because it's woven. The buildings are not covered in green; they *are* green. On this scale — town-sized, valley-sized, open enough that you can stand on one roof and see the next district blurred by distance and mist — you understand the pattern. Giants grew here first. Not trees, exactly, because they never branched right. Columnar trunks, two, three hundred meters across at the base, rising up only forty or fifty meters before they open like a crown, like a mangrove made for the sky. Their bark is black-green and fissured deep enough to walk in. Their leaves are each the size of a dining table, waxy, turned down to shed water.

We live inside them.

We hollowed what was already hollow. We cut doors into cambium walls that bled sweet sap for years and then sealed. We run floors across the interior like shelves in a silo, and light shafts where the crown lets sun through. Between the columns, the smaller growth — the true forest — has been trained, pleached, pruned, grafted over centuries into streets and viaducts and walls. A block of housing might be a single living banyan, its aerial roots thickened into stairwells, its canopy sealed with resin and glass. A factory is a thicket where the trunks were persuaded to grow in a perfect circle, and then roofed with living timber that continues to grow leaves above the smokestacks.

And there are factories, because this is an industrial place, though sparsely settled. That is the strangest part of the census. For an urban build — roads, rail, water towers, switching stations, a whole city-spread — there are so few people. You can walk a boulevard made of interwoven hornbeam branches, a boulevard wide enough for six trams, and see only one person a quarter mile down, their coat flapping. Much space between presences. The city feels like it was built for a larger population and then politely emptied, or like it grew this large while forgetting to make more inhabitants.

The industry remains, and it is loud.

The hollow columns are excellent resonators. Every engine that runs is heard everywhere. The sap mills run day and night right now — low, coughing, belt-driven steam engines that press the fallen crown-leaves for sugar-oil, which they burn to keep the engines going. The sound is a deep, wet *chuck-chuck-chuck* that vibrates up through the soles of your feet. Up in the Crown District, the wind turbines are not built but *grown* — great wooden lattice fans laced with living vines that tighten and loosen to pitch the blades. They moan. At high noon when the wind comes off the northern steppe of air, they all moan at once, a chord that you feel behind the ears. And above it all is the ceaseless rustle, not peaceful, not forest sounds — industrial-scale rustle, the sound of a thousand square meters of table-sized leaves rubbing against each other, creaking like ship hulls.

It is damp. Not wet. There is very little standing water up here. No lakes, no rivers, only where the bark of the great columns channels rain into sudden, temporary waterfalls that crash onto the root-streets and vanish. The dampness is in the air itself. The clouds are low and constant, a normal white daylight that diffuses flatly without shadows. They scrape the Plateau and pass through the crown-leaves and condense. All morning, everything drips. Not rain — just the leaf-tips releasing last night's fog. The air is full of that dripping. It beads on iron railings and on your sleeves. The whole city collects dew in copper gutters and cisterns and tarps, because it is the only reliable water. You can hear it tocking into barrels for hours. Your clothes never quite dry.

Very few animals have adapted to this. That's incorrect — the plants have adapted to so little animal life. Once in a long while you see a high kite, a single raptor far up, its shadow crossing three districts. Sometimes a goat-like thing, shaggy and gray as lichen, will be grazing moss off a roof-bole, and will stare at you for a long time. Mostly we are alone with the plant.

Because the plant is wrong.

That is the breakdown. The weirdness is not that the trees are huge — that's just biology. It's that the architecture does not settle.

You live on the third shelf of Column 17. From your window you can see the water tower made of grafted ash two streets over. On clear days you have counted sixty steps from your door to the mill gate. On foggy days it is eighty. The street is longer when damp. Everyone accepts this. The interiors are worse. The hollow trunks are bigger inside than outside by — well, no one can agree. Surveyors have gone in with string and come out with string that is damp and longer than when it went in. There are rooms that you enter from the north side of a trunk and you exit on the south side of a different trunk a mile away, if you aren't paying attention. The leaves sometimes show you the wrong sky; you look up through the lattice roof of a market street and you see not today's flat white but a deep indigo night full of stars that shouldn't be visible this far north, or a greenish afternoon during which it is raining upward.

If you cut a root, two roots will have always been there. If you graft a new house, you find older graft scars under the bark where your house was going to be, in your handwriting.

The plants are remembering a city that hasn't happened yet and forgetting one that did. Or the Plateau is remembering different versions of itself and trying them on. The engineers have given up on mapping it exactly. They work by precedent now: "This path has taken us to the workshed for the last three weeks, so we will act as if it continues to do so." It usually does.

At evening, the turbines slow. The sap mills idle. The dripping thickens. The light stays normal — that late, cool, even light that makes the green look almost black — until it is suddenly gone. The sparse lights of the town flicker on, electric, strung along living cables of vine that contract when current passes through them. The wind moves through the great leaves and through the avenues and through the hollow trunks and makes a sound like surf, like a city breathing through wooden lungs. And you, standing on the edge of one of those ancient stone steps where the Plateau drops away, can look out and see nothing beyond the city because the city is the forest and the forest is the Plateau, extending past the immediate, block after block of lit crowns fading into the damp air, too large to hold in your head, too empty to feel crowded, too alive to ever be still.
