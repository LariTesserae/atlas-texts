It is high.

Not alpine-high, not death-zone high, but high enough that the air thins and sharpens everything underneath it. A plateau torn up all at once, not so long ago. You can tell because nothing has had time to soften. The basalt is still in black, clean-edged steps where the whole valley sheared upward — a young fault scarp on the western side that rises like a fresh cut, two hundred meters of columnar jointing with the columns still tight together, unweathered, lichen just now finding purchase in the seams. To the east the land drops off in another fresh plane, so the whole place is a table lifted into the sky, a long, lifted trough maybe eight kilometers end to end, open enough to see weather coming from a day away. The sky is enormous and too close.

The light here is brutal. Cool, thin air doesn't filter anything. At noon the sun is white, not yellow, and it makes every edge fluorescent. It hits the basalt and the steel and the leaves and bounces again. You have to squint, even when it's cold.

It is cold. Not freezing — cool the way a springhouse is cool. The sort of cool that lives in shadow. Your breath doesn't fog except in the very early mornings, but you never want to take your jacket off. The sun warms the top of your head while the air keeps your hands numb. 

There isn't much water, and that shouldn't work with everything else you see. There are no lakes. No proper river. The plateau's new rock is too porous, too broken — any rain drains straight down into darkness. But it is not dry. It is damp. Every morning the valley fills with a low cloud that doesn't rain so much as settle. Condensation. It comes off the lowlands and catches on this sudden cold table and precipitates on every surface. By nine a.m. everything drips. By ten, it's gone. The ground is never muddy, but if you wring out a handful of moss you get a cup of water. The only moving water is in pipes.

And the moss — the green — is everywhere, impossibly, chokingly everywhere. Given how little soil there can possibly be, given how recently this rock arrived in the sky, it makes no sense. But this is a cloud-trap and the cloud brings nitrogen, brings spores, and the rock weeps mineral water for two hours a day. So the plateau is furred.

Not forest, exactly. There are no big trees; there hasn't been time, and the wind up here shreds anything that tries to get tall. Instead it's a dense, layered mat two and three meters deep in places, a false forest made of things that act like trees without being trees. Giant mosses that have learned to be woody. Tree ferns that spread like scaffolding. Liverworts the size of dinner plates, overlapping like shingles. Black-stemmed lichens that build trunks you could lean against. All of it packed per volume until you can't put your arm through without touching six different damp, living surfaces. It glows in that white light, almost painful, that luminous green-black of deep shade suddenly lit from above. It holds the dew until midday and then holds the memory of it.

And through it — in it, under it, sometimes as a trellis for it — is the industry.

This was not built to be a town. There are no streets. There are structures, scattered at intervals that look casual until you see the pattern from the scarp: every three hundred meters or so, another one, not quite aligned but following the best contour of the condensation fall. Small factories. Not ruins. Working.

They are low and flat and made of dark iron that has gone the color of the basalt, and glass, great panes of corrugated glass that have gone opaque with biofilm. Most have no walls at all anymore, or they were never given walls — just a roof on steel columns to keep the worst of the sun off, and inside, machines. Looms? Presses? Filters? Something between a textile mill and a paper mill. Long vats where the moss itself is fed in on conveyor belts, macerated, stretched. Racks where sheets of something — cellulose, textile, circuit board, you can't tell where one ends — hang to cure in the cold bright air. Geothermal steam risers, capped in brass, ticking faintly as they vent once an hour. Electric motors the size of houses, turning so slowly you can watch the individual spokes of their flywheels. Every machine is encased in green. Vines have found the oil warmspots. Moss grows an inch thick on the inactive side of every belt.

They are silent. That is the impossible part. An industrial landscape should roar. These don't. The dense vegetable mat is a better anechoic chamber than any engineered foam. The cool air itself seems to damp sound. The machines are old, balanced to perfection, or built on principles that don't need impact. They turn, and press, and weave, and sort, and they do it with a breath-level hum you feel in your molars more than hear. If you stand in the middle of the valley between two mill-houses and close your eyes you hear nothing. The sun hisses faintly on wet leaves. A distant drip. Your own pulse.

There are no workers. Or there are, but rarely. Maybe one person per factory, maybe fewer. A coat hung on a hook still damp. A boot print in the peat that hasn't quite sprung back. A kettle left on a hot pipe, stone cold. A set of hand tools laid out on a felt cloth with moss growing between the handles. Whoever built this scaled production for something that doesn't need to be watched constantly, or for something that grows at its own pace. Great spools of charcoal-grey fabric, kilometers of it, sit wrapped and silent under glass.

There are very few animals. The green is too dense and too uniform to support much else. No birds call. Once in a while a pale moth the size of your hand detaches from a hanging sheet and moves without a sound from one mill to the next. In the vats, small blind white things — shrimp, larvae — live off the runoff. That's it. That is the food chain.

At the far end of the table, where the scarp is still actively weeping condensation in the perpetual shade, you can see where the next mill will be. The basalt has been cut, freshly, the cut still sharp enough to cut paper, the dust still black. Anchor bolts have been sunk. A flywheel already sits there, not yet connected, furred over with a first innocent centimeter of green, waiting years to be needed.

Nothing rushes. The cloud comes. The water condenses and drips. The moss grows a millimeter. The belt turns once. The light is excruciatingly bright and cold and stays that way until night, when it simply goes out.
