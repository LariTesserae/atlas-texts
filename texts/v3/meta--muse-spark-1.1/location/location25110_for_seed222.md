It is high.

Not high like a peak. High like a lifted country. A tableland left up in the air when everything else fell away. The air here is thin and clear and very bright, a white-gold light that has no filter left in it. It hits hard and makes edges. At noon the shadows are short and black underneath everything, like ink spilled straight down. The warmth is not from the air. The air itself is cool when you breathe it. The warmth comes from the light on stone, on skin. It soaks into you.

The ground is old. Thats the first thing you understand. This isn't a raw, broken mountain. This is a place that has been like this for a very long time and has come to an agreement with wind and time. Soft, pale rock - limestone, maybe, or something like it, but harder - laid down in thick beds and then cut back slowly into broad steps and bowls and flat-topped mesas. No sharpness. Everything is rounded, scooped, fluted. In the vertical faces you can see the bedding, and also the holes where harder nodules have fallen out, leaving a pockmarked surface like old bone. The topsoil is almost gone. What is left is a dust of that same pale rock, fine as flour, that holds a footprint for weeks until a wind brushes it out.

There are no rivers here. Not anymore. There are places where rivers were. You can see them as dark, wandering stains across the plain - shallow wadis ten feet deep, twenty feet wide, with undercut banks and beds filled with water-smoothed stones that haven't been moved in a season or a year. In their deepest undercuts there is damp. That's the water of this place. It doesn't flow. It appears.

In the early morning, before the light gets its full weight, a low mist condenses out of the thin air and clings to the cold stone. It beads. It runs in slow, dark fingers down the scoops and hollows and gathers in any cup of rock. By eight it is gone again, evaporated back up, but it leaves behind a damp breath in the stone itself. If you put your hand on the north side of one of the low mesas, the rock is cool and almost wet to the touch. In places where the stone is pitted, tiny pools last until midday — no bigger than a palm — bright mirrors reflecting that savage sky. The rest is dry. Achingly dry. The dampness is intermittent, temporary, but regular enough that the place expects it.

Where that expectation lands, life holds.

Vegetation is sparse and low, as if it is trying not to be seen by the sun. Gray-green cushion plants the size of dinner plates, hard as leather, clinging to the lee of rocks. A kind of grass that grows not as a meadow but as individuals - one sharp tuft here, another twenty feet away, each with a deep taproot that must go down yards into cracks. Near the wadi edges, there is something like sagebrush, but stunted, twisted, its branches polished by dust, its leaves tiny and waxy. On the vertical walls, colonies of black and orange lichen make maps that are centuries old. Nothing shades anything else. There is too much space between.

And yet the place is full of animals.

You don't see them at first because you are looking at ground level. The abundance is up. It lifts off the plateau and holds in the air.

They are gliders, mostly. That's the simplest word. Wide, translucent things with wingspans from a hand to a house, so thin you can see the bright sky through them. They are not birds. No feathers, no flutter. They ride. They ride the thermals coming off the sun-baked stone, hundreds of them at a time, in layers, from just above your head to so high they are specks, all silent. Some have long, trailing tails like kites. Some are almost perfect discs that wobble as they bank. When one passes low, you can see the structure inside its wing - not bone, but a lattice of hollow fibers carrying some darker fluid that catches the light.

They are quiet. Everything here is quiet. The air is too thin to carry sound well, and there is almost no wind most days. Just a faint sigh over the rock lips. The gliders make no call you can hear. Even when their shadows sweep over you in waves, you hear only the softest displacement, a *fuh* like someone turning a page far away. Down on the ground, smaller things move between the cushion plants: six-legged, sand-colored, beetle-like but as big as mice, lifting dew drops with bristled mouths. They are silent too.

There is very little built here. If people live here now, they do not live in towns. Across a valley that is three miles wide and a day's walk long, you might see three structures.

One is a cairn of the pale rock, clearly man-made but weathered until it looks almost natural.

One is a shelter, a half-dome of mud-brick melted back into the mesa it was built against, its doorway empty.

And one is not like that.

Scattered across the plateau at intervals too regular to be natural, there are Threads.

At first you think they are heat shimmer or some flaw in your eye. A vertical line, from the ground up to an impossible height, perfectly straight, absolutely motionless, and so thin it only becomes visible when a glider passes behind it and its edge is refracted. If you walk up to one, and there is always one within sight if you look, you find its foot.

It is a circle of stone, about ten feet across, made darker than the surrounding rock, glassy smooth, as if the ground there was fused. The air above it is cooler. The dew lasts longer there. The lichen will not grow on it. At the exact center, the Thread rises. It has no thickness your fingers can feel; your hand passes through it but with a strange resistance, like pushing through cold honey and static. The light around it bends. Tiny dust motes near it orbit slowly, falling in a perfect, endless spiral, never landing.

There are dozens. Perhaps hundreds across this high country. They do nothing you can understand. They simply hold. They hold position against the planet turning, against wind, against time. You get the feeling they are not built so much as grown, or left. A machine so far beyond a machine that maintenance is not a concept anymore. They just are, like the mesas are.

The animals know them. The small ground things collect under the dark circles for the extra moisture. The gliders use them. You watch a great disc, twenty feet across, approach a Thread high above, tilt its entire body and let the Thread slice through its membrane - and instead of cutting, the Thread sings. Not loud. Under the threshold of hearing, you feel it in your molars. A clear, quiet tone. And the glider is suddenly somewhere else, twenty yards to the side, with no crossing of the space between. It ripples its wing and continues. They are using the Threads as stepping stones, as doors, as a map.

The whole system is active, in motion, but slow and in balance. Light gathers, stone warms, mist condenses, insects drink, gliders ride the heat, gliders jump the Threads, dust orbits, cool air sighs down into the old riverbeds at night. Predator and prey, but done without chase or cry. A shadow passes over the sagebrush, something closes. Silence returns.

If you stay until evening, the light finally softens from white to gold, and the plateau glows. The eroded steps throw long tracks of shadow. The Threads catch the low sun and for a minute become visible, all of them at once across the valley - a great, sparse harp of light lines holding the sky to the stone. You can see just how many there are. You can see that this place is not empty at all.
