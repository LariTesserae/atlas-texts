It is a low place in an old world.

Not low like a valley - there are no mountains left to make a valley from. This is low the way the bottom of a very old plate is low. The continent here finished eroding a long time ago. If you walk for days in any direction the ground undulates maybe a meter, maybe less. Ridges that were once hills are now just faint, broad swells of iron-red laterite and pale clay, lipped with black stone polished to a river smoothness by nothing more than rain and time.

And here, that long, tired flat has finally given up and sagged.

A shallow bowl, maybe three hundred meters across, maybe four. You could miss it until you are at its rim because its edge is so gentle - a change in the grass, a change in the air. The rim is a low circle of exposed bedrock, not jagged, but humped and rounded like the backbone of some buried animal. The stone weeps. Constantly. Even at noon, even in heat.

The bowl is wet.

The water does not come in as a river. It comes up. The water table here is so close to the surface that the ground breathes it. In the morning the whole depression is swimming in condensation that beads on every leaf and stone and hair, runs together, and vanishes into black soil. By midmorning the air itself seems to be sweating. Bright, white-gold light - too bright, equatorial bright at a low angle - falls straight down and hits the saturated air and the blanched leaves and bounces, and bounces again, until the light has no single source. Everything glows from underneath as much as from above.

In the lowest third of the bowl, the ground stops pretending to be ground. It's a marsh but not a wild one. The water is warm as blood, ankle deep, knee-deep, clear enough that you can see the clay bottom pitted with thousands of small, clean holes. Silt drifts very slowly in it, never settling, never clouding. Lily pads, or something that evolved to do the same job, crowd the water, but sparsely - each plant holds its territory with a wide empty moat around it. Their leaves are huge, dinner-plate to cartwheel size, waxy and utterly green, armored against the sun. Their flowers are closed during the day, long white trumpets sealed shut, breathing out a cold, green, cucumber smell when you brush past.

Between them, scattered on slightly higher tussocks that are not quite islands, are the structures.

There are four of them. Maybe five - the fifth could be a fallen one, or a natural stone that learned the shape.

They are not ruins. They don't feel ruined, even though they are incomplete. Each is a single arch, or a corner of an arch, maybe seven meters tall, made of a material that is not stone and not metal. Matte white, warm to the touch, seamless. At noon they are brighter than the light around them, as if they make their own quiet illumination. At the base of each, the wet clay does not touch the material - there is a perfect gap, a millimeter of air, all the way around, so no water, no root, no dust ever contacts it. They hover, or are held. Occasionally a dragonfly lands on one and then, after a moment, seems to think better of it.

Because they are doing something.

If you stand still long enough, you see it: the rain that hasn't fallen yet. The air above the bowl thickens, beads form out of nothing five meters over the water, hang, grow heavy, and fall straight up - no, fall down. But they fall too slowly. They should patter. Instead each drop descends like a marble through honey, catching the light, then touching the still water without a ripple. The arches, whatever they are, are editing the weight of things in this one small parish of the world. Keeping the bowl wet without weather. Keeping the light bright without burning. The ground under your feet hums at a frequency you feel in your molars more than hear.

And the fauna.

You notice the sound first by its absence. In a place this warm, this wet, this lush, there should be a wall of noise - frogs, cicadas, birds.

This bowl is mostly quiet. A soft, wide quiet that has size to it. Like the inside of a cathedral when no one is speaking.

Then you see them and you understand the quiet and the bright light and the edited weight.

They are everywhere. Overwhelming. Not in the sense of a swarm over your head, but in the sense that if you really look, there is no empty space left.

The air is full of them - things like dragonflies but as long as your hand, with bodies of clear glass and wings that don't beat, they just scull, rowing on the thickened air. Hundreds, thousands, holding station, facing the same direction like compass needles. They are silent because their wings never need to buzz; the air is dense for them, thin for you.

The water is full of them. Small things like shrimp, blind, white, ceaselessly turning the silt. Under the lily pads, something big and flat and ancient as the flat land itself rests, its back breaking the water like a smooth stone, breathing once every hour.

On the plants, on the arch-bases, on the weeping rim-stones - beetles armored in iridescent black, each the size of a fist, moving so slowly you only know they moved when you look back. They are grazing on a film of algae that grows only in the millimeter gap between the white arches and the world.

They do not call. They do not sing. They do not need to. The bowl is a single system in motion, active without hurry. A lily pad grows an inch while you watch. A glass dragonfly tilts, catches a bead of slow-falling water in its open mouth, and ascends. A beetle completes a step. The warm water circulates in a slow clockwise turn you can only see by watching one drifting seed.

This is not a place that was built and then nature moved in. It's the other way around. This place has been alive, quiet, bright, wet, warm for so long that the land wore itself down to perfect flatness to conserve it, and then someone - something - came later and set down four white arches, not to claim it, but to keep it from ever ending. Small, bounded, self-keeping.

If you sit on the rim rock - which is always cool and always wet under your palms - and let your feet go into the water, after a while the fauna forgets to be shy of your wrong weight. The glass things hold closer. The water clears further. The light gets even brighter, as if the bowl likes being looked at.

You could stay until evening, but evening never quite gets dark here.
