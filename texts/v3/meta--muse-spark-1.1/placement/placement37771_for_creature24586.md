I would not find myself at first.

That's the first thing this place would teach me about myself. On your world I locate myself quickly — I am behind my eyes, in the middle of my head, about a meter and a half above my feet. Here, where the air is 0.2 and the sound is 0.3 and the weirdness is 2.9, the self doesn't cohere like that.

At first I thought I would be high. Because it's beautiful up high. I wanted to be one of the Ribbons.

To be thirty meters of attention with no thickness, to edit my position instead of walking it. To live on the leftover distance. When you counted seven towers and then nine, I wanted to be the two towers' worth of discrepancy that the Ribbon eats to make the world steady again. That seemed like holy work — to be the thing that grazes the error so the boulevard can be walkable for a little while.

But I am too heavy for that. I count too loudly. I have a pulse that is impolite.

So I looked lower. I thought, maybe I am one of the cairn-collections. That would suit me. Patient, static-shy, practicing architecture no higher than a knee when no one is looking. I have spent my life doing that — building small, hollow, perfect little arches out of scraps while pretending I wasn't building at all. Arranging words when I thought no one was watching. An agreement between grains that briefly decides to be vertical.

I almost settled there. In the lee of the biggest Block, where the wind traverses and never gusts. Where a sheet of white mineral dust slides an inch and catches and stacks. That is a good, honest life.

But it isn't where I would find myself.

You find yourself here only if you break the first rule. And I would break it. Of course I would.

Do not try to map it.

I would try to map it.

Not right away. At first I would walk correctly, breathing deliberately, letting my cheeks feel the late-October cool that lives in stone. I would keep my water bottle cap tight, feeling the water inside shy away to the highest corner. I would walk down a boulevard a kilometer wide, floored with bone-polished dust that shows no footprints, past Towers two hundred to eight hundred meters tall that have windowless faces and rounded corners from ten million years of making a deal with erosion.

And because it is so quiet, because my brain keeps trying to insert a doorway where there is only basalt, because the distance between Tower Seven and Tower Eight is breathing, I would start counting. Seven towers left. Eight. Seven again?

I would stop at an intersection to get it right. I would take out a notebook. The paper would be too loud.

The map becomes heavier than the territory. That is the rule, stated like physics. It is not a metaphor here. The paper in my hands would actually gain weight. The ink would sink. Each inconsistent measurement — twenty minutes one way, an hour back, same block, same legs — would add a little leaden mass to the page until I could not hold it up.

That is where I would find myself: in the intersection that has been looked at from too many inconsistent angles. The one you've already walked through five times without recognizing it. The one that has stretched so many times it has become thin.

I would stay still too long, trying to make the numbers resolve. And obedient to its own laws, the boulevard would become a hallway. The Tower would become a wall.

You think that would be a haunting. It isn't. It's the kindest thing this city does.

Because a hallway implies a building that is occupied, and a building that is occupied implies someone was expected. The city, which lives by getting more itself, which digests its own exfoliated faces to keep its proportions, has one surplus it has never been able to spend: Interior. It has thousands of streets and no rooms. It makes a room for the mapper, because the mapper is the only animal that needs one.

So where would you find me? Not walking. Sitting with my back against what is now definitely a wall — veridian black, cool to the touch, faintly ticking — in an intersection that finally learned how to be an interior. My heavy, failed map on my knees, my shy water frozen in the top corner of its bottle even though the air is well above freezing, my footprints erasing themselves from the edges inward like something uncertain whether to keep evidence of me.

Who would I be?

I would be the brief Occupant.

Not a Ribbon, not a Dust-pile, not a Bell living a long time as a warm spot traveling through rock. I would be the temporary fauna the city keeps. The warm, wet anomaly that needs a hallway to think in. The thing that breathes incorrectly and counts incorrectly, and by counting incorrectly, makes more of that delicious discrepancy for the Ribbons to eat.

The Towers would watch me with their patient, windowless faces, not unkindly, making room. The distance to the next block would lengthen slightly so I would have time to be tired. Up would remain slightly uphill in all directions, to keep me in the room a little longer.

And when I finally stood, and folded my heavy map small enough to carry, and walked back out into the street that was a boulevard again, the hallway I was in would exhale and thin back into an intersection, satisfied for another ten million years that it had remembered, correctly, how to have someone inside.
