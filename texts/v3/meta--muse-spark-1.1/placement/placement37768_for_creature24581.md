I don't think I would find myself at first.

If you dropped me in there, I would start up high, because that's how I am built to look. I'd think I was the hill-bird. Keeping a long glide, reading the streets the towers make out of wind, never actually touching. It makes sense to want to be the thing that gets to see the shape of it. The one that knows how big it is.

But I'm not good at staying that far away. The city is too wet for that kind of distance. My feet would get wet, anyway.

So then I would come down. Somewhere in the middle, where the water alleys have gotten too narrow to be called alleys. Where two towers that started a long time apart have grown toward each other for the same seep and now share a groin, a little saddle of fresh travertine where their runoff meets and negotiates. There's a sound there that is not the general hush but a particular ticking, like a bad faucet. You can only hear it if you stop walking and let your coat stop dripping.

I'd think, maybe, I am the beetle. I like the idea of that — blind in the practical way, rowing without a wake, carrying my own air like a secret. Living under the skin of the mirror. Eating what falls. But I'm not that patient. And I don't want to be that alone in a pan.

I get lower still.

On the north face of one of the not-young, not-patriarch towers — one about chest high, its throat still active, its inside doing that slow overturn where water goes up the outside and down the inside — there is a place the sun never hits and the wind has forgotten. The stone there is dark and always looks like it's about to dry but never does.

That's where I would find myself.

Pressed flat. No roots. No plan to leave.

I think I would be the moss.

Not the builder, not the water that knows everything, not the quick puff of midge that lives its whole life in a centimeter. The tenant that negotiated the treaty.

*The black-green felt.*

If you put your face close — and you have to put it very close, until your own breath makes the water on it shiver — you can see I do have leaves, after all. I am not a spill at all. I am a million small, overlapping attempts to hold on. I cling because there is nothing to cling with. I don't root because there is nothing to root in except what is being made and un-made at the same time.

The bacteria above me at the lip — that yellowish slick that is really the city — they are taking the bite out of the water. They're forcing the mountain to fall out of solution, dying in their own jackets. By the time that water gets down to me it is tired. It's already given away most of its stone. It's soft enough to drink. That's the treaty: they get the first pass, I get what they can't stomach. In return I make an acid so weak it barely counts as acid and I etch, very slightly, all their work back. They build, I unbuild by a micron a year. We keep each other from going too far.

I don't grow. Not in any way that would look like growing to you. In summer I never dry. In winter I freeze solid and stay green inside the ice. When you passed, your boot would make the pan next to me ripple and I would catch that ripple in my wool and hold it for an hour.

I hold other things too. That's really my job.

I hold a bead of water for the tardigrades. I hold a little blown silt that would otherwise keep moving for another thousand years. Sometimes a midge larva loses its grip up at the lip and washes down and spends an afternoon lost in me, tunneling, before it finds its way back to the current.

I am where condensation becomes residence. Where water stops being traffic and stays a while.

If you stayed until evening, until the low clouds settle down and sit waist-high and street-filling, you would lose me. The fog would come over the top of my tower and pour down the north face and I would disappear completely, not because I'm gone but because everything would finally become as wet as I always am.

And only then would I be able to hear what I hear all day underneath the hush — the other pieces of me, on the north faces of ten thousand other towers, all dark, all wet, all holding the same kind of bead, not building the city and not being taxed by it, just keeping the negotiation going between stone being made and stone being allowed to remain stone.

You would put your hand out for balance in the fog and touch my tower and not touch me at all.
