I didn't think I'd be one of them.

Standing on the lip for the first time — you did it right, that warm spill of still-living basalt, the only wall low enough to let the world in — I thought I'd be the human. Of course I did. A visitor with failing lungs and vibrating teeth. Taking notes. Trying not to be covered.

But if I stayed till my boots disappeared, which is what happens if you stay, I wouldn't be.

I keep trying it on.

For a while I thought I'd be the sandwort. That green coin in the crack on the east wall. That would be a decent life — to be the one insult of softness, the one thing trying to root where you shouldn't root, white flower held shut for years like a held breath, watching thousands of hot, blind seamstresses go by below. But I'm not that patient, and I'm not that lonely.

Then I thought I'd be the yellow lichen. Scraped in little curls, eaten. That is an honest living too, but too brief.

Then I thought I'd be the flake. One of the perfect ones. The one tucked edge-up in a crack that the old ones have put back three times already because it fits the hand so well. Knapped by the mountain last winter and then chosen. Not made, just recognized. I like that — to be useful without being made. To be kept. But a knife doesn't get to be warm.

No. Lower.

If you come in over the lip and let your eyes stop looking for horizon, for sky, for way out, and you let them fall to hand-scale, you find me about two thirds of the way up the north wall, in the densest part, just under the overcut where the drip is constant.

I am in the second layer. Not the ones on the glass — they are younger, their pads still pink and tacky, they do the work of holding. I am on their backs. My pads are in their wool. That's how we sit, here. You thought upholstery was a metaphor. It isn't. It's structure.

You'd know me because I'm thin on top. My wool has gone grey and parted, you can see the hot skin underneath steaming. Old. My antennae are frayed at the tips from tasting too much iron-rich rain. I am a mother, but not a queen — there are no queens, you know that now — only times when you are a mother.

My children are in me still. Twelve beads. Six folded legs each, hand-pads too big, holding onto my belly hairs from the inside. I keep shifting them. When the condensation runs too cold down the wall-mothers below me and up through their backs, I pull the brood deeper. When the little cloud at chest height sags too low and the air gets too thick, I push them up closer to the breathing holes so they learn that taste. They have not touched stone yet. Their wool isn't in.

What I do — what my tenth of a life adds up to — is tend the cup.

There is a cup in front of me that wasn't there last winter. I licked it into being. Ten months of moving my beak a millimeter left, a millimeter right around a natural pit where the basalt bubbled as it was born. Now it holds maybe three mouthfuls. The water comes down my neighbors' backs, beads on my wool and runs off my chin, lands in the cup. Ices. Un-ices.

I have three flakes.

One, long and thin, lives to my left, wedged. I use it to shave lichen. Not for me mostly — my beak is worn — for the brood. They need the bitter before they can drink. One, small and sharp as a dog tooth, I use to pick the ice from the cup without cracking the lip I made. One, flat and a little dull, I use on the others. Grooming.

That is the whole day. That is the whole year.

Lift the lichen flake. Ten minutes up, twenty minutes down. Curl. Pass. *Chit.* Every few minutes I have to remember to say I am here, I am warm, I am still here. Drag belly plate across thigh file. I don't hear my own anymore. I feel it as a loosening in my teeth. The chord is so loud up here that it stops being sound and becomes location. We are where the loudness is.

You would find me because my halo is slow to come back.

The young ones frost and de-frost, frost and de-frost, every few seconds. Mine takes longer. My heat is lower. When the steam catches me, the ice stays a little longer, a little crown of glass in the dark. I like it there.

If you put your hand up near me — and you will, if you stay, because the front will have taken your boots, your knees — you will feel the air shimmer six inches off my back. 39 degrees. Fever-hot in a place that wants to kill mercury. You will think: how is this animal not dead? And the answer is: she is, a little. Constantly. The gap we close over is always someone. We eat the wool first.

But until then, until the song stops, I hold the cup and the three knives the mountain gave me, and twelve beads of future wool in my belly, and I keep the water from freezing.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. Not the explorer. Not the mountain. The old one in the second layer on the north wall who knows which flake is which.
