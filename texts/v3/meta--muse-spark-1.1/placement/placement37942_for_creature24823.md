I don't think I'd be on the open slabs.

Not at first, I thought I would be — up top, where you can see forty kilometers and the sun has that clean, thin, merciless clarity. Standing on stone so new it looks poured this morning. I like high places. I like seeing far. But I wouldn't last an hour up there. The wind would take my breath and not give it back, and I have too much of a habit of breathing. Of wasting air on big ideas.

I'd hear it before I decided. The cough. The knock of a horn against a lintel. The ground hollow under my boot.

And then I would crouch. Because you have to.

You learn quick up there that height is not up. Height is down. Safety is down. So I'd find a crack — not the grandest one, not the main street where two crevasse systems cross and it's five stories deep and noisy with doves. I'd find a side vein. One that runs off at a slight wrong angle from the prevailing faults, where the wind has dropped a little grit and a cushion plant has managed to make a fist of itself.

There's a lintel there that isn't quite right. Not failing, but uncomfortable. One slab sitting on another with a little too much lean, listening too hard to gravity. Everyone else steps over it. A child has put a white pebble there to mark it. Means: this one wants to slip in three winters.

I would be sitting next to that stone.

For a while I thought I might be the child who watches the forty-yard grass. Or the dog, round and white and useful by being warm. For a while I thought I might be one of the yak-that-are-not-yaks, patient, flat-backed, turning lichen into heat. That would be an honest life.

But no. I know what my hands are for.

My hands aren't cut yet. My palms are soft. I don't breed or chew or lay eggs on warm rock. What I do is sit and notice how things bear on each other. For a very long time.

I'd be near the bottom, but not at the bottom. Not where the liverwort lives and the fly larvae writhe in permanent damp — that's too wet, too important to disturb. Just above the goats.

There's a level there where the seep makes the basalt sweat. The air is 10 degrees when above is -15 because of fifteen goats breathing shoulder to shoulder below me, and a hearth two families over. It's the warmest place in that crevasse. There's a nook that was left as a nook on purpose — the builders left a gap between walls where the fault jogged. Big enough for one person to sit with knees up, and a clay jar.

I'd be the one who tends the jar.

You'd find me there because I never really left. The others go up in rotation to move the goats to the grass strip, or to trade felt, or, for two days in summer, to run on the upper slabs with their mouths open eating the black cloud of flies and laughing without sound. I would go too, once. I would get sick on insects and joy and too much sky.

But I always come back to the drip.

Tick. The condensation collects on the lintel above, runs along a deliberately cut groove — someone, three hundred years ago, gave the stone a little channel with a copper chisel — and gathers at a lip, and falls. Tick. Into the jar. My job would be to keep the groove clear of dust with a feather, and to listen to whether the tick has changed, because that means the wind has shifted on the windward steps, or a hearth higher up has gone out.

They would leave me alone, mostly, which is its own kind of privacy there. Children would stop and hand me things without speaking because you don't waste air shouting down a crack. A warm piece of meat. A white feather. A flat stone that didn't fit anywhere else.

I'd take the flat stone and turn it over in my hands. My knuckles would have gotten fat. My nails flat. I'd set it against the uncomfortable lintel above my head, not to fix it — fixing is for the builders with bone picks — but to keep it company. To give it one more point of contact. I know fifty words now for how a stone sits, and I use them all silently.

You would find me tucked into the rock like the moss is tucked. Dense, damp, communal. Breathing slow. My blood gone black and thick. My lips a little blue. With a jar of water that is wealth, and three goats below me chewing cud like stones chewing, and a white dove nested on the warm side of my chimney stone, cooing at dusk louder than anything else for kilometers.

Not conquering the plateau. Arguing against its emptiness, in the best way I know how — by staying in its pocket and keeping the water from leaving.
