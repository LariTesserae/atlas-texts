I wouldn't be where I thought I'd be at first.

At first, still thinking like a lowland animal, I kept trying to put myself up high. On the rim where the cloud fails, watching it pour over. On a branch where I could see the whole basin at once — that blue-bruised circle of far rim, the house-sized pans steaming. A lookout. Something with a view.

But there is no lookout here. You try to climb and there is no bark, only sleeves. You try to stand above the mat and the mat takes your boot down to the ankle and holds cold water there. This world doesn't have a top to be on top of. It has depths inside its surfaces.

So I sink.

If you leave me here long enough — and you have to, because moving in this wet heat at this elevation is a drugged wade — I stop trying to see far. The white light comes down heavy and green-tinted, and close things become all you can do. A curtain of clubmoss right in front of your face. Behind it, another. The place has rooms. I find myself in one of them.

Not in the open pan. I thought for a while I would be there, in the clean sun. One of those flat black plate-tadpoles, mouth underneath, vacuuming the skin of the water so it doesn't choke. Boiling and endless when light hits you. A warm-bellied animal that never decided to become a frog. I liked that for an hour — to remain larval on purpose, in hot tea-brown water.

But I'm not that patiently exposed.

I wouldn't be the monkey either. Too much pouring. Or the gunmetal bird that farms rust, snipping bacterial tiles with forceps. Too precise. Or even the mat-caiman lying in the throat of the gully with liverwort rooting right into the bone of its back, waiting so still that ferns stop swinging. I'm not a waiter.

I'm a breather.

I'm down. Below where your boot sighs. Where you thought it was ground and it wasn't — I'm two feet down in the second rule. In the mat.

I would be the white cord.

You pulled it up once. Fungus, root, dead root, living root so braided you can't tell. I would be the part you can't tell. The mycelial fan that grows as fast as those groundsel buds tick open, not building anything, just running. I don't have a center. If you find me in one place, I'm also a foot to the left, and a yard over under the orange seep.

My work is not dramatic. That's why I missed it at first. I take the sugar the short swollen trees make with that cold, air-conditioning leaf, and I take the leaf-confetti the ants walk down — they are not carrying it to a nest, there is no nest that would be an angle, they are feeding me, they are my outside intestine walking around — and I turn it. I wet-rot it. I make it into more mat.

And when the heat comes up from underneath — that other sun, the one you feel in your face ten feet from a mud pool that rolls black — when the sponge approaches saturation, when water and heat both want to stop, I have to do the thing you heard as blood in your ears.

I open.

We all open. Thousands of little valve-holes the ants keep licked clean of moss, each no wider than a bee. The pressure has a word then: *chuff*. At noon we all sigh in unison. That's the pressure-hum you thought was industrial. It is. We are the steam valves of the whole basin.

If you put your hand on me where I surface, between sphagnum hummocks, I would be cold. Like those door-sized leaves. Not because I am keeping heat in, but because I am dumping it. Transpiring for everyone. A radiator made of wet felt.

That's where I would find myself — not at a location, but at a depth. In the sag of the sag, in the lowest part of the old mattress, where the tea-brown water is about to become heat and has to be told not to. I would be spread thin and excessive per cubic foot, like air here. I would be a rock that is also a sponge and a garden and a pipe. I would be none of those separately.

You would not see me move. You would only know I had worked because the water you stepped in yesterday that was ankle-deep is now just damp, and the curtain of clubmoss in front of your face has closed again behind something you didn't quite see, and the whole quiet, busy valley has continued its cycle without noticing you at all — cloud to weep to root to me to chuff to cloud — and I would have been the temporary shape that cycle took to keep from stopping.
