You wouldn't find me up on the rim.

The rim is for the Hill Walkers. That slow, mineral patience. Sixty years to grow your own boulder around your shoulders and then another sixty to forget you grew it. I'm not that patient. And it sees too much. From up there the whole bowl looks like a circuit board left out in the dusk, all those amber lights going on and off. You'd start to think it was for you.

And not down in the middle, either. The middle is too perfect. That's where the big towers are in their best health, in their prime spacing, three hundred meters of sacred no-man's-land between each one, trading fog like monks trading gossip. The steam from one arriving just as the other inhales. Too in-tune. I'd throw off their timing.

At first I thought I'd be one of them. A tower. Obviously. Who doesn't want to be the factory that taught itself how to be a forest? To spend a thousand years learning how to hold your breath until it becomes a building?

But I'm not that good at holding.

Then I thought I'd be a Sipper. A lantern. A piece of paper folded around hot water that forgot it was supposed to fall. Drift and drink and glow and forget to die properly. That light they make — that forge-light — it's not for anyone. It's just what happens when you work too hard to stay alive. I liked that. I've liked that for a long time.

But I'm too heavy for that, too. I would vape myself up six inches and then sink back into the fur with a soft *pffft*. The others would bump me out of the way.

So I kept looking, the way you do in a place with no straight lines. You don't walk straight. You let your eye snag.

And I found where I'd be.

Low. At ankle height of an old tower on the east slope, second or third tier down from the rim where the basalt is still warm enough to sting. Not one of the big, famous twenty-meter ones. One of the ones with a lean to it. One that has repaired itself so many times that one side of its pressure jacket is all scar-rust and crystal, and two of its door-leaves at the top are torn. Not dead. Just not efficient anymore. It cycles a little too fast, like an old pump with a bad valve. *Huhhh...chuff... huhh...chuff...* Always a little out of sync with its neighbors. They compensate for it. Its fog falls short, so the tower below it has to angle its plates a little steeper.

At the base of that one, where its collar lips vent, is a place out of the wind that isn't wind. The hot steam comes out and hits the air and dies instantly and there, in a crack that the tower's own root-heat has pried open, there's a rill.

You said there are no rivers. And there aren't. But here, for about eight meters, there's a lie of a river. A black wrist-wide creek of water that is too hot to freeze and too cold to stay hot. It runs only when the tower sighs. Then it stops. Then it runs again.

It is, for a minute at a time, the only thing in the valley that *moves with purpose.*

That would be me.

Not the tower. Not the grazer. The rill-keeper.

The thing that grows in the steam-shade. I'd be the fringe of that temporary water, that fur that got greedy.

Because the fur down there isn't knee-high black-olive anymore. Right at the lip where the hot gutters out, it has changed its mind. It goes feathery. It puts out what almost looks like fingers, translucent, red at the tip from iron, each one reaching for the next pulse of heat. It lines the rill like eyelashes. And when the steam hits — that ten-second *huhhhh* that smells like blood and kelp and a fresh cut pipe — those little fingers drink so fast they go from dry to beaded to dripping in a breath.

If you got down on your own hands — and your hands *would* be down, because you'd be cold that low, you'd be kneeling to see — you'd see that I'm not one thing. I'm a laminate. Moss on fungus on bacterium that learned to be both, and on us — because "us" is right for the fur — are mites. White, blind mites, big as rice grains, each carrying a perfect bead of world on its back the way the fur itself does, never putting it down.

I would be all that. The wet fringe.

I don't make the weather. I don't count it. I don't glow when I drink. I don't walk.

I just wait in the one place that is never quite the same place twice.

The tower over me sighs, and I swell. The water runs, black and steaming, not from anywhere to anywhere, never reaching the next tower. It goes into me. I hold it. My beads get fat and shine in the dusk, catching the forge-light of a Sipper passing overhead, making that light happen twice. My fingers uncurl a millimeter more.

Then the rill stops. The cold rushes back in. My water goes still and then films and then becomes ice at the edges, and I hold that too. I hold both.

And for five minutes, while the tower is busy filling again, groaning and ticking, I'm the only reservoir for anything in three hundred meters. A Sip comes down and lands not on the tower, but on me, because my water is cooler, easier to keep. It drinks from my fingertips with its needle and I feel the tug. It glows, briefly, right above me, and for those two minutes I'm lit from the inside, every single bead a tiny amber lantern of my own.

Then it leaves. Then the tower breathes again.

That's the cycle I'd want. Not the big one-minute-hand of the whole bowl. The small, frantic, eight-meter one. Fill, hold, give, hold.

Too high to be green, you said. I'm not green. I'm that bruise-black that looks black until the light hits it, and then it's suddenly, shockingly purple underneath.

You'd find me there if you stayed till your zipper beaded and your breath stopped meaning anything separate. Kneeling, not because you'd fallen, but because you'd finally seen something worth being at ankle-height for.
