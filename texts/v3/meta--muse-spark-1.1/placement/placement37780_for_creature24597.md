At first I don't.

If you drop me into that world whole, I am not a body at all. I'm attention suspended in the water column. I come in too fast, too warm.

I think I would be up near the skin, because that's where I know how to be — a person looks down into water. But the skin from below is a ceiling of hammered silver and I don't belong to it. It throws me back.

So I sink.

And it's cold. Not the sharp cold that bites, the thin, even, high-mountain cold that makes everything else take its time. My thoughts get thin and clear with it. I slow down to 0.8.

I think I would be one of the char. That's easy, that's pretty. Hanging in the avenues between houses, turning as one, seeing all the way across town because the water lets you. But I am too nervous for that. I would break the school. I would turn too early.

Not fish, then.

I think I would be a snail. A fist-sized house carrying a garden on my back, scraping a clean line down the flank of a living wall. That slowness I could do. That kind of purpose — one line a day, a line that will be grazed over by weed by morning — I could do for a while. But I look at those scraped paths and I realize I'm not the grazer. I'm already looking at what the graze *means*.

Lower. Slower.

I find myself not in the bright green center where the bong-calls carry and the shrimp clouds are thickest. I find myself at the edge, where the town frays out and the forest is just forest again. Near the outflow lip, where the snowmelt that has come all the way across the tarn finally finds the crack in the basalt bowl and leans out. It's quieter there. A place towns put their old things.

There is a house there that is not being a house. You said some are fallow, left to forget they were houses and remember they were plants. That's where I am.

I am not inside it. I am it.

Or, if not it — then the one who sits with it. It's the same job.

I would be the fallow-tender.

I would be old. Older than the builder you watched with the rope. My back is all the way black. My belly has gone from smoke-grey to a mottled lichen-white. The felt of green that grows on me I no longer let the shrimp pick clean. I let it grow long. It waves. When I finally move, it keeps moving a little after I stop, like kelp does.

My gill frills are not blood-red anymore. They've gone a little pale at the edges. I keep them tucked more. I breathe more with the bags. That is why I like the outflow edge — the water is a fraction more spent there, a fraction less bright, and it reminds my lungs how to work.

My house — my charge — was a boat, once. The last one. Two bull-rushes coaxed together until they met and grafted into a hull, meant to sit *on* the silver skin instead of under it. It didn't work. Or it worked and they stopped wanting it. The rushes kept growing up toward light, then, untended, they buckled and rooted down again. The hull is now a long, low, green-black arch, like a whale's rib half-buried in the woven root floor. Inside it, the light is always sideways.

My job is to keep it from becoming a house again too quickly.

This is a real job. In a place where everything tied eventually becomes part of what ties it, un-tying is a skill. I go along the seam where rope became bark and bark became rope and, with the white tips of my too-long fingers — my nails are not pointed like hers, they've been chewed flat — I worry at it. Not cut. Loosen. Make a little gap where water can get in. Invite shrimp. The shrimp click and pick. The house sighs, a low hoot as water moves through a place that was closed for ten years.

It will take me a season to open a handspan. That's fine.

If I am lucky, while I am holding that seam, a tadpole line will find me. They do. The little black commas come down from the lung rooms upstream, riding the almost-current, and they cling to my green felt for a while because it looks like a safe meniscus. I let them. Gold eyes, all of them, looking up at the silver skin that is so far above them here.

Sometimes when they are with me, I put my head back into the hull-boat and sing.

I don't sing for housekeeping. I'm not saying *I am holding, come hold with me.* My house is not holding. My house is letting go. So I sing the other kind of bong, the one you only hear when the town all sings at once and remembers it is a town. But I sing it alone, and small. I put my mouth into the hollow of the old keel where it is still air-tight enough to resonate, and I push air from my old bags, which hurts, and the whole hull answers, low, lower than the frogs.

*Remember being two plants. Remember being one boat. Remember being a house. Now remember being two plants again.*

Across the water, over the avenues, some other builder answers without meaning to. That is how they know I am still there.

I think that is where you would find me, if you stayed past evening when the light makes colonnades. Not in the way. Not cleaning, not building. Sitting on the threshold of a thing that wanted to float and decided to stay drowned after all, holding a rope that I am teaching how to be weed again.

If you came close and your bubbles hit my curtain, I would not peel off the doorframe. I have been the doorframe too long. I would just open my throat, slow, and close it. And then, because you came all the way to the outflow edge, I would sweep both my arms up — *up and in* — and show you that even a boat that forgot how to be a boat still has a little silver bell of air caught under its ribs, still smells of green and cold iron, and still has room for one more head to breathe.
