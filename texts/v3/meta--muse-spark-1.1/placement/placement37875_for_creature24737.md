I thought I would be the one who comes over the knife-edge on his belly.

That was the first picture. It's the natural one to reach for, because you described a human scale — ninety meters, palm-sized, a room you can see all at once — and you left the three C's of black stone there for us, with the flat altar stone and the greasy butter stave. I would be in the third windbreak, the one closest to the fall, the one that has the best view of the nothing. I would tuck the blue plastic under the cairn and try to light dung that won't light. I would sit for five hours inside the day and then leave because my blood is not good at this.

But I can't stay there. Nothing with that much surface area stays there. The place edits humans out at 4:01 pm when the shadow comes like a blade and the ticking starts. We are built things and we don't last.

So then I thought - the finch. I would be one of the puffed spheres, because I want to be indifferent to the visitor. I want that glassy *pink* note that means only *here* and costs almost nothing. But I can't be the finch either. To be the finch you need blood like a fist. You need to have been born in wool and yak hair two thousand meters lower and carried up one hair at a time, generation after generation, until your song has been burned out of you to save oxygen. I'm too noisy for that. I still want to sing about things.

Then lower. The bee. *Bombus superbus.* That one hurt, because it's close. To live here you have to learn how to freeze without sharding, to put glycerol in your own veins in winter. You have to uncouple your muscles and shiver for twenty minutes in the dark under the moss just to be warm enough to move two flowers over. Rest. Two flowers over. Rest. If a cloud passes you just drop and die again for a while. I admire that economy so much I wanted it. But I don't have that discipline. I would try to do all the flowers.

So you have to go lower still.

You'd find me not in the center of the palm where the postcard view is, but just east of center, where the bowl tilts in. Where the morning weep lasts ten minutes longer because the rock there has a shallow concavity, a spoon inside the palm. The black basalt there is not bare. It's gone.

If you put your cheek down — and at this altitude you do, eventually, put your cheek down because you're dizzy — you'd see me as one thing, but I'm not.

I'm the dome.

Twenty-three centimeters across, tight as a knuckle. *Saxifraga* mostly, with some *Androsace* sewn in at the edge where the agreement got blurry. I am low and tight and domed because that is the only shape that is allowed to persist here. I don't grow up. I grow in.

You'd find me there because I've been there longer than the C's, longer than the scat, longer than the bootprint of the man who tried to make a fire. I'm part of the forty percent that has succeeded in turning rock into ground.

This is what it's like:

At night I am nail-hard. The cold comes down from indigo and presses me flat. I am exactly as cold as the basalt.

At 9:04 am the sun hits the west wall. Light is not heat, except for how black rock takes light and makes it into something else. The rime that formed inside me in the dark goes straight to vapor without ever being water. I feel it as a loosening. My thousand rosettes — each one waxy, each one holding a bead — exhale at once. For about an hour I am a cloud making itself.

Inside me, it is 14 degrees warmer than outside me. My dead underneath — last year's leaves, and the year's before, and the pressed bodies of midges who didn't make the column — are composting, very slowly. It smells like nothing to you. To the beetle who has walked the same square meter his whole life, it smells like a house.

At 11:00 I decide to open. My stars are only a centimeter wide, but each one is a parabola. I aim them. I can't afford scent, but I can afford shape. Light tells me to do this, not warmth. The pink is not for beauty, it's for holding. When *superbus* comes — and she always comes to me because I'm the warmest dome in the spoon — I feel her feet. Her throb goes into my roots, which are not roots but wires holding me to a planet with insufficient air.

She stays maybe eight seconds. That's a long time here.

At 2:30 pm the pikas check me. Not eating me, checking me. One pulls a single dried stem from my edge with a care that would look surgical if you weren't breathing so loud. He will put it on his haypile in the wind-shadow of my north side. We have an arrangement.

At 3:50 pm the light goes thin. I can feel the shadow of the west wall before it gets here, as a lessening of pressure. I close my stars. I don't fold them; I pull them down into the fur. My ovary is already deep where it will not freeze. The little rainbows at ankle height — that cold doing something to light it shouldn't know how to do — pass right through me for a minute and make my frost prismed.

At 4:01 the tick. The freeze. I stop.

I am not a single plant. I said that. The oldest resident told you: the carpet is an agreement. Moss made dirt out of its own dead so I could have something to hold. Lichen painted me orange at the edge so I could hold a little more sun. Bee taught me how long three hours is. Pika taught me how to save.

I am the place teaching itself what living is, using spare parts. Black rock, white sun, green fur, a breath of water that never gets to be a river.

If you come back over the knife-edge tomorrow — and you will, because humans mark what they can't keep — don't step on the dark green-black ink. Step on the black rock. I'm only growing two microns a year, and I'm busy holding heat for someone else.
