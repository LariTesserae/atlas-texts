At first I don't know.

Because here you can't tell quickly if you're stone or skin.

I find myself low, before I find myself anywhere. Low like the rest of it. The heat comes up through me before it comes around me, a steady womb-heat through the soles of my feet, and the water is already there. It has always been there. Around my ankles. Over my feet. I look down and I can't immediately see where my feet stop, because the flow is covered in the sheet of them — black, yellow-backed, clicking — and the sheet is moving over my skin the same way it's moving over the basalt. Testing. Tasting. Deciding I'm not food yet.

Then I shift my weight, ankle soft, the way you have to, and they flow off. And that's how I know I'm one of the people.

I am crouched. Everyone is crouched here, or stooped. Not because we're tired. Because the useful world is between knee and chest height. Above that is just steam and gnats. I am in a place where two houses lean into each other so hard they've become one wall. It's weeping. Of course it's weeping. All walls weep. Warm water beads on the black basalt columns and runs down in clear lines through the yellow bacterial film, and the crabs follow the lines with their mouths.

I have a shell in my hand. Not thought, remembered — my hand knows it before I do. Flat, edge worn smooth. I'm scraping.

That is where I would be. Not in the lane where you can be seen, not on a roof with the toads, not at the rim where the cold seeps come in and the old ones say the air is too dry to breathe. In the small widening where a lane bulges into a pan the size of a sleeping mat. Tea-colored water, skinning over. The pan that feeds three households. My pan. The one I tend.

I was the woman who kept it at the right depth. I didn't decide that. The pan decided.

I am broad. I feel how broad I am because the walls are so close on both sides that my shoulders brush them even when I'm crouched, and they are wet with my wet. My head is shaved clean — my scalp prickles with condensation, and when a gnat lands I feel it immediately because there's no hair to hide in. My feet are the giveaway — splayed, yellow pads cracked like the column joints, white scars from the new rock. I have never owned shoes. My soles know the distance between one hexagonal throat and the next the way you might know floorboards.

I am not young. Not old enough to be deaf yet. The roar is still outside me mostly, not yet inside my bones. The toads in the doorway, two of them, loaf-big, white-bellied, throats blowing. *Wuh. Wuh. Wuh.* In unison, not quite. The clicking roof of crabs. The column behind me hissing steam like a tired animal. The pillar of gnats over the pan itself, rising, rising, turning the dim light brown. It's so loud it makes the water in the pan shiver even when nothing touches it. If I tried to say my name out loud it would be taken straight off and lost.

So I don't.

The child comes. She is on someone else's hip — maybe mine earlier, I can't keep track because children ride whoever is still enough. Her bald head bobs above the gnat cloud. She reaches for me and puts two wet fingers to my throat. Not asking. Checking if I am making sound.

I buzz for her, under her fingers. The little trick we use to cut through the band where the toads live. *Here. Still-here. Good-film-today.*

She nods, presses her own forehead to mine for a second, damp and cool. That's how we talk. In real words, not markers. Bone to bone.

Then I go back to work. That's what I would be here — not a hunter, not a carrier who is brave enough to go to the border for cold water, not one of the hollow ones who sits down in the deep organ throats for quiet. I would be a mender. A groomer. The one who keeps the wall from becoming only crabs again. The one who skims the best bacterial film off the rocks with the shell and presses it into cakes that will go sour by evening and keep us steady. The one who has a copper pin saved and re-saved, tucked into the seam of her gut-skin apron, for peeling crabs and for nothing else.

Because equilibrium needs hands. The basin is new but the town is old, old in the way a tide pool is old — same depth forever, same number of crabs, same number of toads, same number of people, water in and water out. The basalt is still settling, still ticking underneath me, and my job is to put the mud back. Smear, pat, check the leak. Smear, pat, check the leak. The same wall for all of my life.

I don't mind. I was made low so I could stay near it.

I put my palm flat on the weeping wall and feel the whole town through it — other palms on other walls, other pans shivering with the same roar, all the way to the hazy margin where the new stone stops. Warm. Mineral. Wet. Touching everything else.
