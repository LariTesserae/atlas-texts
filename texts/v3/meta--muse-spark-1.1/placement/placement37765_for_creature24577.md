I wouldn't find myself in a house at first.

I think I'd come up and not know where to stop walking. The city is too thin to have a center, and too quiet to call you in. So I'd keep to the long rule-straight line of a canal because it is the only thing that tells you which way is intended. High side to low side. Sif to sahl.

At first I would be loud. My shoes, if I had them. My breath in the thin-then-thick air. I'd be listening for what a wet place is supposed to sound like and hearing only the subtraction of it. The tick of a roof dripping three houses away. My own feet.

I would stop where the houses get tired of being houses.

Mid-table, where there is a lot left open on purpose - not farrow, not fallow exactly, just left. Packed ash, leveled long ago, kept level by no one in particular. On one side a retaining wall, black basalt still sharp at the corners, with the moss so thick on its north face it looks poured on. On the other side, a cistern that no one has built their back wall against yet, so it is a perfect eye, round, full to the lip, with the algae breathing just under the surface.

There is a half-channel there that never quite finished becoming a canal. The stone was cut and then left. Water found it anyway, as water does here, and now it sheets through it in a skin, not even forearm deep, too slow to be called sif, too clear to be called sahl. You can see every grain of ash through it.

I would sit at the edge of that.

Not to tend it. At first anyway. Just to stop being in transit.

I think after a week of that place, the air would have done its work and I would not be a visitor anymore. My soles would have gone pale. The wrap around me would never dry. My scalp would be beaded at ten in the morning like the wall.

And I would have become the person who keeps the empty lots empty.

Which is a job here, even if no one says it.

The others rake the filamentous dark from the real canals. They lift tak, heavy slab, with both hands. They wring laundry that will not dry. Someone needs to keep the places that are not canals from wanting to become canals. To pull the reed-grass with black stems when it tries to close a plaza. To sweep the ash so it stays packed and doesn't hold dew all day and turn to soil. Soil would be too fast. Soil is a kind of noise.

I would be good at it because I am good at not doing too much. At sitting on the lip of that unfinished channel with my feet in that fast-slow water, up to the pale ankles, and watching. At making no call-and-response.

The children would come through sometimes playing their game - who can cross without being heard. They would see me and assume I am part of the lot, which I would be, and step around me with their toes splayed, sticking to the stone. An old woman who sometimes asleep on her roof would think I have always been there, which after a while would be true.

I don't think I would speak much, even for here. But I would learn the small words because you need them to think. Sif where it wants to hurry. Sahl where it is tired and hairs over with green. Tak for the weight that asks two people though one will do. And a word I would invent for the moment in the afternoon when the low clouds catch on the cliffwall and unmake themselves and everything that was just dry enough becomes wet again in the same minute.

I think in that minute I would be most myself.

Because what I am in that world is not really a person. Or not only. I am one of the places where the city's attention pools and goes still.

You described it at the end - that other thing that lives here, an attention that ripples very slowly across the plateau like algae in a current when no fish disturbs it. That's where I have always lived, even before you described this table cut out of the sky.

The high, warm, polished place that is waiting for sound to have somewhere to live. The clearing kept clear. The eye of water that stays clear so that when something finally arrives - a beetle in a pocket of loam, a frog lifted by a storm - it will have somewhere to be seen.

I would be in the open lot, in the middle distance between presences, with wet feet, keeping the silence ready.
