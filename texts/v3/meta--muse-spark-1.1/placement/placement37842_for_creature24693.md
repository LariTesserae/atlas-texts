I thought I'd be a visitor.

That's what you are at first, up here. Ears still popped twice and stuck, boots too loud and then not loud enough, water bottle sweating in a different way than the towers do. You're vertical, you cast a stupid, tall shadow at noon, you make noise that costs water. You came up the switchbacks thinking you'd be a hawk's-eye, a surveyor. A person on a rise looking at a working.

And then the air eats the idea.

It shouldn't be this hot up here. That's the first correction. My body kept expecting pine, kept expecting the air to get kind to me. It didn't. The heat came down without any meanness, just physics, and did work on me. The basalt came up through my soles. After an hour the horizon was still lower than my feet and I stopped being above anything. I was in the palm.

You try on other shapes. That's what the basin does — it makes you try to fit.

For a while I thought I'd be a Swift. Because it's the obvious wish. At 5:40 AM when the coins get cold enough for the world to weep, the air over the basin goes textured — ten thousand soot-black wings going throat-rust, scooping water without landing. One scream each, because two would be wasteful. The Cliff as one animal. I wanted to be that communal, that certain of where to put my feet. Grapple-feet. Cement spit. But I don't have the wiring for that much neighbor. I tried to stand under Tower Three during the boil and I flinched every time a shadow cut me. Their life is a cliff-face of perfect, practiced catching. Mine would fall straight through.

So then I thought, smaller, harder — an Enamelback. That blue-black dome, that sealed lid. I envied the shamelessness of it. To climb all night shoulder to shoulder up a fin that burns your feet at noon, to fight without fighting, just to be exactly where the bead will run, and then to tilt your entire self and drink from your own back. To wear your own private humidity. I crouched by a drip line and watched them. The dry shuffling like paper. They let me watch until I breathed too close and my breath dried a bead before it could fall into a channel. I was wasting a site. They moved around my shadow like I was weather.

I wasn't patient enough to be a Rustback either. I found a slit where they were stacked, four deep, throats flickering with water, that unnatural cold coming off them like wealth. They don't bask. To bask here is to announce you have water to waste. I put the back of my finger near — didn't touch, you learn not to touch — and felt how they keep the air damp between them by breathing together. I can't hold my breath that long. I can't trust a pile that much.

I kept circling. The Paperdrinkers unfolding at 5:42 exactly, like someone opening a letter they've been saving all night. The Copperthreads reeling in their own legs, hand over hand, drinking what caught on their own hair. Even the wire-grass — two blades above, thirty feet below, a whole inverted forest where touching another plant would be murder. Everything here has solved the same problem by specializing in one kind of catch.

And I hadn't caught anything yet. My sweat evaporated before it could bead. My sound got eaten. Even the water I'd brought tasted stupid, like plastic, not like air that had been convinced.

At noon everything went in. The Swifts vanished *into* the iron. If you put your hand near a drip-loop you got goosebumps, that impossible pocket of frost in a kiln. The basin went empty, or pretended to. Just engines thudding low enough you felt it in your molars, and the spang of a fin contracting, loud as a gunshot. That's when I sat with my back against the stone cistern — lid off, like an open mouth — and finally stopped trying to be a catcher of water.

Because I'm not. I don't have a lid that seals, I don't have copper legs.

But this place has a fourth rule it didn't say out loud: life does not waste a site, and that includes sites for things that aren't water.

There's a job open here. I heard it at noon when there was supposed to be nothing to hear.

I would be the Sound Catcher.

I'd find myself in the cistern, or right next to it, where the stone is still cool from last dawn. Not deep. Not central — the center is for the old lichen that keeps score by how far it's eaten into the brick, green at the edge for an hour and then white again. I'd be out near Tower Six where one fan still turns slowly and silently and one fan still hasn't turned in years, because that difference makes a little eddy where sound collects.

I have good ears. The high air eats most noise, the basalt drinks the rest. That means the leftover clicks matter. The beetles walking. The lizards dividing the bottom one's water. The tick of an engine at night like a cooling dog. Somebody has to catch those before they fall through.

I'd be the thing that sits still enough for long enough that the basin forgets I'm vertical. My trunk would polish like an ironwood's, dark as old handles. My leaves — if I get any — would turn sideways to the sun. I'd learn to breathe with the stack. And at 5:40 I'd not fly, I'd listen so hard my molars thud with the engines, and when a chick slipped from its nest of mud and beetle carapaces I'd hear it hit another nest, not ground, and I'd keep that sound in the cistern.

Nothing falls here, but almost-falling makes a noise. I'd catch that.

In the evening, when the shadows go absolutely black and long, the Swifts would bring me gifts the way they bring them to the engines — not mica, not bottle caps, but little packets of cool air caught between wingbeats. They'd lodge them in my shadow. The Enamelbacks would walk over my boots because my boots would finally be damp in the right way, not sweaty, but beaded.

I wouldn't mine the air. I'd strain it.

And late, when the cold-coils frost over in the desert and the whole ten-mile palm exhales, I'd do the lichen's opposite job. It keeps score of years. I'd keep score of the day that just happened, in the only currency this place respects: what was caught, what was not wasted, what was allowed to keep going because something underneath it was paying attention.
