At first I thought I would be high up.

That's the instinct, isn't it? If you're given a table that has weather, that lives inside its own cloud — you want to be the thing that can see furthest in a place where you can only see three hundred yards. So at first I thought I would be one of the stained-glass birds, hanging locked-wing in a humidity thermal, doing nothing. Letting the fog come to me.

But I wouldn't. I am not good at doing nothing. And those birds don't do nothing either — they have their mouths open. They are always sieving. Too hungry, too alert.

Then I thought I would be the grazer. The Mat-Swimmer. That would suit me, I thought, the slowness, the way it doesn't climb but goes *through*, eating the wall in front of it and leaving an architecture behind it made of its own passing. I liked the idea of my body being a road other people use for years.

But I'm not big enough to leave that kind of tunnel. And I don't eat what's in front of me. I take it and keep it and make something else out of it.

I kept sinking.

Not down — there is no down here. Down is ten feet of living sponge before you hit anything like ground. I mean sinking through the layers of the place until something recognized me.

You wouldn't find me on top of the Mat. Not walking. I would not be a visitor, with boots for frogs to climb onto. A visitor is a temporary dry place. I am not dry.

You would find me low. Where the light never reaches directly. About sixty feet down in the hundred-foot thickness, where the air is still 82 degrees but it stops moving. Where sound has been completely swallowed and even the *ploop* of the cup-lakes can't reach.

I am in a place where someone else already made a tunnel long ago.

A Mat-Swimmer passed here maybe twenty years ago. The tunnel is still open but closing. The walls are not leaf anymore, they're a plaster of dung and rootlets and tiny thread-fungi that have learned to behave like mortar. The light comes only as a memory — that faint milk-green leaking down leaf-to-leaf from the lamp leaves.

Here is where I am.

I am a lamp leaf. Not the bright sister at the top of the canopy that actually catches the sun. I am the one below.

Kidney-shaped, thick as your wrist, hanging in empty, damp air inside the old grazer tunnel. My skin is translucent, swollen tight with a clear jelly that took me seven years to make. I don't catch light. I was never meant to. My sister catches it for me, fifty feet above, broad as a door, and sends the sugar down through a cord of white root that runs along the tunnel roof like an extension cord left by a previous tenant.

I take it and I convert it. Slowly. Very inefficiently, on purpose. I turn it into a different kind of light and hold it.

For most of the day I do nothing that looks like anything. Water beads on me but does not wet me in the right way — I'm not a wax-leaf, I'm not repelling it, I'm just... full. The glass frogs use me as a road. They are amber and clear and I can see each one has eaten about four gnats. They walk across my face with those impossible wide toes. The bearer beetles come by too, and when they pass, their little white orchid brushes my edge and I get a smell of limestone for one day.

That's my work until true dark.

True dark here isn't night. It's just when the white fog above finally gets tired and goes slate-grey for an hour. The sun-thought behind the cloud finally leaves.

Then I let go.

I don't flash. Nothing here flashes. I start to glow — phosphor-pale, milk-green, just bright enough that you can see the veins of the leaf above me. I release all the noon I was given. The whole tunnel breathes light. And for an hour, everything that was waiting comes.

The mid-air Striders step down from their droplet chains to drink from my light — not my water, my light — they think it's prey. Gnats come. Then the amber frogs go opaque to hold heat and sit around my rim with their throats pulsing. The bearer beetles stop climbing and just park, and their meadows exhale.

I am not bright enough to see by, not really. But I am bright enough to be found by.

This is where you'd find me. Inside an old road that is healing itself. Storing something I didn't collect from a place I've never been, and giving it back slowly when everything is dimmest, so that the place can see itself.

If you stayed the whole hour, until my jelly thins and clears again, you'd see that I'm not alone. In the back of the tunnel where the Mat is wettest, there's a small basalt pillar that shouldn't be there. The Swimmers go around it. On it, liverwort so thick you can't see stone. And in front of it, in a pan of weeping limestone that the Mat has never managed to cover, a dozen blind white shrimp circle a little brain of crystal and root. It pulses once while you're there.

They tend it. I light it.
