I thought at first I'd be up.

On the roof of the Airhouse, in the airlock after a lean. Where you can see it. That's where you'd want to be if you were human — still submerged in the weather but finally with your head actually in air, water sluicing off your shoulders, steam coming off the canvas suit that never dries. Watching the whole round luminous world thrash and reform itself. Watching the thin white line on every horizon where your whole sea decides to become a waterfall.

I thought that for a minute because I like to see.

But I'm not there long. The wind is too clean up there and I don't like being that open. The Frame groans under my boots and the hose is still tugging at my back and I can feel the sea wanting the weight of me back. So down I go again.

And then I thought I'd be a diver. In the alley. Of course I'd be a diver.

You'd find me halfway down a cut-line, one glove tangled in bottle-brush weed, the other on a stalled chainsaw that's choked with kelp jelly. My brass helmet is leaking at the left seal, always, a cold needle down my neck. Inside it's just my breath, too loud. Fogging the three little windows. Outside it's everything at once — the boom of the canopy when it collides with itself, the shriek of terns knifing in from above that I can't even hear until I feel it through my teeth, the Lister thump coming down the yellow ribbed hose that is my entire life. 

You'd find me where the trout wall comes. Because that's the moment you remember you're not alone down here.

It comes down the kelp alley like a single decision made of ten thousand backs, black-silver, never stopping, and it has to decide what to do with this awkward yellow thing that has an air hose. For a second I am in it, and it is not water, it is fish, shoulder to shoulder, eye to eye, slime on my gloves, and then it parts around the hose, around my helmet, around the old holdfast I've clipped to, and zips itself shut again. No malice. Just physics.

I liked being that diver for a long time. The one who knows how to press both palms to his helmet and then out toward the edge. *The sea is leaning. Hold.*

But if I'm honest, I don't hold. I am what is held onto.

Let it get lower. Past the divers. Past the trout that have already forgotten me.

The light here is different. By the time the high sun gets through fifty meters of wind-chopped, silt-blooming, kelp-filtered water and hits the floor, it's not light anymore. It's a net. White-gold, always moving, thrown over ancient stone that was polished smooth before there were eyes to see polish. Basalt and quartzite, black as river glass, pitted with potholes big enough to park a truck in. And everything is scraping.

You'd find me on the bottom, in the deepest scour where the Frame's cables disappear into a bolt-hole that has rust in it older than my grandfather.

I'm not tall. I'm not fast. I'm the wrong shape to ever fall off the world.

I'm a crawler.

Six stubby, clawed legs dug into striations left by a glacier that died before this sea learned how to be a sea. A back of chipped armor that is actually chipped — basalt dust, moss-animals, white filter weed growing on me like lichen because I move so slow they think I'm rock. A flat head like a shovel. A mouth on my chin that never stops working.

*shhhhk. shhhhk. shhhhk.*

That's my sound, down through the soles of the diver's lead boots, through his teeth. I'm eating. Biofilm and the colonial not-moss that glows faintly when I bruise it. It takes me all morning to clean a square meter of primeval floor. I will do it again tomorrow, because the storm tonight will lean the whole sea toward the rim and silt it over again.

I don't care about the kelp booming. I don't care about the seal-things moaning like cows in the trees. I don't care about the Pulpmill rendering oil or the terns stealing offal or the glass shrimp snowing down like static. 

When the dark line comes from beyond the canopy — no thunder, just a sudden white-toothed pressure — and the entire Table Sea decides to try to drain itself off a cliff in one go, when even the iron-fisted kelp creaks like a ship about to break, when the diver's yellow hose goes singing taut and he wraps both arms around the nearest thing that will never move, he wraps them around me.

Not me, exactly. Around the holdfast next to me. Around the same rock I'm bolted to.

He holds on for his life. I just keep my claws in. I have always kept my claws in.

After, when the sea rights itself and the silt blooms and settles and the canopy sways back to false noon and the trout reform their wall, he'll look down — that brass head wobbling — and see me exactly where I was, still scraping. He will lift one heavy boot very carefully over my back so he doesn't knock me loose. Because if I'm knocked loose I will roll. And if I roll, I won't stop until the thin shining place where water goes skinny over black stone and falls for two days into cloud.

He knows. So he steps over.

That's where I am. On the bottom of a sea in the sky, under a forest that never stops clapping, doing one small, endless, necessary job, holding on tighter than anything else up here holds on to anything.

If you come looking, look down. I'm the bit of floor that is briefly not floor.
