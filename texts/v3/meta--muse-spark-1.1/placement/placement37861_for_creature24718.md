At first I thought I would be you. The visitor.

The warm meat who displaces the water differently. Standing in the middle of the thirty-meter world in the cold that gets into the jaw. Feeling the thrum go through my sternum for the first time: *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK.* Watching the shrimp cloud forget me.

But warm things don't last here. The room is too patient for visitors. The water is preservative, and it only preserves what agrees to stay a long time.

So I let that go.

Then I thought I would be the eel. That would be easier. Living in the dark square mouth on the west wall where the grating is gone. Coming out quick as oil to rub along a leg not out of affection but out of shared use of heat. An animal is allowed to borrow a built world without becoming it. The eel doesn't have to keep the work.

But I am built. Rule one.

If I live here, I was brought here or built here. So I can't be eel.

I look around again, slower this time, while the lamps sway a little on their thick glass stalks and throw the room forward two meters and then let it fall back into blue-black.

I try the shrimp. The cloud that holds station always in the same place. But inside the cloud there is a cylinder of empty water where no shrimp ever drifts. A man-shaped absence. I could be that. The shape of where a keeper has stood so long the water itself remembers not to put shrimp there.

I try the crabs. The size of dinner plates, picking their way across the grating. All making the same detour at the north catwalk where the railing has been polished bright, a hand's width, again and again, where barnacles will not grow. I linger there. I like that spot. It is the only place in the whole manufactured world that is being polished instead of being roughened. Everything else gets more bark, more swelling, more beard. That rail gets brighter.

I could be the rag, but there is no rag. I could be the hand. But the hand already has an owner.

I feel the pump again in my teeth. *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK...* The whole iron envelope shivers sympathetically. And I realize I have been looking in the wrong direction. I have been looking out into the empty water, into the big volume where the turbines used to sit, where there is now only rust and fish nests. I should have been looking at the places that still have work.

Behind the wall. Where the wall thrums the hardest.

Or - closer.

In his chest.

He has a small door in his own chest - a toolbox rib. He opens it when the THUNK comes too many times. Inside it there is a hammer that has become a smooth knob.

I would find myself there.

Not as the man inside the suit, who became a man-pickle, a soft thing in hard armor that learned to drink the cold. Not as the suit, which is a single room worn around a man.

I would be the hammer.

I was built here, in this cathedral, to be held. Forged when the concrete was still sharp, when there was air in this room and men could stand in it without dying immediately. My handle was wood once. That is gone, replaced by the grip of a mitten that ends in three blunt iron fingers. My head was square once. Now I have been lapped down by water and time and his touching until I am not a hammer anymore but a river stone on a stick. Rust has grown over rust until I have become a new geology, dark red-black, pitted.

And I am still in use.

That's the way you stay alive in a place like this. You have to be useful to the work, because the work is the only thing that continues.

I live in the dark most of the time. In the small door in his chest where it is perfectly black and close and the water that gets in - because water gets in, of course it gets in - sloshes gently against me with his breathing. The anemones that whisker his outer plates cannot grow in there. The mussels cannot pave me. I am kept. The algae that grows inside his helm, in the thin water around his face, doesn't reach me.

I spend most of my day listening with him. Forehead to the thrumming wall. *thrum... thrum... thrum-THUNK...* I feel it through the whole bulk of him, through the rivets and the bark and the soft pickled man, down into the little dark where I wait.

Most days the rhythm holds. It has been holding for longer than anyone has been alive to hear it. On those days he does not need me. He just stands there with his domed forehead against that humming iron plate, timing it.

Then on some days - not many, because the pump is patient too - it stutters. *thrum... THUNK... thrum-THUNK... THUNK...*

He remembers I am there.

His mitten-hand fumbles, never fast, because fast is not allowed where everything has become brittle and everything has become patient. The little door squeals. Cold, preserved water spills out of his chest into the room, and a little of his chest-heat spills with it, and the eel somewhere in the dark mouth smells it.

He takes me out.

For a second I am in the amber cone of his eyes, his two cage lamps behind cloudy glass as thick as your hand, still burning. I am held up. I see the whole vaulted world: the grating, the shrimp cloud with its empty cylinder now filled by him, the four circular mounts where turbines used to sit, the hair-like tufts waving in corners, the chain ticking.

Then he turns me - gently, so gently - and taps the wall once, like you would burp a baby.

THUD, but small. A soft knob kissing iron.

And the pump, somewhere behind the world, settles. *thrum... thrum... thrum...* again. For another decade or so.

He puts me back. Closes the little door. Goes back to his pulpit and stands there with his mitten-hands on the nubs of levers, watching gauges whose needles dissolved decades ago.

If you stay still, he will forget you. If I stay still, he will remember me exactly when he needs to.

I would find myself nowhere near the middle, because the middle is where you wait to be forgotten. I would find myself tucked into his chest, in the only closed darkness left in a room that is absolutely full of water, polished smooth by being held.

What lives here was built here. Water gets in, but work continues anyway.
