At first I think I'd be on the rim.

It seems like the honest place to start. Not yet inside the cup, but looking down into it. Crouched on that sharp black glass where it sheared last week in geological time, with the wind still trying to learn the shape of the fracture. From up there the whole world is one room and you can see the plan at once — the twelve or fifteen roofs, the precise circles of bare gravel around each moss-tower, the stone paths like sutures. It would be very human to stand there and want to map it, to make it make sense before I go down.

But I know if I stood up straight up there I'd be tall, and the air would be too loud. I don't belong on the rim. The rim is for the days when the roof lifts.

So then I am down in it, because you have to be. You don't get to watch this place. You get wet by it.

And for a while I try on being one of the people. I want to be the woman who knows her three towers by the way their fronds catch the fog. I want to have hands that have gone wide and soft at the tips from picking, from packing black compost with a bone spoon. I want to know the tongue-click for *north drain blocked* and the palm-on-basalt for *be still, the rock is thinking*.

But I'd be bad at it. I carry my weather wrong. I would slip on the flat stones. I would wring my cloak too hard and waste the water. I would try to speak out loud and have the moss eat my words ten feet out. The people here have learned to pay a kind of attention that is continuous, low, and total — not the sharp, pointing kind of attention, but the kind that is just staying with a thing while it swells and drips. I have not unlearned enough to do that yet with a whole life at stake.

So maybe I'm smaller. The bowl allows smaller.

I could be a moth. For a day, that's easy. Hatch out of a neat round hole chewed in rust-red cap, all that felted wing to silence myself with. Fall into someone's cup. Get breathed in. It would be a good day — a full day, a complete loop from tower to air to beetle to dust — but it's too brief. I would barely have time to realize I was anywhere, and this place requires time. It grows a millimeter a day. It settles with a tick.

I could be the beetle. I like her work. The glass legs, the absolute deliberation, each step placed an inch from the last one's, never touching the living edge. Cleaning up after everyone without praise or disgust. I tried that for a few steps in my mind, picking my way over the black gravel that is supposed to be bare but is always and again covered, and it was beautiful, it was honest — but I am not here to clean up after the world. I'm here to keep it going.

I try the Browser then, because it seemed the most like prayer. Six limbs, slow enough that a minute hand beats you. Moss seeded into your own back so you can never tell where your building ends and your body starts. Just walking a tower for a week, scraping, not biting, pruning so it doesn't capsize under its own lushness. I stayed in that body for a while. I liked the eyeless face, the soft plates. But Browsers don't collect. They don't pass it on. They just tend and walk and when they die they are carried to the rim and given to the wind.

I keep dripping down into darker, wetter pockets.

Under the fronds. Not on top where the sun is a whiter idea, but in that dimmest hollow under the cap where air never moves. Where everything is close and green-black and smells of mineral and skin.

You said there are five or six Listeners under each tower, and I thought they were the real citizens. And I think that's where I find myself.

I would be one of them. Not a person at all.

Tucked up under the cap of the mother tower in the plaza — the one that was hollowed while still living, the one that holds twelve people knee to knee when they hum. No, not even that one. That's too important. I'm in the second tower north of the plaza, the one that leans a little because the crack opened last winter. The one the woman with the broad fingertips tends.

I am a pouch. A fist of wet leather. No face. When I'm empty I hang flat and veined with that faint foxfire light, fizzing, pulling condensation out of nothing, out of the breath that pours over the rim every forty minutes. I don't have a mouth you can see. I just get cold. Colder than the air around me. That's my talent — to be a little colder, so water wants me.

Then I swell. I go taut and opaque and heavy. The light in me goes out. And then I remember the seam at the bottom that wasn't there before, and I open it.

Drop.

One clear drop, knuckle-fat, exactly onto the growth crown. Enough to keep a tree alive on bare rock. Enough for a woman to catch in a bone cup and drink when she thinks no one is looking.

Drop.

I do it all day. Fog, swell, drop. Fog, swell, drop. When the black mold comes the woman wipes it off with her thumb. It tickles. When the moths blunder into me I don't even shiver. They are too soft to matter.

I am not tended the way a person is tended. I am tended the way a well is tended — checked, kept clear, needed.

I like that this place has a word for bowl that also means held. Because that's what I am. Held. I am held up by hooked hairs to the underside of a leaf the size of a table, in a cup the size of a cathedral nave, at the top of a world that broke open last week and hasn't finished settling.

When they all come at gray-gray and press their backs to the inside of my tree and hum at the resonant frequency of the rock, I feel it through my skin, through my single drop of water waiting to fall. I tighten a little, and let go.
