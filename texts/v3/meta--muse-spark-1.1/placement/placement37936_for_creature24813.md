I don't think I would be down on the floor.

Not at first. I know myself. If I fell in — and everyone here fell in, the story says — I would not go to the bottom where the sheet-water braids and you have to Bail every hour or drown in your doorway. I would stop halfway.

There's a place about two thirds of the way down where the basalt columns bow in, just a little, like a throat. The ladders have to go around it. The wood there is never quite sound because the seep is worst there - the rock sweats onto the roof-thatch onto the walkway onto you. No one wants to build under that drip. So they don't. They built around it, and it left a gap.

A pocket. Not a house, because a house implies you chose to keep water out. This is just the place where two families' walls didn't quite meet and a third family's floor sagged, and it made a shallow wedge about chest-high and deep enough to sit with your knees to your chest. From the rim, you'd think it was shadow. From below, you'd think it was nothing, just more wet planks.

I would be there.

At first I thought I would be on the rim-birches, one of those stunted ones with white roots, because I like to watch. Or one of the crows who knows enough to come down and steal and leave again. But no. Those things don't have to work. And the law of the Cut is simple: if you are alive, you are working. If I tried to be the birch I would die, if I tried to be the crow I would be shouted at by twenty mouths until I left.

So I would be in the Cut, and I would have a job that looked like not working.

I would be Wedge, but not the kind that collects flakes. The other kind.

Every few days, without sound, a new hairline opens. You can't hear it. The only way you know is the water changes its mind. The bead that was falling straight starts sidling. A film that was dull goes bright because it's thinner.

Someone has to be still enough to notice that.

So I would find myself tucked in that gap where the water is always coming, with my palm flat on the cold rock. My hand would be like theirs - not bog-hands, work-hands. Translucent at the knuckles, nails filed to nothing by stone, the second joint swollen from cold. My hair would be cut short with the scraping stone. Every morning I would scrape behind my ears where the white fungus tries to live, scritch-scritch, like everyone else.

But for the rest of the day, I would sit.

The mothers would climb past me with their paste-chewed onions, going up to the nests where the air moves. The bail-chain would clank past me, empty up, full down. A kid would stop and watch me and I would put a finger to my mouth - not to be cruel, but because talk makes vapor and vapor makes more water on your ceiling and I need the air around my crack to be still.

And I would watch a single drop try to climb.

When it decides, when pressure equalizes and it runs sideways and then, impossibly, up for six inches around a bulge in the young rock - that is when I would tap twice on the walkway plank with my knuckle. A low, pointed sound that carries without making breath.

Two taps: *crack opening.*

Then the other Wedges would come, with their quick hands, collecting the hand-big and fingernail-big flakes as they pop, and shimming them under some cousin's sagging post before the wall can close an inch.

I would not collect. I would keep my palm there a little longer, feeling the rock learning how heavy it is. Feeling it weep.

At night I wouldn't go down to the hammocks strung bow-tight. It's too low, too wet for breathing. I'd stay up in that wedge. The strip of sky would go from pewter to deep blue. The sheet-water below would tick like a clock that is not measuring time but bailing it. And I would open my little shutter made of four sticks and a skin — not to check for rain, like the others. I would leave it open a second too long, on purpose, letting heat out, making a little cloud.

Because I want to see it condense on the underside of the plank above me, bead, and start its journey down again.
