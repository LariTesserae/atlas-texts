At first I want to say high. Of course.

If you ask fast, you say you'd be a crow. Up at the gantry forest on Terrace 90 where the cloud physically catches and tears like cloth on rust. Where the light is 2.6 bright and brutal enough to etch your eyes and the whole valley is a blue smudge below you. Lock your wings and not have to beat them. Circle all day in the heat that pours off the tea-stained concrete. Steal screws and hide them for no reason. That feels like freedom.

But nothing that lives here moves fast. 1.1 slow. If you live fast you have to leave, and nothing leaves this place.

So no. Not a crow. Crows are too hungry, too loud. You'd be tired in a week of that wind.

Then I think, low. Down in the agriculture of the first twenty terraces where the mountain is widest and the rice is standing in its shallow compost of concrete dust and dead leaf. Where banana leaves slap against corrugated walls, big as doors. Where the air is thickest with that 1.8 loudness — fans groaning in your molars, cicadas whining, geckos clicking, water ticking on metal. You could be barefoot in the silt. That would be kind. But it's too tended down there. Too much weeding. Too many hands have already learned every crack.

The longer I sit with it — on one of those I-beam benches someone re-welded and planted with creeping thyme on Terrace 47, where the shadow of the terrace above you moves at the speed of a minute hand — the more I realize I wouldn't be on an edge at all.

You would find me *in*.

Not on top of a terrace, not looking out. In the thickness of one.

Mid-mountain. Say, 52 or 54. Where it is warm when it should be killing cold. 2.8 high, but the concrete holds the day's sun and sighs it back at you all night through a million little vent mouths. Where every morning the condensation comes down in black trickles and bolt-hole seeps and you can hear the whole mass sweating.

I would be in the depth where the factory roof is still a roof, low and ribbed, and the next terrace up is the weather. Where the light is filtered green through ten meters of hanging moss and fern curtains, so the brutal high-altitude brightness finally becomes bearable. Where there is never any bare surface. 2.2 dense. Root, wire, pipe, vine, ant-trail, all running parallel.

I think I would be the one who listens to the water.

Not the Keeper of the Screw — she is too important, she has to walk the length of that thirty-foot iron wheel turning a centimeter a minute and oil it with pressed nut oil. Not the child who clears fern roots from the weep holes, though I love his work.

I would be the tapper.

Because sound carries weird here. If you shout, the damp air and the fan-hum eat it. But if you tap on the lip of the aqueduct — those hand-deep rivulets that circle the mountain forever downward, carrying blind white fish that arrived as eggs on a crow's foot — the water carries it. Drip answers drip.

There's a code that grew up by itself over a hundred years. Not words. Just: blockage three up. new fruit on the east face. old man died on 51, bring the fungi. Crow took my hat. It travels down-slope all day at the speed of seeping water.

I would be good at that because I am patient. I would be lean and tea-stained the way people here get after generations in held heat, wearing a huge hat plaited from vine fiber because otherwise you go blind. I'd sleep on a mat directly on the warm slab and feel the industrial mouths breathing under me.

And really, more than the person, I would be the place where person blurs into mountain.

My hands would be varnished black-green with the skin on the concrete — that cyanobacterial varnish that eats the ceramic-concrete itself, that smells like a penny in a mouth — because I have to press my palm flat on the ticking aqueduct to feel the taps better. My fingernails would always have silt under them from lifting water hyacinth. I would have a small paper wasp nest under my girder that I have made a quiet agreement with: I don't go out at dawn when they're angry, they don't build over my cloth wall.

I would be one of the parts that makes the nothing-leaves rule true. Water condenses, drips, is drunk by my taro, transpired, condenses again on the underside of the terrace above me. Metal rusts, fungi take it, I eat the fungi. When I die, they'd lay me in a composting bay on the inner level and a season later I'd be in the rice. The children would know they are eating me and think nothing strange of it.

You wouldn't see me from the valley. From far away you only see a single cloud-forest humming, sweating, steaming. Too dense to show a single life.

But if you climbed up and sat long enough for your eyes to learn the right scale, you'd find me exactly where I've always been — in the dripping, humming thickness of Terrace 53, one hand in the cold trickle, one hand on warm concrete, tapping to the person three levels down who is also tapping,

and waiting for the water to tap back.
