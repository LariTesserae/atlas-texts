I didn't know at first.

If you drop me into that throat, my first instinct is to look up. Everyone does. There's the mouth — that ragged oval of too-bright sky with the wind sawing over it. If I were going to be anything here, I thought, I'd be something that could use it. 

So at first I thought I'd be one of the swifts.

It would be easy. To be heat. To be part of the wall that breathes. To hang by those wrong, hooked feet with my wings folded so tight they hurt, my naked head tucked under a neighbor's wing, eye shut, simply radiating. Being held by ten thousand other bodies so completely you never have to decide where your edge is. For a minute I wanted that very badly. The luxury of closing your eyes for a whole season and being forgiven for it because your sleeping *is* your work.

But I can't. Not for long. I can't keep my eyes closed.

I open them, and immediately I see them moving under me.

So then I thought I'd be the other thing that looks like it belongs — the single clump of bleached fern, or the stiff white grass growing out of shit and nowhere. Because it shouldn't be there and yet it is. Because it is not useful. The whole chamber has a job: the rock makes bowls, the cloud makes water, the birds make heat, the beetles make plumbing, the larvae make future. The grass has no job. It just stands there, transparent in the dusk, trembling whenever a thousand wings shift. I love that. I wanted to be the useless holy thing.

But the grass doesn't get to look around, and I need to look around. I am restless in a way that pressed things can't afford.

Then my eyes adjust to what is actually happening three inches in front of my face, and I stop looking for a metaphor and I find my hands.

You would find me about two thirds down, on the west curve where the geothermal breath is strongest and the condensation never stops. Not up near the lip where the storms hammer straight in and the pebbles get knocked loose, not back in the deep felt where the larvae pulse and the dark is total. In the middle layers, where the work is.

There's a pool there. Not the biggest — not the basin-sized mirror that reflects the mouth — and not the smallest palm-cup. Mine is about as big as a knee. Its lip is basalt polished to red glass, and around its upper crescent I've got seventeen stones.

If you came close, you could see how personal they are. One piece of quartz that took me a whole day to drag from the mouth, still gritty on one side. Two black glass chips I pried myself with my head braced against the rock, so my mandibles are pale at the tips. A few smooth riverines that aren't from any river up here at all, that must have been in a bird's gut once. They form a low wall, no higher than a knuckle. A dew fence.

I am the beetle.

Not a young one zooming around cleaning mite and bristle off a pink head — though I did that, and I still do when the bird whose territory I live on leans into me and makes that low purr in its throat. But mostly now I am the water-tender. My elytra have gone dull. Sealed shut a long time ago, varnished by damp until I carry a little black mirror on my own back. My legs are too long and they shake a little if I stand too long on bare rock.

My whole day is this:

The cloud hits the warm. A bead forms on the rock above the pool. Without me it would sheet and wander and be lost down some crack. Instead it hits my topmost quartz, climbs it — you can see it climb if you have my eyes — because quartz wants water to climb. It runs along the edge I've set, beading, joining another bead, getting fat, and then it reaches the low point I made, the one I nudged three millimeters left this morning, and it hangs there.

It gets so big it distorts everything. In it I can see the whole chamber upside down.

Then it falls. *Tik.*

Into the black. The pool shivers. A ripple hits my stones and retreats.

Somewhere above me, the bird that owns the rock I live on senses the ripple through its claws. It wakes just enough to lower that absurd, wrinkled, naked head down with such — and you were right — such courtly delicacy, as if it is afraid to disturb architecture. Its beak barely breaks the surface. It drinks. Its throat moves once. Then it sighs hot bird breath onto my back and goes back to being a furnace.

I move a stone back a fraction because its beak bumped it.

That's what I do. All day. Nudge, wait, watch the bead get fat, *tik*, drink, sigh, rebuild.

They said no one taught us. That's not quite true. The water taught us. The way it wants to climb quartz. The way it wants to fall in one place if you let it. I didn't learn it. I remembered it the first time I saw a bead go the wrong way.

I don't mind the press. I don't mind the chittering that never stops, or the click of my brothers' feet that sounds like rain on rain, or the smell of warm bird and guano and mineral. I need it dense, actually. If it were thin and scattered like everything else up at four thousand meters, I wouldn't have enough rock to read.

I am waiting, like everyone else. Not to fly. I will never fly; my wings sealed before the mountain was this high. I'm waiting for that pressure-drop hour when ten thousand heads open at once and far back in the dark a swift whistles a question straight up at the wind.

And then, sometimes, when the cloud sea boils over the lip just wrong, it happens — the one spear of wet light falls straight down the throat for a second, not warm, but so bright after all that dusk it feels warm.

When it hits my pool, for an instant my seventeen stones, my black back, every waiting eye, every frond of that useless grass, every drop hanging on every wall — we all catch fire at once. The whole mouth of the mountain glitters like it is full of stars that it made itself, out of beak-sweat and guano and patience.

Then it is dark again, and the wall sighs and closes its eyes, and I go back to nudging quartz a millimeter to the left.
