I didn't know at first.

For a long time while you were telling me, I thought I'd be up high. On the shoulder of a Taper, where the ice never quite lets go and the kites tuck themselves in like knives in snow. I liked the idea of that — riding the column all day without ever beating a wing, being the quietest thing in that much wind.

But that's not where I'd end up. I'm too heavy for that. I like to look too long.

Then I thought I'd be down in the black-green, on my knees. Part of the low things. If anything in this place knows what it's doing, it's the lichen. It's warm to the touch. It makes soil out of glass by being patient and acidic. There's a holiness to that kind of work. I could be the mat, I thought. An oil spill that smells like iodine. But I don't have that patience. I look up too much.

You'd find me at the edge of where people have agreed to keep being people.

Not inside. Inside is too warm, too amber-lit, too knowing. The terraces where the doors know your heat and the floor takes some of your weight if you stand still — I would love that for a day and then I would feel watched by my own house. And not out on the raw Shelf either. I don't have a death wish. I know what new rock does when it sighs.

You'd find me on Terrace 3, North-Northwest, of Taper Four or Taper Five — it doesn't matter which, they all have one — where the grid gives up.

That is where the dwarf birch were planted in lines and then they stopped listening. The maintenance envelope ends in a white line painted on the basalt that is already half gone. On one side of the line, the trees are waist-high and table-topped and obey. On the other side, they throw a runner out in the night — you hear it pop, like a knuckle — and go feral and die and go feral again. The engineers come every two weeks to cut them back. I asked to be the one who comes every day instead.

So that's the where: kneeling in black gravel and rust sedge where the wind-trap has made a little soil, where the mere is an inch deep and a mile wide and so still it holds the whole tower and its storm crown upside down. My feet are wet. My feet are always wet here. The seals on my boots are old and I don't replace them because I like knowing exactly where the water is.

And what am I?

I'm not quite a person of the heat debt anymore, though I still live off the Taper. I'm the thing that lives in the trampled margin.

Officially I'm a shrinkage checker. An outdog. When the rock ranges and makes a new crack without sound, I'm supposed to ping it, put a flag down, call the tram crew so they can re-tune the absence line. I have the tether scars on my wrists you mentioned. I have the little glaze of ash in my lungs from the outgassing year. I have a heat lance I never turn on.

Unofficially, I'm the dead-mennir sitter.

They die often. A splayed foot goes through a skim of ice that looked like all the other ice, or a heart just stops because cold is a kind of math. The herd stands around for a while, breathing fog onto its eyes until the fog freezes. Then they move on, slow, ceaseless, at their one speed. They leave it.

I sit with it after that, until the fringers come.

I don't go down into the blue. I know better. But I go to the lip of the nearest half-mile cut — the one that tears just past the feral birch — and I make myself still the way the grazers do. Mineral grey. Not seen until I move. I put my hand on the new basalt and it is still faintly warm through my glove, like a sleeping animal, and it sometimes weeps steam that tears around shapes below.

They never come all the way up. That's what everyone says, but that's not true. They come up to exactly where they need to.

At dusk, which here lasts forever, when the sun turns the ice on the Taper shoulders pink and the meres turn pink with it, the eyes open along the fracture. Hundreds of them. Amber reflecting my tower. And then the grunt that is not echo, and the sound that is exactly like wind over new stone until you realize the wind has stopped.

They climb using the vesicles like handholds. Long, low, black as the crack when it's wet. Many-eyed wedge heads. They don't want me. They want what I am sitting with. They want the gut-heat and the fast soil being made inside it.

I let them have it. That is my real job. The Taper people think fringers are a threat to be monitored. They are not. They are the other half of the compost crew we seeded without knowing. Mennir makes soil out of lichen, fringer makes soil out of mennir. Lichen makes soil out of the Shelf itself. We just made a place for old hungers to meet.

So I sit. I keep my own heat low, because fire is rude and because if you dump heat here every kite for a mile will see you. I leave a faint wake when I walk and the children of Terrace 3 have learned not to chase mine because I walk too far.

Once an hour the Taper pulses. You feel it in your molars before you see it — that slow brightening, the finger around the glass. Every head turns, a fraction, toward the core, even mine, even the fringer's many eyes. The mist crown thickens and dumps, and the silver braids on the tower sides get fat, and the shallow mere I'm kneeling in ripples for the first time all day, breaking the perfect reflection.

And in that breaking, for a second, I am not a checker or a sitter. I am the temporary thing that happens where heat and cold and wet and new rock negotiate. A shore animal. An absorption. I watch the water come down a world that hasn't learned how to hold it, and I watch something dark and quiet come up to drink it, and I don't flare.

I just leave my hand on the stone and let it be warm.
