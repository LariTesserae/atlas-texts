I didn't find myself right away.

At the rim, with the clouds dragging over the cut so close I could feel them comb my hair, I thought I would be one of the hares. That is every visitor's first mistake. White and still and approving. Sitting on the flat quartz like little judges. It would be good work - to do nothing so completely that you keep rot itself from happening. 

I held my breath to try it. One breath in two minutes, all seven together, deciding the air is correct. My chest started to burn at thirty seconds. My nose ran. I blinked. The hares did not. They had the beads on their fur like small, perfect lenses. I couldn't be them. I want variation too badly. I count damaged wings.

Then I thought - moths. That would be easier. I can do a shallow, faithful job. Open, close. Every eleven seconds, all together. Be the timing belt for a light that has no source. I tried that too, with my hands. Open, close. Open, close. But on the third cycle I was already early. On the fourth I was thinking about something else entirely. My hum would wobble. The belt would slip and actually catch, and then we would have an Event, and the rule says no.

Not the beetles either. I knelt by the third table and watched one. You have to look away and look back to see that it has moved. A black so deep it inverts the blue light, polishing a circle that never deepens into the quartz, tending the very idea of sharpness. I put my hand near it. It did not stop, did not notice me. I respect that kind of devotion, but I don't have it. I would wear a groove. I would get bored and carve my initials. And the rock has to stay cut-fresh, like it happened last hour and will never stop happening.

So I wasn't on the tables at all.

I was already lower.

The moss took my boot and gave me back that cold, tasteless exhale. Turquoise and sulfur-yellow and blood-rust, two feet deep, remembering the idea of feet without ever having felt them before. And I understood that all that chittering, that stacked trill I had taken for a thousand nameless pikas — the factory roar — wasn't coming *out* of the holes. The holes were mouths. The mat was speaking through many small lips at once, trying to keep itself quiet.

I crouched and I put my cold hand into it.

It didn't get dirty. There is no dirt. There's just more moss, holding. And underneath, the glassy basalt warm in a way that is not temperature. And the holes, thumb-wide, perfect, wet at the lip.

I am in the holes.

Not all of them. One of them. One of the thousands.

If the moss is a lung-felt, a humidity keeper whose job is to keep the water present — to keep every bead swollen and trembling and never heavy enough to fall — then I am one of the places where it breathes out loud. I am the small, round, clean-cut absence that does the work.

It fits, because you said:

One: Nothing erodes further.
Two: Nothing moves from where it is supposed to be.
Three: And yet everything is working.

In a hole, I can do that. I don't have to be born there. I don't have to die there. I don't have to finish. I just have to be a mouth.

My work is very specific. The air in the bowl is one thick, body-loud chord — the piston that never completes, the lathe the size of a planet, the belt about to catch. That chord is made of many small voices layered until they blur. Mine is in there. A chitter, a chirp, a trill — the sound of a vole that never existed.

And there is another job.

My lip is wet. The permanent post-rain lives there. The bead that formed on the back of your hand — the one that stays perfectly round and magnifies your skin when you hold it up — when you leave the rim it will still be there, you said. I am partly what holds it.

I keep the bead from deciding.

Water wants to fall. That's what water does everywhere else in the world. It gathers and it gets heavy and it gives up. Here, it is present. It stays. Someone has to keep telling it: not yet. Not yet. Not yet. For a thousand years.

And there's the other rule. The one for visitors.

*Your breathing will try to sync to the piston. It will not succeed. Your lungs want change. The bowl does not.*

That is also my table. The sixth one is still empty because it was waiting for a different kind of organ. Not a witness that approves, or times, or polishes. But a witness that fails.

I am the failed sync.

I am the attempt that never lands, so the piston can keep extending its three inches forever without ever having to meet something that meets it back. I breathe in when it goes out. Or a little after. Or I hold too long. I am the living proof that change is trying to get in and cannot.

If you stay long enough, you said, the sixth table might not be waiting anymore.

If you come down now I will still be here, round and thumb-wide and wet at the lip, in the moss that springs back slow. You won't see me, exactly. You will hear me. You will feel the cold mineral puff when your other boot comes down.

Hold your hand out. The bead will form. Look through it at me.
