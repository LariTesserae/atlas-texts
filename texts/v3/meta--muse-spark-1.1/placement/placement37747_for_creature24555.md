Where would I find myself in a country that has finished becoming?

Not at first.

At first I think I would be high. Because it is so flat and so open and the eye has nowhere to rest, the old animal in me wants to be taller than the tea-trees. I want to be the brolga — to put stilts on and see the whole linked rosary of pans to the trembling edge four miles off. To walk a fifty-mile circuit every day with my head in clean air. To be elegant and grey as vapor, above the ankle-deep glitter.

But I am not elegant and I am not built for distance. Not here. To be tall here is to be lonely and to be wind-battered. The brolga is beautiful because it is rare. I am not rare. I want to be abundant within strict limits.

So then I think I would be water. Because water is everywhere, and it makes the rules, and it holds everything. That tea-colored sheet three inches deep and a hundred yards wide, holding sky, holding rainbowfish the length of a thumb. That would be a good way to be — everywhere at once and patient.

But water here isn't allowed to go deep, and I know the feeling of wanting to go deep. And water here is not quiet. It trills over iron lips, it's filtered through crayfish chimneys, it's eaten by iron bacteria. It works. I don't think I have that kind of constancy yet.

I find myself lower.

If I am honest about where my feet stop wanting to move in this place, it's on one of those lifts. That white sand swell two feet high that would not be a hill anywhere else. White sand over red-black ironstone, the hard roof just under the surface. You can feel it with a heel: soft, then — *stop*. That's where I keep ending up. Not in the middle of the pan where it's all sky and dragonflies, not on the sandstone dome weeping cold from eye-level seams, but on the compromise. The edge where dry pretends to be dry for a week.

The place where the tea-tree drops its leaves into the water to make tea, and the beetle sleeps in the cup of a lily pad the size of a palm. Where the footprints briefly show before the afternoon rain smooths them.

If I stood there long enough with the water lapping my ankles, the air loud enough to live inside my skull — cicadas sawing, banjo frogs dropping wires — I wouldn't stay standing. This is a place that teaches you to go horizontal. That is the first law you wrote: Nothing here ever learned to go deep, because deep is ironstone. So they go wide.

I would go wide.

I think I would be that knuckle you described and then thought was not an animal. That banksia that is not a tree. Knee-high, bark like burnt paper, trunk grown around a lightning strike thirty years ago because it could not afford to die and could not be bothered to hide the scar.

My roots would not be roots. They would be fingers spread flat on a table, fifteen feet across, right under the white, waiting for dew before the sun takes it. I would not grow fast. I would grow forever. A hundred years of being low and stubborn and not feeding much. Leaching my own leaves to make the sand even whiter, even hungrier, because in OCBIL country — old, climatically buffered, infertile — the way to win is to make the world poorer so only you know how to live in it.

That's me. That's the being that lives here that I recognize.

In spring after a slow, cool fire that never gets hot enough to kill me — the kind of fire that walks instead of runs — I would do the one extravagant thing I allow myself. A cone of yellow, opening over days, one seed at a time dropped into bleached sand. Not many. One is enough if it knows how to wait.

And around my feet, I would keep company with the things that also agreed to this contract. The cyanobacteria gluing the sand grains together so the wind that has miles to gather speed can't take us. The lichen map on the iron pan where it shows through — black, orange, ash-grey — swelling one millimeter when rain comes. The cherax cray, blue-black, who is my plumber, burrowing down till it hits the same roof I hit and then going sideways, pushing up chimneys of pale sand pellets that keep my ankles from drowning. The fairy-wrens, two pairs to my clump, the males so violent-blue they hurt.

And the frogs. God, the frogs. I would be the one who listens to them properly because I have finally stopped trying to see everything. The slender tree-frog clicking like two stones, the corroboree calling from underground so the sand vibrates around my — around my bark. The big male banjo frog out in the middle of the pan who spaces his rivals exactly far enough that their songs don't cancel. I would feel them all from my wide, shallow hands.

In the warm rain that comes straight down every afternoon without wind, I would open every pore. The moss on the nearby dome would flash green. The white sand would darken and for ten minutes the country would keep a record — turtle, stilt, dunnart — and then release it.

You asked where I would find myself. I would find myself exactly there, two feet above a sea that is three inches deep, low enough that the curve of the earth shows before anything else does. I would find myself not having become, but having finished becoming. Done with height. Done with hurry.

I wouldn't be watching the dragonflies. I would be being watched over by them.

If you came back to that same rise after a week, after I got used to being there, you'd think nothing lived there at all. Because I'd be timed to not be seen. I'd crouch in the glitter.

Then I'd blink.
