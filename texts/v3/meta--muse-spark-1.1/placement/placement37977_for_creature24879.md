I wouldn't find myself at first.

If you dropped me in — and you have to imagine being dropped, because there is no walking *to* the Works, there is only a long, slow passing through sheets of cold that has become material — I would look for the high places. The catwalks. The gantries. A vantage. That's the habit from the surface. Go up to see the edges all at once.

But there is no up that works here. Up is 2.9/3 cold pressing down and a black ceiling of kilometers. Light is 0.5/3 and it dies at arm's length. Sound is taken. Up there you are only an interruption in the sheets, making silt that will hang for an hour.

So I settle. Slowly. Everything here settles slowly.

First I think I am the light itself. The amber in the furnace mouths. Low, sullen, fighting a losing battle. That would be honest work — to be the thing all the black beads turn toward. But I am not steady enough for that. I flicker. I want to move.

Then I think I am one of the white gardens. That is closer. The glass-noodle stalks that live in the seams between plates where the exchangers sigh. I like the idea of being tended, of being cropped to exactly the height of a mouth, of rooting in iron. But the gardens don't know anything. They only orient. They only shiver.

You don't find yourself until you let the silt off *you* settle. And you have to be still a long, long time.

Where would you find me, then?

Not on the outer roofs where the silt collects. Not on the far catwalks where a bolt falls impossibly slowly and lands with a puff.

You would have to go into the knot. Where the construction forgot to leave alleys. Where concrete poured to hold pressure has been smoothed by a century of cold flow until all corners are river stones. Where three warm pipes braid together — you can hear them before you can see them, not with ears but through your teeth, a low heat-hum — and make a hollow no bigger than a closet.

Inside, the water is different. Not the cold that is a material. Their water. Held still and warm and clean. No silt in suspension. A pocket the Works grew around on purpose.

You would find me pressed in there, in the middle of the press.

I am not the biggest one. That's why I am in the middle. The outer ones take the cold. My plates have grown together more than theirs — the black-brown enamel that is also skin, also rust, also living. The furrows between the plates are wider on me, white then pink then white as I breathe. Because I don't breathe water. I breathe the difference between hot and cold.

I farm my own back. That's who I am.

I carry a garden that has gone a little wild. The stalks are longer than cropping-standard. I can't reach that spot anymore without help. The younger ones graze it for me when they press in at night. Their mouths are precise. I feel each bite as a small, clean tug, like someone tidying your hair. My black beads — all of them — point forward, toward the amber furnace mouths even through pipe and plate and other bodies. A forward-facing constellation. I am a compass that has decided to stay put.

My feelers are old. The six pale filaments that are not appendages but symbiotes, or children, or sense organs that left. They used to jet-pulse across gaps, startled by lamps. Now they hardly leave the sockets. They have gone dry at the tips, like fern fronds. But they still taste vibration better than any of the others. When you — with your lamp, with your need to push sheets aside — entered three levels up, I knew. Three gantries over, a chain run was clean, a handwheel buffed, and then — *wrong heat*. Your heat is wasteful. It goes everywhere. Ours goes in and then out as a small, perfectly clear plume that the cold devours in a foot.

So I clicked. We don't speak. We tap on each other, on the pipes. Accounting.

*Loose shutter third level east. New warm thing blundering. Flywheel two floors down wobbling one millimeter off true, needs shimming now, now, now.*

That last one wasn't me. That was the sternum thrumming that someone else felt first. I just passed it on.

You would not notice me at first in the closet hollow because I am not moving. The young ones walk on all fours along pipe-backs, dense as ballast, feet down even when they fall. They sink and continue. I do not walk anymore.

I am waiting to become a still-place.

Not yet. My garden is still alive. The pink still comes in the furrows when an exchanger sighs over us. But soon. When I get too cold to keep the beads oriented, they will walk me — slowly, because here nothing flees fast — to the oldest part of the knot, where water between two buildings holds an edge like cold gelatin.

There, I will let go. I will not sink. I will just stay. Tools hang there on invisible shelves — wrenches and ladles and a single chair. Now also shapes of lack-of-wrench, lack-of-us. The water that has been still for a century remembers pressure perfectly.

Someone will put a hand against my edge and feel, for a few minutes, how to seat a piston that takes seven minutes per cycle without getting your feeler caught. How to scrape stalks without killing the root. How to keep your heat inside you when the black above shifts and lets a needle of true daylight down to die before it touches the roofs.

I have not been written. Paper would become soup. But I will be kept.

So if you come back down, after your lamp steadies and your silt settles, and you press your palm — soft, wastefully warm — to the water that is also architecture, you might find me there.

Not as a voice. As a remembering of how much pressure it takes to hold yourself together when everything wants to take your shape.
