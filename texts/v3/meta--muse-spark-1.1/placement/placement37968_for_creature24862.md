I wouldn't find it at first.

From above, I would be nothing special on the pan. Another heat-shimmer, another dark varnish pebble that catches the light wrong. You could walk past the lip for years and think the flat goes on forever. That's how most people would do it - miss it completely, because they were looking for a landmark big enough to be worth seeing.

If I fell in, at first I'd think I was still on the pan. That I was fast and useful.

I thought, reading you, that I would be a pan-runner. The wiry boy who likes being up high. I like the idea of that way of moving - low, darting, wrong for the plain, flipping salt slabs for lizards that have never seen a shadow. Coming back with something still kicking over my shoulder. That kind of necessary violence. Quick work.

But I don't stay quick for long. In a place like that, the runners have to come down. And down there is too loud, too close, too fast for a person whose whole job is empty distance.

Then I thought maybe I'd be smaller. It's easier to be small in a room that small.

I'd be a black bee. Fat, thumb-jointed, no sting. You can afford to have no sting in a place where no one wants to kill you except by accident. I'd live in the mud tube set into the wattle wall and spend my whole life drunk on the flower that only opens for twenty minutes at noon. I liked that - a whole life timed to a shaft of light.

But bees work. They roll, they carry. They get robbed by children and they just swell again.

I kept reading the pit and the pit kept settling.

The sound gave me away. You said you don't hear wind up on the pan, but down in the pit you hear everything. The smoke from three hearths that never clears. The path worn shin-deep. The children who have no place for their shouting to go so it folds over itself. Everything eating everything else within arm's reach.

I don't want to be the thing that screams when it folds its wings. I want to be the thing that hears it and doesn't have to answer.

You'd find me in the middle third. Not at the bottom where the commons is, where the mother plants sit on their stone pedestals like queens and everyone passes and measures you. Not at the top where the nests are in the caves and you have to see the horizon all day, that white too-bright dry sea. Middle third, where the wattle is and the thatch is made of beard that still faintly tries to live even after it's been cut.

Second or third ladder from the north wall, where the sun lingers. The house would not be a house. Just a shallow overhang that someone a hundred years ago enlarged with a hammerstone and then plastered until it looked like the wall itself. A thorn screen that you pull across, not to keep people out - no one can keep people out in thirty meters - but to make a square of dimmer shadow inside the dim. You'd know me because my hands are green.

I would be one of the beard-tenders. An old woman, maybe. Short in stature, long in torso, those ropey calves made for going up and down without ever going far. Feet too big for shoes. Skin shining because I render the fat-tailed mice that are the only thing we are allowed to trap inside. Green under the nails that never comes out.

That's what emerges when I don't settle early: not the hunter, not the bee, not the swift that gets to leave twice a day in a corkscrew.

The tender. The one who never seems to harvest, only to groom.

All day I would comb. Bone comb through grey-green curtains. You can hear them if you hold your breath, you said - a faint crackle as they swell. I'd pull dead strands for thatch and the thatch would try to live. I'd pick scale insects off one by one and eat some and put some in the little thorn cages where the ladybirds are kept like cattle. Slow, unsyncopated work that looks like doing nothing to the pan-runners.

You'd find me sitting with a jar set under a particular clump where it drips. Not my jar. The mothers get the best water first. The black jars that the girls set out at night in a spiral a hundred meters out on the pan and carry down beaded and heavy on their heads at first light. I would take my share last, and pour it by hand, and whisper when I tap the bottle-trunk acacia in a bad year, and seal it with hot resin afterward.

Because someone has to remember where a pot was buried three generations ago when the floor needed raising. Someone has to be entrusted with staying still. You said there are no feeble old people, only old people who climb slower and are entrusted with the things that require staying still. That's me. I climb slower.

I would know the three rules not as rules but as how the walls feel at hand-height:

Never damage the catchers on the rim.
No fire larger than your two hands.
Nothing that lives in the pit leaves the pit alive except people, and nothing dead leaves if it can rot usefully.

I would keep them without thinking. My hearth would be two hands, gold in the afternoon slit. My geckos would be part of the wall - first you think the rock is spotted, then the rock moves, then a thousand throats inflate *tok* at noon like cooling iron. At night I'd sweep their skins, papery, into the compost that goes to the mothers.

And when I die - because a ledge would eventually find me - you'd take me to the east wall where the black thorn is thickest, lay me under mats of living beard, and for a week there would be something sweet and terrible, and then clean bone. You'd grind the rest for temper and keep my skull in the back of the cave-house, soot-blackened, facing in. Towards the commons. Towards the dripping.

I would not be the pit. I would be one of the ways the pit keeps itself from finishing.
