I think at first that I would be on the rim.

It would make sense — higher, drier, where the ticking is still separate ticks. Where I could look down at the whole county-wide bowl of it and not have to be in it yet. Where the fissures in the roof of the world are still overhead and I could keep looking up for more light that won't come.

But I wouldn't stay there. The rim is not built for staying. Nothing is flat there for long. The rope grooves are all pointed inward, everything was dragged to the edge and lowered. It's a lip made for letting go.

So I go down.

At first I walk like you would walk, boots on wet blue-black stone, arms a little out from my sides because the alleys are too narrow. My boots are wrong immediately. They fill and they slip and they deaden the tap-talk coming up through the floor. I have to take them off. I carry them after a while, and then I set them down somewhere on a capstone and leave them, and they fill with condensation behind me like two small failed cisterns.

About ten terraces down the sound changes. It's no longer ticking. It starts to clatter. The V-cuts in the lips are working harder here, throwing water in low arcs onto the next level. My feet — not splayed like theirs, not furrowed, not yet leather in the middle and white at the edges — begin to read a little anyway. Cold. Grade. Where the stone is trying to undercut.

That's where I first think, maybe I am one of them. Maybe I am new to a course, pinker, my membrane still clear. Maybe I am supposed to be under a leaning wall, learning grade with my back, pushing with my too-long arms.

But I'm not good at holding. I keep wanting to look farther than the next spillway. My eyes are too big. I keep blinking against the spray wrong, top to bottom instead of sideways. And my hands want to be dry. Their hands never want to be dry.

So I go further down, because the basin wants you to go further down. Because maintenance is always downhill — water is always getting away from you and pooling where you didn't intend.

Two thirds of the way down, or what I guess is two thirds because you never see the bottom, the mist gets thick enough that it isn't condensation anymore. You're walking through it. The high white curtain you heard at the top has gone quiet behind you, and the chord starts. All those brimming basins spilling at once, water hitting water, a low note you feel in your molars.

Here the building has failed more often, and the failures have been left to be useful. A terrace sank three inches on one side ten years ago, or fifty, and nobody has jacked it back because the water liked it. It holds a sheet now, ankle-deep, perfectly level, black as tar, reflecting nothing because there is still no light to reflect. Only the close walls and the darker air over them.

I find myself there. Not because I chose it — because my feet stopped slipping there. Because the grade stopped pulling.

I sit down in it.

I think I would be one of the old ones of the course. Not old in years. Old in knees.

I don't climb anymore. My job in the course is not to lift, not to shore, not to tap *Here. Lift.* through the stone. My job is to sit in the failed mirror that failed perfectly, and keep my hands in it all day.

The water in this one stays cold because new condensation routes open above it all night. It goes cloudy a little after noon when the mineral fur in the catchment above sloughs. I can feel it. The fur comes off in clots, trembling gray. It collects against my ankles. The silt that comes with it is finer than mortar dust. It tells me how fast the stone is softening that should never have been expected to soften.

The young ones come past sometimes, pink and translucent, moving through the crawlspace under my terrace on their bellies. They brush my calf and I feel their backs reading. I put a hand on a head that still has its eyes sealed. They pass.

I do drink. I do eat the fur when I scrape it with my square-tipped nails. But mostly I listen. In this low place, low as a floor under a ceiling, that chord from below gets a little clearer when you have water up to your knees and you are not trying to build against it.

I am not a wedge anymore. I am a gauge. A living spillway level.

When they eventually need to know if a new route has opened higher up, they will tap down through the floor to me, two knuckles on stone that I will feel in my splayed, useless, beautiful feet. And I will tap back how cold the mirror has gotten. How fast the silt came today.

And later — not now, but when my feet truly can't read — I will stand up out of this failed basin, and I will leave it level and black behind me, and I will keep going down, down the stairwells that are not stairwells anymore but funnels for water, toward where the water has won and taught the stone how to be still.

That is who I would be. The one who stays still long enough for the basin to use me as a reference.
