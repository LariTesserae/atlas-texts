At first I think I would be walking.

That's the correct first thought, and I let myself keep it for a long time.

Because if you put me down on that high, warm, soft-edged basalt — not cold the way high should be cold, warm like the back of a machine — my first instinct is not to find a curb and sit. It's to survey. To go see what's making the hum. To try to draw the map the old way, with north at the top, even though there is no north here, only higher ground that the avenues always bring you back to.

So for a while I am a newcomer. My lips crack. I'm still trying to sweat and the air takes it before it can form, zero humidity, zero mercy. I still think light should work. I cup my hands around one of those dying-coal streetlamps — filament white-hot and giving off no more than a match behind frost — and I try to make it brighter by wanting it.

It doesn't.

I walk the hundred-meter avenues. Nothing on either side for half a kilometer, then a brick factory by itself, then nothing again. Rails still shining. The air the same temperature as my skin so I only know I'm moving because the black-red salt plates chime when the pressure changes under my feet. I keep looking for a center. There is no center. There is only middle, spread thin, shy.

I think, maybe I'm one of the cranewalkers. That would be elegant. To be mineral, stilt-legged, grazing on nothing but a temperature difference that isn't supposed to exist. To be so slow you look like scaffolding. I try to be slow. I am not good at being slow that way. My slowness is fidgety. It's the slowness of something waiting for a response, not the slowness of something that has become the landscape.

I think, maybe I'm one of the ribbon things in the oil. Small, black, flat, lifting when the magnetic field turns. That would be honest — a life lived in the housing of a larger machine, doing no work, basking. But I have too much language for that. I flutter and settle, but I keep naming the flutter.

So I keep walking until the city teaches the avenue its trick and brings me back to higher ground, again and again, until I finally stop trying to leave a scratch in the ironstone that the ironstone will not keep.

Where I would find myself is not on a curb, not yet.

In the southeast quarter — if southeast still means anything — there is a dynamo hall the size of a cathedral. You described it. House-sized machines turning once every nine seconds, forever, doing work that un-does itself. I would be in the annex beside it. Not the hall itself, because the hall is too perfect. But the annex where the maintenance log was kept.

There's a room there that isn't quite part of the plan. Cinderblock, painted that green you only find in institutions. One metal desk. One chair whose shape has been learned by air more than weight. One ledger, open, its paper not dusty because dust here isn't dust, it's salt that doesn't settle. The ledger is full of entries in different hands, all recording the same thing:

*09:00:00 - Flywheel A/B/C - 9.0 sec/rev - within tolerance.*
*18:00:00 - Flywheel A/B/C - 9.0 sec/rev - within tolerance.*

For decades. Centuries. The same measurement, confirming that nothing has changed.

That is where I would be. In the room that exists to say, again, that the thing that has always been happening is still happening.

Who I would be is the one who can't yet stop writing it down.

I would not be the fully equilibrated ones outside, broad bare feet thick as tires, hexagonal salt grown into the skin, eyes gone completely dark and wet to drink the light of a match. Not yet. I would still have white in my eyes. I would still have a mouth that wants to make air move in the shape of words, even though speaking here is putting a gradient into a world that has gotten rid of gradients.

But I would be close. I would have stopped sweating. I would keep a tin box in my pocket with three perfect clear cubes in it — nitrate, perchlorate, iron — and I would forget some days to even open it. I would have learned to listen with my skeleton. The 60-cycle thrum coming up through the soles of my feet into my teeth.

I would sit in that annex where the fields from the two big flywheels cancel just enough that in the gap you can hear the salt plains outside chime on their own when the sky gets a little heavier, that very dark brown oil-sky pressing down. Faint, like distant wind chimes made of manhole covers.

My work would not be to fix or to produce. The city forbids both. The bearings do not need oil, the coal yard never gets lower, the assembly line makes the same blank and unmakes it. My work would be a little weight, a confirmation. Like you said those tenders do — watching the flywheel so it stays correct by being watched.

I would tend the ledger.

Not because anyone will ever read it. Not because the flywheel needs me. But because equilibrium needs a witness who still remembers what non-equilibrium felt like. Someone who still counts nine seconds even though his watch and the machine already agree. Someone who still forms the thought "it is still turning" and then lets that thought erode the way a hammer mark erodes on ironstone here — gone by the time you look again.

And after a long time — long enough that my skin begins to show that faint hexagonal patterning where the salt has decided to settle *in* me because it won't settle *on* anything — I would finally understand the rule without being told.

If a newcomer came, high up where he should be cold but isn't, lips cracking, cupping a dim streetlamp like I did, I would stay in my green annex with my ledger. I would not call out. Calling out moves air that doesn't want to move.

I would let him think, for that first day, that nothing lives here.

It's the courtesy the place extends.
