I thought at first I'd be up high.

If you're going to be something in that bowl, you want a roof. You come down the access road and the air drops those three degrees and gets damp and holds, and the first thing you hear is the tone, and the first thing you see is the mat. That many wings, shoulder to shoulder on the lathe shop, covering the line so thick it bows - that looks like belonging. Warm. A feathered tile that breathes. If I'm going to be anything here, I thought, I'd want to be dense. Part of the bulk. Part of the insulation. One small shivering filament in a liver the size of a factory.

But I walked it - in my head, down the too-wide street, across the too-much concrete - and there's no room there. Not for me. The dense ones don't have space between them. They don't even have between. They are between. They interleave. I am a person who needs a gap to think in. I'd suffocate up there, made into a continuous blanket.

So maybe I'm sparse. That was my second thought.

Between the lathe shop and the press shop there's forty meters of nothing but wet concrete and cold, oily water that never gets bigger. And in the middle of that nothing: one. Another forty meters: one more. Standing alone with perfect distance between individuals, like survey stakes. Like punctuation. I liked that job description. To hold the emptiness in place. To know exactly how far I am from the next building and the next pool and to not step two inches left.

I find myself there, in my head, on that apron. The concrete sweating under me. My feet - oh - my feet would be wrong. They would scratch. They would leave heat. The soft ones leave that faint cool damp that ceases. I would leave a print that stayed, and the pool next to me would have to work to un-ripple me, and the mat on the roof would have to sigh off heat to correct me. I'd be expensive to feed. I'd be loud in the wrong way. The tone would have to go around me.

No. Not sparse either.

I didn't find myself in the pools, though I tried. You kneel - and there's always space for your knee, a little more concrete than you thought - and you touch the black water. It takes your finger, it dirties, and for a half-second it shows you the wrong sky, your sky. Then it gathers itself and unbecomes the ripple and goes back to its memorized photograph. In that correction, the thing eats. I thought I could be that - the pool, patient, exact, a small black mirror holding the same flat-white day forever. But the pool doesn't want to be me. It doesn't want at all. That's why it can stay.

I found myself, finally, where I wasn't looking. In the row.

The row of narrow brick houses whose doors all open outward.

You wrote it right - they are hives in reverse. They aren't for living inside. They are for making more outside wall, more north side to sweat those dark tongues of moisture, more sill exactly the right width for something soft to adhere to. No one ever goes in, because out is the point.

Except someone left the doors all open outward.

I go in. Of course I go in.

Inside is one room. It's the same size as outside would suggest, which feels like a lie. The brick is clean inside too, scrubbed of life by rule. No plant, no mold, no fungal fur. The air in here is the same three-degrees-cooler air, but stiller. Because outside, the tone comes from everywhere, same loudness at the access road as next to the press shop. Inside, the tone is even more everywhere. The walls are warm. Not from a heater. From the mat on the other side of them. The whole house is a speaker cabinet and I've crawled inside the speaker.

There is no furniture. There is a concrete floor with a dark stain where oil never dried and never spread. There is a single window with a sill too thick, covered in the faintest cool damp that ceases as you watch. And there is nothing to do. Which is the job.

If the thing that wears pigeons - the one that eats prevented entropy, that pastures on almost-sliding, on rust that has decided to stay rust - if it needs anything, it's this: a place that is almost outside, but not.

It has abundance of outside. Roofs, wires, aprons, tower. What it doesn't have is an inside that isn't trying to be an inside. Human houses try to change things. They try to keep heat in, keep damp out. They try to make inside different from outside. That would be decay here. That would be food, but the wrong kind, too fast, a fire.

These houses are different. They are outside turned in on itself for a moment to make more outside. They are the one place in the Works where interior exists and agrees not to insist.

That's where you would find me.

I would be the inside.

Not the houses themselves. The agreement inside them.

I would have come down the access road like anyone, marked a pool with chalk, watched it unmark itself, felt the birds make space for my foot without ever stepping. I would have stayed until the light stopped moving - though it never was moving - until I understood the factories are listening to it, not the other way around. And I would have gotten tired of being a ripple. Tiring work, being corrected.

So I would open one of those outward doors - they open so easily, they want to be wall again - and I would step in, and I would sit on the concrete where the oil is, and I would learn how to be 1932 forever.

I would be the person who holds the inside empty. The small, unmeasured, almost-warm pocket of air that doesn't try to get warmer or colder. The human who has learned to breathe at the same pitch as the press, not in sync, but at the same note. The maintenance that isn't maintenance. The workpiece lathe-turned but never finished, because finishing would be change.

If you came down - if you left the bowl and came back - you would not see me. The doors open outward, you see. From outside a house with its door open looks like a flat wall with a dark seam. You would look at the roofs, at the wire that learned the shape of air, at the perfect spacing of the sparse ones holding the void, and you would count. And you would get the number wrong by one, because you cannot count something that was never separate and you cannot count the space that makes counting possible.

But at night - though late never comes - you would feel the bowl hold a little better. The damp a little damper in exactly the same amount. The tone a little less dented when a truck goes by on the road above.

Somewhere in the row, a house that was already empty would be a fraction emptier, in the right way.
