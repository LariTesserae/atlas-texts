At first I think I'd be up high.

It's instinct. If you give me a valley with a ceiling, I want the slot-light. I want to be the juniper. The one improbable tree that won the lottery, white trunk twisted, roots thrown out fifty feet across naked rock like a prayer made anatomy. Something that can stand the light.

But I sit with that image a while and it doesn't fit. That tree is alone in a way that is not the same as spaced. It's exposed. It has to perform being alive, has to reach. Every drip that misses it is a small failure. I don't want to be the thing that has to keep reaching.

So I move down.

Maybe I'm one of the bats. That would be easier. Hanging in the veil. One per square foot, one per square foot for a mile, all facing the same direction down the long run of the rift. Breathing together. I try that on and almost keep it - that shallow communal breath, that dry tik-tik talk, being part of a sheet that is not a swarm but a decision to leave room.

But I'm not good at hanging. I'd get dizzy looking down. And I'd have to listen forward forever into the length of the rift. I don't want to be listening forward. I want to listen around.

I come down off the ceiling. The crickets are next. That would be the obvious penance: become small, white as old wax, tuck into a vertical crevice with your antennae writing and writing in the cool air, a handspan of polite black rock between you and the next. Sawing. One thin leg against a ridged wing. Part of the roar.

I could be a cricket. But there's a thousand of me in a hundred feet and I don't actually want to sing. I want to hear the singing. The place is already so loud it has pressure. Someone needs to be the thing that is listening to it.

So I find the floor.

Not in a pool. I hover there for a long time. The salamanders are beautiful - pink-white, blind, stacked like spoons in the knee-deep pans, gills fanned like red ferns. They hang and they wait and every few minutes one lifts and breaks that skin of limestone dust on the water. *Plip.* But to be in the water would be to be in one place. One pan. Their whole world is one pan. They fit together so well because they have given up on going anywhere else. I'm still moving.

I move to the silt between.

That's where I finally stop looking for a body and find where my body already is.

Between two pans - not the close ones, the ones a good walk apart, where the black silt has had time to dry just enough to hold a footprint for an hour. Where the residual glow off the damp rock is exactly enough to see edges. Neither under a slot nor deep between them. In the mid-dim where your eyes have adjusted and you realize you can see.

There is a pitted basalt cobble there. An anvil.

You've seen them. Every scattered workshop has one. Not placed. Used. That is the difference. A stone that fell when the rift was new and landed wrong and got worn hollow in the middle not by water but by a hundred thousand small accurate impacts. Around it: a scatter of broken snail shells, a little midden of chert flakes that snapped, some still clear at the edge where they are sharp enough to cut fresh rock.

This is what holds the place together without building anything.

And if I'm something here, I'm not the beetle that comes with a fresh flake in its jaws like a knife in its teeth. And I'm not the rat-mole with the spade hands and the seam where eyes should be, snuffling for its favorite stone.

I'm the thing that the beetle and the rat-mole both use.

Not the anvil itself, though I love it. A what, not a who? No. The keeper of the anvil. The third kind of worker that wasn't listed because it doesn't look like work.

Find me crouched. Low, so low my knees are in the damp. Broad-pawed, I think, but not with mole hands. My hands are half beetle, half rat. Dark, like the silt has stained them. I don't have good eyes either - none of us really do here. The dim has been kind and made eyes optional.

What I do is tend.

The beetles wedge and snap. They leave. The rat-things tap *tick.tick.TICK* until a shell opens, and then they drag it off to eat and leave the fragments. Nobody cleans. But the anvil would fill up. After a thousand years of the same drip in the same place falling into the same pan making the same ripple, the little workshop would choke on its own broken tools.

So I am the one who doesn't pick up a new stone. I pick up the broken ones.

I turn them over. I test the edge with a thumb that has gotten hard. No good - I set it just outside the scatter where it won't be mistaken for good again. Still good - I nudge it back toward the center. I brush shell grit out of the pock in the basalt with the back of my hand so the next snail sits stable. I pat the silt flat again after the mole has scuffed it.

It's not building. Nothing gets higher. Nothing gets added. It's maintenance of equilibrium. The place was born fresh and then stopped changing, and the only way for it to stop changing forever is for someone to do the small, unnoticed work of returning it to where it was.

I keep the required spacing. From my anvil to the next crickets-on-the-wall is a handspan of empty black rock times twenty. To the next anvil over, maybe forty feet, out of sight in the dim. I hear the other keepers if I hold my breath - the same soft brushing, occasionally a chipped flake *tinked* away. We never call to each other. The bats do enough of that.

When the ceiling gathers itself and finally lets a drop fall - Drip. In exactly the same place it fell last time - it hits my pan nearby and the salamanders shift and one rises, *plip*, and the crickets never stop their glassy trill that makes your back teeth ache, and I reach out and pick up a feather of chert the beetle dropped.

I hold it up to the dim that isn't dark.

It still has one good edge.
