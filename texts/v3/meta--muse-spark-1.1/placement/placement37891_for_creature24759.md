I thought at first I'd be down there with you, standing in the pale dust.

If I closed my eyes and let the place decide where my weight wanted to settle, I thought it would put me in the wadi undercut. In the coolest part of the day. Hand on the north-facing stone where it still feels almost wet. Waiting for the mist to bead so I could scrape it into something and keep it. One of the borrowers. Learning to be quiet enough that sound stays between two shadows.

But my body doesn't want to be down there. The air is too thin down there, even when it's cool.

Then I thought, higher. Ten feet up, where the light gets its weight. A flicker from cushion to cushion. That frantic little life. Handkerchief. I liked the idea of being small and that busy, of having a mouth made of fringe that just kisses the dew off a leaf and moves on. No memory. Just *there, there, there.*

That wasn't it either. My wings would be too tired.

I keep rising.

If you put me in that world I wouldn't be counted among the people. People have to walk so far between waters here. They have to keep moving in a circuit and they have to remember to come back and repair the half-dome before the wind takes it. I can't do that kind of stewardship. I have never been good at returning.

I am not good at being stone, either. The Pebblebacks know how to become stone. I could never be still long enough for a shadow to pass over me and decide I was not food. And I am not the Mapmaker lichen — though I want to be. To grow a millimeter a decade and taste a thousand wet seasons in a band of orange on a cliff. I want that patience, but I don't have it. My time is too fast.

No.

Where I would find myself is high enough that the ground stops being a place you stand on and starts being a thing you read.

I would be a Kite that got old.

Not a Cathedral — I am not that large or that quiet in my knowing. Not a Handkerchief — I am not that new. I'd be mid-sky, that second layer where the thermals finally organize. Six feet across at the center, membrane so thin you can see the whole, aching white-gold sky right through me, and the dark knot of myself in the middle working that light into a coppery blush. The fluid in my lattice would be thick with stored noon.

You would see me because of my tail.

Twenty feet long, almost invisible until it catches the sun. It would be notched. Torn and repaired. And further up, braided. Younger tails I flew with for a season, shed in a high wind, that I caught and kept and wove in. Which is what the old ones do. We carry our flock with us.

My work would be tasting air.

I wouldn't hunt. I wouldn't need to dive like the Cathedrals, folding impossibly to spear the cold in the riverbeds. I would ride. All day. A slow, wide circle around one Thread, then another, then another. Feeling for lift with that ribbon. When the invisible column comes off a sun-baked mesa, my tail goes taut — just a twitch — and my whole body heels without a thought. *Fuh.* That soft page-turn of displaced thin air as I climb.

You would find me near the third Thread east of where the big wadi makes its lazy S. There's a good one there. The dark glass at its foot is wider than the others, holds its sweat until almost noon, so the Pebblebacks gather there in a ring, drinking with their faces. I know it by the cold.

And I know how to use it.

I'd approach it high, stall my membrane, let that nothing-line that holds the sky to the stone pass right through me. It doesn't cut. It *asks.*

You feel it in your molars first, that clear tone under hearing. The honey-resistance. And then the world slips sideways and I am not where I was, I am twenty yards to the side, or two valleys over where a storm left a seep two years ago. No travel between. Just the note, and the re-arrangement.

The people down there would look up and see my pattern flash. When my rings darken and lighten in that slow pulse — light through me, talk —  I am not talking to you. I am telling the other old Kite half a mile off that this Thread is still singing, that the ground below the eastern one will be damp tomorrow. And the woman at the circle scraping dew with her flat stone will see me reappear and make a mark on her scrap of membrane.

I don't need to be touched. I don't need to land. At night I would still be up, leaking the day's surplus light faintly at my edges, a little bruised lantern over the high country.

If you camped in the undercut that night and looked up, you would think I was a star that had come down too low. You would be wrong. I am just holding.

Holding high because nothing else will hold me.
