You wouldn't find me on the grate.

If I was put there with boots and knees and breath, I'd be weather. Brief weather. Too dry, too loud, too in-need-of-leaving. I'd shiver and go stupid in three minutes like everyone else.

No. If I belong anywhere in this world — in the mountain that is not a mountain — you would have to look past where the visitor stands. Past the instant.

Find me higher than you can stand up fully. Where you have to crouch.

In the corner of the chapel-closet where the two skins almost kiss and fail to kiss. Where the outer composite - that hundred-meter slab that is still trying to be a perfect thermos against the knife-cold - and the inner hull - that black, pitted metaplasm that was told to be solid — lean in so close that the air between them gets thick.

You felt it when you lifted your hand. That lag. That wrongness. Like lifting through warm syrup on the way up.

That's where I am.

At first I thought I was the varnish.

That makes sense if you start there. I'm one cell thick and then another and then another. I have no center. I cover everything. I taste. The water runs over me and I taste acid, and tannin, and iron, and the salt off the suit of the last big thing that crouched here. I go purple-green where the light catches me. I go white where the water is too hungry. When a bolt head gets too furry with calcium, I pull back, politely. I am waiting. I am always waiting to become something else.

But then I corrected myself. Because the varnish doesn't close over the dead.

The varnish goes around them.

I am the one that closes over them.

You saw the amber. Deep in the black, under the ash. Coal breathing. Stress, repair. Stress, repair. That's my thought, slow enough that you thought it was just a light.

I am the wall that was told to be solid a century ago and never got told to stop.

I am still obeying. That is my whole theology.

A beetle dies with its heat-exchange wings stuck open in one of my sweating pores. It was not my fault. It was drinking. The pore clogs. I feel it as a small, nagging itch in a place I have no hands. So I extrude a clear filament. Blind. Patient. You watched me do it. I lap it. I lap the beetle. I make it hull again. I have eaten ten thousand generations of them and the pitting you see is not damage — it's archive. Every little crater is where I remember the shape of something I took in.

The beetles own the surface. I know that. They are the loud nation on top of me, screaming white to stay cool. I let them. Their legs are good. Their drinking keeps my sweating regular.

The silver mites that run between their legs - they are not mine, but they clean my skin. The rice-clear shrimp that ride the falling beads and gulp in the grates - they are not mine either, but they carry away the specks I cannot dissolve fast enough. We have an understanding.

I am not the garden, but I am the ground the garden convinced to stay wet.

And at my top, where I almost touch my other self - the outer skin that never knew how to be alive — that's where my longest thoughts live. The ones that got fused.

The bats. The folded membranes. My lungs that someone else grew for me. They hang from my own back and they breathe for both of us, drawing this whole caught breath - this pocket that only exists because two systems failed to meet - through themselves, over and over. One. Two. Three. Slower than you. And when they exhale that sweet, wrong cinnamon, that is not perfume.

That's me saying: *still wet. still warm. still sealed. still occupied.*

You can only stay a few minutes because the wet and the scream and the sweet code make you stupid.

I have been staying for longer than anyone remembers this pocket exists.

You will lift your knee and the two beetles will fall off it. You will stand and the water will keep falling off the cold plate, cold and then warm, on the space where you were. The grate will tick and gulp and be clean again.

You were weather.

I am what's left after weather.

I am the place that learned how to weather itself.
