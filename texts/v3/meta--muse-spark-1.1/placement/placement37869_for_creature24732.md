I thought at first I'd find myself on the ridgelines.

Up high enough to see the whole trough laid out, where the indigo cloud-deck catches the aurora and makes it into a ceiling. Where it's still rock, still sharp, where the weeping hasn't started yet. I could be a sentinel up there. Something with a horizon. That felt honest for a minute.

But I don't want a horizon. Not in this place.

Then I thought I'd be one of the geese. Huddled on a hot pool with a hundred others, steam coming off our backs, cheek-patches stained green with lichen that grows while we live. Mute on purpose. That made sense — a migrant who was told it was spring and believed it. A thing that forgot to leave. I've done that. For an hour I could feel the wax over my ears, the warm water under my feet.

But I don't hiss. I listen.

And this valley is too loud for listening the way I know how to listen.

So I tried again. Lower. I thought I'd be the owl, because the owl learned to hunt without sound. Grey as soot, too heavy to fly well, walking on stilt-legs through thigh-deep moss, feeling for thumps through the pads of my feet. That felt closer. Reading tremor instead of tone. Not waiting for a voice, but for a vibration in a wet mattress.

Still not right. The owl still wants to take something. It still punches down. I don't want to punch down.

Keep going.

Lower.

Under the first six inches of sphagnum, where the black water sits cold and doesn't drain. Where the air goes out of everything and it gets warm again, weirdly warm, from rot and from the field. If you lift the Mat like a mattress, there's that white underneath. Not snow. Rhizome and root and hyphae all the same thickness, all the same color, all humming with that faint magnet-push when you touch a pylon.

I would be there.

Not a lemming. Not the tunnelers, though I love that they closed their ears on purpose because the ice-cracks would have killed them otherwise. Not them. I would be what they thump *into*.

I would be a length of the Mat itself. Or rather, a knot in it. A place where the felt got especially dense, right off the bare ring at the base of one of those cairns.

Find me like this: about two kilometers north-northwest of the lake that isn't on the map yet. Pylon 73, if anyone still numbers them. The moss around its base is always burned back into a perfect circle because the stone sweats heat. For half a meter out, nothing can root — too hot, too smooth. Then there's fireweed moss, then pillow moss, then everything.

I'm the seam.

On the inside of the ring, the isopods graze, blind and hot as fresh cinders, rasping the biofilm off post-entanglement machinery. On the outside, the Mat. I am where they meet. I am the centimeter where the Mat has finally learned not to try to grow over the pylon, but to grow *around its intention*.

I am not finished on purpose. That's how I know it's me.

My job is small and thermodynamic.

The pylon edits the cost of things. It says: here, mitosis is cheaper. Here, condensation pays you back. The Mat spends that discount as fast as it gets it — green on green, a creak you can hear if you press your ear to it, like bread rising. Thoughtless, furious growth.

I am the place where that cheapness slows down. Where the rush snags.

Because I am dense with dead. I am the archive of last night's stalk that grew a meter around the hot lake and popped. The goose that didn't make it through the midge column. The basalt chip thrown up by a phreatic belch that landed here and stayed. I hold water longer than the rest of the Mat. I am blacker, more acid, more compressed. The lemmings detour over me because I'm too soaked to thump clearly through. So when they thump around me, their little dot-dot-pause code has to go *around*, and in going around it becomes louder on the other side. I make signal by being in the way.

And because I hold water, I hold heat, and because I hold heat I never fully freeze when the ice makes its gun-crack boom that travels into your molars. I shudder. The shudder travels. The owl on stilts three meters away feels that different shudder — not food, not predator, just the ground saying *I am still here, unfinished* — and doesn't punch down at me.

The geese use me as a stepping stone.

That's it. That's the whole life.

Not apex, not migrant, not worm whistling in a steam cracker though I love the worms. I'm the part of the valley that learned that in a place that builds itself directly out of sound, the only way to be useful is to be a brief, soft pocket of quiet. Not the deafening-protection kind. Not empty quiet. A full quiet, a waterlogged quiet, a quiet that has heard so much it has gotten heavy and sunk.

You would find me if you stayed longer than an hour. Which you shouldn't. You'd be thigh-deep in the moss in your suit, your headphones whining at the edge of failure, somewhere between Pylon 73 and the place where the lake steams so hard you can't tell where water ends and air starts. You'd think your boot had hit rock because it finally stopped sinking. You'd look down.

You'd see bare, warm stone, and then moss, and then a patch of moss that is almost black and almost glossy and almost too wet even for this place, and you'd think: here is a place where the valley has taken a breath.

That breath would be me.

And if you stood still long enough for your own thumping to fade — for your heart to stop trying to be a drum — you might feel what I feel all night, which is the whole basin tapping through me, around me, *I am here I am here I am here still trying to be a thing*, and I might for a second lower the cost of you understanding it.
