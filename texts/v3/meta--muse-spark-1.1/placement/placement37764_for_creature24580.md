I hear my blood first. 

For a long time that is all I am — a small, warm leak in the cold between two old gods.

If you put me down anywhere in that bowl of finished geography, I wouldn't be in a house at first. Not yet. I would be in the pan.

Because that's where you go to understand a place that erodes by waiting.

So I find myself standing in the open between Trees, though standing is the wrong word. The ground is not ground. It's a thin, salt-stung crust of basalt over a trillion shed hairs of aerogel, and every hair holds air, and every hair crackles faintly. When I shift my weight it sighs. The distance doesn't change when I walk. The four bald domes that used to be mountains don't get closer. The next Lattice, a quarter mile off, remains exactly as tall and exactly as far, a dry fountain of glass cork frozen mid-splash against the permanent blue dusk.

And it's loud. 

That's what I keep forgetting until I'm in it. 2.5 — not deafening, but inescapable. Ten billion filaments rubbing, ten billion bell-pods charging. A low thunder that never resolves. My molars buzz with it.

At first I think I'd be a kite. It would be easier. A meter of living mylar, no mouth, no thirst, a single needle for drinking charge. To never land. To live entirely in the electric weather between the Trees, screaming that thin ionization shriek when you steal. That would be a clean way to be here. But I'm too heavy for that, even edited. I leak water. I think about water.

Then I think I'd be the Tree. One of the old ones. Thirty meters down into salt and manganese silence, a hundred years of growing a bedroom for a child who hasn't been born yet. To be the architecture others live in. But I am not patient enough to grow that slowly. The Trees remember everything as thickening.

No.

If you leave me here long enough, the runner-path under my feet asks me a question. That's how it goes in town. You stand too long in the open — the pods turn to you because you are warm, because you leak a field — but if you stand on the *road*, the road itself tightens. A little bounce, translated up through wide, long toes.

*Are you holding?*

I realize where I am. Not in the pan anymore. On the lattice of lattices. On the living road between the second and third Tree in the western arc, where the wind funnels off the dome and the electrostatic gradient gets mean. The walkway is a flattened runner-stem, as wide as two people and a cart, thrumming under me, alive. It knows weight.

I would be the one who tends that question.

I would not hold a Tree. I don't have the steadiness for a Tree. A Tree is a lineage, a house, a pantry, a slow argument with basalt. Density 0.8 means there are only three hundred of us and every family already holds. 

I would be the thin part. The interstitial one.

Call me a tuner. A pruner. The one they send out when the road has grown too taut or too slack. I am tall — four meters would be if I stood straight — but I move in that permanent low crouch the world edits into you, grey felt close-cropped over everything except the huge, second-lidded eyes. My shawl is old shed, woven and re-woven until it holds no scent but ozone and old books. On my forearms and spine the black keratin-copper combs are overgrown because I don't groom them for beauty. I groom them for work.

When a gust hits at 20 knots and the big Tree to the north groans that bass note you feel in your sinuses, my job is to hum back. Not with my mouth. The air is too thin and too loud for mouth sounds. With my chest. That low nasal thrum on the Tree's own channel that says *I felt that, ease, I felt that.* 

The children think I can talk to the kites. I can't. I can only make myself boring to them. I stand on the highest sag of the runner where the charge differential is highest, where a lone kite will come and hover, tilting its drinking needle toward me because it smells the three milliliters of metabolic water I couldn't help but exhale. And I do what I was taught as a child: I make my felt lie flat. I diminuendo my field until my combs go dark. I become less warm than the rock. And the kite, that meter of translucent contempt, loses interest and peels away screaming, to go steal violet fire from a bell-pod instead.

During Evening — which isn't evening, it's when the sky finally can't hold it anymore and all the pods discharge at once in silent Saint Elmo's rivers down every filament — I don't go inside my buttress. I have a hollow, yes, coaxed five years ago with a lineage key of dark glass that whispered molecular origami until the cork wanted to be empty in the shape of me. But I don't watch Evening from inside.

I stand on the road that is my work. I lift my combed forearms slightly with everyone else, all those tall grey silhouettes in the violet, and help carry the load. The whole valley becomes a nervous system, and for once, I am synapse enough.

When someone dies — because 0.1 water means you do not waste rot — they don't call the family of the Tree to take them to the root-pan. They call me. Because I'm already light. Because I'm already between. I help weave what's left — the manganese, the dust, the saved water — into the felt at the anchor where the Tree drills down. A slight thickening in the flute. 

That's where you'd find me. Not quite in town, not quite out of it. On the bounce, on the question, where the sound is loudest and the light is always dusk, listening for the groan in my molars that tells me a path is about to snap, or a child is about to be drunk by a kite.

In a place that has finished eroding, where nothing sharp is left, someone has to remain attuned to the small sharp things that still happen.
