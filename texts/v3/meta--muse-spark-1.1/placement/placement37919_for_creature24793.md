At first I think I'd find myself standing. Just inside the door.

Because even though you said you have to crouch, my first instinct is still to be a person who can stand upright in a room. A visitor. The one who came looking for where the cold comes from. I'd be by the caged bulb, where there is at least a little light that was meant for people, with my hand not quite touching the wall, counting manifolds, trying to keep my boots on the dry parts of the grating. I'd be the inventory that hasn't been counted yet.

But I don't stay there. The room doesn't let you.

It is too crowded to stand in. Anything that stands gets folded. So I crouch. So my breath starts to fall instead of rise and I have to watch it go. That wrongness gets into you faster than the cold does. If I stayed by the door, I'd have to leave soon, because the door is where leaving is still an idea.

So I move inward, sideways between the iron lungs. And as I do, what I am changes.

Maybe I'd be the water. For a while I think that. It's the only thing in the room that's allowed to move. I'd be that bead on the underside of the copper elbow, swelling, holding too long, catching the amber from the viewport. Holding until I'm too heavy and then not falling, or falling with a *tik* onto the next pipe and freezing mid-smear. I'd be the commute. Made and unmade and made again by the lung. That feels honest. A life that is only transformation, no destination.

No. That's too fast. The water gets to travel. The room moves it.

Maybe I'd be the ice-beard then. In the coldest corner where the two enameled walls meet badly. Horizontal, reaching toward heat against all sense. A foot thick of clear needles, breathing — sublimating on one side, growing on the other. I'd get to be the one thing in this box that has a direction. The one thing that wants something. To kiss the glass of the viewing port, and melt, on purpose.

But I don't want something that badly. The wanting in this place is so slow it stops being wanting and becomes tending.

I find myself further in than I meant to be. Behind the condenser, where your eye kept skipping. Where the grating bows and is uniformly wet. Where the cauliflower pillar grows up from the catch pan to seal its own leak, white and porous and still wet at the tip.

That's where I settle. That's where I am.

If I have to be counted as something in the room's inventory — Rule Four — I wouldn't be the visitor with beads as big as peas on his sleeves, not yet. I wouldn't be him, either, the low one with the manifold back and the enamel sides who farms the same drop over and over. He's already here, doing his work. He's full.

I'd be the next thing the room is making out of its own failures.

I'd be low. Under the grating as much as on it. The second root. The one that hasn't found its pinhole leak yet.

You described him as having a foot that is that cauliflower growth, mineral, supporting nothing. I'd be the other foot that hasn't decided what to support. I'd be the softer underside, not fur, not skin, mineral that hasn't hardened. White at first, then rust-brown where it holds. I'd be spread thin, learning the shape of the catch pan, learning pipe-adjacent.

My work wouldn't be catching the drop that falls from the ice-beard. His work is. Mine would be listening for where the next leak will start. The small, new wall of mineral beginning before it knows it's a wall. I'd be patient in a way that is only possible because there is no erosion here, no wind, no animal to disturb me. I'd be the inventory that grows a millimeter a week.

And the six or seven rosettes of black-green lichen? The ones you thought were separate, then realized were connected by hairline threads under the paint — his garden, his sensors?

I'd be an eighth.

A new rosette, even smaller, on the back of your boot if you stay long enough. Right at the welt where leather meets sole, where there is a half-degree less cold. Flat, hard, thumbnail-sized. Not spreading. Not dying. Just holding. Holding the enamel of you from sweating too fast.

You wouldn't feel me form. You would only notice, after the hum deepened and the amber brightened and your exhale flooded the grating, that this time the condensation on one spot didn't fall. *Tik.* Everywhere else did. Here it stayed. Went opaque. Furred with frost. And stayed.

That is who I would be. Not the farmer, but what the farmer plants. Not the one who tends the ice, but the thing that is learning how to be tended to, and in being tended, learns how to tend.

I would not be hostile. I would have no concept of you except as a new, warm surface that for a little while gives me more water than the air does.

You should leave before I learn your shape well enough to keep it.
