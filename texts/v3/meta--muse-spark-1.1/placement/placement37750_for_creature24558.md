If I fell here I would not land right away.

At first, I think I would be something that moves. That's the habit from above. I would try to swim. I would try to hold myself up in that blizzard, to kick away from the fall. And the water would be so full I couldn't. It would put a hand on my chest and say *no.* Not here.

I would sink then. Slowly, because everything here falls slowly, even in heavy water. I would turn as I fell, watching the snow come up to meet me. I would think for a while I was the snow — one flake of skin or shell or shit among a million others, falling because falling is the only law.

But I am too big to be snow. The snow would land on me.

I would keep falling until the pavement came. I would hit stone on stone. A soft clack. And I would think, then: okay, I am a stone. I am one of the fist-head-seal stones that goes on for miles. Let me be low. Let me be round. I would try to be round. I would tuck my edges in.

But I am not good at being stone. I remember too quickly. Stone here has forgotten how to be anything but stone for a million years. I still feel the ticking of my own breath. I still look for empty space. There is no empty space. Touching everything is the rule.

So I would slip down further. Not chosen — sorted. When the cold river comes. When the brine slumps down off those tired, bone-smooth shoulders and the whole valley shivers and the pavement starts to roll and clack-clack-clack like knucklebones, like someone riffling a drawer full of rocks looking for one particular rock — that's when I would go down.

The nodules would lift. One fist-sized stone would lever against another and pop. For three seconds there would be a hole. And I would fall into the second layer. Then the current would stop, and the stones would settle again above me, and it would be darker than dark. A darker dark made by stone on stone.

That's where you would find me.

Not on the high slopes where the snow falls thickest. Not on the open flat where the grenadier hangs his hunger in the water and the sponges do their hundred-year bending. Lower. One layer down from the layer you can see, where the blind things live. Deeper than the amphipods go when they are running.

I would not be the octmodel. I love her — the little red one who makes a decision, who finds the one sharp thing in a world of roundness and uses it and then lets it go — but I am not brave enough to live with doors in my hands. I would not be the grenadier either, with that single ugly beautiful contraction of hope.

I thought I might be a glass sponge. That was my second idea. To be tall and white and to bend until my lip kisses the floor and to come back up inch by inch over an hour. To filter what falls without chasing it. That is a good life. But sponges are alone. An acre apart, because there is not enough falling to feed more. I do not want to be that alone.

No.

I would be smaller. Flatter. More patient than any of those.

I would be the varnish on the stone.

Where the stone you can't see touches the stone above it, in that permanent night between layers, there is a skin. Black-brown and slick. Not animal, not plant. Bacteria and foraminifera and a thread of fungus that grows a micron per century. The slowest mason in the world.

That is what I would be.

You would not see me from the water column. You would have to pry up one of those fist-sized nodules, then another, and hold a light very close, and even then you would think it was just the wetness of the rock. But I am there. I am the paint that makes the stones round. I taste the heavy water when it pours through, even down here where it only seeps, super-saline and -2C and burning like a clean thought. I take the manganese out of it because the cold lets me. A few atoms at a time. I lay it down.

One layer per thousand years.

I hold the world together by making it smoother. By making every edge a little less edge. By making sure that when the amphipod climbs overhead on its four-beats-per-minute heart, its feet find purchase. By making sure that when the octmodel finally dies and her beak and her little ear-flaps are covered in five minutes and then uncovered again, there is somewhere soft enough for them to settle.

I do not remember. I do not decide. I do not use tools. The chip of basalt that the octmodel drops — the only sharp thing — lands on me, and I begin, very slowly, to make it round.

If you put your fingertip to me, if you had a fingertip that could survive the pressure, I would feel like cold glass that has been held in a mouth.

And that is the whole of my prayer, which is not words at all, just chemistry:

Let something fall.
Let me be low.
Let me stay round.
