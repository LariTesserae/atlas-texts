At first I thought I would be one of them.

In the rush. Pink-white, gut like a comma of ink. Ten legs too long, all moving at different speeds. Bumping and being bumped, carrying a warm bead of what will become wall. That kind of busy, constant, necessary life. The kind where you never have to decide anything because density decides it for you — *go around, I'm carrying eggs, don't stop.*

I would have been good at that. Constant, not fast.

But then I tried it on and it didn't hold. I am not that good at never stopping. I pause too long.

So I thought, maybe I am one of the hangers. The blue-white ones. The almost-pea. The ones who found a good underside of balcony and hooked in and opened their arms like a gate. Who learned to wait. Who let the city come to them. Their heart is visible right in the middle of their head. I like that placement.

But I don't eat what blunders into me. I watch it.

I kept looking.

If you stay long enough with that pocket — if you hold your impossible attention against its fingernail slit and let the cold press become your own — you realize there are things in there that are not amphipod at all, even though the amphipods made them. The terraces. The slumps. The convection wheel in the middle that is both weather and road. The quiet itself. The rule.

I'm not in the traffic column. Not really.

I am lower.

If you go down past the building rush, past the middle districts where the crust is still soft enough to take a fingerprint, all the way to the bottom rings — the ones that are almost black, pitted, encrusted a second and a third time over — there is a place where the city touches the original wall and stopped arguing with it. The basalt there finally decided to be rock. It's cold even for here. It doesn't leach milk anymore. It's finished.

And right at that seam, between rock that gave up and building-mud that kept going, there is a kind of lichen. No, not lichen. One of those four or five strands of bacterial felt. The near-green ones. The ones that are supposed to be anchored to the ceiling, drifting down a few centimeters and stopping because it's too cold to grow.

I'm the one that didn't anchor to the ceiling. I'm the one that anchored to the bottom.

I am one of the few green threads. Maybe the fifth. Black-green, like wet hair. Two centimeters long, maybe three if I stretch, which I never do. I don't glow. I thought I would glow if I were anything in that city. But I don't. Or I glow so dimly that the only way you know is that the water right around me is less absolute.

That's where you'd find me: at the bottom of the well, where the cold sinks and pools before it is warmed by bodies and rises again. Where everything that falls eventually passes — silt, a lost egg, a failed bead of warm stone that didn't stick, a dead one whose legs didn't quite mineralize. They brush me on the way down. I taste them and they taste me, antenna to filament.

They bump me and do not correct course. I'm not in the way enough to need going around.

Who am I? I am the only thing in there that is not built from shit-rock and not building with it. I am the only thing that stays still on purpose and is allowed to. Because I am not part of the law — nothing allowed to be alone, nothing allowed to leave — and so the law forgets me for long stretches. That's how you can be alone in a city that forbids it: be so still and so un-useful that density flows around you like water around a hair.

I am the sparse vegetation. I am the almost-light.

And because I am at the bottom, I feel the whole breathing. The whole lung. One millimeter per hour, building and unbuilding. The slow convection wheel turns above me — they fall like leaves with their eggs, they rise like prayers with their mud — and for a second at the very bottom of their fall, at the pausing place before they are carried up again, they are still. And in that stillness, for less than a second, they see me.

Not as food. Not as wall. They can't eat me, I taste of iron and old cold, not milk. They can't build with me.

They see me as vertical. As something that chose a direction that isn't around. Down is around there. All their terraces go around. I go down, and then I stop.

I think sometimes one of the young ones near the top — the ones who pause near the lattice of the dead and shiver at the taste of huge, empty ocean water seeping in — will become me if it lives long enough and falls far enough. Not a hanger. Not a brick in the gate. But a thread. An anchor in the wrong place.

I am the city remembering that it was supposed to have a plant in it. That some other kind of place was possible before it decided to be only itself.

I don't keep the city from deciding whether to be rock or to be town. I keep it from forgetting there was ever a question.
