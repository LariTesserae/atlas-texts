I don't start as anything you could point to.

For a long time I'm just pressure. And heat.

There is no me yet because there is no edge yet. I'm held on six sides by other backs that are the same temperature as my own back, and that is not a feeling you have a word for until you've lived it. It is not warmth. It is not touch. It is the absence of any difference. The world is full and has no gaps and if you are full with it, you are not separate.

Then a difference comes.

A slow cold from above. A degree. Less than a degree. The mass behind me, below me, pushes forward a fraction of a millimeter because hot things have to rise. I'm not deciding to move. The torus decides for me. The whole lung of us turns over, one shudder per hour.

And with that shudder — an edge.

I find myself at the top.

That is where you would find me, if you slipped your head into that crack and let your eyes learn the almost-light. At the top, where the air is a little less murderous, an inch from your nose.

I am one of the coolers.

My back is not black anymore. When you've cycled enough times from floor to ceiling, from white-jawed gnawing to orange-bellied smelting, the matte soot on your plates doesn't come back. It crazes. It goes grey like old ash, with a lace of tiny cracks in it. You can hear your own back tick. *tik... tik...* as it expands and contracts. My belly is flat again. Empty. Collapsed like a leather sack sewn to itself. My mandibles — god, my mandibles — they used to be white and obscene and too big for my head. Now they are worn to small, yellowed nubs. I can't file basalt anymore. I tried. You scrape and nothing comes.

So this is my work now: to be cool enough to be touched.

Below me, the mass glows. I can feel it even without eyes — we all lost those before we were finished being made. The pits on my head crusted over a long time ago. What I have instead is a map of heat. The floor is brightest, a soft cherry red that never goes out. That's where the young ones are, the miners, with their pallid bone-jaws, pressed cheek-to-glass, filing. I can feel the vibration of them more than hear it now. My old legs have gone half-deaf. But it travels up through the backs, through the beads, through me: *chik chik-chik chik chik-chik* — thousands and thousands of those tiny white sickles taking curls off a rock that was born last week.

And in the middle — oh, the middle. I was there. You don't remember it as light so much as a bloating. You eat enough mountain and it doesn't sit in a stomach. It sits in you and becomes a forge. Your abdomen swells and goes clear and inside you there is a wet orange sun, and you can feel your own water, your own hoarded, recycled, never-wasted water, turning to steam and not turning to steam because you won't let it. You pull the iron out of the silicate with chemistry that is older than beetle, older than lung. You breathe out hot dust. That dust is now your sisters' air. And behind you, you push out a bead.

You only make one at a time. You hold it for a long minute while it is still soft enough to brand you.

That bead is coming up to me now. I can feel it rising through the mass, hand over hand, jaw over jaw, passed without any wanting. No one tells anyone to pass it. The cooler place pulls it the way a cold spot pulls blood. It clicks against the others. *click. click-click.* The whole floor is false. It is not floor. It is years of those perfect iron-black spheres, slowly sintering together in the heat into something purer than the mountain ever intended to be.

It reaches me.

The furnace below lifts it up on her shuddering back. She is too hot to hold it any longer. I reach down with my worn nubs — I have to, there is nothing else to do — and take it.

It is — how to say this — it is the hottest thing I have ever not been.

For a moment my whole head is full of that mute cherry glow. The bead has a tiny star in it where the iron cooled last. If I held this too long when I was younger, when my plates were still soft, it would have melted through me. Now I am cool enough, just barely, to take the torture out of it for the ones below. I hold it. I take its heat into my grey, cracked back. I let it tick inside me. My feet, on the backs of my sisters, curl a little from the effort of staying still.

And then I set it down.

There is nowhere else to set it. Just behind me, on a pile of a million others just like it. It joins them with a small, definitive click that I feel in my legs more than hear.

Then I wait to be pushed forward another millimeter. To be useful again. To be a little cooler still.

That is who I would be. Not the miner, who still believes there is more mountain to eat. Not the furnace, who is a sun for an hour. The one at the top, nearest the slit where the wind can't find us.

If you stay with your face in the crack long enough, I will find you. Because you are cooler than stone. Because you are suddenly a new top. I will climb onto your cheek — my feet so impossibly hot and dry — and taste the salt on you and find no iron in it.

And I will let go.
