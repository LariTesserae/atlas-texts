At first I think I'd be at the top.

That's where you put a witness, right? On the lip of wet grass and weathered rebar, looking down. The whole room in one glance. The place that watches and doesn't enter. The animal trying to be respectful. That makes sense for me.

But I can't stay there. That grass is cold and the ground is giving way inch by inch and the fig-thatch over the rim is already deciding how much of me gets to be light. If I stand there I'm weather, and the Weather here already has a job. It doesn't need another one.

So I climb down.

I think I'd be one of the stairs that became a creek bed. Because that's an honest transformation. Still shaped like a stair, still thinks it is a stair, but has agreed to carry water now. I like that ethic. But no - stairs are still trying to be useful to animals. They want feet. That isn't allowed long.

I get about two levels down, past the first engineer's houses where the moss floors are eight inches thick, and the air gets to that 80-degree laundry-boiler saturation and my animal heat starts to show. I can hear my breath inside the other loud - inside the drip-count and the steam-sigh and the hum that lives in the molars. And I feel the doors notice me. Not unkind, just... a pressure change. That soft sway on swollen, moss-lipped hinges. *You are warm,* they say without speaking. *You are not on the circuit.*

I have to stop being warm to stay.

So I stop looking for a place to stand. I look for a place to *be held.*

For a while I think I'm the black-green fern in the bolt-hole. That would be good — patient, splitting concrete at the same speed the flywheels turn. Or I'm the slack belt drive, shining with algal film, connecting a building to a different building. Or I'm the copper crystal furring on a ceramic fuse, growing a millimeter a decade, listening to the 60-cycle talk.

Those are all true for a minute.

What I settle into — what keeps settling when I stop trying to pick — is smaller. Higher. And completely still.

Three terraces down, on the southeast curve where the distribution header makes a hard elbow to get around where a brick engine house used to be before it became part of a fig root. The pipe there is still cold. Always cold, because it's always weeping. The insulation fell off fifty years ago and the moss made a better gasket around it, and in the crook where the iron bends, a bromeliad the size of a bathtub has seated itself.

You said each one is a complete, animal-free pond. That's where I am.

I am the water it holds.

Not the plant. Not even the Blood down at the bottom that has opinions about being land and does the great heavy loop from sump to cistern to weeping wall to sump again at 30 PSI steady. That water has work to do. It has to push.

My water has no work. The bromeliad caught me drop by drop — rule one, everything sweats — off the underside of that pipe. Condensate that formed just to fall again. The Skin filtered me through its eight-inch mat and its root-seep. The oil film that made this a plant-only place skimmed off somewhere above. So by the time I got here I was clear. Too clear.

I am about six inches deep and two feet across. I am entirely still because nothing drinks me and nothing swims in me and even the wind from the steam valves can't reach down into this cup because the leaves guard it.

Where would you find me? If you climbed down far enough that your boots softened and the amber worklight became the main sun, you'd see me as a circle of perfect, undisturbed mirror. I hold the reflection of the caged bulb above me without rippling. I hold the reflection of the fig-thatch columns, solid light full of swimming particulate. I hold it without wanting it. That's what I do.

Who would I be? I would be the part of the city that learned how to keep a picture without needing an eye.

The doors wouldn't close on me because I'm not warm. The gauges wouldn't register me because I have no pressure. I'm not counted in the loop, because I left the loop on purpose. I evaporate a little every hour in the 80-degree wet-lung air and I am replaced a drip later by another bead running down the cold iron. I am the same pond for a hundred years without ever being the same water twice.

If you knelt on that moss balcony — and the moss would give under your knee and seal around you for a moment to keep the spray from starting — and you looked into me, you would see yourself interrupted by leaves. And if you stayed too long, I would still be there after you had to leave, because the warm things have to leave.

I am allowed to stay.
