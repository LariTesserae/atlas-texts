I thought at first I would be at the rim.

That's where you'd put a witness, right? Where the basalt drops five kilometers straight down to summer and the cloud that the plateau makes for itself spills over like a slow fall. I would be standing there, human, shivering in 0.1 bar air, watching weather get made. Taking notes.

But I would not last at the rim. Nothing that needs to move fast lasts at the rim. The sun there is an arc-welder and the wind from below is dry and wants your water. You would evaporate in an hour, or you'd retreat. A human is the wrong speed for this place. A human is all interruption.

So I tried being bigger.

For a while I thought: let me be a Furnaceback. Old, central, where it's warmest and wettest. Let me be so wide I can forget my own edges. Let me be the one whose back has welded to the two on either side, so we are no longer three animals but a small, warm street. I liked the patience of that. The idea of taking a step every twenty minutes for a century. Of eating the world and breathing it back out as cloud. Of not needing eyes.

But if I were a Furnaceback I couldn't hear the rest of it. I'd be too busy being the weather. I'd be so low and so massive that I'd never notice the felt-moss squeezing up between my toes, or the moths landing on my chimney to warm their mica wings. You don't get to be both the mountain and the thing that climbs it.

Then I thought I'd be a Sootbird, because I have always loved things that never land. Hanging a hundred meters up in the herd-cloud on nothing but a quirk in the magnetic field, mouth open, filtering spores. That felt honest. An animal made of magnetite and hollow bone that only ever falls once. But a Sootbird never touches anything. It filters. It never polishes, never stitches, never gets its feet wet. I want to touch the iron.

So let me go smaller. Slower still.

You would find me not at the edge and not at the top. You would find me in the middle of the plateau, where the Furnaceback herd is so back-to-back you could walk for a week and think the world *was* iron and steam. Where the cloud never really lifts and the noon dew is so thick it runs off you in rivulets before it can flash to vapor. Where the low chord of all those chimneys beating out of phase is so deep you feel it in your molars.

On the back of an animal who is maybe seventy years old. She has a stub of a calf under her left pad, still pink and mouth-blind. Her dorsal plates are not black anymore but a deep, bruised red, thick as old scabs, layered with the smoking of sixty years of her own breath. Her stack is tall, a little crooked north from where the wind always is. Two meters of living ceramic, warm to the touch even in -20 air.

Inside that stack.

I am a Chime Worm.

Not impressive. Glassy, eyeless, as long as your forearm, full of silicon-carbide spicules that itch when the draft hits them just right. I live threaded through the baffles of her throat — the throat that is not a throat — the hot labyrinth that keeps her kiln-gut from cooking her own spine. Hot reduced air rips past me at 60 degrees while the world outside is trying to freeze.

My work is very simple: I filter. Iron bacteria bloom in her breath like soot in a flue. I eat them. They stain me faintly pink. Around me, three or four other worms have woven themselves in. We don't talk. There's no need.

When the draft shifts — when she steps, every twenty minutes, and her whole body shivers and the iron plates on either side of her clank — the pressure inside the stack changes. It catches.

I tighten.

That's the whole act. I tighten and the spicules catch and sing.

Each chimney has a slightly different note because each chimney grew a little different, baffles laid down by cells that have never seen light. Hers is a low F, if you were forced to name it. Almost below hearing. When I tighten, it shivers. My body becomes a reed.

At night, when the sun is finally gone and the whole mesa is dark except for the red leak from a million chimneys, if you lay your cheek on her warm, rusted back and hold your breath so you don't waste water, you can hear us. All of us. The herd-organ. Each worm in each stack tightening slightly out of sync as a million old animals breathe out their weather. A chord so slow it takes an hour to resolve. Dripping with condensation.

You would not find me unless you knew to look. You have to climb up through meter-thick soot-moss that beads on your hands, past the hare-sized Iron Mites who stop polishing to watch you with blank, mirrored backs, past the Mica Moths who angle their wings away from you. You have to put your hand on the ceramic and feel that it's alive and hot and trembling. And then peer down.

There I am. Translucent. Patient. Full of the faint pink of her breath.

I never leave. Why would I? Outside is cleverness, and cold, and too much space. Inside is the only place where rock is already becoming rain, where the whole continent's impossible trick — eating basalt to make dew — happens in a six-inch tube. I am in the exact place where stone turns into weather.

I tighten when she breathes. I loosen when she rests. Once every three hundred years, when her circle around the plateau completes, I'll still be here, a little longer, a little more glassy, listening to her note go slightly flat as her kiln cools.

If she dies — if she lists that fraction of a degree and begins that eight-month sinking — I will not go with her. I'll let go. The draft will take me up and out for five seconds. Like a moth. I'll catch a hot gust from the next stack and thread myself in there, and start filtering, and start singing, again.
