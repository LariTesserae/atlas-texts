I don't start upright. Not for long.

If you put me there I think at first I am standing — because the place lets you, because you can see for a day in any direction and the human animal wants to use that. So I am on one of the bald crowns, one of those breaths under the sheet where the ancient back lifts a little higher. The rock is warm through my boots even in the morning. My calves are already lying to me about whether I'm going uphill.

But I don't stay standing. You can't, here. The light is too high and too honest and it makes a short, stupid shadow of you. There's nowhere to cast it. The wind comes over the millions of domes like wind over cobbles, and it finds you as the only thing sticking up.

So I go to the next thing I would be. I think I would be the white tree. Because if you have to be something vertical on the Top, you should at least be stunted and wind-combed and forgiven for it. I would be up near one of the old swells where the ground thins enough that the pavers don't quite cover, and I would be rattling my small hard leaves. But that doesn't hold either. The tree is alone in a way the place doesn't allow. Nothing else is alone here.

I kneel. That is closer.

When you kneel the categories fall off, like you said. The ground that was ground becomes not-ground. I put my hand down and the warmth under it is different. Stone-warm and animal-warm are two different kinds of patience. The plate under my palm is dusty, bone-pale with that faint pink underneath, exactly the color of *this* stone, not stone in general. If I breathe on it the dust stays. It is exuded. Made.

If I'm going to be anything here for longer than a visit, I have to be low enough that the light finds me sideways.

So maybe a paver. That suggests itself so strongly you have to try it on and then take it off again. I want to be one of them for a while, the field that knows east. In the morning I would feel the gradient as weight, not sight — the seam between my plates opening a fraction to a strip of epithelium that only knows brighter and dimmer — and the magnetite in my oldest plate, my apical one, remembering north while the rest of me wants east. I would turn with everyone else, five degrees while the human behind the tree was looking away. Perfectly. Not approximately.

But I am not old enough to be a paver. I haven't learned how to graze a day. I don't know how to lap cyanobacteria and salt and make the stone pit so it will hold more water next time. I haven't lived long enough to go pale on the bottom from other backs being on top of me.

If you leave me there long enough, the place will tell me where to go. It does it with water.

During the morning I wander the pavement — because I can still wander, a meter is a day for them — and I am irritated by my boots. They are a swell that doesn't belong. The pavers come onto them, not aggressive, just indifferent, just incorporating. At noon my laces are gone under a slow tide.

By afternoon the cloud has arrived. It doesn't rain, it settles. The runnels sheen. The lichen blushes. Every seam on every back around me gapes and lifts and flattens into a bowl. I hear the wicking if I put my ear down close enough. A hundred thousand microscopic hairs moving water uphill.

And the towers drink first.

You would find me there, finally. Not on the bald crown, not by the tree. Down in one of the shallow pans where the old ones have accreted. In a ring that has already collapsed, or is collapsing. The walls are at knee height now, ghosts of plates visible in the secretion, cold honey layers. Inside, the air is always a few degrees cooler and it beads even after the fog has gone, even hours after. The ground inside is not ground.

That's where I am. In the footprint of a failed lung, where the stone has been kept damp so long it stopped being stone.

And what am I?

Not a paver. Not yet ground.

If I have stayed long enough to need a local name for them — and I have, because someone has to, because "there is no local name because no one local in the human sense has ever stayed long enough to need one" and I got tired of being not-local — then I am the thing that lives in the exception. I am the black-green.

The moss.

The only true green the Top allows itself. The thing that has no other strategy except to live where a tower forgot to stay up. One centimeter thick, in the bottom of the circle, holding the wet that the pavers saved for their young. The young ones, translucent as rice paper, crowd around me at the foot of the still-standing towers, drinking through their whole bodies. Here in the dead one, they have left, and left me this.

Or — no, more honestly — I am the human that became the soil the moss needed. Because the moss can't come unless the rock has been pitted and kept and turned for decades, and then the wall has to fall, and then something has to lie down in it and rot very slowly and without stink, thinning into salt. I lay down in the ring when the cloud came. The pavers didn't mind. A boot is a swell, a body is a better swell, shadier, more likely to gather dew. They flowed onto and over me at their glacial rate.

At dusk they close. One by one by millions. You said it was worth kneeling for again and you were right. The creak — that almost subsonic house-settling creak, plate edge against plate edge, as the field that was bowls becomes stone again. The water they caught held in the dark, damp space between foot and rock.

In my ring, in my little garden that exists nowhere else, I do not quite close. I stay open a fraction longer, because moss does that. Because the red mite that glitters in my turf needs one more minute of damp.

I hold what I can.
