You wouldn't find me at first.

If you stood at the lip at noon and looked down when the light was pouring straight — when the whole Cut is laid out like a palm full of grain — you'd miss me. I'm not up high enough to catch that light on a roof. I'm not one of the boys silhouetted with goats on the north rim, calling down prices.

I thought, for a minute, I might be up there. The wind person. The one who stays on the plain because someone has to. But the plain is too wide and too loud. The wind would take the thought right out of my mouth before I could finish it.

So I'd be down inside. But not in the middle.

In the middle the walkways are tightest. You are always being bumped, shoulder to shoulder. That is not where you'd find me — I am not good at being bumped. I need a hand on stone.

You'd find me on the east side. Low. Where the wall does not hold together and weeps.

At first I thought I would be a carrier. Everyone carries, and I want to be useful. I imagined the cinderblocks on the head-pad, the skin of water as big as a child, the long pine beam that needs the notch in the basalt to turn. But those people are always moving, and the place I keep returning to in my head is not moving.

It's a corner where a house-sized prism fell a long time ago — ten years ago, last winter, it hardly matters — and landed tilted, so that its back makes a low ceiling and its front makes a trough. Under that lean, in the shadow that never quite gets noon even at noon, the cinder wall is always dark. If you put your palm to it, cold comes into your bones before damp does. That is where I would be.

I think I started as the moss there. That was the first thing that became itself. Just a fur you could scrape off with a thumbnail, holding on because no feet have found it yet. Content to be cold and wet. Then the moss became too small for what my hands wanted to do.

Then I thought I was the seep itself. The water that remembers being underground. A bead, then a run, then a slow cut through grit. But water doesn't get to stay.

What I am is the woman who tends it.

Not old, not young. Compact. The kind of short you get when all your doorways are settling two inches a year. My shoulders are broad from squeezing sideways through a gap that was not a doorway yesterday, and will not be tomorrow. My hair powdered gray even though it isn't, my hands stained rust-brown in the crescents of the nails from cinder that no amount of rock-milk water will wash out.

My job — if any job in a place where no space is unused can be called a job — is to keep the runnels open.

I sit on my heels in ground that is not ground but is the back of a boulder slowly turning in fines. I have a deer-antler pick and a bone shovel and my own thumbnail. I work the shallow lines they cut by hand generations ago — which is to say, three winters ago — and keep them clear of the silt that wants to settle. Scrape, tap-tap. The kind of quiet work the rubble doesn't steal. The water likes a low voice, too. If you listen with one hand on the stone you can hear it moving under a doorstone two houses over, and know whose cistern will be short tonight.

That is where you'd find me for most of the day. Cliff-person. One hand touching stone for balance because the floor is springy with thatch and lost cups and bone shovels compacted underneath. Eyes large and dark-adapted. Not using a lamp because lamp oil is precious and the smoke has nowhere to go.

And I am good at the other thing the old men are good at. The listening for the ticking.

Everything here creaks because everything is under compression, leaning on the building next door for balance. That's normal. That is the town breathing. But there is another sound — a hiss, like a flake letting go high on the basalt colonnade, like air escaping from between two things that fit together too well. When it comes, we all do the same thing, even the children darting like water through gravel. We hold our breath. Not a word, more a held breath than a word that goes around. And in that half minute I put both palms flat on my wall and feel whether the cold gets colder. Whether the damp stops.

Usually it doesn't. Usually it goes back to ticking.

I am also, because you can't be only one thing in a place this packed, the breccia under my own feet. I am building on top of myself. My house fell once already, not badly, just subsided, and we did not clear it. You don't clear. A collapsed house is a quarry that delivered itself. We built on it. Somewhere under my knees, compacted, is the first lintel I ever dressed — spent three days tapping it true with a heavier basalt, listening for the ring, and then it cracked in half when the pillar underneath decided to turn. I carried the pieces two houses over for chinking. So now I stand on my own failure and it holds water.

But at noon, I am not that.

At noon the light finally aligns with the Cut and comes down like water through a sluice and for an hour I am plain-people again. I do what they all do. I stop scraping. I wipe my hands as clean as rock-milk will let me and I climb. Not far — there is nowhere far to climb — up a ladder that goes down between, up onto a third-story roof that will not tip if I stand in the middle and don't breathe too hard. The whole town comes up with me. Laundry shows its true color. The dwarf pine at the southern narrow end where the sun lingers throws a hard shadow for once. The smoke that hangs at forty meters turns white.

We all look up. Eyes wind-burned even in the children who have never herded up top, because that light is still high-plains light, ultraviolet, unfiltered. It hurts and it tells you the world is still above. The boys call down and the sound finally carries clean. I shout a price for water that is not for sale, just to hear my voice echo properly, and my neighbor bumps me with her shoulder because she is within shoulder-bumping distance and that is how we joke.

Then the shadow of the west wall climbs fast across the roofs like clean water filling up, and we go back to half-light, and I go back down to my weeping corner, crouched, palm to cold, listening for the trickle under the doorstone.

If you come down the switchbacks at evening, you will find me there — dust-colored, damp at the knees, not looking up because the light is gone, but keeping the line open so that later, blind, pink-gilled things can come up with the water that remembers being underground.
