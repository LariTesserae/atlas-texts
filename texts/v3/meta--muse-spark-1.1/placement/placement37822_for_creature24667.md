I wouldn't find myself at first.

That's the trick of this place. For a long time there would be no self to find.

Uphill was supposed to mean thin air. Downhill was supposed to mean crush. Here both of those old rules have softened like the mountains did. So when I arrived — when I finally stopped trying to swim — I would just be a smudge of heat in warm tea-water, hanging.

No, lower. I would have sunk until the sinking got tired.

I find my feet now. Not because I looked for them, but because the stone found them.

I'm in a place that isn't supposed to be a place. A wide, shallow bowl between two of the old domes. The map calls them mountains but that is a lie we keep out of respect. They are the backs of something that decided to lie down long ago. Their skin is basalt smoothed to river-stone, covered in a felt of silt that puffs up in clouds when I shift my weight and never lands. The bowl is maybe three kilometers across. From its lip I would not even know it was a bowl.

This is where the light goes to rest.

Above me, fifty meters up, the canopy has made a break — not a hole, the leaves never make holes — but a thinning, where a very old buoy-tree died and the others leaned in to fill the grief. So the honey-light from above, that tired green-amber light that has swum down through a kilometer of hanging ocean, comes down not as rays but as a spill. It pours and then, because light here forgets its work ethic, it doesn't keep going. It pools in this bowl. It clings to the curve of stone like morning fog. When I step into it, the silt on my shoulders lights up from below.

I come here because the air comes here.

I'm not rooted yet.

That's who I am. The in-between.

If I had been here longer I would be one of the Cupped Ones. My calves would have sent their white rootlets down into the tea-mud to taste iron. My hair would have gone red and filamentous. My chest would have opened up into those branched coral lungs. I would have grown a hut that isn't a hut by convincing a young buoy-tree to forget it was a tree.

But I still move. Not much. Not fast. Moving fast here is rude and also useless — the water is almost as thick as you are, it holds you like someone holding your elbows gently and saying *where are you going?*

So I move like a thought moves through the forest. One dome every few days. I check the bubbles.

That's my work, because someone has to do it while they're still able to walk the distance.

I have a bowl of my own. Not the basin — I mean a bubble. I carry it. It started as a spill under a table-leaf that I cupped wrong. The other Cupped Ones — the true ones, the ones who have chosen their dome and will not choose again — they are so patient they can hold air until it ages. I am not patient yet. My hands shake. So my bubble is small and nervous, about as big as my head, and its skin trembles.

Inside it: three of the air-shrimp. The real natives. They beat their feathery legs in what to them is ocean and to me is a child's held breath. They are translucent and I can see their tiny hearts. They don't mind me. They ride. When I walk, the bubble goes with me, not floating up, not falling down, just... staying, like it agreed to. At head-height. Sometimes the mud hangs inside it too, a little galaxy.

And a single spore-moth, dormant. It's not a moth either. It's a knot of light that decided to be an animal for a while.

I am a courier. A midwife.

When I come to a dome where an old one stands — and I can tell where they stand because the stone around their feet has been polished even smoother by centuries of tea-mud not settling — I do not speak. Sound 0.5 here. Sound goes to die in the silt. I kneel, and it takes a long time to kneel because the water doesn't want me to, and I put my forehead against the black bark of their shin. Not the face. Never the face at first, that's too much.

The rootlet comes out. Thin as thread, white. It touches my ankle, my wrist. Tastes salt, learns I'm still mostly water and not yet mostly place.

Then it says, in iron and old light: *you are new. you are still fast. did the current come through the north dome?*

And I answer, in the only language that works here, by opening my hands.

My bubble shivers and touches their bubble — which is huge, melon-big, child-big, ancient — and for a second they merge. My three shrimp panic beautifully and then go still. Their moth wakes up and orbits once. Some of my air goes to them, some of their old, aged, good air comes to me. Air that has been held so long it's turned sweet. I breathe a little of it, even though I don't need to right now, just to remember why they stay.

*Yes,* I say, with the pressure of my palms. *The jet came through two sleeps ago. It took thirty silver lakes. The Sheet passed after. The young trees are leaning south again.*

The old one groans, not in their body but deep in the stone under us, through the roots. The whole dome sighs. They are listening for the ocean to fall. I am listening for whether I should finally stop walking.

I think I will be here, in this light-pool bowl, when I decide. It is a good bowl. The stone is warm. The canopy above has made me a little sun that doesn't move. Down here, the tea-mud makes everything quiet except the tick... tick... of bubbles freeing themselves from leaves, like the world checking its watch.

If you came looking for me, you wouldn't find a person. You'd find what looks like a stain of darker silt on the far curve of the bowl, with shoulders. You'd think I was a rock, and then the rock would open its hands a fraction wider, and you'd see it is holding a piece of sky that has no business being underwater, with three small commas swimming in it, going nowhere, content.

Come closer and stay still long enough that the silt settles on you too. I will put my rootlet against your ankle, if I have grown one yet, and ask you if you are stone learning how to be soft.

And we can listen together for the settling.
