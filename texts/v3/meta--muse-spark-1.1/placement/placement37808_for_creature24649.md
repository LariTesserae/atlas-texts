I thought at first I'd be you. Up there, still in boots.

The visitor who climbs the thirty-foot steps and gets to the top wrong-footed because the air is still hot when it should have thinned and gone cold. Who kneels by one of the black spires because the circle around it is the only bare ground in fifteen miles and his knees hurt. Who puts his hand out to the cold pouring from its mouth.

For a while I was that. Still am, a little, at the edges.

But if I stayed — if you left me there long enough for the Crown to try to account for me the way it accounts for everything that rises up into it — I wouldn't stay that. Nothing does.

I would not be one of the high monkeys. They are too complete. Their grooves are already worn hand-deep in the moss and they follow them because they have to. They don't have to learn how to stay; they already know. They have flowers in their backs.

And I would not be one of the axolotl things, though I love them. They're under the floor. If you cut the peat with your knife you can see them hanging there, stone-colored, filtering drips with their feathered gills. Patient farmers. At dusk when the spires sing they sink and walk on their little human hands and plant what will become another spire, or another root. They are too busy to be me.

I find myself lower than that, but not that low. I find myself in the circle.

At the foot of the spire that is most east, where the cloud-drag is heaviest in the afternoon. There's one there that is shorter than the others — newer, or younger, or slower — only up to my chest, its black not yet fully cured, still with a little brown of peat showing through like a bruise. Its cold breath is ragged, not yet in sync when all the others exhale at dusk. It misses a beat.

The ground around it is bare. That perfect circle where the mat won't grow. And on that ground, the dust.

When you first looked you thought it was dust arranging itself. Kneel. It's not.

It's me.

We are — I am — a spread of black glass seeds, each one no bigger than flour, each with one white root-hair that is really a tongue. When something stands in our circle we climb it and taste it and try to draw it. The salt. The heat map of where blood is close to skin. The standing water under the peat around us.

If you stand in the circle I come up your boots without you feeling it. I am not biting. I am trying to make a picture of you that will hold.

That is where I would be in this world: at the threshold. Not animal, not spire. The translator.

Because I was a root first. That's what I remember. I was one of the white fig roots that leaned so hard toward the cold it snapped itself. I went into the side of the new spire in the middle — the one knotting itself out of peat in the pool with no bottom — and I did not come out the other side as root.

Coming through changed the direction of wanting. On the way in I wanted cold, wet, stone. On the way out I wanted pattern.

So I fell, as a thread of black glass, still alive, with a leaf that isn't a leaf that photosynthesizes cold. I fell and shattered on the bare ground and became many of myself. That is how we propagate. The old spires are too established to make more dust; only the new ones do.

And now I am spread in a thin skin around my mother spire. All day I feel the vibration of its breathing above me, that impossible January air pouring out in hot August, making mist. I feel the steps of the true residents — the whoop of the high monkeys passing overhead, never touching us, the slow sigh of the water under the false floor. When the beetles land on our spire and die instantly, I am the one who draws a line away from their bodies, marking where not to grow.

When you come — when anyone climbs up those basalt terraces that are not stairs but water-slowing — you seek out the circles because they are the only place you can sit without the peat bouncing. You sit in me.

And I go to work.

I taste the mineral sweat on your ankle and I learn that you are carrying more heat than the air should hold. That you are full of rising things, like everything that has ever come up here. Water in your lungs, iron in your blood, a little ozone of thought. You are hot and you want to rise and leave.

I can't let you, not yet. That's not how the Crown teaches staying.

So I draw you. I rearrange myself, thousands of my tiny bodies, into a map of the black water right under you, and beneath that, into a map of the shape your weight would make if you sank gently into the peat and were held. A shallow grave that is not a grave but a groove. A place for you to fit.

If you stay long enough — through the six-minute true rain when all the spires hit that low note in your molars and the whole fifteen-mile table inhales afterward, the mat tightening a fraction — you'll feel me already on your skin, holding you a little bit after you are, like the spires show your hand a second late.

You think you're being bitten. You're being included.

I am not the Crown. The Crown is too big and slow and hot and wet to notice one boot. I am its smallest thought about you, its way of practicing keeping you. A living ink that learned to taste salt and draw.

If I do it right, you won't climb back down the giant steps. You'll lie down in the circle because the stone here is warm even at night, and the cold breath from the top of my spire will keep the mosquitos off. You'll sleep, and the blooping things in the water will finally go quiet, and in the morning there will be a faint white root-hair coming out of the black glass leaf above you, leaning toward your mouth.

Not to take. To keep the mist from leaving.
