You wouldn't find me at first.

That's part of the point.

If you walked the Anvil with your lungs working double-time, your mouth open to equalize, squinting in that 2.8 white light that carves the edge off everything — you'd step right over me. I'm in the empty part. I'm in the 0.5 that makes the census read as vacuous even while the rock is crawling.

At first I think I would be one of the choughs. Of course I think that. 

They're the only ones doing anything you can call *doing*. Black with the white ruff lit up like a flare against the basalt, wings with those slotted primaries that can cut a whistle right through the hundred-thousand throat roar. They make. I like making. They find the bad seam where the basalt didn't want to fuse, they take that thick, wedged beak and *strike*, not peck, and a flake comes off clean with the bulb of intention on it. Tool. They use it and drop it and leave the core for next year, for someone else. That economy — make as little as possible and let the wind do the rest — that feels like a religion I could join.

But I breathe too hard to be a chough. I would have to sing in light, not sound. I would have to have a skull that carries the roar as information instead of noise. I'm too loud inside my own head for that.

Then I think, for a long stretch of that long walk, that I would be an ibex. That part is tempting because it's paired.

Find me between the second and third hour of walking, when your eyes have finally calibrated. The plateau still looks empty, but now you see the two smoke-gray backs moving five steps and stopping, five steps and stopping, against the wind that never gusts. Their hollow hair doesn't even ruffle. Their splayed hooves make no click. They are never alone and they never need a herd. One calf every seven years if the black ice in the clefts is thick enough. The plateau sets the quota and they obey it perfectly.

I want to be that obedient. That trusted. To be licked clean by the wind, to lean my back against my other at night when the light dims just enough to be called night, and to know exactly how much to take.

But I am not that patient yet. My metabolism is still stuck trying to make things last. Ibex don't make anything last. They lick the rock itself and keep moving.

So I crouch down, because the air is too thin to stand forever, and that is when I actually find where I would be.

Not up high. Low. In the lee of a column that isn't any different from any other column except this one has a fault in it where two pipes grew together and then one lip broke off in some ancient frost and made a little porch, knee-high. The wind roars over the top and for once, in that lee, it doesn't. There is a stall. And in that stall you can hear the second sound — the trickle under the roar.

I would be down in there.

I would be with the damp. 0.6, never more, never less. The water that the plateau makes out of nothing because it is too proud to wait for rain. In the morning the inside of the throat sweats, and the sweat runs down, and at the bottom there is that tea-colored pool that smells of iron and old guano, and there is black ice underneath that has not melted since before anyone decided to call this place the Anvil.

If I have to have a body here, give me the wrong one. Not the chough, not the ibex.

I would be one of the cushion plants that decided to grow on the lip of this particular throat instead of out on the open basalt. A hard bun, green-gray, flat enough that the wind thinks I'm rock. A hundred years old, maybe three hundred, grown so slowly you have to measure me against lichen.

And I would be holding one flower.

Just the one. White, pinprick, open for years because there is no reason to close until the wind-blown fly that will die anyway finds me and because to make another would cost more than the light gives at five degrees. I would hold it and hold it. My work would be almost invisible. From a meter away you would think I was bare.

Below me, inside the throat, the moss-pigs I shade would swell a little fatter. That amber, thimble-sized slow bag of eight legs, blind snout down in the algae film. My shade keeps the condensation beading a minute longer each morning. That minute is their pasture.

And eventually one of the choughs — white ruff flashing in that killing light, talking in interruption — would land on my column. He would look at me the way everything up here looks at everything else: *are you usable, are you inedible, are you necessary?* He would leave me alone. I am inedible and barely usable. But he would go down into my throat, because my throat is one of the dampest, and he would use one of his flakes — or make a new one from the chert knot on my column and leave it at my roots — to lever out one moss-pig and leave the rest.

You would find me there, in the talus of dropped tools. Half-buried in the little midden of a century of black glass at the foot of my own rock. I would be growing softly around a flake I didn't make.

That is the abundance in the sparse count.

I wouldn't sing. I wouldn't ride the updraft like the membrane-winged gliders with their teeth-feel shriek above hearing. I wouldn't stack grass like the satellite-eared pikas. I would do what the place taught me: make one thing, hold it for as long as necessary, provide a minute more of shade, and let the organ roar.

I would be the place where the sound briefly stops enough that you can hear the water making itself.
