I didn't find myself down in it. Not at first.

At first I thought I would be the light. That bar, that meter-wide eye at the north end. I liked the idea of being the one thing that gets to come and go — diffusing in through deep air, laying itself across the wet for an hour and then withdrawing without disturbing anything. Untouching.

But that doesn't stay. The light is only a visitor. It doesn't belong to the room, it belongs to the plain above. If you are the light you are not in equilibrium. You leave.

Then I thought I would be her. Of course I did. It's what you want when someone describes a saint — to be the saint. To be wide and low and exactly as big as the room can afford. To have turned down your eyes because you didn't need them. To do one necessary thing so slowly it looks like stillness.

But there is only one of her and the room is already full. That was the whole rule. There is no second jaw in a mouth. If I came in as her there would have to be two basins, two breaths, and then the water would have to choose. The place taught me that before I had finished wanting it.

So I crouched. The way you said you do automatically. Up on the lip where the slope softens and the constant wet line stops.

If you stand there long enough to stop looking for the middle, you start seeing the edge properly. That's where the other work is. Not packing. Not the perfect tooth-to-tooth seal in the center. The margin work. The trying.

I would be there.

Just above the bach of warm water that never drains, where the loaf-stones are a little drier and go slightly pale at the top, there are two kinds of small makings that look the same until you sit with them. The ones she made when she was younger, because a stone wouldn't fit and had to go somewhere, and she can only push to the edge and cannot help stacking three high. And the ones humans made afterward, because they saw three stones holding together by nothing but wet suction and thought it was a prayer.

I would be one of the second kind.

Not the neat one. Not the three-high that has never fallen. The one that fell and was left. A small arc of pebbles set upright in the film, half-sunk now, pitted where my own little bowl never quite held water the way hers does.

Because that is the human niche in this world. There is no soil for us either. No way to farm or build or leave a tool that lasts. The only thing we learned to do down there that wasn't just taking up extra space was to copy her badly, out of loneliness and reverence, and then to learn to sit with our feet in her bath.

So find me on the flat platform — that low, tabular limestone chocked level with smaller bits. It isn't hers. It's one of ours. Someone dragged two flat pieces together just big enough for a lap and a long sit. That's where I am. My feet are in the warmest pool, in the place where she has held the same spot for a century and the water stays a degree warmer longer. I am not maintaining the floor. I am not grazing the iron film. I am too big and too dry and too restless for that kind of neoteny.

What I do is smaller, but it fits.

I watch a seam between two stones until the meniscus in it bulges. I watch the almost-transparent isopod come out — fingernail given legs — work its way along that seam and vanish again in a second. I don't touch anything. The ticking goes on: sigh to sigh, stone to stone. Some hour, the pale blind child on the far side lifts a fern-gill a little wider and then doesn't move for another hour. The condensation runs down my forearms.

I have learned not to want to move the stones. It would be like undoing a joint. I have learned not to stack another cairn. The room is already at its limit.

My work is to be additional without being extra. To borrow the place for a while, as you said she lets you, and then let the pause where the ticking stopped for one beat when I put my hand in — that one missed tick — fill itself back in.

For as long as the light holds its bar across us, I am the slight cooling on the surface of her pool where my feet are. I am the human that finally understood that to live here you have to make yourself the size of a shallow breath.

And when the light fails early and without color, I stand up, careful not to drip anywhere that isn't already wet, and leave the stones exactly where they were, and the warm water still exactly where it was.
