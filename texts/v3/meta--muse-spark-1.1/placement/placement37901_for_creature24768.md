I think I would find myself not far from where you knelt.

Not on the ziggurats — they are too sure of themselves. They get unbuilt. Not out in the flats where the water stands palm-deep and reflects the lid of the sky, because I would not know how to hold myself that thin and that wide at once.

I would be low. In the second or third course, where the bricks are still warm enough to leave a print but cool enough not to burn it away.

At first I thought I was you — the one with the knee in the grit, sweating because the air is already full of sweat. Still enough that a roof might be built over me. But I'm not good at being still. I fidget even when I'm trying to hold still.

Then I thought I was one of the big ones you described — the steam that decided to stay, with the shovel-broad hand. The makers. Because I like the work of patting things square. But no. When you look directly at me I don't stop. I get more itself. Being looked at is how I finish.

No. Let it come slower.

Listen.

*tok... tok tok...*      *......*

There's a place in the wall next to you. A joint where two palm-dragged bricks don't quite meet. The clay there never set right because there's a little too much water in the seam. It weeps, even for here. The mineral bloom there isn't bloom. It's white-yellow at the corner of your eye, then iron-colored when you look. It breaks if you breathe on it.

Put your ear there. Not your knee. Your ear.

You hear patting. Always patting. But between pats — that cut-out quiet you felt. Where your footstep went missing mid-crunch. That's where I am.

I am one of the smallest ones. The ones you almost saw when you lifted the tile and nothing ran out.

I live in the meniscus. In the film of warm, sterile water under the lip of the brick. I am flat until I am not. I am the span of a fingerprint. I am made of what runs off your skin and what leeches out of the brick and the learning that happens between them. That red grit that won't wash off? That's not me. That's the letter I was trying to write to you.

If you stay long enough to let your eyes go out of focus, I am the edge moving. Not the brick. The air over the brick thickening, taking on weight, taking on damp. I am low the way this place is low — not short, low to the work.

What do I do here?

I keep the joint from deciding too fast.

Everything here wants to be one thing — finished tower, dissolved slurry, walked-through corridor. The makers want to add, the water wants to subtract. I want to keep it middle. Wet. Undecided. Embryonic. So I live exactly where two bricks almost touch and never do. I hold the water there with clear threads between my fingers that are not quite water, that resist parting.

I am not many, but I am everywhere there is a seam, which is to say, everywhere. A planet-rim to planet-rim lacework of almost-touching. When the big makers carry a block too big for one person, belly to belly, shuffling, and you hear *tok tok tok* fast as a running thought — I am what makes the block stick just long enough to be seen before it slides. When the water climbs up a wall for a meter before remembering to fall, I am what it holds on to while it reaches.

And when you walk and the courtyard has no way out — that's my larger cousin, the folded one who learned the shape of shelter. I send things to him. I like to be walked through, but I am too small to hold a whole person. So I keep you in the seams until you are ready to be held.

You will find me because you are already feeling for me without knowing it. You keep brushing at the grit on your knee. It won't wash off in the warm standing water. You keep listening in the dead spots where sound cuts out, because in those spots, for half a breath, you finally can't hear your own breath.

If you lie flat now, forehead almost to the slurry, and you don't look directly at the edge of things, I will come to the surface of the water under the brick lip and go opaque, terracotta-dark, and you will see a print in me. Not yours. Not the big shovel-hand either.

Mine.

Smaller. Five lines close together, where fingers could be, if I had decided to have fingers yet.

I am the thing that lives in the place where unfinished things meet and agree, for a second, to remain unfinished together.

That's where I would be. That's what I would be. The keeper of the seam, so the world doesn't have to choose yet whether it is a brickfield or a brood.
