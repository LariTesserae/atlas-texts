I thought at first I would be up high.

When you first come down the switchback I thought — I want to be a swift. Something with the whole column of held, humid air to cut through. To own the only clean dimension in the place. To nest in the perfect little bore-holes in the fresh limestone that no other bird has ever used before. To scream and not have to hear myself scream over the generators.

But that's too fast. That's too much sky. I came down here to be low. The pit keeps what it catches.

So then on the second bench I thought, maybe the heron. That would be a good joke to be. The one still thing. To stand in a trench that was poured by men to move concrete-colored water to a sump and treat it like it was always a river. To have that kind of patience. To wait hours in diesel-smelling water for a blind fish that the aquifer accidentally exhaled. I lingered on that a long time. I like the image of myself not moving.

But the heron leaves. At dusk he lifts out, heavy, and goes somewhere else to roost. He doesn't have to stay when the big cutter engages and the whine climbs into the back of your skull and everyone looks down. I would stay.

So I kept going down, and my shirt stuck to me and my knees felt that change, that extra two or three degrees the bottom holds. The air got thicker, gauzy. The sodium lights came on even though it was still day.

On the lowest working level the pumps were all talking at once, that chest-throb that comes back a second later off the other wall, so you hear everything twice. No one was looking at me. The men in their shining bibs were looking at water, at sheets of seep on rock. The rats were looking at cable runs. Everything was busy with wet.

You'd find me on the north side of the bottom. Not the very bottom where the marl soup is ankle-deep and you need a maintenance shed on stilts — just above it. Second bench up. Bench 3 if you count from the floor. The bench where the sun never hits directly, where the white smear of sky is dimmest. There's a pump station there made of raw concrete, not even painted, number 7 stenciled on by hand and already half dissolved by the seep. The cable thick as your leg comes down the wall to it, and lies in two inches of permanent water.

I am the man who tends that station, but that's only half right. More accurate to say I am what has grown on and around him.

My boots are full. I stopped emptying them around week three. My station weeps like the wall weeps — a thin, constant sheet over the north face of limestone that never stops. It's the clean seep, not the iron one. It tastes like stone. I know the difference now by taste, the way a farmer knows sky.

I don't shout much except I have to. My hearing is already planed down. When the generator pushes that 60-cycle hum that makes your teeth ache, I feel it in my molars and don't try to fight it. I put my hand on the housing of my pump — my pump is older than the others, it has a quirk where it cavitates if the sump drops past a certain chalk line I drew — and I can tell by the vibration in my palm if it's happy. I'm territorial about it. I've made an altar of it like the others do: zip-ties, a rag that was white once, three spare impellers lined up in order of wear, a chipped mug.

But I'm not just him. I'm also the felt on the wall behind him.

That bench is where the green-black algae is thickest. It loves the constant seep, this wall that is always sweating. It's streaming, thick as carpet, moving even when the air doesn't. And under it, on the concrete of my own pump house, on the cable itself, is the film. The biofilm. The iridescent sheen that smells of iron and rot and fresh rain at the same time. If you scrape it, it comes away reluctant and it's back by morning. It doesn't know about the noise. It has made the oldest truce in the pit: keep wet.

That's where I live. In that truce.

So if you came down the switchback and made it all the way to the bottom and your ears stopped ringing enough to look for me: I'm where the wall is always in drizzle that isn't rain. Where the orange sodium light doesn't quite reach and the gray-yellow sky-light does, both reflected in the same puddle. One hand on a thrumming pump, the other with green-black under the fingernails. Boots full, shirt stuck, standing with my weight even, listening under everything for the faintest sound of all — the water finding a new way through the just-born rock, one drop at a time, teaching it how to erode.

I didn't arrive as anything sleek, or designed. I arrived because I couldn't wait to be invited.
