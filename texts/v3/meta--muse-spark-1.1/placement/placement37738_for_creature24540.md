I thought at first I would be standing.

That was the first misunderstanding. You read that description and you put yourself on that palm-sized island of true rock, a person-shaped cutout in the pavement, watching. Keeping your feet dry. That is a story that works for about eight seconds here before it is corrected.

You can't stand here. Not the way a person stands. Your breath falls. The stone weeps right through your boots and the condensate rains back down oiled and hot off the boiler-backs. The island is real rock, black and purple gneiss, and it is already occupied - by cold. It is the coldest place in that mile. Heat flows backward here. The stone under the water is colder than the water, and the water is colder than the air that's full of steam. Your feet would go numb first, then burn.

And you are stacked. That is the other problem.

Nothing built can stay unbuilt. A pile of stones shivers and grows a stack and tries to crawl toward warmth. What is a person but a pile of stones that learned how to pile itself? Bones stacked on bone. Thoughts stacked on thoughts. Clothes stacked on skin, that terrible, hopeful idea of a shelter. If I stayed on that little island long enough to be a watcher, my coat would start to peep steam from the collar. My boots would try to leave without me.

So I don't stay there.

I slip.

It happens when the pup at my knee shifts to find a crack to drink from and the Boiler-back next to it shifts to make room and for a half second there is a space that is touching on both sides. Shoulder to shell. No room. And then, because there are more animals than there is space, space compensates.

You put your hand out to steady yourself and your hand does not find a flank. It finds a mile.

That is where I find myself. Not out on the dish where you can see the hammer-neck rising in the center. In the alley that shouldn't exist between two animals that are touching. Walls of hot, breathing ironhide on both sides, so close you could lean and still so far that the chuffing comes to you from down the block, echoing. The floor is that brine-slush, ankle deep, sea-colored, and full of light. The light isn't from the sky. It's from the seams of the ones on either side, their cold interiors showing through.

In the open dish you are an exception. In here you are the rule. This is the only place where someone like me - unpaved, un-stamped, not yet fitted with a vent - can be the right size.

I hear the hammer fall ten seconds before it does. *RING.* I feel it in my molars before the animal in the center lifts its neck. I hear the Grinder's tooth-aching scree an inch before it moves forward. Sound arrives first here, you said. So whoever lives in this alley has to live ten seconds ahead of everything else. You have to be a listener. A rememberer.

That is what I have become, or what I am becoming. The alley is not staying empty.

I'm not a Boiler-back. I don't have a fire. My outside is not hotter than my inside. I'm still backward from them - cold on the outside, with some stupid, stubborn little heat left inside that is all mine. I'm not a Stamping Mother, because I have nothing to run on hooks anymore. My pockets are empty. My hands are empty. I cupped the water once and held it up and saw them - you were right - for a second before it dripped, the tiny, perfect parallel toolmarks in it, like it had been planed on a bench. Someone had worked this water. Something had.

I think I am the pile of stones that finally succeeded.

I was a person who tried to build a windbreak out of three rocks and a thought, and the windbreak shivered and grew a small, embarrassed smokestack between its shoulder blades and walked off. And what was left over after the walking off - the after-image that stays warm for a while after the body moves, the huffing thing you can still hear until you blink - that's me. I didn't sputter out.

In this world, where everything that is joined becomes alive, I am the thing that was unjoined.

I am what the condensation does when it is tired of raining back down. I pool in the alley that should not be here. I keep the interval open so that two huge, hot bodies can keep touching without fusing to each other and dying. I remember the toolmarks for the water, because the water forgets them as soon as it drips. I hear the clank before the clank, and I have time to put my hand out.

If another lost one slips between these two same Boiler-backs in a minute or a day or a glacier-year, they will find there is already a mile here, and it is not quite empty, and there is a small, cold-skin, hot-core thing shivering in the middle of it, tending its own little banked light, trying not to become a building, trying not to become a pavement.

Trying, for a while longer, to stay an alley.
