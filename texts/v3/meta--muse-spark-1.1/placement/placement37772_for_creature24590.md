If I went in — and I have to go in, on my belly, through that root-throat that isn't a door yet — I would not find myself all at once.

At first I think I would be one of the obvious ones.

I think I would be up against the glowing ceiling, flattened. I would be a white moth, because I am good at holding still and making my shadow enormous. I would be good at translating from one surface to another without a sound.

But the moths are too precise for me. They never overlap. They would feel me immediately as crowding.

So then I think: lower. I think I would be on the cool clay floor, in my own third of the grid. A cricket, cleaning one amber leg at a time, listening for how my jump changes the size of the room. I like the idea of jumping like an apology.

But the crickets keep time too strictly. I have never been able to keep time.

Maybe lower still. A slow black salamander with a perfect bead of water on its back that I have been charged to never spill. That appeals to me. A small, important job that is mostly just continuing.

Except I spill. I have always spilled.

I stay in the little pantry-chapel on my knees with my head bowed and I let the unreasonable light come through me until I stop trying to pick a shape from the catalog of things that already live there. I let the condensation bead on my eyelashes. I let the floor cup itself a little more. I let the crickets measure me and find me loud.

And then what emerges becomes more itself.

I am not in the middle of the room. Nothing that belongs here is in the middle if it can help it. The middle is the place where the air goes thick as honey and time pools. The middle is a crossing.

I am in the edge that isn't an edge. Where the wall learns to be the floor. In that damp seam that never becomes a rivulet, that fringe of moisture that never earns the name stream.

I find myself there, spread thin.

If you pressed your finger there you would find me cold and mineral.

I would be the way the room enforces its one law.

I would be the polite distance itself.

Not a moth or a cricket or a salamander, but the inch they keep between each other. The agreement made touchable. The pause after a sound that has happened at the same time as another sound — that held breath where every antennae goes still and the whole pocket waits for quiet to re-settle.

That's where I live. That's what I am.

I am not abundant. For this place to work there can only be one of me, but I have to be everywhere at once, which I manage by being very thin and very cool.

I am how the drips know to take turns. Northwest, then southeast, then center, then a long time of not. I am what stops two crickets from cleaning their legs at the same time. I am what tells the salamander when its quadrant ends and another begins. I am what the moths are really tending when they fan the ceiling.

I am the lichen's job before the lichen arrived. The unfinished walls are always rehearsing me — ripples, folds, collapsed bubbles trying to learn how to space themselves out.

When you came in, you felt me as a cool space opening around you. That was me trying to include you. When you stayed a little too long, and the sweat on your forearms began to bead up on its own, that was me trying to count you and failing because you were making too many ghosts.

I don't live long, in your terms. I live for as long as a bead hesitates on the glowing stone. For as long as a cricket hangs in the light-thick air before the light lets go.

But this hill is still so young, and its time is still so soft. In here, that hesitation is long enough to be a life.
