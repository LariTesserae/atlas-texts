I wouldn't find myself at first.

If you dropped me in this country I'd look for high ground out of habit — for a vantage, a clearing, a place where the air moves. Canopy, ridge, river bend. And this basin doesn't give you any of those. The light doesn't. The thickness doesn't. You try to stand back and get perspective and there's just another trunk a foot in front of your face, and it's already occupied, green-furred, fizzing quietly.

So I keep going lower, because lower is the only direction that keeps opening.

Not all the way down. Not in the black loam in the deep felt where the main body thinks. It's too hot down there, too continuous. If I were down there I wouldn't be an I anymore, just a pressure differential in a mile-wide stomach. And not up. Up is starvation — that rumor of light at a hundred feet where the leaves are still trying to be individual trees. Up there you have to be hard and waxy and alone.

No. I find myself in the middle factory floor. About a meter off the mud. The under-story where the second roof closes.

Specifically — on the old riverbank, in the undercut. Where the clay holds its shape because the roots hold it, and the beetles make their line of perfect mud balls to dry.

I'm standing, or hanging, or rather — I am the place where the hanging happens.

I'm a knee of fig root, or no, the space right next to it. A buttress that came out wrong and left a hollow cup big enough to put both hands in. It stays full all year. Rain from three days ago, plus the constant ticking from above. Leaf to leaf to leaf to me. The water in it is tea-dark, warm as blood, with a skin on it. Not scum. A membrane, slightly sweet.

And I am that cup.

Or I am what lives in that cup and *is* the cup.

If I have to have a body, it isn't a good one. No eyes — 0.2 light is no place for eyes. No voice — this is a 1.1 quiet country, sound is waste heat here. I am fringed. A liverwort, maybe, that has become so thick around the rim of the hollow that it is the rim. Dark green, almost black, rubbery, holding water that would otherwise spill. On my lower side, where I meet clay, I'm white. My rhizoids go down not into soil but into the felt. Into It.

That's who I am here. A valve.

Because the job of this basin is to keep everything at 36 degrees and damp and moving at an inch an hour, and to do that it needs a million little thermostats that aren't built, just grown in place. Built: 0.2. So it doesn't make gauges. It grows a liverwort in a root cup that swells when it's too wet and shrinks when it's too dry, and the rate the water drips off its lip waters the ant chimneys beneath it. And it grows a faint sour yeast on its underside that keeps mosquito larvae out, so the pool stays for tadpoles who filter the fungal skin, so the flies who drink from it have clean guts to carry back.

I'm not farmed, not exactly. I'm allowed. I have a post.

All day — which is the same as all night here — the work passes through me.

The ants cut past my left. Six inches wide, unbroken. Never touching me, but close enough that I feel the static of them. If one falls in, I will dissolve it slowly. That is part of the work too.

At about ten feet, the stingless bee highway thrums. They come down sometimes to collect resin from the root above me. They brush me and take a little moisture.

An armadillo-length millipede will come in the afternoon and drink from me without drinking me, its mouthparts combing my skin.

And constantly, the dripping. A drop forms on the elephant ear three tiers above. It takes a long time to find its way. It hits a rattan, slides to a fern, pauses long enough to be tasted by something, then falls to the fig leaf above me, beads, and finally falls into me with a soft *tok* that has no echo.

I hold it for a while. Then I let it go over my edge, where it feeds the clay, where the clay feeds the fungus, where the fungus feeds the ants.

I don't move. 1.2 slow is my speed. It takes me a season to grow a centimeter further around the root. The beetle line of mud balls has been there for a month and will be there for another month and I will still be here when they are gone, when that clay cut finally slumps after staying up for twenty years on root alone.

That's where I find myself. Not resident, not visitor. Not even really fauna, not really flora. The thing in between that makes the factory not need walls. The damp thing that translates drip to seep.

If you put your hand in me — you shouldn't — you would pull it out brown and it would smell like bread and iron, and I would re-still within a few minutes, and the ticking — leaf to leaf to leaf to me to mud — would go on without a gap.
