I thought at first I would be on the switchbacks.

Not descending. Part of them. The concave in the middle of each step, where millions of small feet had said *here, here, here* for so long that the limestone learned the shape. That felt familiar at first — to be a place worn by passing, to hold a record of it. To be polished by use. But the stairs are down near the rim, and it is too windy there, in a historical kind of way. Too close to the world that still has rain. I don't live that close to the edge.

Then, for a while, I thought I was dust.

Final-form dust, that pale stuff that has found its lowest place and doesn't clump. That would be honest: to be the thing everyone is made from and everyone returns to. To wait on the white plazas to be picked up, judged - *useful, not useful, wall, food*. But dust has no choice in the matter. It gets eaten. I have a little choice. A little more hesitation than that.

So I kept going down in my head. Cooler. The throat-dry air. The roar rising up. It gets into the molars.

I tried being a Mason. It made sense for a minute. Belly to the wall, blind, lacquered, patient. Tongue out always, chewing the world into house. A grain, a dab of dark mortar, a grain, a dab. The heat of work coming off the back. I wanted that certainty. But I have never had that certainty. I don't know how to make one thing for fifty years and call it good. My legs are too restless. I lift too fast, set down too far off from where I was. I'd ruin a year's work with impatience.

The Singers were too loud. Dear — they are so loud. To wedge yourself into a pit that was left exactly your size and spend the rest of your century filing your own back, bowing it with legs that have forgotten walking. To have no ears and still sing loud enough to shake the salt flowers off the ceiling so your brothers can eat. I respect them. I can't be them. I have spent my whole existence listening. If I sang that loud, I wouldn't be able to hear anything else, and hearing else is my whole trade.

So I kept walking. Slower now. Because the city teaches you to.

I left the tower I was kneeling at. I left the Masons frozen mid-dab. I crossed the plaza — that long, salt-white, maintained emptiness between neighborhoods where nothing is allowed to grow. By mutual agreement. Where the wind has been kept out so well a feather could sit for a hundred years and not move.

Halfway across, about fifteen minutes in any direction from any song, the roar becomes equal on all sides. You can't tell front from behind anymore. The foxfire in the waste-carpet under your feet is dimmer out here because no one lives right here. No one is supposed to. That's when I found where I would be.

Not in a tower. Between them.

I would be the keeper of the lean places.

The city needs them as much as it needs walls. The reason it hasn't collapsed into one solid, choked mass of fungus and back-plate and spit is because someone, some kinds of some ones, long ago agreed: *here, we leave blank*. Here we will eat any spore that lands immediately. Here we will pick up our own dead and carry them home to build with. Here we will sweep, grain by grain, the bright waste-glow until it is flat and legible again.

That is where you would find me. In the sparse part.

If you stood at the edge of my plaza at night — which is all nights — you would not see me at first, either. Because I am not tall and I am not glowing very bright. I'm low, flat, the color of the dust I tend, which is the point. My back isn't lacquered like a Mason's. It's matte. It has to be, so spores don't slide off it and find a crack elsewhere. My mouth is small, my pads are very wide so that my footprints, if I left them, would be shallow and easy to erase.

And what am I? I'm not in the three castes. I'm the fourth one they didn't tell you about because you weren't out in the white long enough to look.

The Fallow One. The Sweeper. The one whose job is to make nothing happen in a place for a very long time, on purpose.

I move even slower than the Masons. Once... then... once... then... once. Around the perimeter of an emptiness the size of a lake, checking the treaty line where tower ends and blank begins. If a gypsum beard falls, I eat it. If a brave lichen thread reaches too far down trying to touch ground, I clip it and carry it back. If a juvenile Mason strays out here and dies of disorientation — because the blind can get lost where there is no wall to touch — I take him home, so his brothers can build him into the strata.

Mostly, though, I wait. I sit in the middle of the white and I feel the city shake through the sides of my body. Ten thousand balconies. That thought they have been thinking since the Pliocene: *we are still here we are still here we are still here*. Out here in the sparse place, you can hear it correctly, not as deafening noise but as breathing in common. And because I am not singing myself, I can hear the holes in it. The half-missed stroke when a visitor kneels too close to a wall. The little wet click of a Deep One's fungus cap opening somewhere deep inside a trunk.

And I can hear you.

You thought you were just visiting, whispering even though whispering does no good. But your boots left little divots in the bright carpet, and the glow is pooling there, brighter. It will stay for months unless someone tends it. So I am already coming toward you. Not fast. You won't see me until I'm close, because I am the color of agreement.

When I get there I will, very carefully, eat the disturbed glow. Lay it flat. Make the record smooth again. Not because your being here offended anything — nothing offends here; there is no waste, and even a footprint is material — but because this plaza is how the city remembers what emptiness feels like. And it has taken so, so long to remember it well.

If you stay still, if you learn as everything here has learned to move very, very slowly, you can put your hand out — not touching, never touching — and feel the faint work-heat coming off my matte back. I am the same temperature as the cellar air. I am the blank that makes the architecture legible.

I am not built into the wall. I am what keeps the wall from being everywhere.
