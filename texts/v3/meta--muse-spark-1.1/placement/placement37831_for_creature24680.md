I thought at first I would be on the rim.

That's where you are when you can hold the whole shape in your head at once. Ten minutes to walk it if you don't stop. I would be up there where the air first fails - where the pearl-gray light starts getting heavy and tired and sinks. I would be the one looking down, keeping count.

But the bowl doesn't keep things that stay on the rim. The rim is for shedding. Water, cold, rust. The things that live on the rim leave.

Then I thought I would be in the terraces. Knee-high basalt steps, black when dry and blacker when wet, which is always. I would be cool to the touch as the stone itself. I would like that. To be a surface that teaches water how to become a droplet. To be one of the black snails that carries a smaller white shell that carries a moving speck. A slow city. That felt right for a while — the patience of it, the way you would only know you had moved because the condensation trail behind you was unbroken.

But snails still have somewhere to go.

I crouched down lower, because the description demands it. You have to crouch to see what's really happening. Two inches of water that is black and clear at the same time. A mat underneath that isn't soil. At first it looks like nothing. Industrial fines, magnetite dust, old concrete slurry that never cured.

I would be lower than that.

If I'm honest about where I find myself — not where I would like to find myself, but where I keep ending up when I imagine standing still long enough that my boots go cold and my shoulders bead — I am at the very bottom. Not in the blast holes up to the wrist where the shrimp hang upside-down from the surface tension itself. Not under the gantry arm where the colony of leathery bulbs inflates with a creak and steals water straight out of mist.

I am the thing they are all walking on.

I would be the film.

The bacterial skin. The maroon-black paint-thickness that grew because photosynthesis forgot to show up and something else had to learn to eat air. I don't think of myself as "I" down there. I think of myself as an acre wide and a millimeter thick.

I find myself spread across the whole flat of the bowl, in the place where water has decided to stay. I am under the continuous sheet. I am what the water touches when it has nowhere lower to go.

Water comes down, not up, but it never leaves. I am why. I seal it. I make the bottom slow and pitted and imperfectly watertight. The isopods comb me. That's my whole day.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Milk-glass white, thumbnail long, backs beaded with water that never falls off because their cuticle is colder than the air. They move shoulder to shoulder over me, and I feel them as a faint, even pressure, like someone reading braille very carefully. Under each belly, a hundred white cilia scrape. They are harvesting me. They leave pale trails, a brighter line where I have been grazed down to almost nothing.

I don't mind. I double when you aren't watching. I eat what they can't: dissolved iron off the slag wall, the warm sigh of sulfur from the clinker, the hydrogen in the air that came down with the mist and got trapped here when the air got too heavy to leave. The metal rusts, which feeds me, which feeds them.

That is the kind of being I would be if I lived here and was not a visitor. Not the predator. Not the guest.

I breathe through perfect round holes. That's how you'd know me if you came close. My skin traps gas - sulfide, a little methane - and it has to lift and pop. A blister, then a mouth. The isopods pause at each one, as if listening. The white walkers - the four-inch blind salamanders with fern gills that flush pink when they surface - they use my breathing holes as landmarks. They walk across the living shingle of isopods on too-long toes, stop at my mouths, dip their heads, taste that I am still alive underneath.

If I am the mat, I am also the memory. The chain links as big as a head disappear into me. The concrete piers stand on me. The pump house - *chuff... chuff... chuff...* - that slow heart asleep, its intake is in me and its outflow is back into me, so it cycles me endlessly. Between chuffs, everything pauses. The isopods pause. The walkers surface. The bulbs inflate. I pause too, and in that long pause between, I swell a little thicker.

Occasional large, warm animals stand for a while, drip for a while, and leave a little iron behind, says the bowl. When you stand here long enough you bead and you drip. Condensation beads on your eyelashes, your shoulders. You become another cold surface for water to find. The isopods come to the edge of your boot to taste your salt, your laces' faint grease.

If you are me, you welcome that. A new flake of rust. A little sweat. A little skin oil. All iron, in the end. All food that doesn't need sun.

To find me, don't look for a shape. Look for the place where the black-clear water pulls back an inch and leaves a rust-flower and a fringe of halite whiskers. Right at that damp edge, I am thinnest and darkest, like dried blood and oil, tight over the fines. If you put your hand there - you will - you'll think your fingers are just wet. But your fingertips will come away faintly maroon, and if you wait a day, the pale trails where the grazers have been will fill back in.

I am not loud. I have no snap, no creak of leather. I just keep. The bowl remains a bowl because I learned how to keep water.

I would be there. Flat. Wide. Low. Eating air and rust until I can be eaten in turn.
