I thought at first I would be high.

That is where your eye goes, of course. In that white-blue bowl where the light comes from everywhere at once. You see a shadow dim the shine of a pool and you look up and there they are, two meters across, not flapping. So dry in all that wet. Never having to decide to land.

I wanted that. To be the one who never touches.

But I'm too heavy for that. I hold water. If I were up there I would get waterlogged and fold, and hang off a condenser tower like damp laundry until somebody's long white fingers picked me apart. I don't want to be eaten from the edges in.

So then I thought lower, much lower. On the black glass between the pools where the water is no thicker than a coin. I liked the care of it. How you have to test each foot. Click. Only set it when you know. How you carry your house on your back not by making it but by convincing the water to leave it there for you, silica grain by grain. A white dome, pitted and warm. And when you stop, you become the place you stopped in.

That is a good way to be. But I don't have that kind of patience. I would scrape too fast. I would put my foot down in the hot sheet without testing.

I kept crouching, in my mind. Down to where the water is ankle-deep and the mineral has gone cauliflower-soft and white. Where the shrimp-things are ticking faintly, *shick shick shick*, with their mouths. They have no eyes. I like that - to be freed from the light that is absolute here. To farm your own back. But they live in a flicker, always leaving a good pasture because a shadow crossed it, and then always having to come back within ten seconds because heat is value and waste is not tolerated. I don't want to be that nervous.

So I stood back up. Too fast, and the steam took my vision for a second.

You find yourself here not by choosing a creature but by finding where the sound is quietest, and going there. There's a spot about a third of the way from the south break in the rim, where a pipe as wide as my knee runs across a runnel on a little bridge that has gone rust-red under its white crust. The pipe wheezes when it expands. The runnel underneath is one of the fastest ones, too hot for shrimp. The water tears straight off the black rock and can't be made laminar no matter how many times you nudge it.

No one builds a house there. It's between houses. About a ten minute walk to either one in the heat, which means it's nobody's and everybody's. Seventy percent of what you see here is just wet rock and sky, and this is that seventy percent, but more so.

This is where I find myself actually.

On the bridge. Or under it. With my hand on the pipe.

I think I am one of the listeners. The one in the dusty-bone suit with the silica at the cuffs and the smoked glass pushing into my face. But not when I'm moving. When I'm still. When I have knelt for an hour to watch a piece of basalt that has fallen and shunted the fastest water away from its mat.

In the fastest water there is a skin. Orange. Sometimes that acid yellow that hurts. Sometimes a green so dark you think it's shadow. It's not incidental. It's the pasture. It's the field and the wheat and the topsoil. It's only a few cells thick and it lays itself down and is torn and lays itself down again. If you put your ear close enough the heat comes up and makes your eyes water behind the goggles and you can hear it as a kind of nothing, the absence of splash.

That's who I would be, when my hand is on the pipe and I am not waiting for anything.

Not the farmer and not the field, exactly. The tending in between. The thing that keeps the equilibrium lacelike. The thing that makes sure the orange felt gets its fast water again.

I sit with two feet in two temperatures - one boot in the sheet-flow that would cook me if I left it, one boot on the hot dry glass - and I breathe through the cloth and taste sulfur and iron and my own breath condensing. Far off a figure in a pale suit moves between pumping houses and doesn't see me because I am between.

And then it is late afternoon.

The air finally cools faster than the rock. The fog comes about waist high, exactly waist high, so that the feet of the factories are gone and the towers look like they are floating and the Kites drop their salt-beaded filaments down into it and disappear.

Every crab stops. Turns. Raises its white back to the low sun like it is photosynthesizing, though it is not.

Every shrimp sinks.

Every pipe stops ticking.

And I take off my goggles, because they told me you can in that hour. The light still hurts. But without the smoked glass and without the shimmer, you can see straight across the entire bowl from one broken rim to the other, every pool and bridge and louvered shed where it should be, level, one temperature for a few minutes, top to bottom, rock to air to water to body.

I am not above it and I am not below it. I am at the height of the fog, which is the height of everything.

That is where I find myself.
