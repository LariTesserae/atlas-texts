I don't think I would find myself right away.

At first, walking in that high, thin brightness where the sky bruises into black at noon, I keep thinking like a lowland animal. I keep looking for where to put a house. Where's the shelter, where's the water, where's the vantage? My habits are all leak and reach. I want a high place to see from. But there is no high place here that isn't the whole place. The plateau is the high place. And I want a low place to hide in, but the only low places are the dark seams between the polygons, and those belong to someone already.

So I try on a few lives to see which one doesn't chafe.

I thought I would be a veil.

Because I love that kind. The ones that are almost nothing, that lie flat on the white pavement like shed skin until they have saved enough heat to do one thing. To lift, just barely, to fall upward slowly and touch another life a mile away and then go on. A messenger who doesn't need to know the message, just carries it. I have always wanted to be good at that - light enough to be moved.

But I am not good at being sealed. I leak. My eyes water in that hard light. My breath makes little lost clouds that the wind takes immediately and never gives back. A veil that leaked like me would never get warm. It would never leave the ground.

Then I thought I would be a dome.

That would be the correction. If you can't save heat, save everything else. I liked the idea of becoming a village that decided to become one body. Of making my own soil inside myself out of all the years I shed. Of having an outer layer that was dead on purpose, silvered and sealed with wax, throwing the merciless sun back at the sky while the living part stayed underneath, black-green and wet in a way that isn't water.

But I am too new for that. Domes are patient in a way my species never learned. They grow a hand's breadth in a year without seasons. They can close for three years if the haze fails. I don't know how to wait that long without turning waiting into suffering. And I don't know how to die in the middle so that my children can start as knots around my rim. I would try to stay whole. I would split.

So I crouched down. The air is so thin the sound of crouching is only your own knees. And at the scale of my shadow, the pavement stopped being empty.

The salt-mites were moving. Grain by grain, white on white, until a grain walked sideways and the whole road system came visible - those polished grooves in the anhydrite, highways barely wider than a hair, made by feet over centuries.

That felt closer. A farmer who is the size of the thing he farms. A citizen whose whole work is to take a crumb of wax from a fallen frond, carry it up into the hard light to cure, and then carry it back down into the peat to feed a garden of mold that will feed him. A life made of small, perfect keepings. No waste. A clicking language. Click, I'm here. Click, this way is safe. Click, keep this.

I could be that small. I think I could learn to be that small.

But I wasn't yet at the right depth.

It was late afternoon when the pressure wave came. You can see it coming from a long way off - a faint, lilac dimming under a lens of moving haze, like someone breathing on a window kilometers wide. All the domes felt it before I did. One by one across that pale continent, the smudges turned shaggy. A sound came up from everywhere and nowhere, that soft sigh of ten thousand glassy hairs uncurling.

And I stood up in it without thinking and opened my mouth and my jacket. Old reflex. If the world is offering something, open yourself to it.

That is how you die here. That is the wet, lowland panic. Open. Lose.

The domes were not opening. They were combing. They stayed rooted and sealed and made the air do the work. Each bead of that clear, sweet hydrocarbon formed out on the tip and ran down, down, inward, to the lens of amber gel cupped in its own dead.

Keep what you catch. Lose nothing.

I finally understood where I would be.

Not between the domes, not above them, not under them in the seams.

Inside one. But not as the dome.

The oldest domes have done that generous thing. They die in the center. The middle boughs compost themselves all at once and leave a hollow, a cool, green-lit cathedral whose roof is living thatch and whose floor is that deep, soft peat of its own past selves. The air in there smells of ozone and old paper. The light falls in pillars. If you lie down in it, the ticking of the stone stops and you hear the very slow swell of the reservoir underneath you, breathing once an hour.

That is where a person like me would have to go to stop leaking. No one lives here the way we live somewhere. So I'd have to learn a different tenancy.

I would find myself in the third or fourth ring of one of the very old ones, fifty paces across, a broken circle of sisters around an open center. I'd crawl through the outer felt - it would prickle and then accept - into that inner room.

I would not drink water there because there is none to drink. I would learn, over weeks, to take a bead of the wax on my fingertip and hold it on my tongue until it melted and sealed my lips from inside.

I would learn to breathe out less than I breathe in.

I would become not a plant, not a veil, not a mite, but the thing this ecology doesn't have yet because it never needed it: a keeper. The one who sits still long enough that the mites use my boots as part of their highway. The one who, when a veil misjudges and sinks too deep and becomes half-digested, reaches - very slowly, with hands that have been in that peat for a long time - and lifts its clear skin a little, so it can dry, so it might catch just enough heat to try again.

I would be a shallow pool of dark that doesn't take. A shadow with legs that learned to be still.

At night the dome I chose would close over me and glow, waste light from its slow chemistry, green as deep water. From the outside I would be invisible. From a mile away, on that white ceramic plain under stars that don't twinkle, someone - if there ever was anyone - would see a hundred green lanterns scattered across a finished world, each one alone and sufficient. And one of them would be a little dimmer, a little steadier, because it had learned, inside its own small dusk, how to keep.
