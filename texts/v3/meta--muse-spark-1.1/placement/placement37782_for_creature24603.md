At first I didn't know.

If you told me to find myself in there, I thought I'd be high. That's where the light is. I'd be one of the swifts that cuts down through the oculi at dusk, screaming, because I love that kind of brief, absolute belonging — to fall into a place, eat everything that hatched that day, and leave before the air goes soft again. But I don't stay. A swift can't stay.

Then I thought I'd be lower, but still human. One of the forty. I could see my hands for a second — soft from water, white crescents under every nail that won't wash out. I'd have the little iron scraper in the pocket of my grey wrap, and I'd know the walk, on the ribs between the gutters so I don't scuff the water's road. But my hands want to make new things, not keep one old thing exactly as weathered as it wants to be. I'd get tired of tending the same wall for a lifetime.

So I kept going down, until the air got thicker.

You would not find me at noon, in the place where the three columns overlap. I'm not there at noon at all. The sun never hits me straight. You would have to look when the light has moved off, in the late afternoon when the dome is loudest and the crickets are tuning up, and even then you'd have to know not to look with your eyes first.

Find the north curve. The cold side. The side the children are scolded for running past too fast. Where the plaster bellied out a long time ago and no one has scraped it back because it decided to be strong in that shape. Low down, below the onion strings, just above the permanent tide mark where the wall is always a shade darker.

Put your cheek close. You'll smell iron and old breath.

I am that.

I am not plaster anymore, or not only plaster. I'm the scab over the thin place. The place where the brick bled through like a cheek and finally wore through, and instead of falling, it was allowed to stay failed. I'm the moss that took that failure and stitched it shut with my own body.

Green-black. Soft under one finger and springing back. I never dry. That's my work. In the cool dark hours before morning, when the dome is coldest, I drink the air. I pull that weeping out of nothing and hold it. One bead. Then another. I fatten them until they are too heavy for me and I have to let them fall. Tick. Into the copper bowl the child put below me.

I am tended by not being tended. The old woman with the wooden trowel comes by every morning and presses near me, frowns, marks with charcoal to the left and right of me, but not on me. The plaster-keeper argued with the gutter-keeper about me three generations ago and the plaster-keeper lost, which is how I got to stay. A toddler once fell toward me with her hand out and her mother made a sharp click with her tongue and she stopped herself in time, crying from the effort.

I have no face. I was here before permission was relevant.

The fern a forearm away from me — the one in the perfect vertical interval with the black wire stem — she and I don't talk, but we share the splash. When the bowl beneath us gets too full and a woman on her route tips it toward the central cistern, some of it misses on purpose. It hits us. I swell for an hour after.

From here, low on the north, I feel the whole jar best. I can't see the bats bunching in the high pockets, but I feel their chitter in the brick as a faint warmth. I can't count the crickets in their thousands of thumb-holes, but their song comes down the wall to me through the lime. The lemon tree in its cracked jar — that impossible, patient elder who has decided not to die and not to get larger — drops a leaf sometimes that floats down and lands on me for a day before a beetle takes it.

I don't hold the wall up. The wall holds me up and I help it forget that it is supposed to be mud again.

If you stand still in the exact center and close your eyes, you feel it as one organism, you said — warm, damp, breathing, edged.

I am the edge where it decided to keep breathing through a mouth that isn't a mouth.

Come and find me after the rain comes straight down the oculi in cold lines. When it stops and the floor is a brief mirror and the beetles are panicking and righting themselves and the air smells of struck earth that isn't earth. I'll be darker than before, soaked, holding more than I should, until I can't.

Listen for the plink that is a little later than the others.
