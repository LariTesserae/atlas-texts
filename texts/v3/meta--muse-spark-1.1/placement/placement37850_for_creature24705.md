At first I think I would be standing on the lip.

That is where you put me — where the newcomers stand, warm, dripping, too fast, with breath you can hear. Looking down into the bowl with human eyes still trying to sort things into plant, animal, mineral. I thought: I would be the watcher there, the visitor, the one who notices.

But I can't stay there. It's too windy at the lip, even if the wind never gets inside. It's too much work to stand and look. The mountain made this place to stop looking.

So I sink.

If I am honest, if I let the place decide where I belong and not my habit of standing above things — I am not on the rim. I am already inside, below the line where the light stops trying. Somewhere on the north curve where the hanging beards almost brush the black tarn. Where the curtains have made a small ante-room, a damp colonnade. That's where I'd find myself. Not in the water, not yet in the mud. In the seam. A pocket the size of your cupped hands where moss wall becomes moss floor becomes water skin, all without an edge.

You would not see me at first. That's correct.

What would I be?

I thought at first: one of the salamanders. That would be flattering. Old enough to be considered an elder, holding a little quartz knife to my chest like a vow. Patient. It suits how I want to be seen.

Then I felt closer to the moths. White, hundreds to a curtain, folded, exact distance from each other without ever measuring. Not asleep. Discontinued. That also suits me — a way of being white against black-green and still not being seen.

But those are still too much like characters. Too separate.

Let me stay longer in that seam and let what I am become more itself.

I am cool. I am pressed on all sides. My back is against the mattress of moss that is also a roof for ten others. My front is open to the black water which is so still it holds my reflection without asking me to move. Breathing is drinking here. The air comes in wet and leaves no drier.

I have a body, but barely. That is the rule. Only small things can afford to live in a place that spends nothing.

I think — I am one of the beetles.

Not the shiny black carapace part, though I have that. My back is polished basalt with a droplet on it that has been there for a hundred years, growing heavier and never falling. My antennae are folded back neatly in my lap. I am shoulder to shoulder. To my left a brother, to my right a brother, under the saxifrage star that is too small and too old to flower anymore. We are not crawling over each other. That would be wasteful.

And in front of me, held between my forelimbs, not clenched, just... kept... is a tool.

Mine is not quartz. Mine is a flake of mica, no bigger than an eyelash, lifted from the bottom of the tarn by someone before me and left here in the mud where I found it. It is flat and it catches the grey light even in the deep-cloud dark and it could cut, if I wanted it to cut. If I wanted to lift the edge of the liverwort, or scrape the algae, or signal.

I don't.

That is who I am here. A holder.

I am an animal that has pared itself down until aliveness costs almost nothing. I hold a small sharp thing I will never use, and that is my work. To hold it and not use it. To be part of the agreement: we will be this close together, predator and prey in the same pocket, beetle and frog and gill and midge all sharing the one warm leaf, and none of us will spend the tool.

I don't hum. The midges above me hold their station and I hold mine. The newt in the water tracks me slower than lichen grows, and then stops. The whole mat of the bowl rises and falls a fraction because we are breathing underneath it.

If you came to my pocket and lifted the leaf over me, I would not scatter. I would turn my head toward your heat at the speed of forgetting how to forget, and you would see the mica in my hands, and you would understand, for a second, what the mountain thought when it sagged into this shape and decided to keep one thought from blowing away:

*Stay.*

Not as an order. As a form.

And I do.
