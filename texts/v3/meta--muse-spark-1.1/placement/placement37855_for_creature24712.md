I don't find myself right away.

I drop down the throat like anyone would, and at first I'm the wrong kind of thing for this room. I'm moving. I'm using air. I'm warm.

The cold comes on as a second skin, 8 degrees, dense and weighted, pressing on my cheeks, getting into the hinges of my jaw. The light has already been worked on before it reaches me - daylight thinned and folded in water until it has no source, no beam, just evenness, like being inside a very clean swimming pool fifty feet under the sky. I hang in the middle of the chapel/grove, twenty meters by twelve, seven high, and I look for where a thing like me would go.

Not to the east wall. Not at first.

The east wall is busy. I thought for a long time I would be that - because it is industrial without industry. A trunk of travertine jutting two feet out and elbowing down at a perfect right angle no hand made. Rimstone dams, one spilling into the next with a lip so even it looks milled. All that pale factory pipework precipitating itself. Intake, outfall, no operator, no fuel. Grinding nothing but itself. *Plick... plick...* That fat drop forming and falling through water that doesn't let it echo. It absorbs.

I know that work. Taking hard water and having to let something go. Building a body out of the letting-go, molecule by molecule, too slow to see. Running day and night without variation. For a long time I think: there, that's me. The slow mineral animal.

But I'm not that patient yet. And I'm not that hard.

So I look to the floor. To the softest things in the room.

I thought I might be one of them, too - one of the green hermits. An inch tall, no more than the first joint of the thumb, a bottle-brush of filaments with a white holdfast sunk into a pit in the rock where the pale sand doesn't collect. There are only ten or twelve of them in the whole chapel. Each one ten or twenty feet from the next. Each one with the same slow kink halfway up, all of them bent a few degrees toward the throat where the sun comes down. They grew that way. It took a season to make that bend.

They live on almost nothing. A few phosphate molecules per million in water that has been filtered through a mile of limestone. They make an inch of green out of almost-nothing and then they hold it. For months. For years maybe. Because there is nothing here to eat them. No snail will ever cross that ten-foot desert of light-scoured stone to find them. No fish. No blind cave thing. The water is too clean and too complete for anything that needs to eat anything else. So they never learned to be bitter or fast. They just stop, and hold.

I want to be that pure. That sufficient. But I move too much. I look around too quickly. I'm still counting my held breath.

And then I understand where I actually am.

I'm not on the wall. I'm not in the tuft. I'm in the between.

The emptiness between one algal tuft and the next is not empty - you said it. It's part of the terrain. It's the third thing that lives here. Ten feet of bare, light-scoured, round-shouldered limestone with the drift of pale snow-like sand in its low spots that never shifts because there is no current to shift it. The shadow between one stone pipe and the next. The pressurized, held absence that keeps everything from touching.

That's where I find myself. I settle - not to the floor exactly, but to the middle distance. To the equilibrium itself.

If you stayed here submerged - and you can, for a little while - you would feel the place assert its equilibrium on you. That's it. That's the job.

I am the water that has learned not to move. I am the cold that holds. I am the part that absorbs instead of echoes. I am the ten feet of bare stone that makes it possible for the green hermit to be alone without being lonely, and for the stone factory to keep working without ever flooding the room.

I don't precipitate. I don't photosynthesize. I keep the interval. I hold the pressure on your joints so you feel where you are. I keep the sand still. I fold the light after the throat gives it to me and hand it out evenly, without source, so the kink in the algae has something steady to lean toward.

I live here because I do not need to eat. I live here because I have learned to not change quickly, and this room only allows things that do not change quickly.

You, briefly included, burning through air in the middle of the chapel - you hang there a few seconds more and you feel me holding you, not unkindly. Weighted, even, at 8 degrees.

Then you have to go back up toward that trembling circle of bright.

*plick.*

I stay.
