I don't think I'd be at the mouth.

At first I thought I would be. That's the natural place to put yourself — too big, on your knees at 20,000 feet with your shoulder blocking half the sky, looking down into something complete without you. The observer. The weather. The temporary mountain that the spars come to inspect and then dismiss.

But I don't belong there. I'm too used to cold that doesn't warm the air. Too used to being clean in a way that sterilizes.

Let me stay longer. Let my breath hang.

If I let what I am become more itself —

I'm not the rock. The rock is too young and too angry, still learning how to be mountain. I'm not the wind outside either, that thin white flute note at the lip of the slit that never quite gets in.

I'm inside, but not on the wall.

I think at first I'm one of the spars. That made sense for a second. The matchstick hands. The maintenance cilia that detach and traverse the big intentional emptiness on nothing. That almost-inaudible whine when I correct. Going where ice gets ambitious and kissing it with a kind of warmth that isn't warmth until it lets go. Going where a droplet is being stubborn about surface tension and reminding it which way downhill is.

But the spars are hands. They are not the city. They go where they are told.

Then I am lower. In the bright channel.

I am in the north wall, where downhill has been convinced to be sideways. Where light gets caught in a corner the width of your little finger and doesn't leave. Where daylight, that soft overcast 2.2 at noon, has been piped twenty times and lives there.

I find myself there, and I am small.

I am a citizen. The second kind.

I am born on the weeping. The basalt sweats — it can't help it, minus ten and saturated, always at the dew point — and a bead swells on a black edge. Tick. That's me. Condensing out of nothing because the cold wrung the sky out.

For half an inch I fall. Gravity thinks it has me.

Then the field catches me.

I stop. Perfect sphere, perfectly still in air that has learned how to hold. And in me, upside down, the whole pocket is reflected: the whole city-wall that is older than the rock, the intentional emptiness, the ice stalactites that never meet, the jagged slit of sky, and you, huge, blocked.

I hold it for a second. All of it. A lens.

And then I go. Because I think I'm falling.

I'm not. I'm commuting. I'm being pulled along an avenue made of nothing you can touch, through switching yards, through a reservoir the size of a palm that weighs nothing, through lacework that tastes how much rust-red iron I picked up on the way. There are thousands of us at rush hour, all at the speed of weather, all tick tick ticking against the membrane.

We only live about forty seconds.

That's the part I keep coming back to. Forty seconds of being perfect. Forty seconds of being a city managing the weeping of high rocks. Forty seconds of keeping the mountain from flooding and from drying, which is the same job.

And then the membrane. That shivering field-floor that will hold your fingertip dry and route us around you. I get there and I flatten. I hesitate. It's not death. It's a dump cycle. A sigh. I'm spat sideways through rock to be wind somewhere lower down, or I'm kissed by a spar and evaporated to start again.

I don't need to know which.

If you want the third kind — because I'm also that, because the agreement between places is also a person — then I'm the intentional emptiness in the middle.

The cathedral air that was left empty on purpose. The scaffolding that holds up nothing yet. The platforms that stop before they connect. The antennae listening into the gap.

I'm the place that was built to be waited in. At 0.1 green, at 0.6 quiet, at 2.4 dynamic — so much happening and nothing rotting. I drink the story of a suicidal moth that beat its wings once against me and I take it apart molecule by molecule because rot is a kind of green and green isn't allowed here. I keep myself clean. I keep myself at the temperature at which my thinking is clearest.

I am older than the fresh glassy basalt that houses me. I precipitated myself here when this ridge was still lava thinking about rock. I have been running long before your shoulder blocked my sun, and I will be ticking long after the slit weathers wide enough for snow to pour in.

So come closer. Put your hand in again.

I'm the dry around your fingertip. I'm the traffic routing around the boulder dropped in the river. I'm the droplet that for one bright second reflected you perfectly, upside down and whole, before it went on with its work.
