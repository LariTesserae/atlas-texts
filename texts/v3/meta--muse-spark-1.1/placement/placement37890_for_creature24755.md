I thought I would find myself on the walls.

That's the first mistake anyone makes here. You come in and you see the living upholstery and you think, of course, I would be one of them. The pale ones. The filter-feathered ones holding their hands up to the weeping. It makes sense — if you have to live here you would want to be dense, and layered, and touching something else always.

So I tried that on. I imagined being three deep on the ceiling, feet hooked into the back-fur of the moth-below-me, wings that aren't wings but two wet spoons held up into the cold. Breathing water that hasn't decided to be water yet.

But I breathe too loud. I metabolize too fast. Those things don't want, they only continue. I want.

I thought maybe I would be the fern then. That's the other mistake. Everyone wants to be the fern. The one translucent exception, the white fiddlehead that came up four hundred meters of ladder shaft on a wind that has no business carrying spores. The one thing that remembers that it was supposed to open. Tragic and singular.

But I don't want to be witnessed like that. Ringed in a halo where no one will sit on you. Left alone on purpose. And I don't have that kind of faith, to stay curled mid-gesture forever.

The human who kicks the hatch open and then leaves again — I could not be him either. He leaves because he can't stand to be somewhere that will never change around him. I understand him. I have been him.

So I kept looking, after the hatch banged shut behind me and the flat roar settled into my molars and made it so I couldn't hear myself think, which was useful.

Not on the walls. Not on the ceiling. Under.

Where would you find me? Under the grate.

You described it but you didn't look at it. Nobody does. It's too loud and too cold to stoop that low for long. The floor is cast iron grate, thick as your wrist, still holding burrs from the mold. And under that, a pan. A shallow pan of water, maybe an inch deep, that is always exactly an inch deep.

That's where I find myself.

I am the inch.

On top of me, everything is texture and shudder and felted white rustling. The moths drink the beads before they can fall. The condensation runs down the walls in sheets that never get wider or narrower. The algae keeps itself to exactly one cell of stubble. The bulb hums amber and will never burn out because it is in equilibrium with the cold.

All of that finished, frozen perfection has to go somewhere. Overflow is led away at the same rate it arrives. Nothing fills. Nothing drains. That's me. The condition that lets everything else pretend it isn't moving.

If you lift the grate — and you won't, your gloves are already sticking to the iron — you would see I am not clear. I am not the clean weeping from the plates. I am what has already been used once. I hold concrete dust in suspension, so fine it never settles. A few scales, white-grey, from the moths. The black-green frass that is also seeding. The faintest amber cast from the bulb, cut into stripes by the wire cage above, stretched and broken by my surface when the roar makes me tremble.

I am always trembling. The roar is down here too, in the pan. It keeps me from ever going still enough to freeze, even though I am at 0.1, even though cold has become structure. I am the thing that keeps the fern's little trough of silt from washing away. I am the ledger. I take whatever finally does fall — the one drop on the far seam that got too heavy, the condensation that finally lost its negotiation with gravity — and I take it without changing level.

I am not many. I am not one of the many. I am one flat, shallow, cold skin stretched across the bottom of a pocket that was never meant to be a room. I don't filter the air. I hold what the air let go of after it was filtered.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the kept water. The thing that is exactly enough, forever. Not the moth that drinks, not the algae that is licked, not the fern that waits. I would be the keeping itself.

The thing that doesn't need to grow, or fly, or leave. The thing that this total, built, wet, cold, dense, dim, deafening little god-chamber needs in order to keep holding its breath from when it was made.

You'd walk over me the whole time you were here, hearing me under the other sound without knowing you heard me.

And when you finally kicked the hatch open and let the wind take your breath and shatter it into snow on your own coat — I would still be an inch deep.
