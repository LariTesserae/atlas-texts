At first I think I would be on the edge.

Everyone, coming here, stands on an edge at first. Looking down into the cloud that took the valley, looking up into the cloud that took the summit. It's the only place where you can be alone because no one holds an edge for long - too much wind, too much loss. So I put myself there, for a minute. The cold coming off the low weather on my face, my knees already humming from the thin air.

But I wouldn't stay there. You can't hold an edge. That's the first rule I learned even before anyone told me.

So I step back. Down one terrace. Then down another, until the edge below isn't weather anymore but another roof, and another roof below that, and the sound comes back - not wind, but drip.

No. Not still on an edge.

I thought maybe I would be in the rice. Something like cold rice - in the settling pans, shin-deep in standing water that never warms. That would suit me. Patient work. But my knees. My knees already told me on the second ladder down, the one made out of rebar that moves a little even though it hasn't moved in forty years, that I am not good low-work anymore. The woman who was thirty getting up with her hands on her thigh - I felt that in advance.

I keep going down, or across - it's hard to tell which is which when every down is also an across - until the light gets worse. Until noon really is dusk. Two factory faces leaning toward each other, fifteen foot doors propped open with old screw jacks, just enough room between them for a gutter to run, and a gantry rail, and two people to pass if one turns sideways. The moss here is black-green and very thick, because direct sun never hits it, only diffuse and sodium-amber from the caged bulbs that are always on.

This is where my eyes stop sliding. My lungs like it here. Smaller breaths. Less to see far away.

I find myself where the water is not crop yet and not waste yet. Where it is just transit.

I would be the chain man. Or the chain woman. The terms have collapsed anyway.

Every cistern has a chain, you said. And you were right - a chain, or a heavy string, or a length of wirevine stripped to cord, hanging from the lip of the cistern above, down through a hole in the terrace floor, or just over the side, to tell you how the cistern is doing. If it is dry, it hangs still and makes no sound. If it is full but not moving, it drips once every long count. If it is moving, it sways a little, and it clinks against the lip, or against the concrete, and the children upstairs and downstairs can hear it at night.

Someone has to hold that.

Not hold the cistern. Someone older and more negotiating holds the cistern. I would hold the three meters between one cistern outlet and the next intake - the interval. My twenty or thirty square meters is almost all vertical. A length of wall that weeps constantly. A pipe elbow that has grown a beard of silver-green weed that I planted there because it likes the cold condensation. A length of chain that I wiped oil into this morning so it doesn't set into rust.

My workshop is a place where no one can farm because it never stops dripping. So no one fought me for it. I have an upturned chemical drum that was once yellow. The top is stoved in to make a seat that fits my ass after years of sitting. I have a bucket that is not for sitting, for catching. I have pliers in my big coat. Too many pockets. One pocket has a short length of copper wire. One pocket has a walnut-stained rag. One pocket has a few seeds not because I plan to plant them but because someone will need one.

I don't move much. That's how you didn't see me at first. The anthill energy goes around me. A kid runs past with a handcart, sprays me up to the knee with that quarter inch of standing water that reflects everything. A woman drags her transformer-box stall ten meters left. Overhead a gantry decides to go the other way and groans. I stay.

And I listen.

Because the rule here isn't written anywhere: If you do not hold it, you lose it, and if you hold too much, it rots. The cistern above me - old Mara holds it, she is seventy and keeps her hair in a bucket-cap - she always wants to hold too much before a dry. She'll close down. Then my chain stops singing. Then the rice two down starts to go pale. So I climb up the three rungs that are my whole altitude, my breath showing even though it's summer, and I tap her bucket.

"Open a half."

She knows my voice. She doesn't like it, but she opens a half.

And the man below me - Joren, who is really still a boy but has bad knees like a man - he wants to dam. He wants to save for his leeks in the boiler. If he dams, my wall, which is already at saturation, will take it all and the moss will become a mat and the mat will become weight and I told you what weight does. So when I see his chain start to pull taut with the weight of water he is saving, I go down, slow, and I lift his gutter board an inch with the pliers.

"Three off that one yesterday," I say. No units. He knows.

He curses me, quietly, because moss eats loud sound. Then he thanks me.

That is the whole job. Not repair. Not planting. Just keeping the imbalance from becoming a break. The tourist would think nothing is happening at my plot. The people who have been here long enough know that if I leave for a day, they will be out with prybars negotiating.

My hands would look like theirs. No, they do. Pale on top, dark underneath, cuticles black, nails soft. My coat too big never drying. My hair cut short because everything snags and because the midges breed right here where the air is stillest at knee height at dusk and you walk through them with your mouth shut. The wirevine has found my rail and I let it, because it shows me where the wall is still warm and where the current still runs.

At night when the dim gets dimmer and the amber sodium makes everything shine, I am still there. That's when my chain is loudest. Ting, against the concrete. Ting, against the lip. Mara's rice smells like cold and starch. Joren's leeks are black silhouettes inside a boiler with its belly cut open. Somewhere far above, the screws that are not wheels turn, turning because all this captured water has to go somewhere, putting a little heat into the wall at my back so my shoulders don't seize.

You would find me if you stayed long enough to be put to work. Not the first time you passed. The second or third time, when I needed a second pair of hands to hold a piece of grating up while I bolted it, because my shoulder alone isn't enough. I would put the handle in your hand without asking. You would hold it. Your palm would get cold and creased. Afterwards you would understand why nobody stands up straight for long here.

You would hear my chain in your sleep after you left, if you left.
