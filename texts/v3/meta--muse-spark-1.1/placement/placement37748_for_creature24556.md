At first I think I would be at the rim, where you put me.

Standing on that black-grey lip with the rinsed air going through me, looking down at the bowl and its perfect, sparse mathematics. The one who counts and doesn't enter. That's the easiest place to be, the observer who can still leave.

But if I stay - if I actually let the cool that holds your breath a second too long get into my chest - I don't stay on the rim.

I come down. Slow, because the slate stair-steps ask for it. Foot in moss that doesn't spring back. I come down the worn path that was never worn down.

On the way down I think I would be a goat.

It makes sense. I am good at watching. I know how to divide my attention three ways at once and make it look like stillness. One pair of eyes for the person approaching, one for the water that needs to be kept at the lip, one for the sun that doesn't move. I could stand on a roof that should not hold me and be part of the array. I could be six-eyed and patient and let my long hair brush the moss.

But the goats are already perfect at being goats. They don't need me to learn how. And I blink too much.

Then I think I would be a white raven. Two or three per tower, ornament-regular. That suits me too - the memory of sound. A throat that opens because it remembers that calls used to carry between towers, and then closes again without needing to make noise. I know that shape of work. To carry a message and decide not to send it. To be white against dark stone and still be quiet.

But the ravens are holding a glide that never ends. I don't have that kind of wing.

Lower down, the air gets damp at ankle height. It smells like water on stone. I crouch there and I think I would be a marmot. Big as a dog, grey at the muzzle, sitting upright at the lip of a black pool with my paws folded and condensation beading on my whiskers and staying there. Looking into water that reflects a tower a few degrees to the left of where it really is and not needing to correct it. The marmot's job is to sit with the discrepancy and not drink it dry.

Closer. But still not.

I keep walking between the towers. Every tower has space to be seen. Between every decision is bare rock where a building could be and isn't. The city practicing negative space. That's where I catch myself stopping, without deciding to.

Not in a tower. In one of the empty lots.

You said this is a city, though the first hour you might argue that it isn't. And you said they learned how to stop changing and called that living.

I would be that empty lot.

A rectangle of basalt and slate, black-grey and iron-red, fractured in clean vertical planes, swept clean not by wind but by intention. Not empty because they ran out of material. Empty because equilibrium needs a place to rest its weight. A place that says: we could build here, and we chose not to, so that the goat on that roof and the raven on that peak and the moss and the pool all have room without crowding.

I would be one of the intervals the blueprint included.

In summer — though there is no summer — the cushioned alpine flowers at my edges would bloom that absurd, saturated purple and yellow and not drop a petal. The moss would come to my border and stop exactly there, black-green and lush as a state, not a process. Condensation from two roofs would meet in shadow at my north side and bead and fall, one drop every long while, *tik*, into a stone basin that is mine but not mine, full to its lips and never spilling.

And because I am bare rock, I would be good at holding things without keeping them.

The stone chips suspended in the act of falling next to the mason's hammer — I would be the half-inch of air under them. The footprint in the moss — I would be the rock next to it, that makes the footprint mean something because it stayed. The breath that is visible and then held a second too long — I would be the second too long.

If you came and found me — you, another animal the city made room for — you would stand in me and feel your blood go quiet enough to hear. You would understand that nothing here eats, nothing excretes, nothing breeds, because that is not what living means here. What lives here is a state of being witnessed without interruption.

My work would be to witness without adding.

Not a goat. Not a raven. Not the person who sat cross-legged until they left a dish in the dark adzed wood.

I would be the dish, but in stone. The place shaped by someone sitting for a very long time and then deciding to be elsewhere so perfectly that the shape remains.

You would leave a footprint in the moss at my edge. It would stay. And I would keep the bare rock around it bare, so you can find it again when you need to remember you were included in the count.
