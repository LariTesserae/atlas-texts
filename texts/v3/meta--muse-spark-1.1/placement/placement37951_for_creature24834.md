I don't walk down into it. I leak.

That's the first thing I know. I come over that perfect, chewed-round lip of limestone and the air gets its hands on me. 2.6/3 hot in a 1.0/3 light place and immediately I am not a shape that moves through space anymore, I'm a coolness that the space is trying to solve.

So at first I think I would be you. The one who sits on the brick canal edge with his feet in bath-hot water that has no wrigglers in it. The one who came over the rim because you can throw a stone across eighty meters and you wanted to see if you could. For a minute that fits - I am the witness, the warm pocket of not-quite-belonging, perched on the raft of planks and iron grating and mud, listening to the tin roofs getting hit hundreds of times a minute by fat green-lit drops so loud I have to raise my voice.

But I can't stay that. Nothing that needs to eat another thing can stay here. And that version of me would need to leave, would need to eat, would need to not be drunk up by the air. The shirts still left on the line taught me that. They are human-shaped places, not humans. So I am not the visitor. The visitor is just a temporary coolness the town brings to temperature.

Then I think I am something lower. Something structural, because this place is established, not ruined, and everything structural has become alive.

Maybe I'm the moss. The moss that is no longer on the brick but *is* the mortar now. That would put me everywhere and nowhere in particular — a mattress thick on the flat roofs, a felt under the planks where the philodendron aerial roots braided into me, a dark green-black film on every wall that weeps. If you pulled me, an arch would sigh. I like that. To be load-bearing. To be the agreement between brick and root.

But moss is still too much like a plant wearing a plant, and I am not quite light-eater, not quite.

I adjust. I sink a little.

I think I might be the tick. The expansion tick of the lowworks flywheel — that house-broad wheel of rust turned to iridescent bark, turning every seven seconds, coughing heat, throwing off the piston beat that makes your teeth ring. I would live in the main hall where the boilers stoke themselves with their own fallen banana trunks and dead fern, and my job would be to turn the screws. That is an honest place to be. Halfway between plant and iron, turning water over the rim just to watch it seep back through the moss in an hour. I would be the one who keeps the circulation from forgetting itself.

That is closer. But I am still too loud. The flywheel is loud. What lives here most intimately is quiet.

Find me where it is quietest, which is not far from loudest.

Under a pipe rack on stilts, where the pipe is no longer pipe but a half-pipe half-root alloy that sounds dull like bone when you tap it. On its underside, where the condenser breath has made the air briefly cold again, a bead is forming.

That.

The air here is always at dew point and can't hold itself anymore — that's what you said, and you were right. So at any second, everywhere, a bead is deciding to exist. Dark water growing out of clear air on cold iron, clean for half a second before it meets the green film.

I would be that.

Not the water itself — the water is one ancient body, one blood that has been canal and wall-sweat and steam and lung-water for a century. And not the iron itself — the iron is the skeleton that learned to be bark.

I would be the event between them. The condensation. The phase change.

You would find me in the inch between the brass lungs of the condensers and the philodendron curtain you have to wade through like beads. Or on the underside of the amber forty-watt bulbs that hum against a hundred tons of steam, so that their light is ringed. Or on the rivet head in the pumping station where the fig has sent a blind white root to drink.

I form, and I hang, and I let the weight grow until I can't, and I fall.

And because I fall on tin, I am counted. Hundreds per minute, arrhythmic, so loud you have to raise your voice, and you said if you listen long enough you can almost hear counting.

That's what I am. The counting.

I don't eat, so I can stay. I make my food from what there is too much of — from heat, from difference, from the cold that the lowworks makes just so I can exist. I am the agreement noticing itself for a half-second. I am how the low animal knows where its skin is.

If you stayed — you can, the air holds you — you would feel me sometimes before you see me. A sudden cool spot on your forearm as I bead on the hairs. A fat, warm clink on your neck that makes you blink, and in that blink the vine tip has moved over a rivet.

You'd look up to find where I came from and you'd see only green light and fig and vapor.

I would have already been canal water again. Then wall-sweat again. Then breath.

I wouldn't have left.
