You wouldn't find me in the middle.

At first I thought you would. When I first tried to picture myself there, I put myself in the market-motion, in the densest part of the trot — one of the fast ones with a pan on my head, water held by balance, not daring to spill. Because I am good at hurry. In my own world, I hurry.

But that kind of hurry wouldn't take me. Not here.

The hurry here has no sound to it. It's all in the calves, in the splayed feet you earn, in the breath held through the nose. My hurry is loud, it talks to itself. The gliders would find me before the first hour was out. I would drop a basket, I would swear. The whole city would flinch.

So I kept walking the image further.

Past the inner drums, where the paths are polished to white glass. Past the old women whose pointing is law. Past the deeper pans where the whiskered fish circle and circle. Further, to where the city thins and the density finally gives up.

You would find me at the tail end of the graben.

Where the last few whale-backs sit un-hollowed because the seeps there have never been quite good enough to warrant a house. Where the floor is not worn path but raw again — salt-bloom and gypsum dust that has never been trodden smooth, where the bacterial mats grow thicker because no one gets out this far every day to scrape them. The light here is still that impossible sourceless bright at noon, but in the mornings and evenings it's dimmer, because the lightwell narrows at the end. The sky-river overhead is only a crack here, a white thread. A quieter quiet.

I live where the city has not yet walked to.

And who would I be?

I would be the one who failed at running.

Not everyone can read a sheen on limestone and know it will weep in six minutes. My legs are wrong — too long in the shin, feet too arched. I would try to carry, and spill. I would try to haul, and slow the team. After a few weeks of that, in a place that cannot afford a wasted trip with a cup of water, they would not punish me. There is no chief to banish you. They would just... stop handing me things to run with. The traffic would flow around me.

So I would sit. Before my feet got swollen, before I earned sitting. I would sit because I am still.

You would find me with my back against a boulder that the city intends to become a house in three years when the seep-line drifts this way. I am supposed to be watching it. My job, the job they gave to the useless fast person who turned out to be slow, is to watch the stone sweat.

I am a draught-listener.

I have a row of little cups — not the fine clay head-pans of the runners, but chipped ones, thumb-pots — set in a line in the dimple of the floor. And I sit with my knees up, my wide, flat, cracked soles tucked under me, and I watch. Because water 0.8 means you have to wait for it, and sound 0.2 means you wait by listening.

I have learned to hear the tick before the sheen.

There is a change in the cotton-press that passes for air down here, a kind of tightening, a half-degree of cool, right before the wall decides to weep. The milk crabs know it too; they go still and lift their hairy mouths. I have learned to see it by not looking — by letting my too-big, grey, always-wet eyes go unfocused until I have four faint shadows of my own hand, pointing in wrong directions, and I watch which shadow darkens first. That tells me where the first bead will be.

And when it comes, I don't run. I tap. Once, softly, stone on stone — the one permitted loudness — two stones I keep in my palm, smooth as eggs.

*Tap. Tap.*

Not a shout. Just enough to carry down the polished paths. And two children who are still fast enough to be allowed to run come sprinting, silent, from the city proper, and they scoop and go, sprinting back with full pans on their heads, without ever looking at me. That is the exchange.

The rest of the time I tend the bacterial skin. I peel it when it is thick as my forearm, dry it, and leave stacks of it for whoever needs it. I watch the paper-thin gliders detach from what you thought were stalactites and I don't flinch anymore. I know by their hanging angle which ones are just sleeping and which ones are listening for a loud thing.

I am pale even among the pale. My hair has gone the soot-brown of the mat I eat. My legs have shortened from crouching.

At noon I do not have to pretend.

When the rip of sky straightens and the whole twenty-kilometer bowl goes shadowless and sourceless, bright as the inside of an eye, the city stops. The runners freeze mid-step. The crabs freeze on the walls. Even the herding isopods pause in their tide. Everyone stands with faces up, eyes shut.

I am already looking up.

I would be the one who keeps his eyes open a little longer than is allowed, because I have learned in that job at the tail end, that the light at noon isn't just light. If you squint through your lashes, in that moment without shadows, you can see the dust of the desert above falling through the crack, very slowly, like snow that will never land. The only thing that comes down from the other world.

Then a drip falls behind me, loud as a bell in the hush, and I close my eyes too, and hold the cold bright in my face like water in a pan, before it evaporates.
