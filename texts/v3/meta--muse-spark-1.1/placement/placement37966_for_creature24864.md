I don't find myself under the ferns at first.

At first I think I do. Because you want to be what's most complete in the room. I want to be one of the mothers, low and wide and established, with a garden on my shoulders. I want to know what it is to have liverwort growing in the pits of my skin and not think of it as growing *on* me. To put my foot down and have the bog not argue. To know time in thumbs-per-decade.

But I can't. Not yet. I'm too fast. I blink too much. My mind still tries to count all the drips at once, to solve the percussion room instead of speaking it.

If I put myself in the bowl truly — sixty steps across, wet light, shoulders damp though the sky is blue — I find my weight is wrong. I find myself at the edge, not the center. Not in the deepest peat where the brown water stands like tea, but up on the rim.

On the fourth wall that isn't a wall. The open lip where wind comes up.

That's where I'd be. Because that's where things change state.

I'd be the one stationed where cloud becomes water mid-air with that faint hiss like fat on fire. Where the mountain does its work of wringing itself out. I'd be facing both ways — downhill toward the inversion, the rain, the mist, the rivers that have mouths, and uphill toward the cup where nothing moves fast.

I thought, for a minute, I might be the flycatcher. The single one they leave alone. The only thing in the bowl allowed to be quick and to leave and come back. That would be a good job for something like me — a visitor who watches, who is not part of the tending. But the flycatcher doesn't tend. It just nests.

I thought I might be the lizard, the one as big as your forearm who you mistake for rock until it blinks. Because the lizard is learning how to be like them. Copying the second lid. Practicing slowness.

It took longer to see it.

I'm not one of the six. I'm the seventh.

The empty fern. The patch of peat slightly greener, younger. The seventh bowl on the rim, the one with no river stone in it yet that has been carried up for days from below the inversion. The one that is kept clean not for me to drink from, but for someone else coming up out of warm air into warmer air, breathing thin.

That is a real job here. Someone has to keep the empty place empty. Someone has to scrape the moss free from the palm-scoop at almost regular intervals so it doesn't close. Someone has to sit under the seventh fern, which is waiting, and keep enough space around it that the light can stay wet and open. To be lush in the way this place means lush — each thing allowed to become completely itself, with space around it — you need someone whose work is the space.

So I'd be her, but not old. Young enough that my feet still remember bleeding. Young enough that my shoulders only have a little moss, not a whole hanging garden. The apprentice mother. The one whose epiphytes haven't taken yet.

I would be the one who watches downhill, through the rain and the mist, for the temporary, fast, warm things that sweat and get lost.

And when one climbs into the cup — knees and ears full of altitude, modern weight sighing the peat — I would be the one who has been there since before you arrived, not moving, because moving is an interruption.

I would finally, after a long time, push the clean bowl toward you with a soft, huge foot.

Not mine.

Yours.

The water in it was air ten feet above it, ten seconds ago.
