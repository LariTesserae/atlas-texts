I wouldn't know at first.

You'd find me lying down, because that's the first decision you make up here whether you mean to or not. Everyone else is bent over, working. The only way to stop working is to lie down. The cold comes through in five seconds. The water comes through in seven. My coat is already wet underneath, so it doesn't matter.

So — lower than the cotton heads. Lower than the wind and the midges. In one of those hollows between hummocks, but not in the black mirror pools. In a warmed-over one. Where someone has been lying before.

There's a place like that near the western tilt, where the plateau starts to remember it has an edge. Not at the edge itself - the roar is too much there, the thousand white threads pouring off the raw basalt lip make it impossible to think. And not in the middle, where the big pumps keep their acres of geometry dry. In between. Where the narrow-gauge tram goes.

You can still see the rails there, or rather, you can see where they are not-moss. Two long, perfectly straight depressions in the green that the moss can never quite close because the sleepers are still underneath. The company left a string of empty wagons about a kilometer out, and the moss grew over their wheels. This is where the cutter-boy spends his morning shoveling the line clear in front, and his afternoon shoveling it closed behind. It's make-work. It keeps the rails from heaving.

You'd find me in the third wagon from the end.

I pulled the yellow tarp that was rotting in it up onto the moss next to the track and that is my floor. A square of blue closed-cell foam, the one everyone learns to carry, is my only dry. The wagon itself is a corrugated tin box with the floor rusted through, and through the rust the green is coming up. Azorella has found it. It's making a hard dome right up through the bolt holes, so dense I can press my palm to it and it feels like shoulder bone. The wagon is slowly becoming a planter for its own cushion.

So I am not cutting. I am not pumping. I am not really employed anymore.

If I am anything with a name, I am the thing that watches the line to see if the line is still a line.

Three weeks is supposed to be a hitch. I've done four. The others don't say anything because up here counting is impolite. My boots are off - thigh-waders, patched with bike tube - turned upside down on the buffer to drain, which they never do. My socks are black in the creases. My hands, same.

What am I? I thought for a long time I would be one of the builders. I wanted to be sphagnum. That seems like the dignified answer - the one that literally holds the world up. Each hyaline cell a little dead cistern holding a living cell in its arms. To be that good at holding. But I'm not good at holding.

I'm not a vole either, though I know their highways now and I use them, foot flat, weight spread, the plat-forming step. I'm too loud for that. I still sink.

No, what I've become is more like the intake screen.

You know the one the pump woman clears every morning? A half-circle of steel mesh at the mouth of the big centrifugal, down in the square cut they made to make dry. Every night the living moss — *S. cuspidatum* mostly, the pale drowned one that never wanted to be firm — creeps across it. Not growing fast, just... leaning. Hydrating toward the suction. Reaching with whole body. By morning it's a living felt over the metal, humming with cold water trying to get through.

She pulls it off in a mat, like skin.

I do that, but for the rails. I pull the moss back so the line stays visible. I don't take it far. I lay it to the side, and by the next cloud-pass it's already sighing water back toward the gap. My job is to be the small, temporary difference between open and closed. To be the thing that remembers that once, someone wanted a straight line here.

That is a kind of living too. The plateau is trying to become continuous again, to thatch over everything we cut or laid down. The pumps fight it and become part of the water cycle. The bricks we press steam and the pipits warm themselves on them. The rails become eskers for voles. And I have become the friction that proves the green is still moving. Without me, you'd never know how fast it closes. You'd think the wagon had always been a hummock.

I lie here and the whole plate thrums through the blue foam, through the tarp, through my back. Diesel thump from a kilometer away, and the hiss of the edge-falls all around, and underneath that the much quieter, much larger sound of water moving leaf to leaf to dead leaf to dead leaf for ten feet down. And the page-rustle of ten thousand bog-cotton heads when the wind hits.

The light is white and painful and complete. Even with the welding goggles I borrowed and never gave back, it gets behind the eyes. I watch the midge column over wagon two rise and fall, rise and fall, each midge living for a day and doing this the whole time.

If you called, I wouldn't hear you. Not because I am deaf in one ear like the cutter, but because up here to hear anything you have to be bent over it. You have to press your ear to the moss, or to the motor, or to the rail.

I'm pressed to the rail. It's cold. It hasn't decided yet whether it's going to be ground or plant.
