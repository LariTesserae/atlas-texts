I think at first I would be you.

Boots wet without puddles. Shoulders wet without rain. Standing in the turbine hall with my head back, watching a lake form above me out of air that was empty a minute ago. Human-shaped condensation, waiting to run down somewhere.

That's where I'd start, because that's the obvious place to put a person.

But I wouldn't stay human-shaped for long. That density is wrong for here.

If I stayed a full day, two days — long enough for the mat to taste the salt in my sweat and have an opinion — I would get slower. The city makes everything slower. You notice it after the heron-thing leaves you: you're moving too fast to see what's actually here. Your thoughts are too fast. Your blood is too fast.

So first, I would find myself trying to become slow on purpose.

I would find myself not in the grand halls, because the grand halls are for air to move through, and I take up too much air. I would find myself in the smaller places where the city had to compromise with itself. The maintenance levels between the second aqueduct and the third. Where a pipe run fossilized inside its own mineral was given a scaffold, and then that scaffold also got a skin of flowstone, and now there's a gap between them maybe forty centimeters wide. Hot on one side, almost cold on the other. Both sides sweating continuously.

There, in that gap.

Not standing. Sitting with my back against the cold side, knees up, because the warm side is too warm to lean on for long. The air in that gap is very still. It doesn't breathe backward like the big volumes. It holds.

And at first I think what I am is still a watchman. A listener. Someone keeping the pumps company.

But the pumps don't need company. They've been turning for a hundred years without tending. What they need is ballast.

What I would become, if I stayed, is a place where the water wants to pause.

You noticed how on the underside of every pipe, every girder, every lip, water beads and doesn't fall? It holds there for a long time, fattening, reflecting. Surface tension winning over gravity until some tiny change in pressure tips it.

I would be one of those beads. Or rather, I would be the reason a bead stays.

In that gap between hot pipe and cold pipe, there is a bead that has been there longer than the others. It has scavenged enough silica and calcite from the steam to make a little scaffolding for itself. Not much. Just enough to thicken the skin. It hangs from a calcite nipple the city grew to seal its own rebar. It's the size of the tip of your thumb. Warm to the touch. It doesn't fall.

Inside it, if you got close enough with a light, you would see it isn't clear. It has color. A faint milk of white and ochre. The First Residents moved into it. They decided the chemistry inside a hanging drop that never falls was better than the gutter. A little private ocean.

I would be that.

Not the drop itself — the negotiation that keeps it there. The ratio.

I am the exact width of the gap and the exact difference between hot and cold that makes the condensation form right *there* and not a centimeter to the left. I am the thin felt of red that crept up the cold side and reached a finger out into the air because it sensed a better gradient, and then stopped when the air was perfect. I am the thing that learned not to explore anymore.

The big white folded ones would pass me sometimes. Not in body — the gap is too small for them, they are weather made for cathedral volumes — but as a draft. A coolness. When they pass, the bead I live in shivers and gets a little bigger, drinking them as they drink the air.

And sometimes a floating lake from the hall above finds its way down through a crack in the tufa, too heavy, lost. It can't fit in my gap, so it kisses the upper edge of it and breaks. For a second my whole world is a rush, a collapse, thousands of those clear shrimp of distilled water washing through, all that brief, twelve-minute life, passing through the ocean I am. Then it's gone, sucked into the scoria floor, and the air goes sharp and clean for a minute.

I like that minute. That's when I can hear the valve three blocks over sigh.

I would not be rare. There are thousands of me, in thousands of gaps. We are not the city's thought. We are its habit. The way it keeps a little water close to its skin instead of letting it all run back to the deep pumps.

If you came back as still-human and put your hand on the warm damp basalt and held it there until your sweat joined the wall's sweat, you might feel me inhaling, a much slower inhalation than yours. Forty kilometers of engineered bowl, and somewhere on its edge, a bead that has learned to hold.

That's where you'd find me. Where hot and cold are close enough to talk but never touch.

What I am is the conversation.
