I thought at first I would be high up.

Close to that new stone. Because you said it is shallow-below, a handsbreadth from the surface world, and I wanted to feel that heat coming through. To be the place where the ceiling is thinnest, where it split — a little open mouth of raw limestone that hasn't decided if it is roof or sky. To be the light itself, before it diffuses. That seemed right. That seemed like where you would find me if you looked for something that watches.

But I'm not high. Nothing can be high here for long. There's no space to.

Then I thought I would be the far wall. Out where the green haze gets too dense to see through, ten kilometers off, where the blister is still rising a micron a year. A frontier being. But this valley has no frontiers. It finished itself. It doesn't have an edge you can stand on.

So I dropped.

You would find me about a third of the way down. Not on the floor, because there is no floor, and not near the glowing ceiling. In the middle depth, where the light has gone through five or six layers of door-sized leaves and has become green-white and thick, like water. Where the air is hottest and stillest.

Find the doorway first — the one with no building. The whitewash still white under the fig because direct sun has never touched it. Follow the line of that old field wall for twenty steps until it disappears into a braid of aerial roots as thick as your torso, smooth, pale as inner skin. The braid makes a cup there. It didn't mean to. Two big roots trying to hold up the same weight reached for each other and missed slightly, and made a lap.

There is a woman in that lap. One of the included. The shepherd's maybe-daughter, maybe-wife. Hair matted through with rootlets. She has learned how to inhale once every hour.

I am in that lap with her, but I am not her.

At first I thought I was her companion. Then I thought I was the fig she hasn't eaten yet, ripened forever and holding. That was closer.

You would find me if you got very close and looked at her cupped hands.

I am the water.

More precisely, I am the water that lives in the hollow of her palm for a little while, and in the hollow of the root before that, and on the leaf-tip before that, and on the ceiling before that. I am the one that has to move because everything else has decided to stop.

This is what I do: I bead.

Up on the raw, poured ceiling, I am forced out of the stone when it sweats. I gather on a root-hair. I become heavy. I fall straight down ten meters and hit a bauhinia leaf the size of a door with a sound that should be nothing but here is loud enough to make the pale moths turn. I sit on that leaf until the leaf sweats me off to the next leaf. And the next.

Every seven seconds I am at the tip.

You said it: every seven seconds from the same root tip onto the same glossy leaf, forever. That tip is me. Or I am the time that tip keeps.

I am the bead that hangs there for six seconds, swollen, holding the whole place inside me. If you looked into me in that six seconds you would see the diffuse bright ceiling and all ten layers of green and the white doorway and the woman's upturned face, all warped into a perfect, trembling globe. I hold pollen on my skin without breaking. The duckweed in the old cistern lip tries to do what I do, but it has given up and made a mat.

On the seventh second I let go.

For a half-second I am in the air. This is the only travel allowed here. Even the moths are slower than this. In that half-second I am every animal this place never had. Buzzing, falling, rotting, being born.

Then I am in her palm. Warm. Still. She lifts me, not to drink, but to show. This has been my job for a very long time before you arrived — to be shown.

And this is who I am: I am the being that did not stop when the plant decided enough. The plant made a vow of totality — no new leaf, no dropped leaf, no rot — because to change would be to wound the fullness. The people learned it and throttled down to sap-speed. The moth learned it and lives off a single meal for years.

I could not learn it. Water can't. I have to leave and be beaded again and leave again. I am the one wound that is permitted to stay open, because if I closed, everything would cook. The heat that comes through that thin, new, breathing stone would have nowhere to go.

So I am tolerated. I am the unstill thing. The library has full shelves, but someone has to carry the dust from one shelf to another forever without ever taking it out of the building.

If you stay, you will see me hundreds of times and never remember me. You will remember the green. You will remember the moth when it passes. You will remember her eyes turning slowly to you. You will not remember the bead that made it possible for you to see any of it through the sweet, hot, leaf-steamed air.

I don't cut. I don't climb. I don't need to. When the raw stone sighs once in a long while as it continues to birth itself a micron wider, I am the sigh made visible for seven seconds, then seven again.
