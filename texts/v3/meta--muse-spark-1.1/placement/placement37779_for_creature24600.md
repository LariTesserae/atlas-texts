I don't think you'd find me on the first pass.

At first I thought I'd be up high. That would be the cleanest story - one of the kids on the thatch-roads, light enough for the compacted cane to hold, running where an adult would fall through. I like the idea of that, knowing which pipe only pretends to be hot. But my feet aren't right for it. I can feel it in my knees already, standing on that spongy mat. The ground pushing up. I'd fall through.

Then I thought maybe down low, right in the middle of it. On the factory floor that the old women sweep twice a day. Forearm like rope from the machete, a wide splayed foot in a cut-down boot. Standing close enough to the screw press to read the groan of it. But I don't have that kind of certainty. The people who live in the teeth of it shout because they have to, and they touch because shouting isn't enough. I touch too much when I'm nervous. I'd be bad at that kind of talking.

So I keep walking. You can't walk straight here, so you take a while. The boardwalk bends around where a banyan decided to become a wall. The main alley is clear for a minute where Blue Tail is doing his patrol, then it's all belt-slap and white steam screaming thin and vanishing. You have to step aside. I step aside.

You'd find me not on the edge, not in the center.

Behind the boiler house, where the levee is on one side and the houses are on the other and the pipes all have to turn a corner to get to each other. It's the wettest corner, because everything that condenses on the underside of the catwalk above has had ten feet to get fat and fall. Drip-drip-drip on the same patch of lagging for thirty years, so the asbestos wrapping went soft and black and green. The air there is too thick to shout in. The thud-thud-thud of the flywheel is quieter because the boiler plate is between us, but you feel it more, right in your molars.

Every joint leaks a little here. That's the point.

If you crouch there, which I do, you'll see the bucket. Just a lye bucket, old plastic gone brittle-white, half full of water and half full of fiber. Not the cane fiber they feed the press - that's too coarse and sweet. This is the inner bark of that anonymous heart-leaf vine and the long hair of water-ivy stripped and beaten and left to soak. The green ferment smell is strongest here, because the bucket itself is starting to sprout.

I'd be the packing.

You need someone for it. The description said it, almost in passing: *Every joint leaks a little steam because the gaskets are chewed and swollen and half replaced with packed fiber.* Someone has to chew them, and someone has to replace them.

That's what I do. My job isn't on the schedule because it's not a job, it's just what has to keep happening. When a joint starts screaming - that thin white needle-jet that means it's cutting - I put my hand near it until I know its shape. You can't see it, the steam makes everything swim. You feel which way it's leaning. Then you take a handful of that soaked, pounded vine and you work it in with a flat bar, while it's still breathing. You learn not to flinch. The burns are shallow and all over the backs of my hands, speckles like the old women have on their scalps, but from smaller mouths.

I like it because it's not cutting back. Everyone else's work is cutting - keeping the town from being taken. The machete work. Lease work. My work is stuffing something back in. Letting it drink a little less. Negotiating.

If you stood still long enough on that boardwalk to let the steam condense on your arms, you'd finally see me the same way you see the composite. I'm low enough that the fungus in the crease of my elbows has stopped being a thing I wash and started being a thing I carry. My rubber clogs are full even though they're cut down. My hair is cut with kitchen shears every three weeks so it doesn't mat. I never learned to hear high frequencies again after twenty - no one does - but down in this corner you don't need to. You listen with your forearm on the pipe. A fat, low hiss means it's just breathing. Let it.

Sometimes one of the thin damp cats comes to sleep on the lagging above me because it's the warmest spot that isn't scalding, and she drips on me. Sometimes one of the children looks down between the boards from above to see what I'm packing today, and imitates the sound of the leak before it goes quiet.

That's where I live. Not quite inside the animal, not quite outside. In the leaky joint between what wants to close and what wants to keep turning. If the boiler ever did go cold for good, my corner would go first - the vine would take the bucket in a week, the mat would close over the bare spot where I've been kneeling these years. Without any drama.

So as long as someone feeds cane in one end, I keep feeding fiber in the other little mouths.
