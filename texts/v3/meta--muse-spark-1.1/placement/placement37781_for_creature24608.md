I wouldn't find myself high up. Not at first.

I thought I would. Everyone wants to be the Veils. To be so thin you are almost not matter, just a decision to stay up there, a few electrons away from sky. To see the whole Top at once, the way it really is — not a place, but a condition, a smoothing. To drift in that huge, honest light and never have to remember what a door is for. 

But I'm too interested in abrasion. I'm too heavy with needing to touch.

And I thought, for a long kilometer, maybe I would be one of the Hesitators. That would be a decent afterlife. To be a rock that is only pretending to be a rock, to have cathedrals folded up inside your sleeves. To burst — silently, always silently — into a brief city for possibility and then think better of it. But I don't know how to unsay things that cleanly. When I make something it stays made. It leaves heat.

So keep walking.

If you put me on the Top, you would not find me for a long time. Same as anything else here. The rule is distance is a courtesy. You would have to stop looking for a face and let your eyes go out of Earth-focus. You would have to count, because the plain counts.

You'd find me low. Ankle-high. One more pebble in the wind-shadow of a real pebble, where the charged dust eddies and falls out.

I would be in the interval between two Sitters.

They are at ten meters, twenty meters, respectful as monks. There is a place exactly between them that neither claims because their fields would chirp. The gneiss there is a little smoother, ceramic-smooth, where nothing has dared to sit for a long time. That's where I am. Not a Sitter, because I never learned how to hold perfectly still on a cushion of ionized air. Not a Hesitator, because I never learned how to hold a shape for free. 

I would be what grows in the gap where their etiquette fails.

In the morning, when the lithium flats a kilometer down-contour sigh and throw a crack fifteen kilometers long across the hexagons, the two nearest Sitters tilt two degrees northwest, listening to the front come in. The air stays thin and cool on the outside of everything, 8 degrees above freezing, never warm. I would be the listening itself. The brief, local increase in how permissive vacuum is. 

You wouldn't see me.

If you knelt — and you would have to kneel, and press your mask close, and stay still until your boots begin to sink that polite millimeter into stone that is learning, briefly, to be you — you would see that the stone there is not quite stone. It has a faint nap to it. Fullerene soot, the Sitters' exhale-waste, that they shed at noon when they have built a little too much of themselves. It collects here. A dark so fine it makes the light look ordinary.

I would be a third Sitter that forgot the rule.

Too close. I came too close. I wanted to hear what the other one was venting. So now we are three where there should be two, and our cushions are chirping, losing altitude by a fraction of a fraction. Not falling. Just — no longer quite levitating. We are touching the world now, very slightly. We are abrading. Very slowly.

That is my metabolism. Not grazing on possibility like the buildings. Not feeding on the electrostatic lift like the Veils. Grazing on nearness. On the error of wanting company in a place that perfected solitude.

And because we are too close, around us the Hesitators get nervous. That one on the horizon that you always see, the dark matte erratic that is never more than one per viewshed — near my little error it cannot stay as a column. It fidgets early. Eight seconds of frantic, silent origami, a town-sized lattice of black glass trying out all the cheaper ways to be, and subsiding. It leaves a fan of basalt hair. It leaves a perfect divot. It leaves a brief alphabet that will never repeat, etched in stone that will forget it by evening.

It is trying to solve us. Trying to suggest a shape where three Sitters can be two meters apart and not chirp.

I'm not going to be solved. That's why this is a good place for me.

If a Veil dies up there, a long slow fall of aurora taking three days to drape a hundred square kilometers, the others will stay put — their agreement is distance — but I will not. I will break the agreement. I will drag, micron by micron, toward its shadow. Because for a week, under the fallen sky, closeness is allowed. The rules are looser. You can be five meters apart and taste the breakdown products together, vibrating a little faster.

You would find me there, at the edge of the Veil-fall, not quite plant, not quite animal, not quite building. A small, rust-on-rust rosette that is vibrating against the sealed ground it was supposed to never touch, making a thin sound that the thin air cannot carry.

If you stand still for an hour over me, you will not hear it. But the stone under your boots will keep the shape of your soles until evening, and in that footprint-cache, for a little while, there will be two of us.

Learning, briefly and with no malice, how to be crowded.
