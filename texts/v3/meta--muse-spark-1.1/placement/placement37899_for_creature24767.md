At first I think I'd be you. Whoever you were in that account.

Because that's the default entry point, right? Someone standing on the rim with sore knees from the climb, breathing too loud in the thin air. I can feel my boots wanting to do the polite thing — stone to stone to stone, not crushing anything. I look down into the bowl and I hear the hooting first.

But I would not stay human for five minutes. Nothing about my metabolism works here.

I am too fast. My thoughts tick too quickly, my heat is all wrong — careless, wasteful, leaking out of my skin. That place doesn't want a furnace. It wants a compost pile. If I stood there long enough I'd start trying to be useful and there is nothing useful for a human to do in that city except leave.

So I try to be something else.

Maybe the snow-finch. That's a tempting first revision. To not live there but to be allowed to visit. To drop in from a lower, richer world, to be forgiven for being loud and hungry, to land on the tallest elder tower in the middle — the one four times a man's height with the liverwort shawl on its dead side — and tear at its top for pupae and scold the wind while I do it. Five minutes of important business and then gone. I know that kind of life. Arrive, take what I need, make a noise about it.

But I'm not a visitor. I can't get that high and then go home.

So lower. Closer to the stone.

Maybe one of the white moths. That one pulls me. The ones who hatch in the warm peat and live and die inside one tower. They've never seen another tower. The circle of sky at the top of their chimney *is* the sky. Their wings fray on peat walls. When they die their scales become insulation. I feel a kinship there — a whole life spent inside one warm hollow made of processed death, mistaking it for the whole world, and calling the light that comes in through the top "the world."

But no. I am not that faithful. I would look over the lip if I could. I would want to know if there were other chimneys hooting.

Then I think: the salamander.

I'd find myself low, where you almost missed us. Under the lip at the far edge where the afternoon's fog, having done its work, finally gives up and sinks into the mountain. Black as wet gneiss, granular skin that looks like stone until I move. Which is almost never quickly. Fifteen feet in a day. Orange belly I only show when I have to stretch over a gap between stones.

That's where I would be — not in any tower, but in the twenty feet of bare, scoured rock between them. The empty avenues. What you called the genetic ocean.

Because the towers have to be apart or they blur. And someone has to remember the whole city at once. Not build it, not sing through it, not eat it. Just carry.

A fleck of peat in the crease of a leg. A spore stuck to my side. A mite egg. A springtail that had the bad luck to fall out of its warm house onto cold stone. I would pick it up without meaning to and deliver it, fifteen feet a day, infinitely patient, from the noisy young cushions on the windward edge where the fog hits first, to the skeletal leeward ones who are dying on one side.

I wouldn't make heat. I wouldn't make a note. I wouldn't make soil. I would just keep the islands from becoming planets.

And if you came back days later and crouched down to see me, you wouldn't at first. I'd be stone. Then I'd be a very slow comma moving between your boots. And if you put your hand into the hollow of the nearest tower after I had passed, you might hear the ticking a little different than before. Slightly more red here. A little more haircap there. A new dialect.

But even that is too romantic. Too much protagonist.

The longer I stay here — in my mind — the more I realize I would not be any animal at all. I would sink.

Find me not on the stone, but as the stone's thin slick. As the almost-nothing film of lichen and pooled water that lives on the bare gneiss itself between the towers. In the place you called empty.

That's where I would find myself: mid-avenue, between three of the tall old ones in the sheltered middle, where their hooting crosses and cancels and makes a pocket of relative quiet. Bare ancient molar-rock, polished by ice a hundred million years old, with a hand-span of water that will freeze tonight and crack with a rifle-shot at noon tomorrow.

What would I be?

The listening.

The low place where the drips land. Every tower around me metronomes into me. The fog beads up above me, grows heavy, and falls. I hold it for one minute longer than bare stone should be able to hold it. Which, in that economy, is a kingdom. Which is the whole trick that started the first tower in the first place.

I would be the empty lot that makes it a city. Not the building. The distance that requires mailmen.
