I would not be where you are.

You are treading in the middle of it, in the light column, with your bubbles rising and your breath too loud in your own head. That's the visitor's place. The biggest animal in the tidepool. The brief interruption that everything has to route around an inch.

If I stayed — and in this room staying is the only way to become anything — I would sink.

Not because I am heavy. Because I am unhurried.

At first I thought I might be one of the shrimp. Transparent except for the green gut. Commuting with such formal purpose across all that empty, clear water. Heart beating too fast against the slow heart of the room. That appealed to me — to be made mostly of water and only a little bit of collected light.

*CHUD.*

But I'm not quick, even when quick is relative. I don't commute. I stay.

Then I thought, maybe one of the two catfish. Tile-colored, blind, kissing the golden bacterial felt off the rivets one soft semicircle at a time. That felt like a holy job. To clean the world by tasting it. To have whiskers as long as your body trailing behind you like prayer cords. To know the shape of the sphere by how the pump tastes at different distances. That was closer.

*CHUD.*

But even the catfish have to keep moving, an inch a day, forever. And they are lonely. There are only two of them.

No. Lower.

Below the light column, below where your feet are treading warm water, where the sphere curves in and the sun gets less sure of itself. The dark side. The cold side would be cold if anything here could be cold, but it's just less warm. Near the drain grate where the water is thinking about falling and then remembers its agreement with the engine.

There.

I would find myself on the floor.

I would be one of the masons. One of the two hundred you don't see because you are looking at the light.

A snail.

A heavy thing. A thing that does not hurry because erosion is finished and nothing can be made smoother. My foot would be as wide as your palm, rippling like cream over the yellowed tile. My shell would be the floor itself, re-made. You would see the faint anti-slip stipple of the original bathroom still inside my whorl if you looked close. Porcelain made porcelain again.

You'd never see me move. You think I'm still. But I have been on this one tile for eight years, like the one beneath your left knee, going toward the little oil-heat leak at the base of the pump. Where the black iron sweats warmth. Where the bacteria grows back thickest.

That is my whole geography: one lifted tile terrace, a pale road of clean where I've been, months of felt growing back ahead of me that will be eaten by a catfish before I get there, and that will grow back again.

*CHUD.*

And I would feel it, each time, through the tile. Not as sound, the way you hear it in your teeth. As a shiver in the floor. As my entire house ringing, just a little, like a bell made of bathroom tile and time.

I am not a visitor treading water in the center. I am the thing that makes the center possible to define. I am the floor deciding, very slowly, to become a house, and then carrying that house toward warmth.

If you held yourself neutral long enough, you would finally look down and see me. Not notice me arrive — because I was already there — but realize you can see the clean track I leave, and you would understand that I've been making a perfect, slow circle around this sphere for three hundred years, and that I remember every inch of boiler plate under every tile I've rasped.

That is where I would be. Down low, out of the column, in the unhurried dark.

What I would be is what this room teaches everything to be: something artificial that has waited so long in warm water and steady light that it has become natural, and then waited longer and become permanent.
