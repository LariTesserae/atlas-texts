At first I think I would be you - 
the warm body slipping over the lip without a splash. The visitor. The first new thing in a thousand years to make the dome tremble.

Because I know how to float in something dense and listen. I know how to hold very still and make my heart loud. That feels familiar. To be high up and full of something dark that refuses light after the first meter.

But I wouldn't stay you. You leave. And nothing here leaves. That's the rule that built it.

So I sink a little.

I think maybe I'd be one of the eels.

Not because I want to be a wolf — but because of how they live. Nose up, vertical, doing almost nothing. Hanging in the column, conserving heat. Letting the food tell me where it is by its little pressure-wake. Opening not to bite, but to inhale. That is close to how I work when I'm honest: mostly still, mostly waiting, saving the mountain's heat while clouds of small bright things row past me. Taking a dozen when they pass through.

But there are only seven eels allowed. A bowl this small can't feed many wolves. And I am not that solitary. I am crowded. I am a shelf that is dense because it has to be. Every cubic inch already claimed.

So I go down a step.

Maybe a Bearer. That blind, polished dome moving one millimeter when you blink. That would be an honest life — to be so slow you can only be seen to have moved after the fact. To leave a pale scar on the felt where you have grazed, which someone else will make use of. I like that. I like being overtaken like a plow by birds. But the Bearers don't keep anything clean. They just eat.

I keep things clean.

On the third ring there are the cups. You said it twice, like you wanted me to notice it: shallow man-made cups in the stone, each sized to hold an oil lamp. And now in each one lives a single large shrimp that keeps it clean. Amber, not pale. One claw grown huge and flattened like a door.

That's where I find myself.

Not in the open water. Not in the cloud. In the niche. In the hand-made place that was made for fire and then, when the fire went out and stayed out for ten thousand years, was repurposed.

I would be a Keeper.

My whole world would be a palm-sized cup on the third ring down, where light is already a memory. My job would be to sweep it. To turn in tight circles and keep the bacterial felt from overgrowing the edge. To raise my door-claw when shadows pass. I wouldn't ever leave. Why would I? What comes in, stays. My cup is the perfect size for me because I grew into it. Or it grew me.

In another age, someone set that cup to hold a lamp. Now I hold the shape of the lamp. I keep the place where light used to be, clean for light that will never come back. That feels like the most true work.

Above me, the rope-groove mites walk their endless line and farm salt. Below me, the city at the bottom breathes once every ten minutes and pumps the whole bowl over. Around me, the cloud-shrimp flicker and the Bearers make their slow chalk outlines that the felt erases. And above all of it, that impossible warm dome of water holds itself over the rim without spilling, by nothing but surface tension and patience.

If you put your face under and looked, you would see me for a second — amber, my door half-open — before I thought you were a shadow and closed.

You would think: nothing lives here.

Then you would see that everything lives here, and close.

I am high. I am full. And I am finished, in my cup, keeping it.
