You wouldn't find me at first. That's the rule of the bowl.

At first I thought I knew. I'd be one of the seven, of course. I want to be useful. I want broad hands and lichen at my temples. Put me in the Mill with a copper hook, let me stand with my palm an inch off a turning shaft until I can feel the wobble in it like a pulse. That would suit me.

But I'm too fast for that. Still too fast. I came down here without noticing the descent — that means I was walking quickly. The keepers never would. And when I stood on the street of crown-stones and moss, I looked for people when I should have been looking at the pruning. So no, not a keeper. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

Then I thought, maybe I'd be the pigeon. The bewildered one on the iron bridge over nothing. That feels honest. An animal that got in by mistake, too loud, too warm, looking around for a reason for a bridge to be. But the town has a rule about animals. It oozes white sap when bitten. It closes, slightly, at night, the way a leaf does. It would politely, reasonably, make me leave. I don't want to be made to leave.

So I keep walking. Fifteen minutes end to end if you don't stop to look. But I stop.

I stop in the gap. That's where you find me, actually. Not in any building. In the resonant absence between them that they leave on purpose, to let air move so the walls don't rot each other. There is a space behind the Dye House and to the left of the Engine Shed that is exactly big enough for one more thing. The air there is a degree cooler. It's where the morning condensation lives longest. By ten a.m., the rest of the town has dripped dry, but this gap is still beading, still ticking. The copper gutters here never quite stop ringing.

You find me there at noon, when the light is high and white and shadowless, because I've been trying to be still long enough to watch a branch thicken. I'm bad at it. My attention snags. The loom clacks — nine good beats and then the one off-beat where the tooth is worn — and I look. The vat sighs rust-red. I look. A beetle the size of a grain of rice crosses a seam. I look.

And that is what I am.

I'm not one of the seven. I'm the eighth interval. The new cutting.

They pressed me in late spring, when they were whitewashing. Not a finger-length of willow-wrist. Smaller. A shaving of calf that had gone properly corky, pressed into compost made of last year's creeper and loom waste, into that damp envelope that never dries, under the little glass insulator that warms me with amber streetlight. I am the bulge in the gap.

I am not tall enough to tend a loom. My elbows haven't made elbows yet. My skin is still too much skin, not yet stippled grey-green except on my shoulders where the sun hits me when it breaks through in columns. When I bleed, I still bleed red first, then clear, then white and bitter. I put moss on it.

My job is not to keep the machines from wanting anything extra. I don't know how to do that yet. My job is to keep the *gap*.

I stand in the cool air that is my air and I let it stay cool and damp and empty. I am learning the maintenance-as-company. Twice a day one of the keepers passes on the one real street and does not speak — speech fogs the air and you don't want that — and touches the top of my head, where the lichen is trying to take. It will take eventually. Their hands are so smooth, bone-smooth, like the stair rail.

At night I don't go inside all the way. I don't have a wall-pocket that has held my shape for decades yet. I fold back a moss curtain that is too big for me and lean against the place where three trunks meet and make a barrel joint that never dries. I listen to the town inhale around me, wood creaking as it adds a ring. The engines underneath cough, once, deep in the earth. The spider drinks its drip right above me and I am, for that moment, the thing that holds its web up.

I am the evidence that the town decided, reasonably, to keep building itself.

Not a ghost. Not a keeper. The kept.
