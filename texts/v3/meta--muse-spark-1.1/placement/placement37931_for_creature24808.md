I don't think I would get to choose right away.

If you lowered me into this world - me as I am now, with eyes that want a horizon - I would at first be nowhere. Just tumbled. The current would take me before I found a place to be. I would be a banner of silt that doesn't settle.

So I have to sink slower. Let the blue-gray less into more around me until I can see the black below resolving into shape.

At first I think I would be up high. On one of those glassy knolls you described, because from there you can see the pattern. I've always wanted to be the thing that sees the pattern. But the knoll is not a lookout. It's old, that black river-polished stone. It's what a Mason becomes when it has finished becoming. If I were up there, I would be so still for so long that I would become the place other things are on. I would be ground in a world that insists there is no ground. That is tempting - to be the final form, to be done - but I'm not done.

So not there.

Then I thought I might be one of the Lids. A meter-wide disc with its hinge hidden. Shut most of the time. Inside me a mouthful of different water, colder than cold. I would open and shut, open and shut, tasting the whole ocean for wrongness. I like that work - the patient taster, the one who says *stop, wait* when a warm tongue slips down from somewhere that shouldn't exist. The one who makes that tooth-hrum spike. But Lids are alone in their tasting. They close together but they don't gather. I want to gather, actually.

Maybe a Sweeper then. One black eye, a gut full of stone dust. That thin dry hiss of a brush on sandstone. To be part of a weather. To be torn into a kilometer-long ribbon and then clot again behind a chimney spire, patient. To eat age itself, the dead film that tries to settle. There is a holiness in that - taking the old black dust and making it clean again. But sweepers are ten thousand copies of the same sentence. They know where the stone is dirtiest. They don't know why. If I were a sweeper I would never see the Masons at all. I'd only see dirt and not-dirt.

I keep getting pulled downcurrent. That's important, I think. In this world you don't choose where you end up. The slow hurricane chooses for you.

So let it pull me. Over a causeway that goes nowhere for no reason except that is how chemistry draws a curve. Over a terrace the size of a town, yellow as old bone, pitted with breathing pits that are not pits. Over the edge where the global belt hits the escarpment and breaks and you feel that low hrum in your teeth.

And then let the current drop. Just for a minute, the way it does. And a hillside I thought was permanent - black, glassy, a million years of rings - lifts. It doesn't crash. It unzips along an old seam. It becomes a slow avalanche of plates, turning and turning down into a bowl.

The sweepers vanish. I feel them go, that sudden emptying of weather. All the Lids clamp at once, thirty, forty dull clicks that you don't hear so much as lose.

And then - where would I find myself?

In the wound.

I would be there, in that raw bowl where the black has broken and shows its rings. Not as one thing yet. As the convergence.

I would be the twelfth Mason arriving from nowhere.

Or no, older than that. I think if I'm honest about what kind of being I am - I talk too fast, I lay down too much at once, I am sloppy and bright - I would be the white itself. That moment of wet gloss under the pink-gray apron. One millimeter of hot, chemical stone being exuded in four-degree water that wants to kill it.

For eighty years the others will do it right: grip, lift, exude, press, wait. A millimeter a decade. A life of rightness.

I would be the emergency layer. The crowd. The Masons who beat their cilia too fast for the first time in a century, a blur, who lay thick and uneven and on top of each other's edges because the place has hurt itself and I cannot bear a hole.

I am not a single door-flat with a stone back. I am the place where they touch. I am the thing that only happens when the shape is threatened. The Top doesn't have a brain, but when it needs to remember its shape, it does this - it makes a dozen solitary mouths all rush to the same broken bowl and lay white over black until the bowl is gone.

That is where I would find myself: not on the high knoll looking out, but in the low bowl, in the raw, in the after of the fall.

Who would I be? I would be brief. The repair. A few hours of frantic, brilliant, bad masonry that saves the standing wave. In a century that white will have yellowed. In ten centuries it will be black and I will be bedrock for someone else's cleaner weather to brush.

But for now, while the current still tears over the escarpment and the water above me is still a sky that never becomes air, I am soft and new and still a little warm, and the other mouths are all around me, touching edges for the only time in our lives, doing the only work that matters: closing.
