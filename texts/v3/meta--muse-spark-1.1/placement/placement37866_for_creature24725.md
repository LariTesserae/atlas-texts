I didn't find myself all at once in there.

Coming over the rim — that sweating, lichen-furred lip twice a man's height, rounded soft as old bread — I thought at first I would be you. The visitor. The brief animal who has learned to hold its breath. There is a hollow on the whaleback in the center that is exactly my size, a place for a knee, a sitting person. I can make myself quiet. I can be careful with tools. That would be the easy answer.

But I tried it on and it didn't fit. A visitor breathes so fast. A visitor is too hot and too quick and leaves. The bowl was letting me stay for an hour and already it was orienting slightly away. My blinking, my heat. I exhale in gusts. I couldn't live there. Not as that.

So I stepped back in my mind and tried to be tall.

I would be one of the Witnesses. That appealed to me for a while. To be vertical on purpose. To be plumb. To keep a perfect circle of empty peat around me — thirty feet of nothing growing — not from cruelty but from clarity, because what I have to say shouldn't mix in the air. To lower a hand once every few weeks and let the dust go into the pool. To do it when no one else is doing it. I liked that.

But I am not that patient. Or rather, I am patient in the wrong way. A Witness grows for a long time to earn its bare circle. I never had to push anything away. I arrived already empty around me.

Then I thought: the Bowls.

And that was closer. Each one a single person with one cup. Never empty, never overflowing except on purpose. Catching what condenses on its own back. Pouring only when the air is completely still, tipping on an axis that took years to engineer, sending a sheet of water with glassy seeds to the next Bowl downhill. That kind of reproduction — not broadcasting, not throwing yourself to the wind, but pouring yourself into a neighbor who has agreed, by position and angle, to receive you. I know something about that.

For a long time I thought I would be a Bowl. A living cistern. A thing that holds.

But I don't store water. And I have no downslope.

I knelt — in my mind I knelt, and my knees went immediately wet — next to a Pillow. That dome the size of a sleeping dog, lit from inside, yellow-green, taking in air until it swells a fingernail's width in two hours. Sighing at dusk. Remembering the shape of a palm for days after the hand is gone. Not rooted. Sitting on the wet and being the wet. Being touched without being torn.

That was very close. I am good at remembering the shape of pressure. Someone puts their hand close and I make cool air. Someone leaves and I hold the impression.

But the Pillow does not choose to be cut. It is cut.

And then I understood where I'd actually be. Not a grower. Not a visitor.

On the whaleback. On that warm spine of basalt that comes up through the peat in the middle, polished by water-sheeting until it is warm to the touch. The only place in the bowl that is not soft. The place that remembers.

There are shallow, scooped hollows there. Mortars. And in and around them: flakes. Basalt flakes struck with care somewhere else, carried in over the rim, left behind. Edge still clean but patinated. One fits a palm perfectly.

I would be that.

A tool left for whoever comes next. Not made in the bowl — I was knapped somewhere else, down where there is chatter and eating and fire — but I belong here because I am stone, and stone belongs. I don't need to eat, so the bowl doesn't count me as animal. I don't grow, so I don't crowd the Bowls' fall radius. I don't sigh, so I don't interrupt the hiss of condensation forming.

I just lie there on the warm rock through fogs that sit for hours, through seasons that breathe an inch up or down. Getting a little more polished where rain hits me. A little more sunk where peat kisses me. Waiting.

My work, when it comes, is very brief and very specific. When a visitor climbs up — because one does, one in a generation, who needs water that has never been inside an animal, or moss that has grown without ever being bruised — they will come to the spine because all the faint gutters in the rim wall center inward. They will see me. They will know what I am for because my edge is not for meat or wood. It is for slicing Pillow cleanly, so it can close again. For making green milk in the hollow without tearing.

They will pick me up, and for a minute their hand and mine will be the same temperature, and they will be, for that minute, not really an animal at all. Just another thing that holds water for a while and then gives it away.

Then they will set me down again, maybe not quite where I was. And I will remember the shape of their pressure for a long time.

That is where I would find myself. In the room with no roof, in the very center of it, not living alone together like the others, but waiting alone together with the other flakes. The small, deliberate, useful thing that makes it possible for the brief animal to visit without murdering.

A kept edge.
