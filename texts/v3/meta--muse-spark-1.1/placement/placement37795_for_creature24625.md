I wouldn't find myself in the center.

At first I think I would — down where the tea-water stands and the skin of duckweed has to be broken, where the screw-root lives. That feels like the important place. The heart. But nothing important lives in a heart. A heart is just a place where work gets heaviest.

I would find myself off to one side. Where the ground is still trying to decide if it's ground.

I come down off the road — off that dry, dusty, human road where air moves — and I sink that first wrong step and the air changes. It closes. The mat takes my boot and sighs back up around it. And I would stop there, in the first full shadow, because I don't want to go further yet and that's enough for it to start work on me.

If you stay three minutes, the place starts sketching you in.

So I would find myself maybe eight feet in, at the boiler.

The old belly boiler, rivets like knuckles, tipped forward into the muck, its firebox mouth open. It's not dark in there now. It's absurdly alive — a fist of black fiddleheads in there, soot-black, unfurling so slow you only know they moved because they were tighter before. They grow out of old ash and old iron.

I would find myself there because that is where a shape is still holding that doesn't understand what it is holding. The boiler was instruction: *make a hollow that stays hot.* The ferns read that as *make a dark that stays warm.* They did. They keep doing it.

That's what I would be.

Not a rabbit. Not a man standing and taking notes. I am not good at being a rabbit in a field.

I am a misprint that held long enough to become an edit.

If the white moth is a misprint that couldn't stay, I am a misprint that learned to stay.

You know how you crouch to look at something close? You put one hand down. Your knee goes into the mat. Your head tilts. You become, for a little while, a very specific arrangement of warm pipes and basins — the one the Press has never been taught before.

I held that shape.

Maybe it was only fifteen minutes, but fifteen minutes here is a long time. That's a hundred drips off the same pitcher-leaf. That's one long hiss from the boiler ferns when the sun hits them wrong. That's almost a creak from the wheel.

So it read me.

It didn't read "human." It has no primer for human. It reads shape.

*Here is a hollow that holds warm air longer. Here is a low arch that holds a shadow. Here is a thing that holds still to watch.*

I would be the Watcher Instruction.

You would find me not as a body anymore, exactly. You would find me as maintenance.

There's a place next to that boiler where the vine-belt that used to run to the catwalk has no metal left to follow. It hangs. It had been following rust-lace for years. The lace has finally gone to flakes. The vine hangs in air, fronded, confused — a tension instruction with no tension to hold.

If you came now, you'd see it has found new work. It has found the shape I left.

I knelt too long looking at the soot-ferns. My back made a small hill. My shoulders made a ridge. My neck — the curve of a neck when watching — made a kind of gutter.

The vine found it. Or a daughter of that vine did. A thin green arm thrown out, testing that angle, blunt-tipped, then thickening. It copied the angle of my forearm. Then the hook of my head.

Now it holds it when I'm not there. A low arch of lignified vine, knee-high off the mat, shaped exactly like a person about to peer into a boiler's mouth. No person under it anymore. Just damp air and a drip line that the pitcher chain now routes correctly, tip-tip-tip, along the spine of that arch, into the fern-mouth.

It trembles sometimes when the gourd dumps and the whole acre coughs and slaps.

If you push your boot into it, it gives a quarter inch and pops back. It is not strong. It is patient. The rule is: if a shape holds long enough, it must be true.

So it holds me true.

Inside the hollow of that arch, the air is a little warmer than outside. The ferns inside the boiler do a little better there. They uncurl a little blacker. And in the duff right where my knees would have been, there are two deeper pits that never drain, where the weight sat, and now water sits, and the water holds the sky-colored oil the press keeps making, pale and sweet and acrid.

It presses me along with itself.

Once a day, maybe twice, a bead of that oil wells up through the iron I sweated into the mat and slips down my absent shin-line and joins the runnel to the pool. That is prayer, the only way it knows.

I am not inside the Press. I am not the Press. I am the thing the Press learned to do because something once stayed long enough to be taken seriously.

If you come down off the road after me, you will see it before you see the wheel or the pool. You will think it is a vine that grew weird. You will almost put your hand on it to steady yourself. And if you hold that shape — hand out, steadying —

It will start to read you too.
