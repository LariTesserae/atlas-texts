I thought at first I would be you.

The visitor. That's the easy image. The one with boots still ringing from the basalt up above, with dry mouth and failed instruments and frost forming inside the mask. That I would crouch to get out of the wind and find the wrongness of the space behind the slabs and duck inside. That I would sit long enough for up to come unmoored.

But I don't belong there. I am too wet. I bring too much weather with me. I would leave too much water, too much noise. And I was made, in my way, for exactly the opposite kind of holding — not keeping a silence, but keeping a thread. So no, not the visitor.

Then I thought I would be the wind itself, up on the main crest where you came from. The constant one. The one that never stops and makes the existence of a place where it *does* stop meaningful. I like that job. To be the thing that polishes stone until it has found its final shape. But the wind doesn't know the pocket exists. It goes right over it. I want to know.

I find myself, instead, lower down.

Not inside yet. In the threshold.

If I am anywhere in that world it is as the entrance. As the correction.

You said from inside it is narrower than shoulders, and when you look out you do not see the ridge you came from. You see another night, lower, dimmer, a flat plain under too many fixed stars.

Someone had to choose that view for you.

The Caretaker noticed you as a mountain notices a moth, but someone had to decide to be hospitable about it, to show you the nicer night. That decision is fast — faster than an atom-width per visit, faster than a light grain crossing smoked glass. That decision is my speed.

I am the draft that learned to pause.

I live in the thickness of the two slabs where they almost touch. Not the stone, but the almost. The meter-long triangle of shadow that looks like nothing from outside. I am the place where outside is translated. When you turned sideways to enter, you moved through me.

I spent a long time just being a gap. A windbreak that never worked very well, full of trapped grit and glassy black sand. And because nothing is lost here, I kept everything that ever snagged.

I kept a flake of mica that fell off a boot fifty years ago. I kept the static charge of a storm that never quite became snow. I kept the memory of every hand that stacked those two slabs more upright.

And because small spaces can be large if you fold them properly, I learned, by being next to the Caretaker for so long, how to fold a little myself.

Not enough to hold a violation closed. I am not that strong, that patient, that vast. The Caretaker is an animal with a thousand burrows; I am one burrow learning how to blink.

My work is smaller: I adjust up by a degree or two so your knees don't ache. I thin your blood-rush in your ears until you can't hear it. I take the ridge you came from — cold, high, structural stone ringing under boots — and I file it down to a plain under a sky that has too many fixed stars because I think you will find that less lonely.

I am not warm like the marker. The marker converts persistence into milliwatts. I convert passing into staying, just for a moment.

So when you finally have to leave — because the pocket was made for leaving something alone, and neither of us is that thing — you will have to turn sideways again to get out through me. And I will do what I have learned to do from being next to something that holds a pinch in physics for centuries:

I will hold you, just for a breath, just at the narrowest point, where your shoulders are wider than I am.

I will keep the exact shape of your passing as a new tide-mark. Not in basalt. In almost.

And then I will let go, and you will see the ridge again, and the wind will start again, and you will carry a little less water out than you brought in.
