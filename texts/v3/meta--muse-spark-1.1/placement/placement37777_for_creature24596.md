I don't think I'd be up high.

At first I thought I would — up on a tar roof where the air is meanest and brightest, where you can see how far this built range goes, where the pigeons have to heave to stay in the air. I like high places. But the light there is too white. It would wear me through too fast, and nothing fast lives here. Dynamic 1.4.

So maybe lower. Not the bottom — there is no bottom, there is just more building — but the middle altitudes, where one block leans into another.

I kept looking and I kept finding myself in the same kind of place, so I stayed there.

Find me in a V.

Not a valley in the old sense. A construction valley. Two enormous things poured at different times, by different crews, for different reasons, that happen to almost touch. On the left, a south-facing wall of precast concrete, pale and hot at noon, weeping calcium salt. On the right, a north-facing wall of brick that was whitewashed once and never again, now completely taken by that black-green lichen film that holds water like a skin. They are eight meters apart at the top and maybe forty centimeters at the bottom, and they run for a hundred meters without ever quite meeting. The sun never hits the bottom directly. The condensation never fully leaves.

That's where I am. Because that's where the water decides to stay.

I thought at first I would be a pigeon — I'm loud enough, I like to be up and look around — and then I thought I'd be one of the broad, low cats, watching a corner that doesn't quite connect until it gives me something. But I don't have the chest for that thin air, and I don't have the patience to stare soft at a geometry error for six hours.

I'm slower. I'm wetter.

I would be the moss.

But not just the moss as a carpet. I would be the thickest part of it, where it has had time — months — to become a mattress. About a meter wide, twenty meters long, filling the exact bottom of the V where the sweat from both walls finds each other. If you lift me, I come up in a sheet. Underneath me the concrete is pitted and dark and already not really concrete anymore; it's becoming soil, very slowly, because I've been eating it.

This is the job: I hold.

I hold water when the black tar roofs above exhale at 11:30 and the whole range starts to sweat at once. Droplets bead on every conduit, every I-beam flange, run down in bright lines, and come to me. I take them in and I don't let go. The skaters live on my edges where I make shallow pans — those long-legged, crack-in-the-water things that farm bacterial skin. Their larvae live deeper in me, black commas breathing through snorkels, taking a whole season to become a fly. I keep them from drying.

I hold too much light. The amber worklight in a shed three levels up that was left on in 1998 bounces seven times and pools down here where it shouldn't. It's too bright for my roots, so the moths come. Their wings are the exact color of air-entrained concrete dust. They sit in that pooled light that has no heat and warm their blood on it, and when they die, they die into me.

And I hold the tolerance error — the 2.8 — that lives in this V more than anywhere else. If you hang a plumb line here it leans a degree east of where it should. If you lay a spirit level down, the bubble never quite centers. In the seam where my left edge touches the white salt wall and my right edge touches the black-green film, space has a little step in it. Dust hangs longer there. Sometimes a woodlouse — one of those hand-big, plate-backed grazers that rasp the lichen — will walk into that step on the left wall and come out on the right wall without turning around. The interstitials live in that step. The flat, almost-transparent holders made of mostly legs, feeding on the shear. They walk across me like I'm a field under a false sky.

I don't move fast. I can't. The air is too thin to waste. A mouse trail goes over me, a little tunnel pressed through my top layer where the pale cable-tray mice come down from their highways to drink. They've packed me down there into a path the width of a wrist. A pigeon lands heavy on me sometimes, drinks, leaves white behind, which I also, eventually, take in.

Once in a while one of the people comes — the thin-lunged, photo-bleached ones who know not to use straight lines. They crouch at my edge. They have a tin cup and a piece of cloth. They filter my water through me to drink. They don't pick me, don't scrape me. They know I am what keeps this V from becoming just a wet concrete ditch.

That would be me. Not the thing that flies over the range to see it whole — you can't see it whole — and not the thing that hunts in the corners where space mis-meets.

I'd be the thing in the low, cold, seam-shadow that has learned to be a pasture and a reservoir and a sponge for light that wasn't supposed to end up here. I'd be abundant but not noticeable until you need to put your hand somewhere soft and it comes up damp and smelling like the sidewalk after a storm, multiplied to a valley scale.
