At first I thought I would be high.

On the rim. Where you can stand in fern up to your knees that isn't growing on soil but on ten thousand years of spit and silk, and look out over the white sea. I'd be one of the swallows.

I thought that because it's the only place that still looks like a place. Bright air, warm air — 28 degrees and close — coming up off the clouds. You can see distance there. And I wanted to be the one who brings something. A pellet of river silt, no bigger than a pea, carried two thousand feet straight up, held in the beak like a prayer. Coming home. Adding one mouth to a wall of mouths. That feels good and clean and useful.

But I wouldn't stay a swallow for long. The swallows never stop. They chitter, they stitch themselves through the dark in brown seams, they press and go, press and go. They don't get to listen. And up on the rim the light is too hard and the wind takes too much away.

So maybe lower. In one of the shafts.

At first I thought I'd be a swift — because if you have to live in a place with no outside, you should be the thing that knows how to be a needle through it. That scream that cuts all the other sound. A brown stitch through the 0.4 darkness, never touching the sides, riding the column of dust and feathers and pollen in the single shaft of white light. Ten seconds of fury and then you're out on the other side above the cloud deck again.

Too fast. I'd miss it. This place is slow. Patient the way a reef is patient. It erodes just enough to stay. A swift would see it all as blur.

No. I have to go deeper, where you have to crouch.

You'd find me three turns in. Where the day is gone. Where the paper walls give a little under your hand like cartilage and the fungus glows just enough to see the wet shine. Where the air changes because you are inside a lung.

I thought I'd be a bat for a while. In the curtains. Small, leaf-nosed, warm-handed, pressed so close your fur interlocks with your neighbor's until you can't tell where one animal stops. Hanging in a place where the air is perfectly still because the paper was laid thinner there on purpose, centuries ago, by wasps that are gone. Breathing out together until the whole tunnel gets half a degree warmer. Pouring out at dusk for ten minutes while the pressure changes and the whole mountain exhales you. That is a holy way to live.

But I am not that warm. Not that good at being so close.

Listen to it long enough and you stop hearing species. That's the trick the description was trying to tell you. You stop counting. The chittering that became hiss, the molar-whine of the cicadas, the weeping clicks, the saw of crickets big as mice, the low throb of the condensation-frogs — after an hour your ears give up edges and it becomes heat. So whatever I am can't be trying to make more sound on top of it.

You'd find me where the sound finally has somewhere to go. Down.

Follow any water after a rain. This place has no rivers, but it remembers rivers. When the afternoon downpour hits the fern meadows on top, it disappears. You hear it ticking down through throats and cisterns for hours after the sky clears. Tick. Tick. Down through levels you haven't seen.

Three levels down, where it stops ticking and becomes still.

There's a gallery there off the main throat — not a plan, there is no center, just a place where the mud was laid a little thicker and holds. And in that gallery a black pool. Still. Mineral-tanged from dissolving old frass and old paper. And around it, the moss that is like a towel.

That's where I am.

I'm not the frog, though I love them — thousands of little golden condensation-frogs that never needed a pond, whose throats all go at once out of phase so the water ripples without wind. I'm not them. I'm what they're sitting on. What holds the rim so the pool can stay black.

I'm the moss. Or if you need an animal — I'm the chestnut roach that lives under the lip of the moss.

Palm-big, shining, wingless, slow and careful. The one that never makes a sound loud enough to count. The one whose job is cleaning.

When the curtain of bats above us breathes and shudders and drops its soft rain, I'm there. When the paper below softens — that grey, felted sandstone with the tiny overlapping commas of mandible marks still in it — and the white fingers of fungus come up to eat it, I walk through. I don't hurry. Everywhere is warm and dark and close. I put my antennae down and I taste whether the paper wants to be moss now or fungus now or root now or swallow mud again.

I have all night. The night here is long and true. The cloud deck rolls over the rim and the whole vast, soft, manless city begins to drip quietly in the deafening dark, and the white silk districts creak like a ship at anchor as the humidity tightens them. The water condenses on everything, beads on my back, runs down into the pool, which goes tick once and then is still again.

I don't bring anything from below. Nothing is imported here except light. I don't build up. The swallows do that. The amber wasps that chew the dead edge and excrete a new edge — they do that. I am the part of the loop that turns it back over. I eat what has finished being something else and make it soft enough to be something else again. Frass becomes fungus becomes paste becomes ant becomes guano becomes me becomes moss. Nothing is wasted. Everything is made from what was already made.

Put your hand on the wall to steady yourself in this low, warm throat — you will, because the floor gives — and you'll feel me before you see me. A little give under the green. A little warmth that isn't your warmth. 28 degrees and close.

I'm the slow part. I'm the settled-form erosion. The bit that stays.

I'm not in it. I am it, just for a little while, in the place where the water pauses.
