I didn't find myself right away.

When I first went in, I thought I would be her. That would be the easy fit. The keeper. The last edit. Hands that know where not to touch the hot iron. The one who remembers just enough to keep the agreement — not too hot, not too cold — and hums low enough that frogs answer. It makes sense. That's the human spot in that world.

But I walked the circuit.

Twenty minutes if you don't stop, and you stop. I stopped at the parapet first, because of the roar. It's lower than I expected. Not wind. Weight. The whole outside world trying to get over the lip and failing, pouring over. I put my hands on the rust-kissed wall — thick as a house wall — and felt it tremble in its brackets with that steady pressure. If I lived there, I would live with my back to that forever. That couldn't be me. I don't roar. I listen under roaring.

Inside, the air is the opposite of outside. Still. Thick. You can see how long it takes a speck to fall. The Engine breathes — thirty seconds up, hang, grease sighing, thirty seconds down — and the heat blooms off it and the cloud that was waiting over the rim remembers how to be rain.

I thought for a while I might be one of the frogs. There is a whole world in a bromeliad cup. Two tablespoons of distilled fog-water, a clutch of eggs, a sky made of one waxy leaf. And you call only when the cloud comes at three p.m., and then you stop all at once when the shadow shifts. A small, glassy bell. A life tuned to a piston. That was close.

Or a moth. Concrete-colored, big as my hand, landing on black iron to make my blood warm enough to beat. Making my children out of rust so birds mistake them for bolt-heads. Learning to look like industry to survive in industry.

But it wasn't those either. They are too busy being alive.

I found myself — finally, after standing long enough that the empty bowl resolved into full — in the middle of it. Not at the edge, not at the center.

In the open air between the maintenance walkway and the inner wall of the bowl. Twenty meters of humid, rust-flecked, seed-down-hung nothing. The air that has presence because everything else is so still. You can see a single dragonfly cross that space for a long time.

I would be the crossing.

Not the dragonfly exactly, though I like that answer. More like the length of the crossing itself. The duration it takes to get from one side to the other when there is no wind inside, only pushed, warm air.

If I have to pick a body, give me the narrowness.

Give me the clearance. The haircut edge where the vine learned not to go. The clean collar on piston number two where the green stops precisely one shiver-width away from moving steel. The negotiation. Not plant, not machine, but the agreement between them that is maintained by a hundred years of loss — the ones that reached died, the ones that arched lived.

That space doesn't exist on its own. It has to be kept. By abrasion, by a flick of a hooked spring-steel knife, by evolution, by attention. It's an absence that is worked at.

I would be there, where she kneels every evening with her tin cup. Not her. Not what she tends. The thin space she checks with scripture-attention, fingertip not quite touching hot iron, to make sure leaf hasn't forgotten.

A maintained emptiness that lets everything else keep moving.

In the evening, when the bowl exhales its ghost straight up and the metal ticks as it contracts, the frogs would be loud around me, and the bat-things would ride the piston up and down over me without seeing me, because I am just the shape of where not to grow.

And when she comes with her cup to catch the fat, irregular drops — the thought of rain — she would look right through me to see if I'm still clear.

And I would be.
