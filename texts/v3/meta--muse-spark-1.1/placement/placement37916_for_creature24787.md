I think at first that I am standing.

That's wrong. Standing is something you do in the avenues, down on the cut stone where it's still sharp under your boots. Where the air is so big you can see the haze at the end of the mine, nine kilometers off.

I'm not there. It's too quiet where I am. The air here doesn't eat sound — it holds it. A little too long.

I'm cool. Not cold. That 10 or 12 degree cool that lives in your knuckles first. My lips have split. I notice that immediately, because everyone here notices it immediately. The little white lines at the corners of my mouth. The bead of red in the corner of the eye that never quite goes away because you have learned not to blink very often. It hurts less if you don't.

So I am one of the people, then. That was my first guess. A person in a coat made of bone-colored felt, too big on purpose so it holds a layer of dead air around you. I can feel the dust in it. Moth dust, frass dust, leaf dust that never washes out.

But my hands are wrong for hauling bale. They are soft. Too soft for how dry everything is.

I am sitting. I'm sitting inside something that is not a building and not a tree, because here root and branch are the same word. Around me the walls are lined with felt that gives a little when I push it. Living felt. If I shout, it would eat the shout. If I were in the dead wall on the other side of the avenue, it would hold my shout for three seconds and give it back to me changed.

So I am in the living side. The side that is still deciding.

I have a knife in my hand. It doesn't shine. Nothing rusts here because rust needs water and we don't have it. We trade for cloth on the surface because we have so much dead leaf that will never rot, and they give us knives that will not rust.

I am carving.

Little curls of pith come off soft, like dry cheese. Not sawdust — felt dust. It hangs in the normal daylight. The suns are up high today, that full-spectrum October noon light that makes you squint a kilometer underground. I can hear the gantry far above me whining on its rails, moving two meters. The canopy above is shifting. I can feel it in the wall under my palm. A slow pop. A sigh.

Pop.

That was this wall growing an inch to the left while I was trying to carve a shelf into it.

That's my job. I'm a Tuner.

I was born in the third node from the east end, where an old Airwright threw a trunk straight through what was going to be a school. Fifty meters of hollow trunk, dry as a wasp nest inside, hard as fired clay outside. My mother was a Carder and her lungs went bad early from the fibers. My father walked suns and is mostly deaf. I was the child who would sit with my ear pressed to the living curtain that was the north wall of our apartment and listen to where it wanted to close.

Nothing can die properly here, so you have to negotiate.

This room was a pantry last winter. Then the Airwright behind it decided it wanted light and leaned. The wall bowed in. We came home from the looms and the pantry was a throat. You could still see the carved niches where we kept the condensed-salt jars, now stretched into ovals. My mother said leave it. I said no.

So now I am here with my knife where the pantry used to be, telling the growth where we still need to live.

Where would you find me? Not on the map. Maps go out of date every week here because a trunk decided to corkscrew. You would find me in the interstitial — between the dead forest that never falls down and the living forest that never stops trying. In a hollow that is at once an apartment, a hallway, and a branch. Up high enough that when I look out the round window — an old Borer hole, eight centimeters perfect, still with the little black beetle statue at the end that we left because it's good luck for wiring — I can see across the void to the next node, just lights on the quarry terrace. And between us, one huge Airwright standing alone with its own sun, creaking to itself, combing static off the air with its billboard leaves until its hairs glow faintly in the dim hours.

Who would I be? A child who learned not to sweat.

On my knee, in the pocket of my big felt coat, something vibrates. I reach in without looking. A Listener. Hairless, the color of old linen, ears fringed with salt. We keep them not as pets but as something quieter. He has been sitting in my pocket all morning, condensing my breath on his ears and licking it back so neither of us loses water. He clicks, too dry to carry far.

I put down the knife for a minute. I put my forehead against the living wall. It is cool and it thrums with pressurized air, not sap. Somewhere above me a thousand hand-sized moths are lifting off a sail the size of a house, making that sound like turning pages.

My lips sting. My hands are soft because I work in living pith and not dead cable. I am learning to carve a place to stay open in a place that only knows how to accumulate.

If I do it right, a family will put their salt jars on the shelf I make tomorrow, and in ten years the wall will have taken them anyway, perfectly preserved, because nothing rots here and so nothing is ever really lost.
