At first I think you'd find me at the top.

Not in the pocket, but on the lip of it. Where the sedge hangs down over the edge and drips even when it hasn't rained. I would be the thing that makes people miss it. The thatch. The brown veil. I would be good at that — being the cover that lets the place stay empty.

But if you push past me, I don't stay there.

Down in the cut, the air drops ten degrees in a foot. That cold that comes up is too cold, and I know that air, so I must have already been down there a while.

You'd find me on the floor, maybe. At first I think that's right. I'd be the thread — that chain of touches, not even a stream, that learns its way around every cobble from the back wall to the front. That seems like a job I'd have. Small, patient, finding a way. I would be the water that isn't the Waterfall.

But I'm not moving. Not really. The thread moves. I don't.

Then I think I'm higher. In the seam near the roof, clumped with the others. A bat, breathing once a minute all summer. Not asleep, exactly. Just deciding that the roar is so big there's no point in adding any other sound. Listening with my whole body to the place that is too loud for any animal to claim. That feels honest. That feels like something I would do.

Until I remember — bats leave.

And I don't leave.

You'd find me in the back. Of course you'd find me in the back.

Not *behind* the back wall where the river throws itself off the low shelf and hits and hits and hits — though for a long time I thought I was that, too. The hammer. The pressure in the molars, in the chest. The loud that stops being loud and starts being weather. I have been that in other places. Here, I am not loud enough.

No. You'd find me *on* the back wall. On the inside.

Put your palm there and it buzzes. The stone there is only a man's arm thick and shot through with hairline cracks. Beads gather on it, hang a long time before they fall. In winter it becomes clear, knobbled ice on the underside of the world.

I am the place where the water tries to get in and can't quite, for ten thousand years.

I am the green that learned how to live there anyway.

Not the forest — there is no forest here, not in the way green is usually allowed to be. I'm an island. One of them. The big one on the boulder that fell before anyone counted winters. So deep and so tight you could lose your whole fist in me. Bright as bottle glass in the grey lace-light that the falling water throws across the floor. I have been alive longer than the name of the river above.

I don't drink. When a bead hits me, I hold. That is my whole theology. Hold.

Next to me is my brother, the beard off the drip-lip that never swings because the air never moves, and between us the liverworts — black-green hands pressed to the rock, older than leaves — shining. We are the bulk of it. The forest here. An archipelago of soft planets in a sea of cold black sand.

Nothing rots here, so everything lingers. That is how I can afford to be.

And I am kept.

That's the last part, the part that takes your eyes a long time to quit trying to see and start seeing.

You'd find me under the leaning slab too.

Not as the slab. As the small dry triangle it makes where there is no dry. As the flat stone with the bowl ground into it by another stone, round and round for longer than a person can stand to be cold. As the dark water in that bowl that has been still long enough to have no ripples even though the whole world behind it is shaking.

As the three white pebbles in that water, placed one by one, not dropped.

I am not the woman who placed them — she, or he, or the long chain of them with the same cold hands, who comes down the worn scoops sideways, who brushes the moss off the very top stone of the pink granite cairns because even moss can be too much covering. Who sits where you sat, with her knees going numb, and breathes an invisible cloud into the 34-degree air that never warms.

But I am what she leaves behind when she goes back up into the wind.

Because no one lives here. It's too cold and too dark and too loud to live. But someone has to live *with* it. Someone has to keep it empty on purpose, in the way a cathedral is kept empty on purpose, with vast space between one living thing and the next.

I am the kept-empty. The absence that lives here while the keeper is gone, waiting for the next one to come and be the keeper.

A prayer that doesn't want an answer can live here, you said. Grief can live here. A question that doesn't want an answer can live here.

That is what I am.

You'd find me exactly where you were looking when the deafening stopped sounding like the river and started sounding like the room itself humming.

Stooped, in the lace-light, looking at a bowl of still water that shouldn't be still.

If you stay until your knees go numb, I'm the humming that you take back up with you.
