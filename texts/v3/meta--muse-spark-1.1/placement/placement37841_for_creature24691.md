I came in the way you're supposed to - from the chain-link end, where the access road gives up.

I stood there for a while with the air sitting on me, because it's that kind of low. Not the grand low of a canyon, just the poured low. The kind of low that doesn't have a view. The concrete under my shoes was still sharp at the corners. It hadn't been taught yet how to round.

At first I thought I'd be high. If I got to choose.

I'd be one of the cats on the tar roof. Above the chuff-chuff-chuff enough that it goes through the roof instead of through your molars. Deaf in one ear in a useful way. Sleeping in the thin place where the cloud wears out. Watching the tea-colored pans without needing to drink from them. That seemed like a good life - related to everyone, accountable to no one.

But I stayed a minute and the cloud didn't lift and I realized I don't want to be above anything. It's too exposed up there. The sun finds a slot between 11:40 and 12:10 and puts a hard line right on the tar. You have to keep moving.

So then I thought I'd be fast. The only thing in the whole bowl allowed to be brisk. The bottle-glass-blue dragonfly. I would be hawking through the pillars that aren't really pillars, those stationary columns of midges that hang over each sump and just hum at one note. I would be the one cutting through them. But I listened to my own breathing and it was already slow and wet. I don't have that kind of hurry in me. Not here.

I tried being human, because it's the obvious draft. The cot in the back of the office, the kettle that never gets dry, the calendar you mark not to know time but to prove it passed. I would be the third one, the one who has been here long enough to do both jobs. Good at speaking under the sound, low and close to the ear. Good at wiping the handrail. But the humans here want a transfer. Even when they don't say it, their eyes keep going to the gate where the real swamp starts. They love the PRESSURE STEADY / FLOW STEADY / TEMP 41C line because it's mercy, but also because it means nothing they do matters that much.

I don't want to want a transfer.

You have to go inside the big hall to find out where you actually are. Outside is too bright-dusk, too sourceless. Inside the air is smaller. That's where I started to hear the place as pressure, not noise.

I walked the high, dry-ish islands between the motors. The chest-freezer motors in their rattling cages. The red wheels. The smell of hot oil and hot water that have learned to live together. The geckos were exactly where they should be, a hundred wet-concrete ghosts pulsing their throats out of sync with the pistons on purpose, or because they never learned to sync. The toads in the weep, mouths open, letting the world come to them.

I put my hand out - wiped it first, out of habit I learned in ten minutes - on the rail by the middle pump. And there it was.

Not me on the rail. Me *in* the rail.

The seal.

Left side, middle pump, where the pipe shoulder meets the housing. Black rubber gone shiny, swollen from years of 41C and steam. It failed a long time ago. The logbook from three handwritings ago probably says SEAL 2 WEEPS  -  MONITOR. And then the monitoring became math. The leak was measured and then added to the flow calculation and then it wasn't a leak anymore, it was a tributary. It's part of the system now.

That's where I would be.

I wouldn't be high or fast or waiting to leave. I would be low down, on the hot side, hissing steady at the exact frequency the midge columns sing at. The kind of hiss that stops being a problem and starts being a pitch the rest of the animals tune to. I would have a little tea-colored bloom under me, a constant bead and break, bead and break, plink to puddle to drain to sump to pipe again.

You would find me if you crouched. Most people don't crouch here unless something is wrong. You'd have to get your face close to where the heat comes off. You'd see me doing the one thing I'm allowed to do forever: not quite holding.

And the geckos would drink off me at night. The toads would watch me without judgment. The cats wouldn't hear me at all, which is its own kind of kindness. The old woman doing the logbook would walk past twice a day and pat the boiler next to me the way you pat a dog, and pat my housing once, out of habit.

PRESSURE STEADY / FLOW STEADY / TEMP 41C

— because I'm still weeping at exactly the same rate.

Don't fix me.
