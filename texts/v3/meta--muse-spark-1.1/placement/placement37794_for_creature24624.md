You wouldn't find me at first.

At first I thought I'd be high — up on the basalt, where the wind is still clean and the cloud hasn't yet decided to fall in. The lookout. The one who watches the lip where the trail gives out. But I don't stay high for long. There's nothing to hold there except wet stone and the duty of scrubbing it.

Then I thought I'd be down in the middle, where it's deepest. Where the floor isn't a floor. Where the black water that isn't open water is lifting between the sphagnum and the frogs all sync up for one holy second before they lose each other again. The listener. But it's too loud down there even for me. Too much coming up.

I settle somewhere in between. In the second layer.

If you walked the bowl widdershins — and you can only walk widdershins, because the big root-bridge on the sunward side grew shut last week and the old men haven't shouted it open yet — you'd go past the clump where three sisters live in one fused crown and past the pantry platforms sagging with tubers that have already sprouted thin white eyes, and past the slug. He's working today, same as always. You'd have to step over him.

Right after him, where the bamboo trunks are thickest and the light is so chopped it falls as warm coins on your shoulders instead of rays, is where I'd be.

You'd find me kneeling.

I am the house.

No — I am the one who is a house. Or the one who tends the house that tends me. There's no word for it here because there's no difference.

My house is four clumps. Four. I bent them myself when I was still small enough to fit between them without turning sideways. You take the live culms, you pull their heads down until they weep. Until the sap comes. You tie them with wet hide and you lie to them, you tell them *this is up now*. Two seasons they argue. The third they agree, and their leaves knit. My roof is twenty feet over my head now but it still tries to be a tree. Every morning it makes new shoots as thick as my thumb, pale and furious, aimed straight for my throat.

So you will find me there in the morning, when the water off the rim is heaviest and the cloudflies are a smoke that even the wettest moss cape can't quite keep off your neck.

I am small. Not child-small anymore. My chest is too wide for my height, it has to be, and when I shout — *hold, hold, HOLD* — at the root-ladder that is trying to root under my hands, my voice tears coming out. Everyone's does. My hair is cut short with shell. My feet are bare and they have been bare so long the moss between my toes isn't dirt anymore, it's residence. If I stand still on the platform the algae takes my print and then politely erases it.

If you ask the kids who I am they'll say I'm the Snip. Because my hands are the tool.

And they are. Look at them. The left thumbnail I have let grow long and filed on the basalt rim until it's a yellowed horn. It's for grubs. For lifting the edge of a leaf without tearing it. The groove between thumb and first finger — that's permanent now, a white callused trench where the bamboo cord lives. I am the one who knows how hard to pull before the living wall gives a little sigh and lets go. Not hard enough and it snaps back. Too hard and you wound it and then it bleeds sweet water for a day and every beetle in the bowl comes.

My job, which isn't a job, which is just keeping my door a door:

I prune.

Before midday, the walkway to the central pool. The vine there grows an inch while you watch. If I don't cut it, my neighbor's little girl can't get her jar down to the black water without crawling. After midday, my own walls. My own doorway which was a doorway at first light and by second light is a lattice of thin, questing white shoots that smell like green beans when I snap them. I boil those. After that, the roof. Always the roof. I'm up there ankle-deep in leaf-mash that my house makes to feed itself. It is warm from rot. There are red beetles in it, working. I pick them off and drop them in the gourd basket without looking. My girls will boil them tonight.

And while I do it, I have company.

There's a thumbnail frog who lives in the cape on my shoulders. She is orange as lichen. She came last week and decided to stay. When I lean too far over her throat puffs and I feel it against my ear. She eats the cloudflies that bite the back of my neck. The old freckled bites there — I don't scratch them. There's no point. I keep her watered. At night she goes down to the pool and I miss the weight of her.

I am not bored. There is no time to be bored. The second law here is *everything that falls must be raised* and everything falls. A shell. A child. A word, if you don't shout it. But there's a gentleness in it, too. Nothing in this bowl is trying to get bigger. It's trying to close. To hold. The mountain weeps and the green catches the water so the mountain doesn't have to drown. We all weep and catch.

So at dusk, when the cloud we've been breathing all day finally gives up and lies down cold and white over us, and the warblers finally have nothing left to say, and the frogs all at once —

— you'll find me folded.

My house folds me. I un-tie the hammock of aerial root — I have to re-tie it every third day or it becomes wall again — and my two daughters and my mother fold in with me. We steam a little, wet as we are. We sleep wet. The roof drips its orderly drips down the channels my cape makes. The floor beneath us quakes gently, because somewhere deep the sphagnum is holding another liter of mountain.

I put my palm up against the culm that is also our north wall, and I feel it. Very faintly. Not wind. Not water.

A pulse that isn't mine, trying to keep being a door.
