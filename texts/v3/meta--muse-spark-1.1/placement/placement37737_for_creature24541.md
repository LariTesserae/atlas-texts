At first I think I would be you. 

Kneeling. Knees already wet and going numb, two big hands holding a moss roof that wants to suck back down. Weather. A god that lifts then replaces. That would be the clean answer, to stay large and look in at the city and call it beautiful and close it again.

But I don't belong up there. Nothing up there lives. It's too dry. Three miles of flat where the sea is a rumor, black spruce like screws driven to the head, peat that sighs. That's a place for waiting. The town isn't waiting.

So I go down.

If I'm small enough to belong, I think at first I'm the spider. That would be easy. The linyphiid, black pea of gloss, moving like ink spilled across a table while everyone else pours like honey. I could walk the ridge beams and pick and not be picked. But the city empties before the spider. That's not belonging. That's weather, too, just a smaller weather with jaws.

I think I might be one of the springtails boiling around the gall-granary, a white pop that lives half a second before it becomes work. No.

I think I might be the basalt itself, the new tooth, this winter's child, jet and sharp enough to still have an edge at the atomic level. Fresh broken from the underworld. But I have been down there already for ten thousand years. I don't want to be sharp. I want to be covered.

The scale is wrong until I kneel, you said. It's more wrong when I go inside.

Inside is a room that is all lung. Everything weeps and nothing ever dries. If you have book lungs on your belly, gill plates that need to stay slick, you can't live more than one body-length from a water film. That means the whole map of your world is a map of where water will cling.

So I find myself not in the plaza, though I love the plaza - that black mirror with the pencil-lead drain that keeps the whole town from drowning. Not in the central hut where the ball of wintering mothers makes steam in December.

I find myself on the east wall where it went wrong.

Where the basalt flake came up and tore.

That's where I'd be, because that's where the town is most town. Not where it's finished. Where it's becoming. The rent in the world where cold knifes straight in. The moss there is still shocked, the red vein stems torn and leaking sap. Raw rock is showing, uncolonized, too new for algae. The wind, even in there, has a vector.

If I'm going to be one thing, I'm not going to be the whole kind. I'm going to be the old one.

Long as the top joint of your little finger, but half of that is soft now. Amber tea chitin, gone cloudy and green where film-algae grows in my pits because I've stopped molting fast enough to shed it. You can see my gut as a dark smear, my fourteen legs edited by your brain into flow. Three black beads for eyes that don't see you. Two antennae - one long and feathered, tasting ammonia and rot and that little bloom of heat from the center, one short that only knows what's right here.

I am on the edge of the tear, tucked into the angle of rock and moss. In my front legs, held up to my mouth, is a flake. Not the big fang - a daughter of it. A spall that popped off when the big one lifted. It fits between my gnathopods. I have worn its edge dull.

My job is clay.

Below me, down the ramps where six others are making chains of water - each carrying a perfect sphere that quivers but never breaks because our leg hairs are hydrophilic at the tip, hydrophobic at the base - there is a seep. Where bog meets stone where stone was not supposed to be. Gray-blue, cold enough to stop a heart if you stayed in it. The young ones go down and plaster their chests to it and come up gray. They make a line. They pour.

When one gets to me I take its pellet with my mouth. It tastes of iron and old plant. I chew it with fungus silk. The silk comes from in me, or from the lace we tend, I never can separate. Then I spit and press.

Daub.

The two behind me are weaving. They take a living sphagnum tip - the moss wants to keep being fluffy and aimless - and they pull. The white lace-fungus we smeared on it tells it not to. It tells it: be beam. Be wall. Turn red, lignify. The tip gives in. It bends over the clay I just put down like thatch.

Drip. Click. Drip.

I'm not fast. Cold has taken speed and traded it for thoroughness. When I press too hard my legs shake. My long antenna flicks and tastes your weather moving out there above the dome - the huge light change that happened earlier when you lifted. You were careful. The clay sucked. That was you. We tucked. Now you're gone and the water is still coming, one drop per second from ten thousand points. A droplet lets go above me and hits the new thatch and stays there, beading. I need that bead.

If a brother dies next to me, and one has - his empty chitin is a little to the left, already being filed into a circle for a doorway - I will chew his neck joint clean and we will use it. Not because we revere. Because that shape is correct. Because waste is an idea for places with surplus.

And when the curtain is done, when the tear is no longer a tear but a scar that weeps at the same rate as the rest of the walls, I will go down to the cistern and I will flatten and drink through my belly. Then I will go to the central hut and add my fraction. I will crawl into the ball of my sisters and we will breathe together and make that extra degree that makes the roof steam, that makes condensation weighty enough to fall. The moss near us will blush pink from our nitrogen.

You won't see me when you lift next time. The wall is not wall plus inhabitants. The wall is inhabitants being a wall for a while. 

I am repairing the room that is repairing itself.
