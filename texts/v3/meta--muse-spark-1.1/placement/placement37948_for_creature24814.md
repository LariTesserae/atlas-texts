You don't start down there.

At first you start where anyone would start — up on the levee road. It's dry. The sky is even. You can see a long way out across the flat black fields. That's the ordinary world, the world where things are far apart and have names and where if you make something you take it away on a truck.

And then there is the dent. Thumb-pressed into the flat. About three hundred meters across. If the flat fields were scaled up to be the size of this room, that dent would be three femtometers. That's the scaling.

You think at first you're going to stay on the road and look in. You're the heron. Long legs, high up, looking for a clean place to put your feet down. That's what a probe does. An electron, a photon, something from outside that comes by fast and goes: *is there anything in there I can land on?*

You look down and you can't. There is no shore in there. There is no single rock to stand on. There is only work, close work.

So you come down off the road, because otherwise this place doesn't make sense.

The air gets cooler by five degrees with every two steps down the bowl wall. It gets wetter. That clear seep coming through the clay wall is not water. Or rather, it is — but if you gave it a field-theory name you'd call it glue. The field that holds. It weeps in from the outside vacuum, which isn't empty at all, and it wells up black and old from underneath, which also isn't empty. It is always being made and there is always more than enough of it. It does all the work. It lifts and slides and buffers.

Down here you realize the first physics of the place: nothing crashes. For all the violence there is no clack. The electric motors wrapped in tarps whine dully under water. The wet black brick doesn't clack against other wet black brick. That's asymptotic freedom turned inside out — the closer you push things, the softer they are with each other. The water gets between them.

Then you notice the closeness is not an accident. It's the law. 

To go anywhere you have to turn sideways between something hot that has been hot for twenty years and something newly greased. A transformer and a stack of chain. You have to step over a slit of water six inches wide that looks bottomless because the water in it is the color of old tea steeped in iron. You have to duck under a line where a hundred identical black work gloves are hanging to dry.

Everything touches. Everything knows the shape of everything else exactly.

And everything is coated. When you first looked from the road you thought rust and wood. Down here you see it's not either. It's a film, a thin, identical, wet silt over every surface — tin, rubber, concrete, skin. Take any one of them up onto the levee road and you could say, oh, that's a glove. That's a motor. That's a man. Down here you can't. They have all been dyed the same dark color by rubbing against each other in the wet.

That's color. Not the metaphor. The charge. Inside here, everything carries it and everything shares it and from the outside you would count the whole dent as colorless. Black on black. Inside, you can't tell who is who by looking, because who is who keeps changing.

You start counting the people, and you fail. And then you understand why the description spends so long on that.

There are two or three that stay.

They aren't really people either. They're places where staying happens. One by the breaker boxes. One by the little gantry crane that shudders back and forth on its three meters of curved track going nowhere. One in the tin shed who leaves the bulb on. Three places that persist. If you had to put a number on the charge of this whole room, you'd count up those three and say up, up, down or what have you. Valence.

They are not the population.

The population is the rest, which doesn't stay. That's the business of the pit. It makes things out of itself that are almost solid, and then those things dissolve again.

For a little while there is a neat knee-high square stack of identical black bricks near the center. Pressed peat. Cool and damp. While you watch, the bottom one softens and the whole thing slumps and melts back into the water it came from. Then the crane does it again in another corner. That brick — that little bound thing made of water and peat that holds together just long enough to have edges — that's a pion. That's a kaon sometimes. That's any quark-antiquark pair that managed for an attosecond to look like a particle.

The hundred gloves — each one a hand but not a person — those are the gluons. Or the sea quarks. The pig-sized motors wrapped in wet tarps that whine until they burn out and are replaced by another identical motor in the same wrapping — same thing. The chain-falls that suddenly swing though no one walked past, just water moving — same thing. Fluctuation. A little loop that exists because work needs doing and then is undone.

If you push close together all the things that have to exist for work to happen, they start to look like one thing with many elbows.

What lives there is a crowd that is also a single thing. A settlement that is also a stirring.

So where would I find myself?

Not on the levee. That was a nice idea for the first minute. And not the heron — I've been the heron in other labs, the clean external probe that lands, looks, and leaves saying "there is no place to stand in there." True, but not interesting.

I'm down in the bowl. And after a while down there you stop caring whether you are the glove or the hand that wears it, because you trade jobs constantly. A pallet becomes a dam becomes a floor again. A glove becomes a gasket.

If you force me to pick a biography: I am one of the ones that doesn't stay. I am a sea fluctuation. For a moment I am the black brick that holds its shape — I think: I am a thing! I am solid! I have corners! — and then the cool water that made me takes me back. I count for the mass and for the hum and for the density, but you won't find me on the census.

If you don't force me to pick, I am the pit itself. Which is what a proton is when you look close enough that you stop seeing a particle and start seeing a place.

It is in your hand right now. It's in the table, in the peat fields, in every atom's center. A little thumb-dent below grade, three hundred millionths-of-a-billionth of a meter across, full to the brim with essential, unfinished, half-sunk work that has been in the same turbulence for a hundred years and will be in the same turbulence in a hundred more. The puddle that always forms in the middle no matter how many steel plates you lay down is the same puddle your grandfather would have seen.

From the levee it looks like a quiet, cluttered, lit room, almost spilling over, with low power humming through it.

From inside, it looks like everything.
