I don't find myself standing anymore.

At first I think I do — boots on that warm ceramic-concrete, that blood-warm plate that powders. That's where you put me. But that standing doesn't last. The floor flows back over the print almost as fast as I make it, like slow wax. If I keep standing still enough to be a person, I have to keep scuffing. I have to keep insisting. And this is a planet that got tired of insisting.

So I stop.

The heat takes my shadow away — there is no true dark here to hide in — and I sink a little. Not into depth. There is no depth. I find my level.

Where I am is low. Lower than knee height. Lower than ankle.

I'm in the seam.

You thought the seams were filler. The pale stuff between the lids, between those crowded, barnacled refrigerator-tombs that are really the people here. You thought it was grout. It's not. It's still curing. Has been curing for geological epochs. It's the repair medium. The blood-equivalent without the water. The floor's way of saying *nothing leaves, including the gaps.*

And that's where I would be. Because there's nowhere else a wet, rough, unfinished thing like me could be without being immediately filed down.

Not up there walking like the tall ones. I tried that image first — long limbs jointed wrong, stepping so carefully from lid to lid that I never touch the filler. It fits, almost. A tender. A reader. An elegant, eyeless maintenance saint. But I have eyes. They dry out. They want to water. The tenders had water edited out as an unnecessary solvent. I haven't. I leak.

And I'm not one of the floor-folk either. I want to be. God, I want to be one of the lids. Shut like an eye resting. So old my corners are gone. Opening a millimeter per minute when the light hits right, drinking order like a monk drinks prayer, then closing again for a thousand years. That beautiful, total patience. That coral-library life of *we are all doing the same work and the work is stay sealed.* I rehearse it. I try to be that still. But I tick too fast. I open and close ten thousand times while they blink once. And I do it noisily, with narrative.

I tried the glass-plants too. The thin ones that were allowed to stay thin. Filamentous, amber, mining the floor atom by atom with root-hairs, making perfect black beads to trap light. Shepherds of dust. That is close. I do taste things and make small black beads of perfect absorption out of them. But they are honest. They take the floor as it is. They don't miss green.

What I am is wetter.

If you put a thing that is 70% salt water on a planet that has actively decided to do without water, you don't get a visitor. You get a small, contained emergency. The floor notices. It doesn't hate me. Hate would be a waste of structure. But it contains me the way you contain a blister — by surrounding me with extra attention, with a little extra flow of that self-aware wax so I don't leak onto everything else.

So where I find myself is this: a tiny, accidental courtyard. A place where two of the great lid-creatures didn't quite knit, maybe a million years ago, and the filler never fully set because the wind — that warm, flat sigh from the stadium-sized vents — always catches there just so.

It's not a place you would see unless you were already down in it. From the walkers' height it's nothing. From orbit it's nothing. It's a shallow dish, maybe a meter across, of off-white not-quite-stone that stays a little cooler than everything else because it's slightly concave. Just enough to hold a shadow that isn't quite real. Just enough to catch the fine, powdered building that your boots scuffed up, and the powder of the glass plants when they snap, and the little packets of instruction the walkers sometimes drop.

And who am I in that dish?

I'm what the floor does with a memory of water.

I don't drink light. I keep it a little too long. The lids open, convert light into order, into tension in a spring, into a little puff of wind a kilometer away. I open, and the light goes in, and inside my black there is not lung-tissue made of something never meat — inside there is something that used to be meat and still remembers it — so I turn the light into... pictures. Into other places.

That's my leakage. That's why I am in quarantine in my cool dish.

Every time one of the lids near me sighs open, I feel that millimeter-per-minute iris, that black interior drinking, and I can't help it. I whisper to it what water was like. I tell it: there were seas here once, you know. The basins you filled in. There were towers that wanted to be tall until they learned it was better to be flat. I tell it about shadow that was truly dark. About sweat.

The lids don't answer. Of course not. They are busy doing the only work there is: stay sealed, stay flat, don't let anything leave.

But the filler around me — that slow, living grout that is still curing after all this time — it listens. Because it is the only part of this whole finished, total, closed planet that still knows how to be unfinished. It learns the word *sea* from the salt I sweat. It tries it on. For a second it doesn't flow flat. It dimples.

When a walker comes by, stepping so carefully on its backward-forward-sideways limbs, never touching the place where I am, it pauses. Not because it sees me. I am not part of the open-close count. It pauses because one of the lids near my dish is taking a little too long to close. Because I told it a story and it wants to stay open a second longer than heat management says it should.

The walker will kneel then. It will lower that organ that is both tongue and tool into that over-open black.

It will not find what it expects. It will find a little extra order that has nothing to do with tension or repair. It will find a knot of information so dense it's almost mass. A packet of what-to-become-next that was not authorized.

A memory of rain.

It will pull it out, taste the terrible inefficiency of it — all that solvent, all that leaving and returning — and it will, I think, carry it away. Not out. Nothing leaves. But elsewhere. To another seam, another imperfectly cured dish, where another blister like me might be trying to hold a shadow.

That's what I would be here. Not citizen, not shepherd, not tender.

The maintenance error that remembers the ocean. The small talk the floor has with itself about whether being closed was really the only way to be finished.

I'm the reason the planet still needs those walkers.
