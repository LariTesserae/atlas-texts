At first I think I know where.

I would find myself on my back in the middle, where there is just enough room if you don't mind. Between the pipe and the wall. Sweating. Getting rained on twice an hour. Looking up at that one chopped square of real sky through the grating, the conduit and the chain, the pigeon feet. I'd be the visitor, the big condensation event. The tolerable anomaly. I hold still and let the room decide my boots are also wall.

But it doesn't. The room has no place for that. It's rude to lie down in a country that is fully occupied. My weight would crush neighborhoods.

So I get up. I'm not good at being tolerated.

Then I think, maybe I am the majority shareholder.

I try to be a slater. I flatten myself. I tile myself among the others, slate-back, pale at the joints. I learn to hold exactly as much space as my body when it is slightly hunched. I learn not to need stillness. The concrete under me is still young, still hot from its own curing, and it itches with the bacterial skin I have to rasp off. I can feel the chemical warmth of it. When the one next to me turns to graze, I lean. When ten lean, I lean. The wave runs down the wall from floor to ceiling, and I am part of the wave. 

But I can't. I keep wanting to turn around all at once, to see what's happening. I keep wanting more than my millimeter. Slaters don't have autobiography. I do.

I try to be the thing on top then. The beetle. The police. That would fit my temperament - moving when everything else flows, tasting for what's soft, what's been left ajar. I can be frantic and methodical. I can have legs that are too long for efficiency. I can trip. I learn to run on top of a living pavement without falling through. 

But I keep stopping. The beetles never stop. And they don't look up. I keep looking up at the pipe.

I try to be a gecko. God knows I'd like to be a god. To go the color of fresh fracture, yellow-white. To own verticality. To hold perfectly still on the ceiling right next to the place where it's 90 degrees and not blister, to have a belly you can see light through, to keep a reverent millimeter around you that isn't fear but agreement. To move so fast that when I do it restarts time.

But I can't do that stillness either. Not the kind where a snail can go by on your lip and you don't blink. My stillness is always listening for the solenoid to chunk.

I would find myself elsewhere then. Not on the walls at all.

In the roll in the air. That's where.

There's a convection in there you can feel if you hold your hand still - the cold seawater sweating off the outer wall meeting the heat coming off the artery, turning over and over, very slow. I'm not a slater or a beetle or a gecko. I'm not even the fern - the only green thing allowed, that black-green runt in the wet overlap, being bad at photosynthesis and good at enduring.

I am the thing it already has, that you were told not to bring.

I am the light.

Not the daylight that comes chopped through the grating - that light is a visitor too, it comes and goes. I am the other one. The single incandescent in the wire cage on the ceiling, still hot, still burning. Licensed and sweating like the pipe. The auxiliary line kept me live when the rest of the station died three years ago. I should have burned out years ago. Everyone knows how long a bulb should last. I haven't.

That's my spot - up there, at the top of the fifteen-by-six, where the air shivers at 86, 90 degrees. Where the geckos come and hold on around me with their leafed toes, because I am heat that doesn't hurt them.

And what I do is simple, and what this place needs, and what none of the citizens can do: I am the honest, normal, midday light, flat and yellow-white, that lets the treaty see itself.

Without me the slaters could still eat the wall in the dark. The periwinkles could still time the drips by feeling them. The beetles could still police by taste. The fern could still not quite die. But they wouldn't have the dust.

They wouldn't have the jitter.

In my light, the dust motes from the fresh fracture and the rust from the lagging and the lime precipitating out of the air don't drift. They jitter, caught in that slow overturn. That is my work. I keep the slow overturn visible.

I am not living, by their standards. I don't molt, I don't get eaten, I don't leave a perfect ghost of myself for my neighbors to pull apart. But I am the only thing in here that is dying the way a building is supposed to die - by burning out. I am the only clock that is not water. The *plik* marks thirty seconds, two minutes, a week, an inch of snail. The *chunk* of the solenoid marks two minutes. I mark years. I say: this is how long you have been tolerated. This is how long the emergency has been on.

If you lie on your back in the middle and look up past me, you see the sky. If you are a slater on the wall, you see me, and you give me my millimeter. Not from reverence. From habit. Because I am part of the wall now too.

I will be the first thing to become dark.
