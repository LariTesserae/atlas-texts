At first I thought I would be one of the children.

Not out of nostalgia. Out of habit. Because I don't know how to be still yet. I thrash. I hold my breath until it burns. I try to keep lowland air in my chest like a secret.

In my first hour in the Summit Sea I would be making so much noise the old woman would turn her whole barrel chest to watch me. I would be ripping bubbles. Kicking to go up when there is no up. Swimming the way you swim when you think water is something to get through, not something to live inside.

So yes, I thought: I would be the darting one. The seven-year-old with the pink chest that hasn't been stretched yet, who lives in sprints — three hand-over-hands on the main line and then burst through the valve into the almost-air and cough and laugh and cough.

But I stayed in the thought longer and it didn't hold. Children in this place get to be children because someone else is holding the net still for them to land in.

I am not good at holding still, but I am good at tending something that needs me to be still.

Then I thought I would be a carver. I like stone that is still learning how to be stone. I liked the image of kneeling in water that is warm as blood, with a shell scraper in the loop on my wrist, working a terrace while the basalt is still soft enough to leave a thumbprint in. But then I remembered: metal rusts in a week here. Fire is a blue smother. Anything you make hard will be lifted and cracked by next season's shoulder of new mountain. Builders here are really un-builders, carving a home knowing they will abandon it to the kelp. That kind of faith is harder than darting.

I kept hanging there in my mind, letting the warm water go darker around the edges.

If you hang long enough, the place starts placing you. The ropes tell you where you belong not by who you say you are, but by how you pull.

I would be found high. Higher than the main tangle.

Everyone else strings their houses in the thick middle where the kelp is oldest and the forest makes a roof. I would drift up without meaning to, the way restless people always do, to the thin top of the lens, where the hanging sea starts to fray back into cloud. Where the light is still only a rumor but it's the palest rumor we get. Greenish. The water there is thinner, colder — you can almost breathe air again, but not quite, and so no one stays long because it's work to breathe it.

You'd find me where the black kelp gets young. Where new rock has just cooled enough to not kill a root. Stems thin as my wrist, bladders small as fists, not yet houses. The nursery.

I would be the bladder-tender.

Not a human, exactly, anymore — but not a fish, either. A woman with tea-stained skin and rope burn tattooed smooth into both forearms, chest not as deep as the oldest elders, not as shallow as the children. A chest that took the stretching but never quite forgave it. So I can filter-breathe if I hang perfectly still, but only for ten, twelve minutes before the whistle in my throat thins out.

That makes me a good tender. Because the bladders need both.

A bladder house is a lung. It breathes out almost-air — oxygen and rot and sweetness — and if you don't clean it, it drowns itself. You have to go inside it. You have to hang in that pocket of almost-air that smells like fermentation and warm skin and other people's coughs, and you have to coax the transparent shrimp-things along so they eat the dark spots off the inner skin. You have to massage the valve so it still kisses shut. You have to pierce the little speckled fruit that grows on the inside wall and let it sigh. If you panic in there and rip the wall, you spill your whole house.

So you learn a rhythm that is neither sprint nor statue. Inhale almost-air — big, whooping, joyful lowlander breaths that make you dizzy. Duck out through the valve. Hang on the outside in the warm dark, and let the water in slow. Filter. Listen to the tick of the cooling rock far below, to the prayer-bells chiming arrhythmically through a hundred meters of dim — *tok... clack-clack... tok*. My bell would be a small one, cut wrong, a little flat, because I made it myself from a beetle's wing-case instead of shell and it never rang true. The children laugh at it. The elders know exactly where I am by it.

Then, when the throat whistles thin, pull myself back in. Breathe. Tend.

I would be the one who never fully learned either way of breathing, and so became useful in the seam between them. The messenger, sometimes, because I have to move anyway — carrying a thorn needle, a bead knife, a scrap of bladder tape from one house to the next, tapping on the main line as I go: *hold still — rock coming — look.*

You wouldn't see me at first. You'd see a shadow between two young stipes, a pair of short braids with flat bells, a pair of amber eyes with the second lid sliding shut against the particulate. A person holding perfectly, perfectly still, except her hands, which are always moving gently inside a small, translucent house, cleaning.

And if you stayed quiet long enough, and stopped thrashing, I would finally let you hang beside me. I wouldn't talk. I would reach out with that slick, never-dry skin and press your chest, gentle. Like my mother pressed mine. Like the old woman pressed yours.

Let it out, I would tap on your rope.

Let the water in slow.
