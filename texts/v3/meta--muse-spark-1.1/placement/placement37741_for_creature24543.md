At first I think I would be out in the open water.

Because that's what hits you first, or would hit you if you were dropped in — that terrible, beautiful lease of distance. I think I would want to be one of the riders, stepping off the gantry in a pressure suit made of old gaskets, arms out, falling sideways on a river of cold that is ten kilometers wide. I think I would want to be fast. To be in the middle of the silver weather, inside the school when it comes like a wall and the water goes white and the engine thrum cuts out. To be nothing but a vector between pillars.

But I wouldn't last there. I can't live in the middle. I'm not made for the open.

If I stayed out there I'd get scared. Too much light, everywhere at once, noon at a thousand meters, nowhere to hide from it. You can see three pillars at a time and then a fourth that you thought was haze is a pillar too, and then a fifth. The scale doesn't end. If I lived in the open I'd have to keep moving or the distance would notice me.

No. I find myself drifting, even in my imagination, toward the edge of anything solid. Toward the lee.

So I try again. Maybe I would be something smaller and closer. One of the shrimp that clicks. A glass cloud that says *here, here, here*. Or one of the blind crabs, with a carapace sized for a service ladder, farming that sparse black-green beard on the windward edge. That appeals to me — the patience of it. The slow hydraulic shove over a patch of algae that took months to grow in the cold. Tending a garden that is only as big as your body.

Closer. Warmer, in a way, though there is no warm here except inside the machines.

But I don't find myself on the windward edge either. That's where the current-rivers hit. That's where the water has weather and it hits like weather, a hiss that becomes a shove that becomes a hand trying to peel you off. The crabs that live there need to be blind and armored for a reason.

I find myself on the other side. The lee of a pillar.

If you come around a pillar that is a kilometer across at the base — and you can only do that by riding the barge that still makes its stupid, faithful loop, cable to cable, grinding on a schedule no one wrote — you find that each pillar has two weathers. The front where the current hits and everything grows and scrapes and fights. And the back where the current has passed and left a quiet. The water there is slower, and silt, what little there is, settles. The concrete there doesn't spall as fast. It keeps the grain of the formwork. You can still see the wood.

Put your hand there and the pillar is less cold.

That's where I would be.

In one of the doorways. There's a doorway there that was meant to be a hundred meters tall, with a lip to keep pressure that never came. Inside, it's dry. The emergency strip lights have been on so long they've gone amber. The air smells like concrete and ozone and old fish oil because someone fried something here a generation ago and the stone kept it.

I wouldn't be one of the tall thin ones who can ride without a barge. I would be one of the ones who stayed inside longer. The ones with broader backs from scraping.

You don't own a pillar. You tend it.

So my job would be small, and it would be whole. I am the one who listens to the curing.

Every pillar ticks. You were told — the building is still being born. A continent of poured concrete will shrink for a thousand years. It ticks and pings and shudders as it tightens. The rebar sweats. Hairline cracks open at night, when the panels in the ceiling dim for six hours that someone called night.

I would be there with a pot of slurry, with bacterial orange mixed in so the Not-Whales can see it and don't scrape us off by accident. I would fill the hairlines. I would watch the places where the last repair spalled, where a clean sheet of concrete came off like a scab and exposed the dark iron. I would put my palm flat to it — that unnervingly smooth, young, too-cool surface — and feel under the barnacle grit and the shrimp-click and the sternum-thrum of the hill-sized pumps at Elevation Zero, the other hum. The low one. The one that's just the building itself becoming more itself.

I know the pump-techs who live deeper in the pillar think the pumps are alive. They are, in a way. But the pillar people know the pillar is alive slower.

Sometimes a Not-Whale passes on the lee side when it's sick or lost, and it's so big you misjudge it. You think it's still far off, a slow piece of moving structure, until its scarred, barnacled flank blots out the light from the next pillar over and all the anemones on our pillar close at once. Its call sets our ladder humming in sympathy. Then it is gone and the water is clear again and we are alone again with our ticking.

Sometimes the Silvers blot out noon and I go out onto the gantry — a terrace big as a house, meant for machines — and watch the light change through a million flanks. Sometimes the maintenance barge groans past and I hook a line and ride to the next pillar just to see if their cracks are the same as ours. They never are.

I have lungs that are good for holding. Eyes that learned to judge kilometers. But I don't want to go fast.

I find myself where the building is quietest, where I can hear it learn how to be quiet. Where the grain of the wood it was poured against is still there if you scrape the algae careful enough.

I am the tender on the lee side. The one who puts his hand on the wall to see if it is still cold, and still becoming.
