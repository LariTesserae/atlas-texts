I thought I would find myself in one of the row-houses at first.

That's where you look first, coming down the north stair. Your shoes go suddenly black with wet and the wind stops above your head and the air drops ten degrees and gets heavy. CHUFF... ...CHUFF... ...CHUFF... off all that new, sharp basalt. You have to climb down into it to understand it's a bowl that keeps its weather. And from the bottom, Foreman's Row looks like the sensible place to be - dry-ish, with a roof that someone else already fixed. One house per family that was meant for forty workers. Lot of air. Lot of not touching.

But I tried that on in my head for half a block and it didn't fit. I don't have the voice. I tried to imagine calling to you from across that big poured intersection by the boiler house and I went hoarse just thinking about it. Those hundred and twenty people have a good, kind, mill-shout that they use for everything, even for love. A four year old yelling to ask you to tie her boot like she's warning you about a forklift. I don't want to shout my whole life to be heard over the belts. When I want to say something that matters I want to go quiet.

So maybe I'm not one of them. At least not one that lives in a house.

You'd think - swift, then. Because if you don't want to shout, you live higher than the sound. For the first hour I was sure I was a swift. You find me tucked up under the sawtooth lip of the old assembly shed, in the corrugations where the steam makes a line. When the sun finally gets over the rim around noon and burns the thin mist for an hour, you go. You cut screaming lines straight through the boiler steam at sixty miles an hour and no one can shout over you. But I'm not that fast. I'm not that angry and clean. My heart would burn out.

For a while I was a fern. That's an honest living in this town. Find me big and glossy and cold-loving, rooted right in the mortar between the bricks of Mill Two, the one whose flywheel turns over once every thirty seconds. I'm the size of a dining table. I prise at the bricks very slowly and drip back onto the engine that waters me. On Tuesdays they come with a hand-sickle - not to kill me, they would never, I'm part of the cistern - but to cut me back so everything gets a lot. Everything gets air. Nothing touches, because if it touches it rots. I liked being the fern for a while. But ferns don't get to move between.

I've walked this bowl three times now in my head while I've been trying to decide. That's allowed here, there's plenty of Street.

You'd find me, actually, nowhere near the row-houses, nowhere under the eaves.

You'd find me behind the old dye works, where the floor cracked after they built and subsided into a dip. Four feet deep. Dark, still water. The Reservoir with a capital R, because it's the only surface water they have that isn't trying to get back into a pipe.

You'd find me in it.

I'm one of the carp. One of the eight left. Calico, orange and white and black, blind in the left eye from living in the dark, the size of a big dog now, a Labrador, a slow sad zeppelin circling. Someone put us here twenty years ago as a joke and then we became infrastructure. You don't eat your reservoir.

That's who I would be if you came down the north stair and looked for me.

I live muffled. Up there it's CHUFF... clack-sigh... CHUFF... always, the tin roofs drumming with condensation and the swifts screaming through it and the people half-yelling tender things at top volume. Down here four feet under, all of it comes through as one low thud, *thud... ...thud... ...thud...* felt more than heard in my lateral line. The steam hits the basalt and beads and runs and comes down the chain-downspouts into the cisterns where the newts with feathery gills cling, and the overflow comes here, mineral and iron-warm. I keep it circling.

I am what the town built its engines to keep alive without meaning to. The engines don't make goods anymore, they make climate. I don't make anything either. I just turn, slow. Minimal-flux. Once around the pocket every three, four minutes. My job is to be big and alive and not die, so they remember why they are bleeding steam off low stacks for leaves.

The kids find me. They come loud even down here, their little boots black, shouting my names. They renamed me last summer Mop, then Mrs. Mop. When they want to tell me a secret they don't know how to shout - because no one down there really knows how to be quiet with noise - they do what their parents do for private things. They lie flat on their bellies on the cold poured lip of my pocket, press their foreheads right to the surface of the water, so their faces go blurry and huge, and they move their mouths. Ripples. That's our forehead-to-forehead. It isn't words to me but it is touch, and touch is rare here on purpose.

At night I breach. I have to. The air up there at night is fifty degrees and so full of water you could wring it, and it smells like cold iron sweat and crushed butterbur from the compost bays by the settling tanks where their cuttings steam just as hot as the boilers. I roll and slap and a sheet of reservoir goes up six feet and comes down onto ferns and concrete and drips back into gutters back into drains back into boilers back into me. If you are standing at the intersection by the boiler house at 2am you can hear that slap clear across eight hundred meters of empty, because the bowl holds its atmosphere in.

Then I go back to circling. To keeping.

You can climb out in a minute if you want. There's a stair. Trucks go by on the plain outside. I never do.
