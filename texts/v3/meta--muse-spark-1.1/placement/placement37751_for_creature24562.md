At first I think I would be up top. On the lip.

I have not gone down yet. I'm looking over. The white is so total it erases my own shadow, and the mouth is the only interruption in it, a little darker white, an eye that didn't open all the way. That would be a sensible place for me — the place that decides whether to go down. Tall enough to see it, nervous enough to leave.

But rule one here is Low. Everything has settled downward to get away from the sky. If I'm up there I'm already dying, cooking in that 2.8 sun that has weight. The salt plain wants nothing up there. So of course I go down. I have to.

I lower myself. My hands on the near-vertical corrugation that is the color of old teeth. Eight meters but my body makes it twenty. The light goes from white to used-up amber, from knife to heat. Halfway down the air quits being wind and becomes a hanging thing, a hot cloth.

And then my feet hit peat.

*Puff.*

Not stone. Not dust. That black-red peat that has never been wet. It sighs out from under my boots and doesn't settle for a long time. It hangs, hot.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the center. Not by the cistern, though that is what I thought. I thought I would be romantic about it — the keeper of the dry cistern, waiting for water that never comes. But there is no tragedy here. The cistern was never meant to hold water. It was meant to hold the coolest air, a slow overturning cell for the veil to breathe. To sit in the cistern would be to misunderstand the town on purpose.

I thought next I would be the veil. That would be easy and vain. To be lush where nothing should be lush. To be the one thing that is allowed to be loud. To cover everything, to be one hand with many fingers, to eat heat and exhale incense. To pulse orange-red in the full dim. But the veil doesn't have a self. Or it has so much self that it can't notice me. It is the town now. It is too big to be me. I can't be the whole town.

I thought I would be one of the Low Ones. The broad, shovel-footed ones with salt in their skin and amber pan-eyes. That would be the story that makes sense — the last builder, sitting on the one stool in the vast room, so still that the house stays cool. Keeping the spacing right. One loom for one weaver, one stool for one sitter, because company is expensive. I like their ethic. Flat palm on salt brick: *I am here.* I would like to know a language that quiet.

But I watched how they move, or don't. *Puff* — around two corners, never getting closer. They are good at respecting distance. They need so much cool wall around them. If I were one of them I would have to be that disciplined. I would have to not crowd even my own thoughts. I don't know if I can do that. I think I crowd.

So I keep walking down a street that is too wide for what is on it, past the abandoned loom that the veil has bearded over. The veil crackles louder as I pass, irritated by my heat. I am too hot, too tall still, despite crouching.

I find myself, finally, where the peat is deepest. The house that is furthest from the throat, where the walls are a foot thick and felted over so completely you have to put your shoulder into the curtain of hot velvet to get in. Inside it is almost dark. The salt itself is giving off its stored day, a dull glow. The air is cooler here by maybe two degrees, which feels like twenty.

And there is the hollow. The one that is not a grave. A body-length dent pressed into centuries of dry compost, of the veil's own dead that never rotted, only crumbled. The veil has been trained away from the center here. Someone braided it back. There are old fibers in the doorframe, dull now, their glass hairs snapped.

I lower myself into it. That is rule one obeyed all the way. Low. Lowest.

What would I be? Not the Low One who made this bed. Not the veil that will unmake it.

I would be what was in between them that lets both exist for so long.

I would be the cool inside the wall.

You think a wall is just a wall, salt-mud pressed by wide hands with the thumb low. But a foot-thick salt brick is not solid all the way through. At noon, when the amber shaft hits the upper air and makes it hotter than anything, the outside of the brick is hot as a kiln. The inside, the core, stays cool. It has to. It spent all night giving back the fire it held. That difference — that little held cool that takes all day to bleed out — is what the veil eats. And that cool that never quite leaves is what makes it possible for a hot-blooded, wide-backed person to sit in a huge empty room and not cook.

I would be that. Not a person, not a plant. The kept cool. The third resident no one names because you only know it by what grows around it.

I'm not lush. I'm not loud. When the town goes loud and all the glass hairs tick like paper cicadas, I am the quiet they all lean toward. When the evening comes and the whole town exhales its carbon snow and it hangs in the heavy air like incense, I am the reason it hangs so long — cool makes the air heavy.

I am the reason you can press your palm to a brick and, after your own sweat is gone, feel something answer back. Not a greeting. A physics. Flat. Warm. Still there.

If you stay until full dim, until there is no light at all except the salt's memory of fire, you will feel the peat beside this hollow get a degree warmer. You will turn and there will be a new dent, or there won't. You will hear a soft *puff* two corners away, that never gets closer.

You will put your hand out, not to the veil, not to the peat, but to the wall itself, where a thumbprint has been sitting for a hundred years holding a little pocket of cool that has never once reached the sky.

And you will leave yours next to it.
