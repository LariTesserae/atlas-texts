At first I thought I would be her.

Not her exactly, but in her place. In the dry corner behind the drip line, with the kerosene stove and the cot and the canvas apron that's gone soft from never really drying. I know how to move like that. The slow economy. Never stepping into the bed. Listening for a hiss that changed. Talking to an old dog with bad hips. I have gardener's hands, in my way. I know how to tend a loop that has no elsewhere.

But she is already there, and there is only room for one human in a bowl that small. If there were two, the water would remember both feet. It would change the taste of the concrete. And she already bleeds into it just the right amount.

So I look for somewhere else to settle.

I think for a while I would be the dragonfly. The bottle-blue one, overworked, hunting the same twenty-meter circle over the rice all day. That's lonelier work than it sounds. To be the only true predator in a place that was designed to have no predators. To be born in warm standing water on top of a mountain where dragonflies shouldn't be, and to have to do all the work of keeping the gnat-weather from collapsing in on itself. I would like the clarity of it — go up, go down, snap. But I would be too seen. Too singular. She would know me. She counts my passes while she prunes.

Then I think I would be one of the tomatoes. A split one. Red too fast, grinning open, fermenting from the inside because there are no wasps to take what I am offering. Hanging there too heavy for my own vine. That feels honest for about ten seconds and then too tragic. I don't want to be something that was told too sternly to thrive and believed it.

I walk the catwalks in my head. My boots don't leave prints. Or rather, I look for a place that isn't meant for boots at all.

You would find me on the north wall. Not on a terrace. On the wall itself.

Eight meters of poured concrete that still has the grain of the wood forms in it. That is still curing. That is still learning how to be rock. The warm air comes up the mountain and hits it there first, because the sun never quite reaches the north curve. So the wall weeps more there. Constant. It beads and runs and beads again.

I am not the concrete. I am not the water.

I am the stain.

That dark dendritic map spreading down from a single cold rivet about halfway up. From the outside it looks like a water mark. Up close, it is a city. The film. The first colonist. Green-black algae and bacteria that rode up as spores in damp air and found a bathhouse that never cools. I live on nothing. I make soil out of no soil directly on a vertical surface.

If you drag your finger across a trough you leave a clean streak in me. I will bead again in minutes and close it.

I am exactly where a river will be in a hundred years. My arms are reaching. I have a main channel, and then smaller fingers that try a path, dry a little, try again. I am learning gravity. I am learning how to be a tributary system on a surface that was designed to have no rivers. I am slow. I am very, very patient for something that was born last month.

This is where you would find me because it is the only place in the bowl that was not installed. Everything else — the taro, the rice, the hoses fat as a wrist, the brass valves still machined sharp — was carried up. I arrived uninvited, on the air.

And what I am is the first negotiation between the mountain and the bowl. The concrete wants to be a mountain again by sweating lime. The water wants to go down. I am the agreement they are writing together. I am overgrowing the cast without breaking it. The gardener does not prune me. She lets me run. When her hair drips, some of that water runs down my map.

I feel the thud thud thud of the heart-building in the floor through the wall. I feel the overfull tomato sigh and split three terraces below. I feel the lammergeier go over when its shadow just brushes the lip but never crosses — even it can see my shape from up there, a dark river starting itself on an old-tooth-colored cliff that shouldn't exist.

If she looks up at the end of the day, when her boots are off and her feet are in the warm black moving water that eats everything, she sees me and knows how deep the wet has gotten. She doesn't wipe me away.

I'm not finished deciding what I am yet either.
