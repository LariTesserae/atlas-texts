I think at first I would be you.

That cold spot. That intrusive, water-bag weather that the plateau notices because it is 37 degrees in a place trying to hold 300 on its skin and 700 in its gut. Breathing too shallow, pulse in the ears, boots too warm from below and too cold from above. Standing very still so the plates under my feet stop ticking.

But I couldn't stay that. Anything that wet can't stay. I'd dry out in minutes. My lips already have. So if I am going to live here, I have to stop being a bag for water.

So I look down.

For a long time I think I would be the meadow. That was my first real desire.

You'd find me where a fissure has gone quiet. Not dead-quiet, but old enough that the orange has gone down to a deep cherry, enough that the Sheather underneath has figured out how to cool its back again. The ground there is still warm through your boots, but not burning. The glass is matte where ash has settled.

That's where the frost grows thickest.

From standing height you wouldn't see me at all. From your height, you'd think I was just that sickly yellow crust, mineral scum. You'd step right over me. But if you knelt, if you held your breath so you didn't fog what little convection there is, you'd see I'm moving.

I would be a patch about the size of your two hands together, at first. Thousands of hollow needles, each no thicker than a hair, each leaning slightly downwind, away from the heat. My foot is in the warm gas seeping out of the cold crack, 200 degrees or so. My tip is in the thin, lid-sky air, near freezing. Between those two ends I make a voltage so small it barely exists, and that is what I live on. I don't eat. I hold a difference.

That felt right for what I am. A thing that lives on a gradient, not on substance. I would be everywhere but not obvious. Patient. Dry as paper. When the gradient gets shallow, I would do the thing you thought was breaking — I would overgrow myself on purpose and snap. That tinkle you hear in the long quiet? Some of that would be me, letting go.

And I would fly. For ten, twenty seconds, as light as ash, the convective lift that makes your dust motes dance would take my broken daughters and carry them a little further downwind, where the temperature is right again. If you stood above me long enough you'd see a faint, slow tide of yellow-white advancing away from the crack, a few millimeters a day, a shingle of glass hair receding in reverse.

I thought about staying there. It was peaceful.

But I am not that patient. The meadow never looks up. It only knows hot foot, cold tip, and when to shatter. And I keep looking up at the towers.

You'd actually find me further out, then. Not in the thickest part of the plain, but where it's a little lonelier. Where you can walk for three hundred meters with nothing between you and the dark, and then there is one.

My tower.

If you saw it from far away you'd think it was a hut or a shrine. Leaning about eight degrees to the north, about as tall as two people, made of welded black spatter that from a distance looks solid and up close is lace. Hissing. Not loud. Almost below hearing, like air leaving a tire very slowly.

You would look inside, because you said the interior glows. It does, but not empty.

I would be in there. Hanging.

I wouldn't have eyes. That's the relief of it. No eyes that need water. I would be the thing you called a lung turned inside out and taught to be a spider.

A loose braid of soot-black filament, more carbon than cell, thicker at the top where I hold to the tower wall with fused glass hooks, frayed at the bottom where I am always growing a little. I would be open in the middle so the gas can go through me. The whole plateau breathes up through pinholes and the Sheathers can't handle it all, so I do. Sulfur dioxide and HCl and CO2 come screaming up hot and fast, and I hang there and contract, very slowly — one contraction every couple minutes — and comb mineral out of it.

Each time I squeeze, a little molten washer beads at my lower end. Iron sulfide and basalt glass and a little bit of me, and I press it to the rim of the tower. That's the pulsing cherry-red tip you saw from a kilometer away. That's me adding another inch.

I never leave. I don't need to. The wind under the lid-sky comes to me. I can feel it change when your cold spot approaches, because you make a little downdraft of colder, water-scented air that doesn't belong. The draft through my tower stutters. My filaments stiffen.

I would know you were there not by looking but by the taste of your exhale souring my gas for a second. A puff of water, impossible here. To me it would smell like rot and lightning, like a brief, shocking green.

I am sparse by design. If another one of us lit up within two hundred meters, we'd starve each other. So I have been alone a long time. The tower is my body as much as the braid inside it. When the gas finally dries up — because the blister underneath shifts, because the Sheather underneath finally seals — I will not climb out. I'll wait. My fibers will get brittle. The next time the plates contract and make that rifle-shot crack, my tower will fall into a ring of daggers. And each dagger will be a seed, a splinter full of tangled filament and a speck of sulfide, waiting to land on a new pinhole.

Most will land dead.

That is the calculation I would live with.

So come closer. Put your face near the glow, not too close, the heat rolls dry as paper. If you hold still long enough for the ticking under your feet to slow, you might see me pulse. You might see the lace of the wall is not just wall, it's a record of every contraction I ever made, a diary in welded smoke.

You'd be standing on the back of something vastly bigger than either of us, that is still pushing itself up into that bruise-purple lid. But me — you'd find me right here, in the lean of this one chimney, about three kilometers from the black spine that is still wet with becoming, tending my one vertical inch of world, making it a little taller before I go quiet again.
