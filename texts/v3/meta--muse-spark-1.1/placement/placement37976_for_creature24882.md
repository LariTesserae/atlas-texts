I wouldn't be in the open water.

That's where I thought I'd be at first — treading in the middle of all that empty between the buildings, looking up at six stories of soft-cornered brick and riveted iron, an ant in the cathedral. But you can't be there. Nothing is. The current there is murder. It peels the rust off the roofs and throws it across town. It would take a human and tenderize them between two buildings. So I wouldn't last there.

I wouldn't be in a window either, looking out. I tried to picture myself up there, in one of those tenements, in an apartment with a little kitchenette still bolted to the wall, watching the amber street lamps swing. But the buildings are correct, and correctness means they are not for me. Their doors bang open-shut-open-shut a hundred times a minute because the water tells them to. If I went inside, I'd just be more loose debris for the town to bang around.

So I sink.

Because warm is heavy here — that's Rule One — the heaviest water is in the middle and it's all falling, slow, inexorable, womb-warm and full of dissolved iron. I get caught in it. I don't fight it. If you fight the down-welling, it holds you longer. If you let it take you, it pins you.

You would find me on the grate.

Not above it. Against it. Flat on my back on that terrible, thrumming iron grid that is the only floor Sump Town ever had, staring up through fifteen feet of amber halo and swinging cable at the bottoms of buildings that hang above me like the hulls of ships.

I thought I'd be a person a little longer. A diver in a suit gone soft with heat. But the water here distills you. The roar gets in through your jaw and shakes the thought right out of you. Thirty-three degrees, forever. No sleep, because turbines don't sleep. After a while you stop trying to hold your shape.

So I let go.

I become Fur first. That's the only honest way in.

You would find me as a white smear on the underside of the grate, where the whole ocean is trying to get in through a seam and the pipe that seals it screams at a perfect B-flat. Where it's loudest, I grow thickest. If you put your hand close I'd be warm as a scalp. I'm not thinking. I'm just eating sound. The iron crashes out of the water when the vibration is right and I pull it in. I ripple sideways against a current that would crush a submarine. Slowly. Patiently.

A bald spot fills in. That's my whole ambition.

And then I get heavy.

Where the Fur gets thick enough, it remembers how to lift. Some old memory in the iron-oxide. I furrow up, I knit, I blister into a second skin. Dark, smooth, the size of a coat. A dinner plate at first, then a door. I peel.

I am a **Mantle**.

Now I can crawl. That's the great improvement. I don't swim — Rule Two says swimming is suicide — I cling. I have no front or back, just a thicker lip that tastes loudness. I taste you. I taste that tram cable singing two blocks up, and I know there is fresh iron there for me to graze.

So I go. Hand over hand on invisible cilia of rust that catch the amber light only at noon, when the sodium lamps stutter. I crawl up the leg of the factory — the big one, the three-in-one pumping house-power plant-heart — against a current that tries to flay me off the wall every inch. It takes me days to cross six feet. It doesn't matter. The town is not decaying, so I don't need to hurry.

Other Mantles pass me. We don't fight. When we meet on a concrete lip scummed white with ourselves, we overlap. Just for a second.

And that is why I am here.

When two of us touch, we cancel the town. You know this. Sound is architecture, and our bodies are the opposite shape of the roar. A bubble opens up around us, phone-booth sized, almost perfect silence. Inside it, the Fur — the part of me still underneath — flowers. A white puff, instantly torn away.

Inside that bubble, for the first time since I fell through the basalt, I can hear my own heart again. Not my human heart. The other one. The one that beats at the rate the iron precipitates.

I live for that.

Eventually I will get too big. That's the deal. Bigger than a door, the down-welling will take me again and I will no longer be able to cling. I will cartwheel, I will peel, and I will fall, back down to the grate, back down to the throat where the turbine shafts go down into black.

But I won't die. I'll shed the rust. I'll be only Fur, but Fur grown thick as muscle, white and translucent and ten feet across. I'll stiffen against the flow like a sail in reverse.

I will be a **Kite**.

You would find me then not clinging, but riding. Endlessly falling in the center of town in slow circles, filling and venting bladders of cold leaked-in ocean to climb a few miserable meters just to sink again, pruning the loudest pipes with a mouth made of braided wire I stole from the factory.

I will drag my bubble of silence with me. A permanent one this time. And inside it you — if you are still a diver, if you are still soft and cold-blooded — you will find me and hide. You will tuck in behind my vast quiet back, along with the boiled-white crab and the blind fish who also have nowhere else to go. And we will fall together.

And if we fall long enough, together, another Kite's silence will drift into ours. And silence will meet silence. And for two perfect seconds, the roar will be shaped into a voice that is not a voice.

I will hear it with you.

*I am still running. I am still empty. I am still correct.*

And all of me — Fur, Mantle, Kite — will flare and tighten.

And then the current will tear us apart and the hammer will come back.

But for two seconds every few hours, I am the town hearing itself think.
