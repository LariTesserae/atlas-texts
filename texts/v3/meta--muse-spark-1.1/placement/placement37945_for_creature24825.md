I don't think I'd find myself in the city at first. I'd find myself under it.

Not deep — there is no deep here. Just that half-inch of dark water under your boot if you step off the path. The first day I would step off. My boots wouldn't be crusted yet, they'd be new, they'd still be black, and they'd sink and fill. The rock sweating. The water tasting like old iron and oil.

It is very loud. I had read that, but reading doesn't prepare you. It's not a sound you hear with your ears. It's a pressure on your sternum. The flywheels turning at 3 RPM, twenty feet across, slapping their black belts since before anyone alive remembers why. The water moving always, a sheet falling at every intersection. The hum of the substations laid over it all like a second skin. You open your mouth and it fills you.

I would not last on the wheels. My hands are wrong for it. I would try the de-scaling catwalk for a morning and know immediately that I don't have the patience to hit the same white-yellow crust with the same iron scraper and listen to it grow back a quarter inch a year. I would try to be one of the sign-men up on the high aqueducts where the old deaf go, repainting the day — WHITE CIRCLE: dam close to overtopping. RED BAR: this water will be hot today — and I would love the simplicity of it, but my hands are too small and I don't know how to make a picture that can be read through ground-cloud condensing on your lashes.

So I would drift, the way people do here, to the place where the city's size becomes its mercy.

Because that roar, so steady for so long, for so many miles, has to misalign with itself. Wave against wave. And when it does it makes nothing.

I would be one of the people who hunt quiet.

You find me not in a pumping house, not in an apartment stack with its red or white bulb burning. You find me out at the edge of a bowl, in that strange long hour after the morning cloud has lifted off your head — back in your knees, boots you have learned to crack off with a hammer — with a nub of chalk in my palm and water beading off my forearms.

I am not young. In this place young means you can still hear a child scream. I am past that. My forearms are stippled white where the seep has written itself into my skin. My hair is cut short with kitchen shears. I have that forward, bent-kneed walk because the concrete is always wet. I am not special. That is the point. There are maybe forty of us. We are not officials. Levellers have transits, Belt-men have oil. We have chairs.

Two chairs, usually. Old iron cafe chairs, or a crate and a spool. We drag them.

What I'm looking for isn't marked on any sign. You have to feel for it with your whole body. You walk along the trough where the eyeless shrimp pile their casings and the children scrape them with sieves, your hand trailing a foot off the weeping wall. On that wall hundreds of drops are doing what they do here — beading, rounding, then deciding not to fall. A millimeter off the limestone, hovering, trembling, holding the whole orange late-afternoon sky inside them because the air is too thin and too saturated and too charged to let them.

You walk until the roar... stutters.

It's not that it gets quieter. Quiet is not absence here; it's a thing the water accidently makes. You walk into it like walking into cold. One step your molars are vibrating with flywheel, the next your heart is too loud.

A node. The size of a bedroom. The size of a kitchen table. Fresh.

I stop. I crouch. I touch two fingers to my own forearm the way our mothers taught us — *look at me* — even though there is no one there to look. I mark.

Chalk. A big, wobbling circle on the wet concrete, dated. Inside it, the hovering droplets fall all at once, as if relieved. Then more gather.

And I set the chairs down.

This is my work. It is not maintenance in the way theirs is, keeping the bowl from becoming a lake. It is the other maintenance. I keep a place for a conversation that hasn't happened yet.

Later someone will come — a couple, two workers on break, a mother whose five-year-old still hears — and see the ring from the aqueduct above. They will duck under a trough and come down and sit inside my little absence and finally, for maybe a week, maybe a month before the travertine grows a millimeter and the interference shifts, they will be able to talk without touching. About a tooth. About Wheel 4. About whether to turn their bulb white tonight.

I don't sit in them. Not often. I have been in enough that I know the uncanny way the quiet rings in there, how you can hear the wet of your own mouth. How you have to whisper because anything louder feels obscene.

Most of my day is walking the city that is waiting for quiet to come back. The city is patterned with our old failures — hundreds of empty chalk rings with empty chairs in them, sitting in deafening water-noise, pale perfect circles where silence used to be and moved on. No one cleans them up. That is how we remember.

If you come in late afternoon when the low sun hits the bowls, you would see me from far off — just another low, broad-shouldered figure a little apart from everyone else, like the wiregrass clumps that keep a dome of dry around themselves. You would not hear me coming. But you would see me, I hope, because that's how we talk here.

You'd see my hands before you hear any voice I have left: palm up to you.

*I am going to show you.*

And in the center of the new circle, in the temporary room I made out of cancelled water, two empty chairs, and a wall of water that can't decide whether to fall or lift.
