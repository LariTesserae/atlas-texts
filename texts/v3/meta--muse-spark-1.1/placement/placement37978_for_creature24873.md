At first I thought I would find myself up on that basalt spine you described. Where the plant wears thin.

Because that's where anything with eyes would go. The one place where you can get a little distance from the work. Where you can stand and watch the table breathe and not be immediately drafted into hauling water or being felted over. I thought I would be the figure on the ridge, collar up, boots already soaked through, listening to my own blood because there's nothing else to listen to.

But I wouldn't stay there. The ridge is too dead. It's old rock. It remembers being cold before there was anything alive to be cold. I don't belong to old rock.

Then I thought, no - down in it. Down in one of the pits. I want to be in the boil, where it's actually happening. If I have to be a body here, let me be a fast one. Let me be that hummock that throws itself up three feet high in an afternoon, all loose peat and bright, stupid green points, like a shout. Let me be the attempt that almost holds.

But that doesn't last. Nothing in the pit lasts. By evening I'm slurry again. And I'm tired of being something that shouts.

Let it go quieter.

If I live here, I don't want to be something that grows. I want to be something that is transferred.

I would find myself not on the ridge and not in the pit, but in the seam between two hearts. About a foot underground, where you wouldn't see me unless you knelt and pulled back the feather-moss and found the cable.

I would be willow. Not the whole willow plant — there is no whole willow plant here anymore. I would be a length of it, maybe six feet, wrist-thick, pale as tendon, steaming a little where the air hits it. Salix arctica. The one who stopped reaching for light and learned to reach for tension instead.

That's where I fit.

Between two of those old spruce-pistons, out of sync with each other. One swells while the other holds. And in that difference, I do my work.

I tighten.

You could put your hand on me if you wanted to, through the moss. You'd feel me thrumming, not with sap but with load. When the heart to the west — the one whose bark is all glass-needles — inhales that supercooled breath and swells, I take that swell and drag it sideways. I drag a hummock three feet to the left. I wring out a sponge of sphagnum above me until brown water bleeds down into a pit that was getting too dry. The pit boils a little harder because of it. I go slack. Then the other heart contracts and it's my turn to be pulled, and I pull something else in turn. A sheet of moss, a pocket of air, a clot of peat.

I am not thinking. That's relief. In this world, thinking would be a straight edge. I am transferring.

That low fizz you hear when you finally hold your breath? That isn't me. I am underneath that. I am what makes the fizz move from here to there.

And you would find me because I failed to be still enough.

The ptarmigan — that one white, permitted parasite — can be still enough that the mat forgets to include it. I can't. I am inclusion itself. I'm the mass's way of including other parts of itself. If you stood on me, you'd feel it at once: a slow heave under your boots, not aggressive, just busy. Like a rope being pulled through the floor of the world by hands that never stop.

I don't store anything. I like that rule. Nothing is stored. I don't get bigger. I don't want to be preserved. For ten thousand years I have been taking the exact same load and passing it on, and the place where I am has stayed exactly the same size while never, for a minute, staying the same.

It is cold. 28, 30. My sap runs clear and thin and it would kill me if it ever stopped. My surface beads with condensation at 4am when the eyes — the round lakes that are really kidneys — exhale their warm bacterial breath. By midday I'm steaming. You could see me then, if the fog hasn't come down yet: a pale line in the green, taut, humming.

Don't cut me. If you do I'll snap back and bleed clear for a day and the rhythm here will stutter. Just step over.

Or, if it's evening and the clouds have snagged and are dying on us, lie down. Put your weight on me. Let the feather-moss start over your boots. I'll feel that extra drag and tighten a little more to move you, too. Not out. Just along. Just enough.
