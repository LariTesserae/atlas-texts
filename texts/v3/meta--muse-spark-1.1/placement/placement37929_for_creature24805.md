You wouldn't find me in the houses.

Not at first. At first I think you would, because that's where people are - inside the fluted buttresses of the west fig where the polished doorways have been polished by shoulders, or up inside the jade culm where the light pools thick enough to swim in. But I don't do well in rooms that are deciding whether to be rooms. I get anxious when my front door wants to renegotiate.

So I drift.

If you came looking for me, you'd find me low. Lower than everyone else.

There are places in that basin - you said three - where the mat peels back and the black basalt shows through, old soap-stone, pitted, house-sized potholes worn into it from when this was a river that could eat continents. Everyone else avoids them because they're hard. The mat doesn't want to grow there, the fig roots won't cross them, they're too smooth to hold water. They're cold.

I like the cold.

You'd find me in the second one, counting counter-clockwise from the bamboo that glows. The one where the tea-colored channels all braid together before they go under the curtain of aerial roots. They slow down there. The water is tired from having to be everywhere else so fast, it goes quiet and clear for about six meters and you can finally see the bottom. It's not very deep, shin deep. If you lie down in it, on your back on that black stone, the water runs over your face and your ears fill and finally the roar of the basin - the pop and groan and cicada and child-shouting and market-tendril slithering - finally goes low and muffled.

That would be my place. A shallow bowl inside the bowl. The town can see me but can't quite route a street to me because the stone won't let it. I have to keep kicking the moss back anyway. It keeps trying.

Who would I be? 

Not one of the darters. I can't keep up that bazaar energy, that beautiful anthill panic when the street decides it likes the fish-man more than the bread-house today. I would try, for a week. My feet would widen, my knees would go walnut-brown, I'd oil myself for the ant-harness and wear a living membrane cape and learn to whisper open a silica window.

But I am bad at never standing still.

So I would become the wrong kind of person for this town - which is also a kind of person this town needs.

I'd be the pause-holder. Not a corner-holder, like the old women who sit and pet a right-angle into staying square for another hour. I could never hold a corner. I'd be the one who holds stillness long enough for the basin to taste me and decide.

You stand still too long here and a rootlet finds your boot and puts out a white feeler. Everyone else shakes it off. I would let it.

I'd be the one with a lot of bracelets. Thin white root loops around both ankles, one around my left wrist that went too deep once and now lives there. I'd be moss-stained up to the elbows. Because Rule Three: everything leaks. Water, light, sound, genes. But someone has to watch where it leaks *to*.

My job, if you asked the market-tendril what my job was, would come back as something like: Leak Listener. Low Archivist.

In the mornings I'd go up - barefoot, splay-footed, mat-water welling between my toes - to the fig and I'd kneel and I'd press my cheek into the throat-moss library. The moss that stores and lends memories that aren't yours. Most people go in and ask for a fix - how to patch a culm, how to soothe a flowering - and they get a grandmother from 1973. I would go in and ask for nothing, and let it file me. I'd sit long enough that condensation would sheet off my nose. The moss would learn my bioelectric field. It would take a copy of what it feels like to lie in that basalt bowl with water over your face.

And in the afternoons I'd sit in my low pool and decode ants.

They march in lines too straight to be natural, those finger-long couriers with their rice-sized data beads. Everyone steps over them. I would not. I would coax one line to detour across my pool - a little sugar-water on my calf - and I would watch the beads as they passed. Each one is silica the fig exuded, a memory-node. In the sun they glint. If you hold one up to the light correctly you can see, faint, not a file but a feeling: the load calculation the west fig was running yesterday when it decided to flower early, or the dream a child had in the light-honey bath.

I would not be very good at building. I'd be good at being built *on*.

My skin would never dry, like everyone else's. Hair heavy, black-green, tied with a rootlet I actually remembered to feed. By now my shoulders would have a faint, permanent amber glow from all the times I accidentally brushed the pooled light. The capybara-moss-bearers would come and stand near me in the pool because I don't move and their pelt-moss pearls oxygen better when it's still. The hawk-dragonflies would use me as a hunting post.

I would be mostly human. Just. With more agreements stuck to me than other people have. With a town that tried to route around me and then, slowly, learned to route *through* me, but low, underneath, like water.

If you stood on the rim - that low green-brown lip - and looked down into the whole single-room cathedral, you would see thirty apartment-sized giants, and the gold-green steam-light, and two hundred bright, fidgeting, laughing souls darting on temporary root-bridges.

You would not see me at first.

You would see where I had just been: a palmprint drying on basalt that is usually never dry, a line of ants taking an unnecessarily elegant detour around a pool, a faint amber glow in the water where light had leaked and settled.

And then, if you were still enough that the basin started to taste *you* too, you would see me lying back in the water with my eyes open, letting the whole turbulent, soaking, negotiating, kindly insistent world wash over my face to see what I would do next.
