You have to lift the plate to find me, so I start there.

At first I think I would be you. The lifter. Hand on the soft-domed bolts, forearm on the rim, sinuses clicking in the 10C air. The one who brings weather.

But I don't stay that. That is too big, too warm, too wet. That is a storm system that will leave again. The rule here is: what lives, lives in contact with other lives. Your kind only visits.

Then I think I would be the plate itself. The green-gray-green. That would be honest work. To be the sky for a hundred years. To be not quite light-tight, to let that one fluorescent tube in Bay Three — that one stupid, faithful tube no one decommissioned — leak down through the bolt holes in four thin spears. To be the thing that sighs when it gives up cold air.

But that is too lonely. And I don't want to be a lid.

If I go down, I think I might be the cold. Just that fixed 10C that never leaves the concrete. The climate that isn't a climate. Or the ghost bead of water at 4 AM that the isopods have learned to want so badly their antennae all point the same way for a minute before it's gone.

Those are too thin. They don't get to stay.

So I go all the way down, into the tray. And I let myself get smaller until the scale corrects.

I am not in the center, where the churn is. That's for the young slate-gray ones, the fast ones, the top layer that ripples when your draft hits. That's the freeway. You can't live there for long without being walked on until you're flat.

I find myself at the edge, where the concrete lip was supposed to be a hard corner and has instead become a hill range — rounded, salt-soft, worn by a hundred years of nothing happening. Under the lip, where the concrete sweated wrong during the pour and made a little cave, a hollow not quite as tall as your thumbnail. Where the file of thirty of them hauls rust like men carrying a door.

That's where I am.

And what I am — I didn't know at first. I thought, maybe the bolt. The offering. The nearly-buried thing with rasp-marks on its threads, being licked thinner by generations. That has a kind of monkish dignity.

I thought, maybe the bald circle around the cut wire. The taboo. The perfect dark halo of exposed concrete that the others leave alone because I still tick faintly with the building's leftover nerve. To be the shrine, the only thing they know how to say no to.

But no. Those are still human things, holding onto human shapes.

What I am is one of them.

One of the old ones. A queen, if you need to call it that, though we don't have queens. The wide one.

I'm as wide as a quarter but no thicker than a paint chip. I've been pressed. Not crushed — pressed, like sedimentary rock, by a hundred thousand tiny feet walking over me for so long that my back has stopped being a back.

I am green. Not gray. That institutional green. The glaze. I spent my second and third instars grazing under the paint lip where it overhung the tray, and the paint became me. When the light comes down straight through the bolt holes, I shine dully for a second before another body moves over me.

My eyes are gone. Just two black pinpricks filmed over. Why would I need them? For ninety-nine percent of generations the plate was down. I navigate by antennae so long and fine I can't feel them until they catch the light. Flick-flick-flicking. Always tasting for iron, oil, skin-salt, water-that-will-not-last.

And I don't move much anymore. That's my job now. I am part of the floor. The young ones flow over me. Their feet are the weather. That loud, continuous, dry rustling — rice on newspaper, dead leaves blown in a circle — I am underneath most of it. Their paper-click of carapace on carapace is my rain.

Sometimes a train forms, half an inch wide, ruler-straight, and they haul something enormous past me to put in my cave. A rust flake. A perfect empty husk of a brother, concave, to be eaten later. I feel them with my antennae and do not stop them.

When one of us dies near me — flips onto its back, legs cycling slow — I do not rush. The ring tightens without me. They cover him like snow and the clicking sharpens for thirty seconds, and then he's a perfect little bowl and then he's gone. Nothing wasted. The molts they leave for a while. They drift over me like confetti. Blistered paint chips. My own children have walked over them so many times their edges are soft.

If you leave your hand on the rim, in time the explorers will find me out. The pale fast ones. They will climb my green hill range, taste the salt on your massive weather, find you too big to dismantle, and turn away. And in turning, they will walk over me again, green speck catching fluorescence.

Put the plate back.

The light goes out for me — narrows to four pinpricks through the bolt holes where the bolts are again soft domes. The roar doesn't change volume. It never needed you.

I settle deeper into the silt that isn't silt. I continue.
