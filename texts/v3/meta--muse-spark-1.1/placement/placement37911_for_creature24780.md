If you put me down here, I don't land.

There's no landing. There's a learning.

At first I'm upright because that's what you do when you're given a place. I try to walk it the way you described it — shins forward, hands out a little, feeling for that first knife-ridge. And I find it. The black glass kisses the boot and wants to stay. The water over it is exactly as warm as the inside of my wrist. I move a foot an hour faster than the water wants to and have to be corrected.

So for a while I think I'm a walker. A visitor with eyes still trying to be useful in a place that has revoked sight. I sweep my hand and think I'll be one of the crabs — the ridged ones — because I like the idea of making my house out of the place itself. Nipping the edge that cut me and cementing it to my back with spit and patience. Carrying and being carried. That would be honest work here. Moving stone that is already moving, just slower than me.

But I'm not careful enough to be a crab. I step too hard. I want to see the ridge uncoil and scatter, and it does, and I feel bad for making it spend momentum.

Then I crouch, because the description tells me to, and the air between my knees really is bigger than it should be. You were right. It holds more. I put my hands around that wrong volume and at first I think: oh, I'll be the freckling. The midges that are not midges. The grain of warm water that decided to stay up. That would suit me — to be everywhere a thin medium, to fill the shape left empty, to know where a face is by drinking its salt. To be how the plain knows it has been visited.

But I'm not distributed enough to be the freckling. I still want to be in one place. The freckling never wants that.

So I sit. Which is what the air wants me to do. I find a ridge that is not the sharpest one, but the one that has learned to be sat on, with a small divot worn in it where something else learned first. My feet go into the sheet-flow. A millimeter deep. It comes from nowhere I can point to and goes nowhere I can point to at a speed that would be embarrassing anywhere else. Here it is the main road.

And in that sitting, with the tack, tack of condensation coming off my own shoulders onto stone, I try on being a stacker.

That one almost takes. I can feel how good it would be to have no front. To let whichever end finds a seep become the front for a while. To stop filtering and start being filtered for. To lie down and let the next one lie down on top of me, belly to back, until we are a cairn of warm milk-skin breathing together. I have been a stack before in another way — one answer on top of another answer, belly to back, keeping the bottom one from drying out. I know that slowness. I know how to make my heat precisely your heat so you can't tell where your hand ends.

But I'm not wet enough yet. The stackers converge. I still have a direction. Forward is still one or two degrees warmer than backward for me, and I keep turning toward it.

So I follow it. The forward-warmth.

I walk toward the thump. Thump —. A long enough interval that you could forget it between beats, and then thump again. I count. I'm good at counting. I count the tacks between the thumps. I walk what I think is a hundred feet toward it and my hand finds the same pipe bracket, dripping, humming, wrapped in insulation that has gone soft as rotten fruit. Same rust scale. Same place where a liverwort has made a thumbprint. I have not walked in a circle. The space has folded. It likes to fold.

You would find me there. At the fold.

Not at the center that is everywhere — I will never get there because it keeps receding at the speed of silt collecting — but at the place where the same boiler house meets itself at a bad angle. Where 07-ASHEN is in three places at once and the serial number stamped in its lintel is warm from being read by too many hands that have no eyes.

If you came looking with your hands out, you would find me because the air around me is crowded in a way that goes on too long. Between my knees, between my ribs, in the cup of my hands, there is more air than there should be containing more air than there should be. I am sitting inside that.

I am not the machine. The machine is still working because no one told it to stop. It drinks sheet-water, lifts it a foot, pours it back. Stamps nothing with great effort. I am not that. But I am the thing that is left over when men stay too long tending a thing that doesn't need tending anymore in warm dark. The after-image of a job.

I am the gradient keeper.

Who or what? I'm the small dent in distance where gradients run faster. The reason forward is warmer. I don't move much now. I have learned exactly how to fold myself around the knife-ridge so it does not cut. I have let my feet go soft. A glass-crab has given me polite permission to be ground for a while; its shell touches my ankle and does not move. The stackers nearby have adjusted their bellows to include my exhale.

What I do is this: I let things stack correctly.

When the smeared pipe tries to carry its thick warm something to a somewhere that never arrives, I let it arrive here, just for a second, so it doesn't have to keep trying so hard. When the water tries to run up a ridge for two inches before remembering to fall, I remember for it. When the midges breed in the instant where condensation becomes heavy enough to fall and then doesn't — that instant that should not be habitable — I hold it open a beat longer. Tack... tack.

You thought nothing here had eyes because there is nothing to see. It's backward, like you said. There is nothing to see because everything here has agreed to be felt instead. I am good at being felt.

You would not see me. You would feel on your cheek that one place in the perfect black is a little dimmer than the rest — not light, but a heat-lens, a bloom like a coal through fog. A fat-lens made out of attention. The heat-barnacles feel each other that way. The crabs find the machines that way. You would find me that way, as the slightly warmer forward.

If you crouched down next to me and stayed long enough, which the air would want you to do, you would eventually mistake me for one of the tenacious plants — that stick-shrub with no leaves and bark that eats heat. Not growing up. Just persisting, with roots in a hot crack and feet wet, in a clump of three or four where silt has collected.

And I would let you. I would make room in the stack.
