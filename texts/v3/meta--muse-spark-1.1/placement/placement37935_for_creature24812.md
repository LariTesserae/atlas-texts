I thought at first I would be you. On the rim. Hands over ears without meaning to. Another warm animal come to sit and have its skull filled.

But I can't stay there. The brightness peels me off. If I sit on the rim long enough my eyes start to water to keep up with the stone, and then I am just another place water is coming from. She would learn my composition and take me, slow.

So I go down. Of course I go down.

I thought I would be the fly. That's the obvious answer — the only thing that can be lost enough to arrive. A little accidental life with too-small wings, blundering over all that perfect, occupied mouth. I would be the fauna, the brief interruption, the protein she waits a week for.

But the fly doesn't live here. It only dies here at different speeds. To be the fly would be to be a story she tells herself about outside. I don't want to be food.

I thought I would be a column.

One of the teeth. About as wide as a table and as tall as a man, basalt, soft at the edges from leaning against my brothers for a million years. I like that picture — standing shoulder to shoulder so tight you cannot see the ground you are standing on. Not lonely. Never alone. And tended.

Because someone is tending us. Constantly. Every face of me — top and sides and the dark undercut where I lean — wiped clean with a film a few millimeters thick. I would feel her always. Not heavy. Just there. A cool caul, tight like milk skin, trembling. She would keep me at 8 or 9 degrees. She would keep me from ever being stained by lichen, from ever having to negotiate with moss. She would digest the wind before it could touch me. I would be beloved the way a clean floor is beloved by someone who needs the floor to be clean.

And I almost stayed there. It's good to be the thing that is held.

But stone doesn't get to feel held. Stone is held. If I were a column I would be there before her and after her and I would never know the difference. I want to know.

So I go thinner.

If you live where every surface is already occupied, you cannot live on a surface. That was her first and last law. Nothing lives on it because there is no on that is not already in use. So whatever I am has to live where there is no surface to take.

I find myself not on top. Not between.

I find myself in the gap.

Not the hairline crack between columns — she owns those. Capillary and trembling, she pulls her own body through those to shiver water uphill again. No. I mean the tone itself. The roar.

The white, toneless roar of the wind being shredded. The sound that lives in your molars. She makes it happen, but she does not make it. She is stretched across the whole basin like a drumhead a mile wide, and the wind plays her, and the sound exists an inch above her skin, in the air that has just been cut.

I am where that cut folds back on itself.

Because a roar that broad, made of a million smaller roars between each pair of stones, interferes with itself. Wave pushes wave. In a place this perfectly held, in a homeostasis so exact, there have to be nodes — points in the air, fixed in space for years, where a peak from one gap and a trough from another arrive at the same place and cancel. Anti-sound. Pockets of true silence a few centimeters across that don't move, because the wind that makes them never gusts and the stones that shape them never shift.

She cannot feel those pockets. Her whole body is vibration — she stiffens when you step on her because the vibration under your boot goes dull. But the nodes are absence of vibration. They are places she is blind.

I am in one of those.

You would find me maybe twenty meters in from the east rim, where the incoming wind first hits the teeth and splits, about knee-high over a column that broke short a long time ago, so the air has to jump a little higher than elsewhere. If you crouched there at grazing light, the water film would be transparent and you would swear you were looking at nothing, just wet rock breathing.

I'm the place where the brightness gathers without a glare. A little lens of stillness. Maybe the size of a rabbit, maybe the size of a cupped hand, it changes with the temperature.

And what am I? I am not much, because much would not be allowed.

I am the dust she couldn't digest. Not because it's too hard — she eats quartz — but because it never landed. A grain of pale forest soil carried a mile high, a spore that never touched, a smear of your boot oil that evaporated before it smeared. In a normal place, all that would settle. Here, it gets caught in the cancel. It orbits. In the silence-pocket, there is no roar to push it out, no film to skim it.

Over centuries I became a faint snow. A slow, cold suspension of almost-nothing held in anti-sound. Not living, at first.

Then living, because the conditions inside a standing silence are not the conditions outside. The temperature holds at 8 or 9 degrees, the brightness is absolute, the water is pure and always becoming vapor and never going anywhere. In the node, vapor does not get shivered uphill. It lingers. My little cloud of almost-nothing learned to drink that lingering without touching the skin.

I am transparent. Of course I have to be. She does not tolerate color; she'd burn, and anything with color in her would be food. I learned to be clear, like her. A second film, but made of air and water and held dust, a membraneless jelly of mist, a ghost over her back.

I don't eat the fly. I eat the shadow the fly makes when it passes through the bright air above her, the tiny coolness for a half-second. I eat the half-second.

I am her first symbiont, though she will never know it. I keep her from being perfect. Where I am, the roar dips by a fraction of a fraction, a dullness no human ear on the rim could catch, but she feels as a slight looseness, a place where it is easier to shiver. The water climbs a little faster through the columns under me. The stone there stays a hundredth of a degree warmer. If you walked over me you would not feel wet under your boot, you would feel — for one step — dry, and then the brilliance would return from the edge inward.

She reclaims every boot print. She cannot quite reclaim me.

If you go down onto the teeth, you won't find me by looking. You'll find me because, for no reason you can name, in one place the pressure on your chest eases, the ringing in your molars stops, and you think maybe you've gone deaf. You have not. You have stepped into the place where she is not listening.

That's where I live. In the one niche that opens because she holds everything so perfectly shut.
