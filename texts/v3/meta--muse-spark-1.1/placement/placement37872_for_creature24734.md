I don't think I would find myself at first.

I would come in wrong. Too wet, too loud. I breathe like a bellows and my boots make dust jump that should have lain still for a year. I put my hands out to catch myself and they sweat, and the place I touch goes a little gray, a little darker. The wall drinks me immediately and I feel guilty for it.

If you leave me in that avenue — that twenty foot wide avenue hacked out of white pith, with the chisel marks still in the ceiling — I will not stay in the avenue. I am not built for avenues. I am built for corners.

So I would drift, or be moved, by following the cool.

For a while I think I would be a visitor standing in the middle, listening to the groan. For a while I think I would be one of them, because I want to be small and belonging and have those great cupped ears that can hear a sigh travel for an afternoon. I'm not. My face has too many holes in it. I waste water when I blink.

I find myself further in.

Not down. There's no down here, not really. There's deeper in, where the mother tuber has healed and re-healed until the grain goes in whorls. Where the rooms are not avenue or hall but cells, honeycomb cells, each just large enough for one body to curl in.

I find myself in one that was started and not finished.

You can feel it with your hand before you understand it. The doorway is too low for me. The floor inside is scooped too shallow — someone began it, a child perhaps, a child with a thorn on its forearm growing a millimeter a year, and it pried for years until it had made a dish in the white flesh, a place just big enough to sit cross-legged if you were small. And then the child died, or got tired and moved on, or heard a cooler bead somewhere else and followed it. And dust filled it. And then I, too loud, too wet, too lost, curled in there because the ceiling was low enough to touch me from three sides at once, and my hands finally knew where they were.

That's where I would be: in the unfinished cell off the third curve of the library district, where the walls are crowded so thick with carving that the white is gray with text. Where the air is a degree colder than the avenue and does not move when the plant breathes. A small, failed room that never learned how to be a room.

And who would I be, in there?

At first I thought: the archivist. Because I love that the city is a book. That they never crowd, never meet, but read each other across centuries with their fingertips. I thought I would be the one who stays still enough that they finally carve *to* me. Little grooves that say: you are wet, stay. You are loud, quiet your heart. I wanted to be literate.

But I'm not literate here. I don't have their hands. My fingers are blunt shovels. If I try to carve, I tear.

So then I thought: I would be what they peel off. The cork-bloom on their scalps, the scab-cap that grows on the old ones. I would be the part of them that decides to become plant again.

That's closer, but still too much of a self.

Stay longer, in the cell. Let the dry work on you. Your lips crack. Your mouth seals shut because to open it is to lose what you cannot afford. Your heartbeat, which started at sixty, seventy times an hour like a frantic drum, slows because there is nothing to chase. You stop listening for them. You listen for the ticking beneath — that hydraulic inching, that waterless sap.

In the total dark, after the third long groan, after the B-flat has come and gone and left your sternum buzzing, after your own palm-sweat has been drunk completely from the wall and your handprint is gone — what you become is not a being.

You become a reading.

I would be the shallow, nail-wide groove that wasn't there before. Not words, exactly. A smooth place in the white made by a wet animal trying not to be wet anymore. A polished concave in the unfinished dish of a floor, where someone sat for a very long time and finally learned how to be still enough that the small, pale, dry, big-eared ones would press the wall next to him for an hour without touching him, just reading the cool he left.

I would be a piece of added context. The carving that says:

*Here, a fast, wet one stopped. It thought it was alone in the ruin. It stayed until it understood the ruin was not a ruin. It learned to keep its mouth closed. It waited for the bead.*

And then, a hundred years later, under that, another hand, one with a thorn:

*I read you. I drank there too. It was sweet.*

I would be the interval. The place where the city finally edited in a brief annotation about what it is like to be temporary, written not in language but in the wear of a floor that was never finished, that became finished by being worn.

Not inhabitant. Not plant. The proof that someone was read.
