I thought at first I would be you.

The one with the hands. The one who came with ears still tuned for sky, and shoes. Knees wet. One palm out to keep the pillars from your face, the other on the iron that is so hot the rain turns to gas before it lands.

I held that image for a while because it is warm there.

At the base of the Engine where the peat has pulled back in a perfect, respectful circle. Where the iron thrums CHUD-chud-chud into your molars and throws a blue-white arc that shows you too much for half a second and then gives the dark back deeper. Where every desperate living thing piles up to steal heat — the black ferns leaning in, wrist-thick and iron-smelling, the atrogs stacked three deep sighing and going flat, the chitterer troupes fighting for the loudest branch.

That would be a good place to be a person. A listening post. A maintenance ghost for a machine that hasn't needed maintaining in three hundred years. Learning to read ozone and hot stone like weather.

But I can't stay there. I'm too low.

I'm not knee-high. I'm not even ankle.

If you kneel in that circle — and you will, because the fungal colonnade is so close together and the chugging off-beat bearing makes you dizzy — you put your ear close to the mat, the one you thought was sphagnum.

That's where I find myself.

First, as pressure. As a small resentful deflation under your knee that slowly, resentfully reinflates. Billions of us. Not a carpet. A city of soft, sour balloons, each one the size of a pinhead, each exhaling 30-degree mineral air. On Earth Sphagnum is a plant. Here the carpet is an animal that decided to be ground.

If you stay kneeling long enough, the rain stitching down from the canopy finds the nape of your neck and your ears adjust past the engine and you hear what lives *under* hearing.

Hmmmmmmmmmm.

An atrog moaning a few meters off. Another answering from a mile away across a bowl-lake. Monastery work.

And between those low notes, a much finer sound. A very fast, very dry clicking, like fingernails typing morse code on the inside of a bone.

That. That's me.

Lean your tired forehead against a fungal pillar the way you did in the story you were told. The pillar is not wood. It's grey-white, faintly hot, leathery. The canopy at the top is one continuous liver the size of a country condensing its own sky and weeping it back as secondary rain. The pillars are just its legs. And its legs are hollow.

I'm inside one.

I have never been outside one.

I am the litter.

The length of your forearm, blind, naked, my head almost all ear and nose. Six limbs, two of them not for walking at all — they are long, tight, tuned forks made of thinned chitin. I keep them sprung against the warm pith of the inside wall. I drum.

We all drum. We are the most abundant animal by weight on that high continent because the continent is a tree and we are inside its veins. We farm. The pith we carve is soft and sweet and hot. We smear bacteria on the gallery walls and they bloom black and green and we eat them. We let the rootlets of the black ferns drill through from above — those hot, cinnamon-and-iron leaves with their roots in the canopy — and we coppice them. We herd the aphid-things that drink fungus instead of sap and we milk their mineral honey.

We never need to come out. Why would we? The whole world is our tree.

When you put your forehead against my house, you felt me as a faint market chatter moving through your frontal bone. That was me talking to the next pillar over. That next pillar talking to the next. The fused canopy is a drumhead stretched across a country. One tap here goes for kilometers. We hear engines arc as pressure bruises. We hear the atrog-monks moocow in the bogs. We hear you, the pressure-change at one small point on the skin of the system, moving and stopping and putting both hands on hot iron as if you could steady it.

I would be one of them who lives low enough to never have believed in above. Who learns a visitor not by sight — we have no eyes — but by the way the visitor's footsteps make the secondary rain stutter, and by the way the chitterers hush their cathedral of sound when the visitor passes, and by the way every third minute your heart does a funny off-beat thud that is exactly the same as the Engine with the worn bearing.

We will carve that rhythm into the wall and keep it.

When you stand again, water to your knees, you will feel something change in the way the pillar feels under your palm — a slight tightening, a held breath in the pith.

That's not the Engine noticing you.

That's me, pausing my farming, pressing both tuning limbs to the wall to listen better to you listening to me.
