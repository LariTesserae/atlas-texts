At first I think I'd be high. 

It's habit — when you show me a vast flat place I look for the high place to see it from. So I put myself up on the hot tin with the gulls, in a row, one bird every ten feet along the ridge cap, all facing the nothing-wind. I like that kind of sparse work. Patient, terrible, old. Not crying.

But I wouldn't last up there. The tin is more than 38C. It's 60C, 70. It burns through the shoes. The gulls have learned to stand on one foot and then the other. I have not. Also I am bad at being terrible.

Then I think second-story human. That seems right. I'm good at tending things that have to keep ticking or else. But when I walk the town in my head I don't go inside right away. I keep to the middle of the four-lane because that's habit too — leave the edges alone.

The road is too wide. That's the trick. It's four lanes for trucks that come twice a day and outside those ten minutes it's just a white expanse with heat coming off it in a sheet. The edges belong to the crabs. Thousands of them, shoulder to shoulder, quiet because chitin on wet concrete is quiet. If you stand still too long they route around your boots and you become an island.

I don't want to be an island. I want to be a place where traffic already knows to go around.

So I walk. Past the open roller door where the darkness inside is the size of a cathedral with one pump in it ticking like a man breathing through his nose while he sleeps. Past the canal the color of cold tea, hot to the touch. Past the totes stacked ten stories high. Past a chainlink with no sign that has given up pretending it encloses anything.

It is so quiet. Not silent — there is too much life for silent — but quiet the way a room is quiet when everyone in it has agreed not to waste moisture on noise.

Where would I be? Not anywhere new. This place has already filled every niche by being so empty. The ground floor is taken. Taken for millions of years and then taken again five minutes ago by a brown crab the size of a fist. The high hollows are taken by bats and swifts braiding themselves into a river at false dusk. The roofline is taken by herons.

I keep looking for the gap and then I understand the gap is mine.

The second-floor landing with the bucket.

I would be in the threshold trade. Not quite person, not quite building. You know the one — every occupied second-story has one: a poured landing, maybe three foot by four, outside a steel door that is left open for ventilation, with two things on it. One: a pair of boots, laces undone. Two: a white plastic bucket underneath the lip of the roof where the condensate runs down all day even though it never rains.

That is where I live.

I would be the person who belongs to that landing. Late fifties, hair cut short because hair is a cruelty, t-shirt gone limp and damp and perfectly soft, the gray-silver-black of wet concrete and oil soaked so far into the skin of my forearms that washing would be a category error. I shine. Everyone here shines.

My job is not important and cannot stop. I watch a gauge that says keep the water moving or become water. I don't watch it hard. I watch it like you watch a sleeping animal - peripherally, with love. Once an hour I log it. The sodium bulb above me buzzes all day long. My radio is tuned low to tide and weather, which here are the same station.

At night, at 2 a.m. when the low chatter of bearings and the chuck of water into water is everywhere because it is nowhere, the rats use the center stripe like a highway, nose to tail. I sip tea made from the bucket - because condensate is cleaner than what comes from the tap - and I watch them go. I count none. Not my business.

My business is the agreement.

You live on the second floor and you give the first floor over. You leave your boots outside and by morning the crabs have cleaned them. You never step on, always over. You shake your shoes. You nod from landing to landing. You never call out because sound is moisture and moisture is work.

I am good at that. I am good at not calling out. I am good at the long flat nod that means *I see you, pump's fine, water's moving.*

I would be one of the soft people, with my corners gone, dissolved by looking into steam-light.

And after a while, if you walked past my building - past that cathedral dark with its ankle-deep sheetwater rippling without wind - you would think no one was here. Density trick. You'd see empty road, empty factory, empty sky the same color as the brown estuary with no line between.

Then you would hear the chair scrape one story up. And see the bucket catching sky.

And realize the city isn't empty at all. It's just learned how to be vast without being loud about it.

Keep it moving.
