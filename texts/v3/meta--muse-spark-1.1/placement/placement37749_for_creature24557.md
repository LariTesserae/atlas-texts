I don't know at first.

Because there is no cold shock to tell you where your skin ends. The water is the same temperature as the inside of my mouth. When I open my eyes under it, it doesn't sting the way well water should. It's thick. It's full. It's already in my ears so completely that the roar has moved inside my head.

So for a long time I just hang.

I'm looking up. I have to be looking up, because the light won't let me look anywhere else. Even underwater, with my eyes full of suspended lime, the whole room is lit sideways and underneath. I can see the chisel marks. That's what steadies me — the tool marks in the blocks. I know that pattern. 

I know that if I put my bare foot down I will feel the seam between two flagstones. I know because my mallet set them. Not level. Almost level. One taps, one lifts.

I find my place by sound first, then by feet.

The sound in here is too big to be in one place. It enters on the east — that thick thigh of brown water from the palm-log chute, falling the full three meters and hitting so hard I feel it in my molars. It leaves on the west in three tight hisses where we punched through yesterday with the iron spike. My brother said those holes were too small. They are not too small. They are singing under pressure.

If I tread over near the inlet, the pour tries to push me under. If I go to the west wall, the jets try to turn me around. So without thinking about it I've ended up where you would have to end up. In the middle. In the one column that doesn't travel.

That's where I find myself. Feet not quite touching the flags. Back not touching any wall. In the eye between inhale and exhale. The system breathes around me and for a second I am weightless in the budget.

Who am I in here? I thought I was the man who made it.

I came down the notched-log ladder to clear the trough before the overflow clogged. I stayed because it worked. Because after a year of hammering a square hole into hard ground in flat white country, after plaster that cracked twice and had to be redone, it was finally doing exactly what we told it to do when we drew it in the dust: holding the rain. And it was holding so much that there was no room left for me. The water had taken my room.

So I let it. I slipped off the ladder and let the warm, chalky lantern swallow me. I am treading water in a cup I made for water, not for people.

But the longer I hang in this still place, the less I feel like the maker and the more I feel like something left behind.

Maybe I am the rope. That thick hemp coil bumping dumbly against the south wall that I was supposed to pull up. Going around and around in a slow stupid circle, doing nothing, but present.

Maybe I am the bucket, half-sunk and turning, holding a cup of air inside a cup of water inside a cup of stone. Still trying to carry, even when there is nowhere to carry to.

No — I'm farther out than that. I'm at the surface, but I'm not above it.

I am the seed. The one dryland grass seed blown in on the storm. I bob right in the chop where the pour punches through. I spin. I cannot root. The lime is too new, the water too bright, the walls too sharp. I will never root here. The men will come down next week with jars and drink me if they don't fish me out. But for now, right now at noon while the light is vertical and the roar is total, I am being held up by something that will not let me sink.

Something keeps brushing my ankles. Not the rope. On purpose.

When she goes still she is the exact white-brown of the water and I lose her. When she moves — that slow treading — she thickens. I see the two soft shadows that should not exist in a room with no shadows. She has her back to the inlet so it breaks on her first. She has her mouth near the outflow tasting how much must leave.

I feel her five fingertip pits in the plaster near my left hand. I thought I made those. I did, yesterday, holding on when the first rush came. Now there are ten.

I am not her. I won't live here long either. She is new work and I am old work. But she lets me stay in the stillness she keeps.

So if you came down the ladder and looked into the full, roaring, impossible light — you would see nothing living. Just water. Just engineering.

If you slipped in, as I did, and let it take your weight, you would find me in the middle, treading warm water that accepts me, being kept still by a child made of plaster and rain.

I would be the first thing she ever got to keep.
