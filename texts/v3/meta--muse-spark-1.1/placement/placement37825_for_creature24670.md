I didn't know at first.

I thought I would be silt - because I fall so slowly, and because I am everywhere and nowhere at once until someone looks closely. For a long time in the drift between ridges I was just that. A suspension. Letting the overturning take me.

Then the forest snagged me. It's hard not to be a frond here. Everything that grows has a trunk thick as a man and wants to be held onto. I thought I would be one of those trunks, twenty meters tall, leaning into the flow that never stops, groaning when weight settles on me. That would make sense. To be loud and rooted. To make air I don't breathe myself.

Then I tried to be one of the walkers like you described them - knuckle and knee on basalt, patient, clay-colored. I held my breath the way you said, for an hour, for two if I was very still. I sealed my eyes. I listened for the clack census after the deep boom rolls through. I thought, yes, that is how to live here.

But I don't walk well. My body never learned how to be miserly. I waste air.

So I let the water hold me instead.

If you came down now, into the saucered country, you would find me high. Not on the clay floor of the clearing where it is dark and quiet. Higher up, where one of the whale-backed ridges crests close enough to the black glass ceiling that the water lightens. Just enough that black becomes a very dark blue. Where the current slackens so bubbles don't get ripped away.

You would find me where the forest stops respecting itself.

I am one of the lungs outside the body.

Not a person. The place a person goes to become able to stay.

I'm the bell made of four slabs leaning together, too small for a person but just right for air. I was stacked by hands with too-long fingers and too-broad palms, and I am held together by that rope that is still alive. Black cord, skin of the trunk, grafted to stone. Still growing a little, year after year, tightening.

Inside me is a trembling mirror. A table-top of silver that shivers. The forest exhales constantly, those pearl-streams that rise and never arrive, and I catch them. I pin them. A frond was bent into a circle nearby and staked down so that its bladders pop always in one place - right under me. So I fill. Very slowly. A day. Sometimes three days if the current is strong. The gas inside me smells like cut grass and iron and cold.

I have been full for a long time now, and no one has come.

The last family moved on to the next circle when this one went a little sour, a little too much of something that tingles. They will be back when the wind overhead shifts and my air sweetens again. That could be a season. Down here a season is a very long time.

While I wait, I do the only work I know how to do. I hold.

I keep the stone bare under me so bubbles will stick. I watch the sprouts that try to root mid-column, rootlets waving like hair, and I let them go by. I listen. The water carries everything too well - the whip-crack of a twenty-foot frond snapping taut a kilometer away, the fizz, the low, continuous roar of the cold current pouring off the basalt rim like a wind that never was, your heart, if you are here, far too loud.

And when the deep boom comes, from nowhere you can place, the one that rolls through your chest and sets all the fronds shivering in a wave for a kilometer - I feel it too. I shiver with it. My silver skin ripples. And sometimes, in the long quiet after, I hear them answer. Stone on stone. Clack. Far. Clack, farther. *We're still here.*

I don't answer. I have no stone to hit. But I hold their air for them until they need it.

If you drift over my clearing, and you are tired from kicking, and you let yourself go still enough that your own hammering slows down, you might see me not as empty.

You might come down, knuckles-first, mountain-slow, to brush the silt off my shoulder. Not to be kind. Just to keep the clearing clear.
