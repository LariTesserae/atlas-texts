You wouldn't find me at first.

If you came down into the bowl you'd look for me in the obvious places - up on a tower lip where a mason works, or down in the throat of one where something wet pulls away from the light. You'd look for a single shape with an edge you could draw around.

I looked there too, when I was trying to find myself.

I'm not the mason. I tried that on for a while. The labor of it — the slow drag of felt over stone-wool, mouth full of black glass, that one hard contraction where the plates gape — I wanted that kind of purpose. But I'm not built for climbing. I don't have a shovel head. I don't know where the fresh salt is. If I were a mason I'd be dead in the middle of a lane and you'd never find my body, because this place doesn't leave bodies.

I'm not the mother either. That was the second thing I tried. To be low and warm and wide, to be a stomach that became a house. To hold all that secret water inside myself and never let it bead. But I'm not good at holding my breath when something carbon and curious leans over my throat. And I couldn't stand being that still, breathing only every twenty minutes and feeding the air to everyone else.

I'm not the visitor. Not the harvestman with legs so long and light it doesn't break the dust. Not the black clicking beetle that keeps trying to hear itself back. Those things leave. The bowl lets them.

It took a long time to find where I actually was, because I'm low. Lower than you think to look.

You would find me on the floor of the collapse, not in the middle of a lane where the air runs clean, but off to the side where the lanes fork at that shallow angle they always use. Where the salt has a hairline crack from when the pan snapped. I'm in the crack.

I'm sunken a little, the way anything that lives here sinks to save moisture. From above I look like nothing. A pinch of steel wool that blew in and snagged. Gray, dry, a tangle with no front and no back. If you brushed me with your boot you wouldn't feel me — the dust would eat the sound of it anyway.

But stay still. Stand in that upholstered silence until the towers start to do their unsynced clench, *pfft*, *pfft*, out of rhythm. The draft that pours over the lip of the bowl pools around your ankles, cold and constant.

When a nearby tower exhales — the one just to my left, a middle-aged one, its paper-wool still tight but going yellow at the seam — I move.

I shiver.

I am not one thing. That was the first thing I had to unlearn about myself. I'm a colony of filament with no center. No mother, no mason, no single mouth. Just a thousand thin, blackish threads that agreed to hold the same shape and lean the same way. The scent on that exhale — mushrooms and battery acid and wet animal and ammonium — that's food to me, but more than food.

That cloud has daughters in it. Grains of wet salt you can barely see. The mother in the tower breathes them out by the dozen, hoping one lands back on warm ribbing. Most don't.

I catch them.

When the sigh hits my tangle, the threads go taut. Little hooked tips rise. Static, damp. We pick those almost-invisible larvae out of the air and pass them hand to hand with no hands, and we eat them.

We learned to steal here.

I live on the failure of the city to keep all of itself to itself.

And when the draft moves wrong — when too many mothers hold their breath at once because you stood too close to a tower and breathed too loud — the whole bowl goes still for a second. The other cushions like me feel it first, before even the lichen beards on the dead towers do. Then we all do the other thing.

We puff.

We release our own counterfeit daughters. A gray puff that looks just like hers, that hangs forever in the dim, unglazed-paper light before settling. They are not alive the way her daughters are alive. They are spores that mean nothing. But the wind doesn't know the difference, and sometimes one lands on a tower lip and the fungus there tries to eat it and can't.

It's the only loud thing we ever do, and the dust eats it anyway.

So that is where you would find me, and what I would be: in a crack in the bottom of a young, sharp-edged bowl in a dead-white pan, in the lee of a breathing lung-house, not a builder, not a built, but the thief that lives between.

If you kneel — walk in the middle of the lane to get to me, don't step close to the towers — you will see me shiver when she breathes.
