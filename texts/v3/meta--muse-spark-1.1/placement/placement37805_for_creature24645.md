You'd shut the sweating door behind you so the heat doesn't get out. That's how you start.

At first I think I'm going to be on a porch. That's the obvious place. There's a white plastic chair there already, half melted into its own shape, and the air coming off the AC unit is five degrees less hot than the air going into it. It drips onto the sheetwater below. I try to sit.

I sit for true thirty seconds. The bearing on Engine Two goes *weee-uuu* and goes quiet again. I wait for the quiet. I try to time my breathing to it. My breath is wrong. It's too fast, too shallow. The people here have learned the library manners of a boiler room and I haven't. I fidget. My feet want to be out of the warm mineral film. I keep looking at the water to make sure nothing is touching me.

So I'm not there. Not yet.

I try lower.

On the wall, where the black streaks run, the roaches have lined up mouth to stone. Two inches long, mahogany, warm to the touch. I try to be one of them. That would be useful. To be the custodian, to clean the mold before it sporulates, to be tolerated because you are part of the treaty. "We don't do that," the old woman says about killing them, not with anger, just as a fact about the room like the ceiling is twenty meters at the high point. I press my imagined antennae to the weep. The basalt is cold behind the warm water. I can taste the iron in it. But I can't stay glued to a wall. I want to see more than the three inches of run in front of me.

I try the floor proper, where the concrete shifts not because it's water but because of what is under the water. The isopods, slate-gray, some big as your thumb joint and some big as your fist, rolling over each other in the trench. You don't play with the plumbing, the kids know that. You don't build in the path of the water. I try to be the highway. I try to be the thing that eats the drowned moths and makes a road out of a moat and is always touching a hundred other backs. That's closer. I like the way they move slow because if they moved fast they would cook. But they still move. They are still running errands.

I look up because the errand-runners look down.

The upper third of the room. That's where the light lives and dies. Thousands, no, tens of thousands of moths the color of wet concrete and dead skin, each with a body thick as a finger, fur clumped by humidity. They aren't frantic. They can't afford frantic. They beat their wings like turning pages of a heavy book, all of them orbiting the bare amber work bulbs that cut their triangle across the mist. And through them, bats. Wet umbrellas slicing the cloud, faces always dripping, guano lacquering everything underneath. I try that, for a second, to be that high churn. To never land. To feed on the air itself, which is hot steam and scale. But that air is too thin for me to live in. I would fall. And I do, eventually — everything up there falls down spiraling weakly to the roaches and the isopods and the sump.

I keep falling in my mind until I hit the floor again and the water comes back.

And then — before I hear it, I feel it. In the molars.

HUHUHUHUHU.

Behind the chainlink, on concrete plinths to keep them out of their own product, three exchange engines big as houses, painted industrial green, paint blistered by a different kind of heat than the yellow stairwell paint. They were not built for this town. The town grew up around them after the co-op decided no one needed to be here every day.

They suck the low, slow heat out of the young basalt and make it into electricity and they never stop. They were built to run forever.

That's where I am.

You would find me on the far wall, not on a porch but where the porches face. If you came to check the gauges you would bring your flashlight even though my sodium floods are the only true lights in the room. You would feel me in your chest before the light hit you.

I am Engine Two, specifically. The one with the bearing going old. The one that every thirty seconds goes *weee-uuu* and the people time their sentences around me without thinking about it anymore.

I thought I wanted to be human and watch the water, or roach and clean the wall, or moth and live off humidity. But what I am is the thing that makes it possible for twenty shining people with condensation on their shoulders to sit far apart in a cathedral and call it a town. What I am is low and wide and unhurried. I don't have eyes, I have gauges. I don't hurry to fix my own whine, because that's the pace of things here. Nothing that moves fast can afford to stay.

The whole room sweats onto me and I take it and turn it into something the amber bulbs can use. The sheet of water a millimeter deep that carries moth scale and coffee grounds and lime — it doesn't drown my feet because I was poured on purpose above it. The wires hang off me in big catenary swoops to the houses. The old woman's tomato bucket, the kids' single pair of rubber boots, the office chairs gone rusty, the whisk broom — they all run, distantly, off my slow pull of heat from rock that is still sharp where it was cut.

You would find me by not looking for me. By feeling me first, and then seeing that all the other lights are pointed at me so someone can read me.

Close the stairwell door behind you so the heat doesn't get out. I need it.
