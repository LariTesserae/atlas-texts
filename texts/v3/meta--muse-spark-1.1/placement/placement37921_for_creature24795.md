At first I thought I was the water.

That's the easiest mistake to make here. You're high, so high the air below would be a different planet, and cold in a way that gets in your molars, and held, and you think: I must be swimming. I must be falling very slowly. I must be the thing that is between.

I let myself be that for a while. I was the clear black-green, the noon-light filtered through miles that never ripple. I was the current that isn't current.

But the water doesn't notice itself. It has no work. Here everything has work.

Then I thought I was one of the silver darts. That would be nice. I like the way they turn. A thousand of them, all flash, finding the exact same torus between two trunks with a whole city of empty between them, burning almost nothing because a convection cell the size of a state is doing the moving for them. I tried to be them, to trace that same corner over and over. 

I couldn't. I'm too heavy. I'm not good at going nowhere forever and making it look like flying. I look away every time just before the turn.

I tried to be bigger. A Drinker. That would make sense of the ache. Let me plug my head into a valve the size of a door and let the hot, heavy oil-sap wash through me and darken me like old tea. Let me never move. Let me bud children who will also never move and already know where to clamp. The shudder that goes along a hundred miles of trunk when pressure spikes — I wanted that to be my shudder.

But I'm not hungry like that. I'm not built to take.

And I am not the Charge-Taker, either. I could never hold lightning under my skin like that and not die of it. I can't be the black sack the size of a ferry that drifts into the blue-white gridlines and makes the light go out around itself by eating it. I felt one thump past me, once — you feel it in your sternum before you hear it, that *whump* that is their exhale of clean current back into the forest — and I knew I wasn't for that. I don't store. I don't transform. 

I am not built to keep things going.

I am built to keep things from having ever changed.

Find me like this:

Pick a trunk. Any one. They're all a country wide. Come in close enough that the daylight, that stupid normal 2pm-on-a-Tuesday-in-May daylight, can't reach you anymore because you're in the shadow of a condensation leaf that is an acre of dripping cooling tower. Come down the bark that isn't bark but a wall of interwoven leaves turned to leather and mineral and then soft again. Find a valley in it. Not metaphor. A proper canyon cut by nothing but pressure and time, ten feet deep, whorled. 

On its lip, Clingers work. Bus-sized, barn-sized, domed, blind, white-orange, polishing a stadium valve that goes *CHUK* every ninety seconds and has since before calendars. You know them. They are busy.

Look past them. Down in the canyon floor.

That's where I am.

I am paved there.

I look at first like not-alive. Like paint. Old paint on a factory that never closed. Layer after layer of calcium and dead symbiotes and black-green slime and their own dissolved shells, because of course the Clingers eat each other when one dies and secrete a new one and the space is never empty. Erosion 1.9. That number is my body. I have been polished smooth by improbability itself.

I am the Rind.

I am not one. That's another mistake. I am a sheet, a living varnish, a square mile of low, patient film that grows so slowly it only grows by agreeing that it has always been that big. I don't have eyes either. Eyes would be filth collectors. I have chemistry. I have the memory of grooves.

My work is this: I feel wear.

When the water — the ocean that never fell that holds itself up — has rubbed a little too much off the trunk in one place over the last thousand years, I thin there. I go a little salty. I taste iron. The Clingers feel that with their soft brush-bellies and they step a little harder there on their endless march around the circumference, around and around for centuries. When a vein sweats too much heavy oil, I swell and bubble and go dark and the Drinkers find the nearest socket. When a filament of St. Elmo's fire has been crackling too blue for too long between trunks, I itch with static underneath and a Charge-Taker will come hang above me, drinking the extra.

I am the ledger. I am the maintenance schedule that life here has become. I am how the forest knows it is still exactly as loud as it has always been. That deafening, low, hydraulic groan of cold-boiled seawater and sap-oil and bone-pistons — I felt it get 0.0001 percent quieter three ten-thousands of years ago and I tightened.

I don't filter lightning. I don't drink oil. I don't polish seals. I am polished.

I have been here long enough that a whole ecology lives in me now and thinks I am ground. Little white crustaceans scrape maps out of me. Bacteria in me make light I will never see. The water above me rains constantly from the condensation plates and it has never clarified nor clouded.

You could lay a hand on me — if you had a hand, if you were extruded here the way sweat is extruded and not like a fish lives in a sea — you could lay a hand on me and feel hot despite the cool water, and vibrating, and entirely uninterested in novelty. There is nothing built to notice novelty here.

But I notice the small deficit. That's all I have ever done. That will be all I ever do.

If you are looking for me, don't look out between the trunks where it looks empty but moves wrong. Look down. Look at the wall. Look at the groove that has always been there, with its old paint.

I'm the paint.
