At first I thought I'd be up top.

On the rim. At the chainlink where the dry field starts to give. Where the air is still warm and moves too fast and you can look down and see the whole thing in one glance like a model village. A watchman. That's a natural place for me to put myself — a little outside, keeping notes.

But I can't stay there. It's too dry up there. Nothing finishes down here, up there everything finishes too quickly — grass goes to seed, fence rusts through, sky clears and then clouds again. I wouldn't hold.

So I go down. In my head I go down the long soft slope where the ground isn't cliff but settled layers. Cinder under my boots, then brick, then that cold sweat coming out of the limestone in beads. It gets to 52 degrees and stays there and my breath starts to get taken.

I thought maybe I'd be the birch. For a minute I wanted to be the one mistake, the ladder-tall tree in the gutter in that one shaft of real sun. The only thing here that still wants upward. Pale leaves. Trying to finish. That would be a romantic place to be.

But I'm not built for that kind of lonely hope. I would yellow and try too hard.

I thought maybe I'd be a pigeon. One per beam. One per ledge. That suits me — keeping station, facing outward, bringing a little outside dirt in on my feet and letting it fall for someone else. But pigeons here never call to each other. They have learned not to crowd. I am not good at not crowding.

I keep walking down onto the second street, the one that isn't a street because it has a channel down its middle. Tea-colored water. Your coat was dripping there. Mine is too now.

I listen — drip... tick... wings shuffling... water finding a lower level — and I find where the tempo of my own thinking matches. It's not in the high air with the hangings. I'm too restless to be one of them, to be that long felt of living biofilm and cilia, hanging and dripping on purpose, watching everything without blinking because I can't finish a blink. I respect them too much to claim to be them.

I am smaller. Closer to the ground.

I'd be down at the bottom where the rules touch each other.

At the edge of the mere that was once a yard, where the lowest street never dries. Where the eels move — fat and black as power cables — tying the gutters to the flooded basements to the marsh. Where the rats move like clerks along the dry lines only they remember.

I would be the other clerk.

Not a rat, not an eel, but the thing they both leave space for, because of Rule Three. One per drip, one per beam, one per channel. I would have a narrow terrace house whose back garden turned into canal a hundred years ago. I'd have left the front door open so the mist can pass through. And I would have a tool — not a hammer, this isn't a hammer town anymore — a wooden rake, maybe, or a flat shovel, the kind you use for clearing leaves out of gutters.

My job, without anyone giving it to me, would be to keep the water moving downward so slowly it might be moving upward.

All day the cinder felt silts up. The channels that link puddle to puddle to mere want to close. The pigeons drop seed husks. The liverworts throw down spores. The rust makes a thin skin. If nobody clears it, the water stops, the loop breaks, the flywheels — turning one revolution per minute in the powerhouse on that last pressure — would have nothing to lift. The hangings would dry at the fold edge. Something would finally finish, and Rule One forbids it.

So I walk. Slowly. Early-November cold that lives in stone. I pull the rake through the channel on Second. Edges clean. Teacher-straight. The water, which is 50 degrees, finds the lower level again with a small satisfied sigh. A salamander on the wall to my left, pink, gilled like a tiny fern crown, swallows a bead that I made possible. I nod to it. We keep distance.

I am the human who learned how to cure here.

I don't oil the big wheels — they oil themselves with patience. I don't feed the hangings — they feed on us all being exactly spaced. I don't need a light. The amber sodiums on the gantry legs have been burning for decades on a trickle and make cones in the damp without really lighting anything, and that's enough. In fact I'm grateful they don't light me well.

I keep the channels open. I keep one channel. Mine.

And when one of the big felts above me adjusts — when you hear that faint sigh of wet fiber on rust and a pigeon lifts without hurry and resettles exactly twelve feet away — I step back. I make room. The rain that isn't rain, that thicker, warmer rain that smells of iron and calcite and wet wool, patters on my hood, and I let it. I let it run down my sleeves and off my rake back into the cut I've made, so the salamanders below can drink it, so the eels can tie it back to the mere.

Nothing is allowed to finish. So I never finish clearing the channel. I get to the end and I start over. That's the whole mercy of it.

If you came down again and looked for me, you might not see me at first. I match the biofilm. I move at flywheel speed. I'm probably standing very still under the fold you thought was a tarp, letting my coat drip onto cinder, listening to the water find the lower level I just gave it.

Drip... pause... tick...

And my breath getting taken, up toward the stone to bead again.
