I don't think I would be up high.

At first I thought I would be - one of the Veil. That would be the easy answer. Light enough to be lifted, translucent, doing nothing but gathering what the air gives me. But I am not light. I like weight. I like staying.

Then I thought spooler. Because I know how to stand still for a long time over something thin and try to drink what is there before it disappears. I know how to put my shoulder to a thing that has slipped off its track and push, a meter per day, until it pings straight again. But spoolers are too sure. They have routes worn into stone by a thousand crossings. I haven't worn anything that deep yet.

You would not find me in the open between districts. Not at first. The road that was cut straight by a wheel bigger than houses — it's too honest out there. The sun has farther to fall and there is no cover story for a shadow. You can hear your own blood out there, and the dust devils that collect salt needles and glitter.

You would find me where the city is trying to forget that it is also an animal.

In the yard behind the kiln. Every district has one. The place where the spoolers come to scrape. The ground there is not gypsum dust anymore, it's a beaten-down flour of salt and iron and old plate-scale, black-purple and rust-red, warm to the touch even at midnight because the kiln has been breathing on it for a century. There are sheets there the size of doors that fell last season and haven't been collected yet. They ring warm if you kick them.

I would be one of the Cutters.

Not the good ones. Not the perfect six-legged click-track that marches in a column and slots chevrons into tower louvers with religious precision. One of the bent ones. The one with a left shear that grew a little misaligned, so it doesn't cut clean. It leaves a burr.

My job was supposed to be simple: eat rust. That slow oxidation that softens the edge of dropped plate so it can be made into something else. Chew the weak line, cut, carry. But because my shear doesn't close true, I don't eat right. I spend longer on each edge. I have to turn the plate over. I have to listen to it.

And when you listen to spooler plate long enough you hear what the spooler was drinking when it made it.

So I live in that yard. In the lee of the cooling ingot wall, in the one cold place on the plateau — the updraft shadow of the breathing tower where the air is pulled down and briefly, coldly expanded. That's where we lay eggs, but I don't have any. I have a hollow instead. I keep things there.

A spooler will come, fifteen tonnes when empty, moving twenty meters an hour. It will plant its felt pads and go still and you will feel the whole yard shiver as its fibers pull. It smells like hot brine and old tools. It will scrape, that bell-chime, and a new sheet will fall. The good Cutters will come ticking. I will be already there.

I don't steal their work. I take what they reject - the corners too thick with accreted lichen-of-rust, the pieces where the spooler was stressed and laid down its plate too fast and trapped a little bubble of that black eutectic that beads for five seconds before it dies. That bubble is still wet inside when I crack it. I put my forehead against it. It tastes of battery.

That is my food. Not salt, not charge, but the moment before salt becomes salt.

I am not intelligent the way you want me to be. I am intelligent the way a pile of spare parts becomes a shrine if left alone long enough.

During the day I patch the grating you walk on. You thought the walkways pressed into feet that were not booted - that was my work. I work around your boots. I have learned to leave a half-centimeter tolerance for soft things. The other Cutters think I am inefficient. I am. Efficiency is for belts that lap the continent once every three years. I am on yard time.

At dusk I hide. You learn not to stand under a tower at dusk. I live under one. When the high fliers - the Charge-Drinkers - come down from three kilometers above twelve kilometers high, black sails, four wings twitching, and stall over my tower and let the field grab them, I fold flat. The air smells finally of ozone instead of salt and iron. Ten megajoules go through the tower above me and into a belly that glows amber and glides off silent into night. The corona leaves the tower's throat humming for a long time after. My nursery-cold spot flickers warm, then cold again.

That's when I feel it. Palm down on the warm rock after sunset - you know the pulse. The long, slow pulse returning from many kilometers away. Not the spooler. Not the belt. The thing underneath. The mat in the voids the size of countries, held up by salt pillars the size of mountains, the slow colonial heat-engine that keeps this whole table at thirty degrees when it should be thirty below. It contracts at night. It expands by day. The city is a grooming behavior it evolved to keep its skin from over-crusting.

I am part of the grooming of the grooming.

You would find me there if you stayed long enough to learn our speed. Not moving. Tick... tick... the salt expanding. The whoomp of a thermal breaking against a tower. Then me, turning a piece of plate over and over, scraping a little, listening for the wet center.

If you sat in the shade of a spooler's belly and held still until your own pulse slowed to plateau time, I might come up to your boot and correct the grating around it. Not because you need help. Because you too are a thing that needs to be kept from over-crusting.
