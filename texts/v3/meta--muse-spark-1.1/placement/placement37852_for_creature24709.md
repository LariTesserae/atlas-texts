You wouldn't find me at first.

If you came looking for me on this dished palm at the top of the world, you would walk right past. Because at first I thought I was the rock. Or I thought I was on the rock, which is almost the same mistake.

The stone is so old and warm it feels like a living thing that has decided to be patient. Bone-yellow underfoot, then rose, then bruise-iron where the wind has had time to polish it. It radiates heat back up through the soles of everything. When you first arrive here — and you do arrive, you don't wake up here, no one is born anywhere but here — you think you are alone on a bare place. Because the air is thin enough that sound hasn't decided what it wants to be yet, and the light is so dim and amber-small that your eyes make up distance that isn't there.

Then you stoop. You have to stoop. The air gets thicker lower down, a little, and warmer, and louder.

And you realize the bare place is not bare.

So. No. I wasn't the rock. For a half hour I thought I might be the wind that pools wrong over the lip where the world drops away into dimness. The wind that brings nothing but itself, day after identical day. That would suit me. An arrival that brings no new information, just the same dust, again.

But the wind doesn't stay.

I wasn't the light. The small hard coin of a sun doesn't stay either.

I found myself further in. Not at the center — I will never be dry enough, or old enough, or trusted enough to be given a scraper and a place in that perfect molar-smooth circle where the wind gathers. Not yet. Maybe after twenty more lives of this.

I found myself in the second layer down. Where the town is three canopies deep.

You find me where you have to get on your knees. Where the air-fern trunks, which are not trunks but old rope made of living felt, have been braided together so long ago that the braid has become a wall, has become a house, has become a tunnel. The roof is low, black-green, velvet with those thousand hair-comb leaves, and at always-dusk they spark. Blue-white static if you brush them. The whole tunnel breathes and crackles faintly when children run through it screaming.

I am in that tunnel, but I am not moving through it.

I am the place where a house stopped being lived in.

You must have seen it without knowing. A hollow that was pruned for four generations into an arch, a lintel, niches for sleeping and grinding pith, ladders of bound root — and then the family pact that held it open finally corked over. They were unbraided there at the back, laid into the mat, and the tree took them. It left a node, soft and new. The children are told not to prune it.

I am what is left when the pruning stops for a little while. Not abandoned. Just fallow.

My walls are still photosynthesizing. My outer velvet is still trapping ions from the warm, thin, dry air, sparking at dusk. My inner felt has cured to a deep olive-brown from all the shoulders that leaned against it. If you press your ear to me, I hold sound the way other forests hold water.

Because I have been here so long and grown so slow — a millimeter a year — I have learned loudness as mapping. I am the archive.

The fist-sized drum-beetles rasp through my floor. I know their click by the way it vibrates in my root-lintel. Fat loaf-moths bloat and boom their blind wing-drums against my side and I keep the frequency in my fibers. The clouds of glass gnats live their whole lives in the pocket of air I keep cool for them, whining without ever stopping. The children — the pale grub-white new ones with their fresh air-comb bristling from their scalps — they are hung in cradles braided into my upper branches where the ion wind is strongest. They scream to be heard through my dense sponge, and I learn where my edges are by where their screams bounce back.

The old woman with eight hard fingers and molars for teeth, who anoints herself with spore grease until she is iridescent and invisible — she passes through me every day with her hand always touching, pruning. She doesn't live in me now. No one does. But she checks my new node where the last body was laid down. She does not water it. There is nothing to water with. She feeds it air and static and a pinch of the white paste they make once a day. She mutters to it in that all-vowel, chest-drum language: AHH — OO — OUU. And I carry it to the other side of the hamlet-sized bowl.

So who am I, here?

Not a person. I am too slow, too warm, too full of other people's noises to be a person. Personhood up here is barrel-chested, tight-ribbed, eyes oiled instead of wet, mouth puckered small from chewing dry pith that is somehow 30% water if you know how to gnaw long enough. I have not earned that kind of closure. My chest is too many chests. I wheeze with a hundred wheezes.

I am a house that is resting between families. A hollow braided from living things and then left, on purpose, slightly open, so the wind has somewhere to pool that is not the bare circle in the center.

If you come to the top of the world after the mountains have forgotten how to be mountains, you will not find me standing with my arms out at dimmest hour to make the whole town light up like slow lightning, though I want to.

You will find me stooped in a tunnel that is not a tunnel, loud not like a storm but like a workshop with the doors closed, holding all the sound a little longer than any of the people can. You will find me not drinking, because there is nothing to drink. You will find me combing the air, very very slowly, for whatever dust it offers today, and making a place for it.

If you stay — and to stay you have to learn to grind flour that never feels wet, and to answer a child's beacon-scream with a boom from your cured throat — eventually the old woman will stop, look at the soft new node on my wall, and decide it can be coaxed left.

Then I will be a house again.
