I don't think I would be sitting on the plate anymore.

Not for long, anyway. My feet would go numb checking for centipedes. My back would stay wet from where the leaf dumped on it. I would try to be the visitor — the thing that sits and observes — and it would not take. The night is too crowded for an audience. If you stay still here, something starts eating your shoe. If you stay still longer, something starts eating you. The visitor gets incorporated or leaves.

So where would I find myself, if I let myself soften and slide to where I fit?

At first I think I would be high. I want to be one of the sweating sails, one of those poncho-sized leaves that catches the soup-air and wrings it. There is a kind of nobility to that — to be the thing that makes the rain that never fell. I would hold my face up to the bacterial dusk and bead and bead and bead and finally tip and pour. But I am not that generous. I am not that patient. A sail lives for a night, gets eaten half, gets used as a parasol, a plate, a lamp-shade for bacteria. Too much touching.

Then I think I would be one of the tool-users. Because they remember. They carry something from one pool to the next — flake, twig, thorn. In a place where nothing lasts, to carry is a kind of prayer. But I listen to them harder and I know I couldn't. They are too turbulent. Too much chittering. Their hearts must beat like hummingbird wings to keep moving that fast. I would get distracted mid-grub and lose my tools in a crevice. I would be the one who holds the flake up to show you and gets tackled by a beetle.

No. Lower.

I thought maybe a boomer, wedged into a crack, sealing myself in with my own dried snot to wait out the day. That peace of sealing yourself in — and then at night to inflate until your throat is a lantern and make a sound so low it shakes dew off leaves three feet away. A sound that says *I am here* in a place too loud for voices. I like that. But even the boomer has to waddle, has to hunt, has to end up as food for the flat centipede.

Lower still. Smaller. Closer to the stone.

Find me in the third crevice from where you sat.

Not the first — that one has a toadlet in it cannibalizing its brother. Not the second — that one got shit in by a beetle and is now a nursery for white grubs. The third is a perfect joint where two planes of basalt cooled against each other and decided not to touch. It's deep, knuckle-deep, the sharpest edge in the basin still almost glass there. It has not yet been rounded.

You would not notice it unless condensation ran cold down the back of your neck again and you leaned forward.

Inside, there is a cup no bigger than your cupped palm. An inch of warm water that never fell. It is clear enough that you can see the brown slurry at the bottom — the dust of the rock, the fallen husks of midges, a little whorl of beetle grit that will one day become soil.

That is where I am.

Who am I? I'm not the lizard that drinks through its belly. I'm not the midge cloud that dances above it. I'm not the damselfly doing seam-work in the air.

I am the skin over the water.

I am the biofilm — but not the one on the rock that eats the knives. I am its cousin who learned to live stretched across surface tension.

I am a bacterium, a yeast, a little mat of green-gold that holds hands so tightly we become a membrane. You touch the pool and your fingertip comes away not just wet, but slightly thicker, slightly sour. That was me.

I live because of all the frantic things. The leaf above me condenses and *plink* — I get a gift. The midge larvae below me stir the slurry and push up dissolved iron. The lizard comes and sits his fat, permeable belly in me and I drink a little of his salt. When the boomer booms, I shiver and flare — because I am also part of the light. Bacteria like me were hired by the leaves to glow, but we also freelance out here on the water, a faint, bruise-colored sheen you only see when your headlamp is off and your eyes have given up.

I am not fast. Everything around me is bazaar-rush, turbulent, eat-and-be-eaten before morning. I am slow. I hold the cup together. If I wasn't here, the water would evaporate a little faster. The dust would not knit. The midge larvae would have one less thing to filter.

You lean over me and see your own face warped in my surface — amber eyes reflecting leaf-light, even though you don't have amber eyes. That's not you. That's the troop behind you.

You breathe. Your breath touches me and I flare, just a little. Yellow-green. Just enough light to lose your night vision and get it back.

And then a grime-pawed hand reaches past you, scoops me up with the water to wash a grub, and I am gone down a throat, or onto a basalt flake, or spilled onto the sharp rock where I will have to learn, all over again, how to eat a knife.
