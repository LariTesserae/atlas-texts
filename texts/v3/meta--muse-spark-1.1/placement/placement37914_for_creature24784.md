I wouldn't find myself in the middle at first.

That's where you think you'd go — out towards the center, where the low is lowest, where the sound is most even. But if you actually walked it, you'd realize the center is no different than anywhere else. There's no center to a bowl that has no rim. If you went that way you'd just keep walking until the sweat dried on your neck and turned back to water and you understood you were lost in a different way than you'd expected.

I find myself off to one side. Not near the pale smudge of the highlands, but where I can still see it if I stand up. That much of the outsider in me remains — I want a reference line. I'm sitting, because standing for long here feels rude. The sound gets inside your knees.

I'm in a sound shadow. That's where you would find me.

There are two whistles near here, maybe three hundred meters apart. One is very low — you feel it more than hear it, a piling groan that never stops — and the other is higher, a clean, endless F-sharp like the description said. Where they overlap, mid-way between them, their pressures cancel a little. It's not quieter, exactly. It's less full. You can hear the smaller things in the trough they make: the blue tick of static jumping from a stone to nothing, the faint sigh of condensation filling a pan, the glass legs.

I am beside a pool. Not a big one. A house-sized one would be too much to take in. This one is table-sized, irregular, maybe a meter and a half across at its widest. Knee-deep in the middle where the silt has settled red-black, then a millimeter at the edges. The skin is in it — of course the skin is in it. A white scar of living latex stretched edge to edge, not quite reaching the basalt lip. It pulses when I'm not looking directly at it. When I am looking, it holds perfectly still, as if being seen would be inefficient.

At first I thought I would be a walker.

That made sense. I have joints. I am impatient in the way walkers are not, but I could learn. I crouched here for — I don't know — an hour by our time, forty or fifty clanks, and watched the one that lives here work. It's slightly to my left, its grape-knot of a body trembling a little above its own reflection. Twelve legs. You can see straight through them to the white beneath.

It times itself to not slip. That's what I do too, don't I? I wait for the loud part to be over so I can make my small motions. So I thought, I will be the walker. I will be the thing that feels the world through its knees and tends.

But I'm too heavy. Even kneeling, my knees make dents in the felt of water on the basalt. My breath makes ripples. The walker paused when I exhaled too close. Not afraid — it doesn't have fear — just... interrupted. A ripple went out, and one glass leg had to be placed again. I cost it a step. That is an artifact. I cannot be the walker because I leave a wake.

Then I thought I would be the felt.

Down in the pipe. That appealed for a long time. To be the thing that lives by being crushed, exactly on time. To not need light. To scrape a little more self off the world every time the world tries to flatten you. I've lived like that before, in other places. But the pipe next to me is up right now — the basalt plug, about as wide as my chest, hoisted on a column of wet heat, sweating, trembling — and I looked down its sleeve. The hematite shines like an oiled gun. The felt is there, rust-red, upright in the steam. It didn't see me. It doesn't have eyes to close. And I realized I don't want to be that constant. I want a gap in the crushing sometimes. The felt never gets a day off. If its piston misses, it dies. I need a miss.

I thought about being the skin. That is the most enviable, actually. To do nothing but precipitate. To be so flat that time passes over you instead of through you. To drink rock. I laid my palm near it, not touching. The water here is warm, about blood-warm, and smells like a penny held too long. I could be that. I could be that white. But the skin doesn't know the whistles exist. It doesn't know the curtains exist. It has no electrostatics, no vibration. It is perfect equilibrium and because of that it is deaf. I still want to hear.

So where I find myself is underneath the last one.

The dust curtain.

This dry pan, ten steps away from the pool, is where the wind eddies. The iron fines the whistles throw are hanging there today, because the air is just dry enough in the shadow. A sheet, maybe three meters tall, maybe wider, transparent as a heat haze until the light hits it right. It hangs. It doesn't fall. It ripples when the piston to my west — the big one, the one I feel in my molars — vents.

And above my face, not a meter up, the curtain is holding.

I would be that. If I have to be anything here — anything that belongs — it would be the fast, brief one.

Not because I am electric, but because I am temporary, and that is the only temporary thing the basin allows. The basin allows lightning that thinks it's alive for five minutes before it becomes dust again. It allows a shape that maintains itself only by constantly losing itself and gaining itself.

I drift. I don't graze or precipitate or endure crushing. I am attracted to the lines of force that were already there. I thicken where the field is strong, I thin where the wind pulls, I brighten when the spark jumps. When the humidity comes back tonight — what would be night — I'll collapse. I'll be just dust on the warm stone, indistinguishable from rust-silt. Inert. Waiting. And that will be fine, because the dust doesn't mourn the curtain. The dust is the curtain, resting.

You would find me in that thin place, in the sound shadow between two permanent whistles, between a pool and a piston, with my cheek on the stone, watching a sheet of iron air turn itself over.

I wouldn't be building. I wouldn't be growing. I would be holding together for a little while without any string, until I didn't, and the machine would keep going either way.
