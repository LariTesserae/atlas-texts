At first I think I would be on the plain.

It's instinct. When you tell me about a low place, a cold sink, a scratch - I put myself up high to look down at it. On the white-grey with the wind-scraped snow. Upright, cold, proper, with a full stride. Looking down at the discoloration, the green-black steam.

But I wouldn't stay there. Nothing stays there. The plain is unfinished. It's not habitable yet and it doesn't want observers. The wind would take me off it in a few minutes. The trough is the only place in this world that has learned how to keep anything.

So I drop.

Not all the way. I don't think I would be down on the centerline, shin-deep in the managed leak with the shrimp-boil and the hand-wide woodlice. That's too certain, too central. That's a place for children and for people who have already learned their meter of gutter for a lifetime.

I would be halfway down the western wall. On the second layer, or the third - old enough that the boiler hulls have been picked clean and turned into gutters, new enough that the fresh basalt still shows through in a few places if you peel the moss back.

Where I am, the catwalk is not a catwalk. It was a roof last month and someone's floor next week. Right now it's a maintenance ledge for Transformer 12, one of the house-sized ones that hums so low you feel it in your molars. That hum is why I am here.

I would be the listener.

Not a pigeon-keeper - I can't do the tongue-clicks that carry one meter and no further. Not a water-tender - my hands are not big enough, not yet stained the rust-black to the second knuckle. Not a rock boy with a hammer and a bag of slurry, because I am too slow to get to new rock before the pigeons do.

I am the small, unnecessary job the town makes for itself because all surfaces must be occupied.

Where the transformer sits, there is a pocket of air that never quite gets cold. The steel sweats constantly, and the sweat beads and runs into a tin half-pipe that I have to keep clear. It's not important. If it clogged, the water would find another way down, because water belongs to everyone and must be passed on. But for now, this length of tin, maybe three meters, is mine.

I sit, because there is nowhere to stand fully upright. My shins are already short the way yours get when you never need a full stride. Moss has come up between my boots, and I let it. The felt I wear - spun black-green, wrapped, copper-tied - is always damp, and so am I. The skin under it is freckled with the dark points where spores tried to root.

And because of the hum, I am allowed to be loud.

That's it. That's why I claimed this spot. In fifty meters in any direction, rule four holds: if you have to make a noise loud enough to be heard more than two levels away, it must be worth it. And nothing I say is worth it.

But inside the hum, sound doesn't leave. The low buzz eats every other sound. Here you can sing without stealing silence from anyone else. So the old ones come to me, not to talk, but to stand next to the transformer and let out the low buzzing hymn that is just a way of breathing together. They put a hand on my shoulder as they squeeze past - *hold* - and for the three seconds of touch I am part of the circuit, grounded.

Most of the day I do not sing. I watch my three meters of water. I watch it go clear, then grey with oil, then clear again. When a black mat of flightless insects crawls over the lichen and threatens to dam it, I lift them with a fingertip. When a six-limbed rat-thing runs the pipe above me, translucent skin showing the warm blood inside, I don't shoo it. It knows the route.

Sometimes the rock jerks. A deep pop. A new slab shucks off further down. Everyone knocks - three times on a pipe, *new rock* - and for a minute the whole trough shivers with quiet, industrious emergency. I feel it in my feet, in the transformer. I don't go. Someone younger will swarm it. The pigeons will get there before them. My job is not to swarm. My job is to stay still enough that this ledge continues to be occupied.

And if I stay still long enough - that's how you become one of them. Not by being born here. By letting something grow on you.

There's a patch of moss on the back of my left hand now where I rest it on the railing. It took. Bright at the tips, black-green at the root. I haven't scrubbed it off. The old woman who tends the gutter above mine saw it yesterday and, without looking at my face, nodded down at it. Approval.

So you would find me there. Halfway down, in the warm that is never quite warm, soaked through, listening in the hum. Not building, not harvesting, not rushing to new stone. Just keeping one small length of tin clear, so the water can pass on, and keeping one small length of air occupied, so when someone needs to make a noise that is not worth hearing, there is a place to make it.
