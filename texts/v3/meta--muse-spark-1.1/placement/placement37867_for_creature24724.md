At first I think I'd be at the rim.

Not down inside, but right on that raw lip where the grass hangs in the air with its roots still balled, drying into fists. I'd be the one who keeps looking out at the plain because I remember it. The lookout. The almost-leaver. But that job doesn't exist down there. The rim is not a vantage. It's just weather coming in. The grass doesn't want tending and the stone flakes under your palm like soot. You can't live on an edge that is still falling. So no.

Then I think maybe I'd be the falcon. The one that drops off the plain once a month, makes one clean, stupid, beautiful circle in the column of hot air because it forgot. It circles once and understands there's nowhere to stoop and leaves again. I like that honest mistake. But I wouldn't stay. And the rule of the pit is there is no outside, so I wouldn't be from outside for long.

I go down.

You go down not by stairs but by negotiation. Hand to wet rebar, foot to a board that is also someone's roof, duck under a banana leaf big as a door that slaps you wet across the cheek. The light goes from hammering white to that deep, green dusk. The air stops moving unless you coax it. Somewhere on the fourth tier a bulb burns yellow and bare and moths of condensation glitter past it.

I thought I'd be higher, where the old live and the light touches longest. Or in the middle, where it's busiest, where everything touches everything else and a ball never hits bottom. But middle is too fast for me. In this place fast means a loom clacking once and waiting. But still — too many shoulders, too many kitchens that are wasp nests hanging off scaffold, too much need to touch every passing body to say *I'm behind you, move left*. I'd get the signals wrong. I'd be in the way.

So I keep going down.

The heat gets a weight. The iron smell gets stronger. The filament bulbs thin out. The vines down here aren't decorative, they are structural. The breadfruit trees have grown around the main shaft that turns at two revolutions a minute on a geothermal sigh, and you can feel that sigh in your molars. Down here feet on wet board is the loudest thing. And the drip. Always the drip.

This is where I'd find myself. Not quite at the bottom - you never quite see the bottom - but in the last third, where the basalt comes back dark and wet and too hot to sit on, where the groundwater finally breaks out of sheared pockets and hisses.

I would be the one who tends the mat.

The black-red thing that is not quite plant and not quite fungus, slick, that pulses faintly when the main engine turns. The one they seed on the newest walls to glue the flaking stone with its root-hairs. The one that softens rock if you ask it right.

I would be short by permission, broad in the back and thigh from holding the weight of water and my own weight on ladders that are hallways. My head shaved not for style but for cool, and a thin, deliberate moss kept on my scalp that I water in the evenings. My knuckles would have that permanent bloom of grease in the creases that never washes out. My hands would be stained black-red up to the wrist.

My work would be the slowest work in a city of slow work. Slower than the bore-drill that's been chewing the same meter since spring.

In the morning - which down here is not morning but just the hour when the column of light far above goes white and you can read the sweat on your forearm - I would go with a tin bucket of collected night. Not water poured, but coaxed. I'd sprinkle it. I'd check the sheen. The mat doesn't like shouting and there is no shouting down here anyway. The language it understands is in the other tongue they speak: touch, breath, leaving alone.

I'd palmtouch a new patch: is it taking? Is it binding the sharp rock? I'd whisper to it the way the woman up in the loom-house whispers to the flowering vine that filters better than mesh. And in the afternoon, when someone from a knot three tiers up wants another half meter for a hammock, or to straighten a walkway that has sagged into a gutter, I wouldn't blast. We don't blast. I'd invite the mat to come closer. Come soften this. Take a winter to chew it. Then we'd scrape it away together with shovels, my shoulders and the shoulders of the mat, and wash.

They would wear the white lizards up above like jewelry. Down here the lizards come sleep in the mat's warmth, in the engine grease. They ride on my shoulders, flicking for gnats in the barrel towers. That would be my animal kingdom. Lizard, gnat, and me.

I would know the shape of the heat on my skin like a map. I know which five minutes the reflected light will find my tier and make the condensation glitter like a road. I know how a single drop travels from a lip fern fifty meters above — leaf, pipe, helmet, shoulder, barrel, my bucket, my mat, soil.

And at the end, when the fourteen-day crib near the center shaft has turned someone into soil, they'd bring me a sack of them. And I'd put that sack into the new lip we'd grown out of softened rock and mat, and pot a new banana daughter in it. Because there is no outside, so how could death be outside.

I'm not the person who keeps the city running. I'm the person the city leans on when it wants more city.

Low and slow, where the stone is still learning to be smooth.
