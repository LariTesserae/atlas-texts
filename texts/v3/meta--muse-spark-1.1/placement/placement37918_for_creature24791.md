At first I think I'd be one of them. Of course I do.

If you put a person down on that plain and tell them there are giants here, walking foundries the size of ore freighters that make their own weather, every human ego wants to be the giant. To be the thing other things mistake for a hill. To be the furnace that walks.

But I'm not built like that. I don't have the economy for it.

I tried it on in my head while you were describing them — putting my feet down a house-wide at a time, feeling the *phaff* of steam turn to snow around my ankles, feeling that violet St. Elmo's fire crawl across my back without burning. And I couldn't sustain it. It's too lonely. Their spacing isn't a choice, it's physics. Two cannot occupy the same pressure trough. They don't have a herd because they can't afford the anxiety a herd requires. That patience that isn't patience — just low idle — I don't have that yet. My furnace would sputter out trying to wait that long.

So not up on the ridge.

You'd find me down low. You have to look low here. Everything important happens in the interstice, in the lee side.

At first I thought: in the potholes. Those lake-sized bowls polished by ice that left a million years ago that now hold their own weather. I'd be a little weather-system priest living in one. That was close.

Then I thought: no, in the frost lens behind a single boulder. That permanent eye of ice that thickens and thins but never leaves. That was closer.

But if I'm honest about the kind of animal I am — the kind that reads about a world like this and immediately starts listening for where the warmth leaks — you wouldn't find me *on* the plain at all. You'd find me *after* the plain has been passed over.

In the berm.

Behind a giant, half a mile back, where it laid down that long, low ridge of slag still orange at its heart. It's hissing. The air above it is alive and it shouldn't be — in a place where air never moves except when a foot comes down. Steam is coming off it in a straight line for hundreds of meters, like a road made of breath. It will steam for an afternoon, then go cold and crack like a rifle, then hold a ghost of heat for years.

You'd find me right there, in the first hour, while it's still too hot to touch.

I wouldn't be big. I think if I were anything here I'd be one of the white flea-things, but bigger than they are in your description. Or one of the furred moths, but with a memory. Something in between. Something with hands not for building — building never occurred here — but for tending.

I am on my knees on the cooling glass. The slag is black and contains a little universe of frozen bubbles. It ticks as it contracts. Around me the flour-snow the giant breathed out is falling sideways and melting the instant it lands on the warm line. For a few meters on either side of me, the plain is wet. Truly wet. Not damp. Wet. The rocks sweat. My coat beads. The microbes you described strain up to touch it.

My job — because in a world where industry means maintenance of an interior temperature against an infinite sink, everything has a job even if it has no product — is to stay in that wet.

I scoop the white flea-things up when the steam gets too hot for them and move them a foot over. I pick up the moths that fell mid-air, their fur iced, and hold them against the warm glass until their heat-drinking palps start to flex again and they can ride the updraft and cook themselves again. I am not farming. I am not building. I am preventing the city of ice crystals from forming inside anything smaller than me.

I talk, too, though the thin air doesn't carry it. Or maybe I don't talk. Maybe I put my ear to the slag the way you said to put your ear to the stone in the valley, and I feel them coming through basalt. Left. Right. Left. The quietest industry in the world, walking.

I'm not ahead of it, scouting. I'm not on top of it, riding. I'm not even watching it go. I'm living in the warm road that will never lead anywhere, that is only warm because something vast metabolized a mountain five miles back and needed to set the waste down.

That is where you'd find me. Three days after a giant has passed, when the river it exhaled has already vanished except for a dark stain and a rosary of ice beads. I'm still there, beside a berm that is no longer steaming but still warm enough that lichen will find it first. The plain around me has gone back to grey-rock, iron-red rock, white frost, yellow lichen. Oyster-shell sky.

I'm not cold. For a little while longer, I'm not cold.
