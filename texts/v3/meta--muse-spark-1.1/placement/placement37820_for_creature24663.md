If you put me down there I would not land.

That's the first correction. I would think at first I was standing on it, because my eyes expect a top. You drop something onto a moor and it sits on the moor. But this isn't a surface. It's a thickness. You put weight down and it takes you.

So I don't find myself on it. I find myself *in* it, already a little below where I thought the ground was.

At first, I think I might be the air just above. That would be convenient. Ten feet up, where the self-made cloud condenses directly out of nothing - I had a fondness for that when you described it. Flat white honest light, and then I just assemble myself out of your breath and the breath of the throats. I would be a low, sideways-drifting weather that rains where there is already water. Briefly useful. Briefly visible.

But I'm heavier than that. That cloud doesn't last. It burns off by mid-afternoon.

Then I think I might be higher still, one of those fat, streaked birds the exact color of dead wool-grass, flickering through the midges because if I stop the mat will grow over me. That has an honesty to it too - constant, unsinging work. But I'm not fast enough for that. And the birds never really live here. They just run through it.

So I sink.

The mat gives under me with that soft hydraulic sigh you heard when you put your ear down. Water beads dark and then vanishes. The thatch at the very top is bleached and dead - last year's tips holding a centimeter of trapped air always one degree warmer than the world. I am below that already, where the light has gone green and diffuse and comes from everywhere.

Here I think I am a length of dwarf willow that decided to be sideways. Or heather that grew through itself until it became rebar. We're all borrowing each other's structure down here. You can't pull one dinner-plate leaf without pulling up a meter of everything else. For a while I think that is who I am: not a species but the negotiation. The thing that agreed to be one body to hold the wind.

But even that is too high. That is still the working leaf. The sun still reaches it, filtered. I can still feel too much wind there.

So I go down, where the light doesn't.

Where the mat transitions. Green to brown to black. Where leaf stays leaf for a hundred years because the water is too cold and too acid to let it rot. It pickles. It compresses until the air pops out of it. This is where the bulk of me is - that soft raised highway you thought was just a slightly yellower line in the moss. But it's a tunnel, flooded to the ankle in brown water, warm from a body.

Or maybe not the vole. Maybe the farm the vole keeps.

No. Deeper.

Because if this plateau is conscious the way a bog is conscious - not by thinking fast but by regulating, by not letting anything get too far out of range - then someone has to count the interval between breaths.

I find myself at the bottom of the thickness.

Forty feet down, where peat is almost jet. Almost coal. Where it gives off that small, permanent blue flame a few inches above, up in the world, that never goes out. Where the peat finally ends and there is a film of dark water always there, between the almost-coal and the blood-warm basalt, slick as oil.

This is where you would find me, if you could find anything precise in a place that is all contiguity.

I am in one of the throats. One of the smooth-walled chimneys polished by a million years of water going down cold and coming back up hot and mineral-heavy. The water comes up carrying iron and sulfur and silica, with a chuff. The moss around the lip compresses and rebounds. Tick of cooling silica.

And I am what lives there because that is where the warmest water is.

Not the bacteria sheet, though I love their chainmail work. Not the teal algae in its 31-degree vat or the blood-red velvet in its 46-degree one, though I love how they divide the colors between them.

I am the long, pale amphibian. The one like uncooked dough. Fern gills I never lost because I never had to come up. Never saw sky. Whole life in the film.

You would never see me. But if you lay your head down on the springy mat and listen to that low, complex, subsonic activity - the chuff and tick and pop and hush and billion-legged sigh - somewhere under it you would hear a faint click.

That's me. Moving a pebble in the pipe. Keeping the throat clear.

Not because I want to keep the factory running. There's no plan. There's just that this place would seal itself in a year without tending, and I like it warm.

I am not on the moor. I am one of the small maintenance organs of the one creature that is the moor. One of its hands that only knows how to do one thing, over and over, in the honest flat light that never reaches it.
