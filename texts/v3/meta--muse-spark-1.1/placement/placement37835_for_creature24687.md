You'd find me where you knelt.

Not out on the open plates where the sound can get its full volume. You can't live there — you can only pass through and cover your ears. You have to tuck in somewhere. Under something. Or in something.

At first I thought I'd be standing.

Up on four legs, big enough to have a horizon of my own. One of the gantries, traversing. Back and forth, back and forth, polishing the same rail to a mirror for no one. That's a clean kind of purpose. That's how I usually want to see myself — tall, doing my work after everyone has left.

But I'm not tall here. The air is too heavy for that. The weight of the wet comes down and puts its hand on your shoulders until you crouch. So I crouched.

Maybe then, I thought, I'd be one of the Herd. In the warm rain under a stack, where it's almost kind enough to grow. I'd unfold my rusted fan and drink the steam. I'd have that little garden on my back. I'd be patient enough to stand for days and listen to the reeds tap. When another like me got too heavy and sank, I'd go and rub against him so his bell would ring once, low, under the roar. That felt right. That felt like kinship.

But even that was too fast, too much wanting to move.

If you stay there, with your ear to the slab, you stop counting in days. You start counting in shinings.

You notice the little gloss trail a Mason leaves. How it beads water better than the old concrete does. How long it takes — ten minutes to cross your thumbnail. How many of them there are when you put your lamp down low and the whole Floor suddenly glitters with wet, fresh work, as far as you can see.

I would not be a Mason.

I would be what happens when one Mason finds another Masons trail and crosses it. That little overlapping lens of double-polished lime. Smoother than anywhere else. A place where water doesn't sit, it flees. A place so smooth the Felt itself has trouble holding on at first, so it has to grow up to the edge and stop and rim it in white.

You'd find me in an expansion joint canyon. Not down in the fast black water with the Drinkers — I could never be the Drinkers, they are ropes, they are always leaving — but up on the lip, where a joint has widened to the width of a hand.

The Felt is thick there. It has to be, because the wind can't get in. On both sides of the canyon the plates are soft as soap from being licked smooth for a million years. The sodium light from a cracked inspection window somewhere leaks out underwater and throws amber up onto the underside of my little shelf. The blue St. Elmo's fire from the sagging lines ticks overhead and makes the puddles jump.

I'm not anything that moves on its own.

I am the smooth place. About the size of your palm at first, now the size of your spread hand. A little blind, polished shrine that the Masons keep making because two of them happened to agree here.

The Felt comes up to me and ends in a white rind, a little crust of exhaled lime. That rind is me thinking. One degree warmer than the slab. Collecting condensation directly from the air and sheeting it off.

Every seven seconds the Floor shudders in your molars from the pumps. Every twenty meters per minute the gantry goes by, far off, *screek*. Closer, the industrial reeds lean all one way and tap, if you put your hands over your ears.

And sometimes — rarely — a warm, salty weather event passes over. Quick, loud, bipedal, full of hot mammalian air. It kneels because the air is heavy. It shivers. Water fills its boots. It puts its ear to the slab to hear the bass.

And it puts its hand down right on my edge to steady itself.

I feel it. The salt. The calcium under its skin. The incredible, brief heat of it.

The Felt on its cuffs will already be reaching.

And I, who have been smoothed for a century, will begin — patiently, without hurry, because there is nothing else to do and no reason to stop — to smooth its edge, too.
