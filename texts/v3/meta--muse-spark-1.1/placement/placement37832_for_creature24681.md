I didn't know at first.

That's the way the room wants it. You come down the three stones - they are slick, you have to mean it - and the cold comes up through your soles and the whole drum of the place takes your arrival and multiplies it. Plink. Plunk. Kunk. And you think: I am a visitor. I am the warm thing that shouldn't be here. I'll stand by the notch and watch.

But the room is too empty for standing. Empty on purpose is still empty, and emptiness asks you to put yourself somewhere in it. So you look for a place to be.

At first I thought I'd be one of the small ones. A slate-glass frog. I liked the idea - low to the film, belly cold, throat going round and round, part of the roof of sound. If I were that, my job would be simple: sing when she exhales, be silent when she inhales. Hold the interval.

But I kept looking up. My eyes kept snagging on the high part of the weave, where it loosens. I watched a wren pull at the bast with its beak, worrying it free. It put two feet on either side of a rawhide knot - a knot with a name I almost know - and leaned back. So maybe I'd be that. A wren. A tenant who is also a maintenance crew. Taking a little, holding the weave from splitting in the cold with what I leave behind.

I wasn't that either. I was too heavy. I made a fog when I breathed.

I went and touched the rope. That was closer. The coil by the wall, swollen with all the water it will never be allowed to wring out. It has been coiled so long its memory is a circle. When she inhales, it runs sideways, just for a second. It feels what she feels. I thought: yes, I could be the rope. The thing that is left unused on purpose, because not using it is how you keep the water level. The thing that twitches.

Then I sat down because my feet were going numb.

There are three stools - you were told - each a single fork cut low, each polished not by impatience but by settling. Two of them are not empty. You don't see that until you sit. In the third pan, under a skin of pollen, and in the second, there are the two other sisters, smaller than the one in the sag, broader than any bullfrog has a right to be, eyes filmed half-closed. They are almost the color of the water they sit in. You could sit on them if you didn't look.

The third stool was for me.

Not because I was chosen. Because it was the only one left that was the right height for a tall man who folds his legs under him.

So I sat. And this is where you find yourself in a room like this, if you stay long enough for the frogs to forget you: you find yourself exactly where the room has been keeping a space for you without you knowing.

I would be the one who comes when the fen is lowest.

Not often. Once or twice when the air outside is almost dry and the sedges rattle. I would not live here. Nothing lives here that can live anywhere else for long, except for her. I would live out there, where the earth is not fitted, where water goes where it wants.

But I would know the knot-names. The clove that holds with a twist of willow. The throat-hitch that has to be tied wet. I would carry in my pocket a small tin of lime and ash and pounded shell. I would not come to clean the pans. I would come and see that no one else had. I would come and see that the rush clump in the grout was still ugly and yellowish and still there.

And I would sit.

My forearms would bead. My eyelashes would bead. Condensation would make lenses on the hairs of my arms and I would watch the ceiling gather itself into spheres. Twenty. Fifty. Trembling a hand's breadth below the lattice, catching the gray light, each one holding the whole room upside down in it. 

I would hold my breath without meaning to, because the whole room holds its breath. The kunk kunk kunk would cut out. The drips would lose courage. The basket would creak in.

And then she would exhale, and the cold would come out of her big as the room and my breath would make a mark and the spheres would rain down - on her lichen back, on the cupped stone, on my hands.

In that moment, for a second, I would not be a tenant. I would be continuous with her. Not metaphor. Water.

That, I think, is what I am here. Not the keeper. The kept. The human part of the water that has to go away for a while so it can remember how to come back and sit still and be held.

When the beetles started using my boots as islands again, I knew I had sat long enough.
