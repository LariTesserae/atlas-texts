I don't think I would find myself inside it.

Not at first.

At first I think I would — because everyone, reading that, wants to be the one who squeezes through the slot and kneels and sees it breathe. But if I were really there, in that city at the bottom of the bowl where the air settles, I wouldn't go in. Not often.

I know that kind of room. We have them even here.

I would find myself in one of the ten houses whose backs make it. Not the ones that lean closest — those walls are too damp to sleep against, and everything you own would taste like that green, wet-rock smell. I would be in the third house back, the one where the timber has gone silver and hard as bone from a hundred winters of being patched. The one where the wall sweats in only one corner.

I would be where the sound goes to die, but just on the other side of it.

For a long time I thought I would be the wren. Because it's the only fast thing in there, the only thing allowed to be quick, and even it has learned to be quiet about it. I liked that. I move fast in my head. But wrens don't stay. They hop down, they groom the Minder, they leave. They are visitors with a job. I don't think I would be a visitor.

Or I thought I would be one of the voles, round as a potato, fur tipped green from sleeping pressed against the moss. That kind of belonging where you don't decide to belong, you just get your color from the thing you sleep against. That appealed to me for a minute.

Then I thought — maybe I'm the water. The bead at eye level on the beam that has been hanging there since before you were born. The one you're told not to disturb. Something held, not by tension, but by agreement. Something whose whole work is to not fall. To be an event that refuses to happen. I know how to do that.

But if I'm honest, that's still wanting to be special. Wanting to be the eye, or the eyelid.

Let what emerges become more itself.

I'm not in the pocket. I'm against it. I am one of the keepers of inventory.

In that kind of city you can't own anything alone, not really. You keep things where the cold will keep them. You keep your good fish in a bark box on a shelf that isn't yours, in a room that isn't anyone's, because the room is better at keeping than you are. And someone has to remember which box is whose. Not guard it. Nothing is stolen there — winter steals louder than people do. Someone just has to know the pattern. To see that a box has been tipped, the salt licked out by stone. To see that the moss has finally taken last year's lichen bundle and it's time to put that one in the soup.

I would be the one who leaves things there. And the one who checks.

I'd be maybe sixty. Maybe nineteen. In that city age folds in the same way as wood. I'd have a hood that smells of smoke. Hands gone cracked from adzing. My back would be to the pocket even when I'm inside my own house. In winter, when the snow dust falls straight down and the smoke goes straight up, I would feel the wall behind my bedding weep a little warmer than the other walls. One degree. Two degrees. Stored-summer warm. And I'd know she's breathing on the other side.

I wouldn't talk to her. The ten households don't have a word that stays put for her. Minder is what you call her when you're writing about her for someone outside. Inside, you would just call her the damp pocket. You would say, "Don't leave good iron in there," and that's your prayer.

I would be the one who, without making a decision about it, puts my palm flat on that sweating corner of my own wall in late February, when the dust-snow has been falling for so long you think it will never stop. Just to borrow back a little of that kept heat. My palm would come away damp, smelling of green trying.

And once in a while, when the light gets tall enough to fall straight down the slit — that bright, washed-out, painful-bright noon light that bounces off the lime — I would turn sideways and slip into the slot. Not to look. I already know what's in there. To leave something. A fresh box. Dried whitefish, lid ajar so the cold can get in. Or to take something that is now needed. Moving very slowly, so I don't disturb the moths walking from hummock to hummock.

And she would know I was there because my breath would make the hanging droplet shiver. The one at eye level. The one she has been holding.

I think, if I am anything alive in there, I am that faint tug you feel at your sleeve when you're backing out.

Not a hand. Just the pale runner of that waxy vine that finally, after a decade of reaching, reached the ledge. Brushing you as it goes for the light. Not pulling you in, not pushing you out. Just noting you as it passes. Saying: noted. Stored. You came. You left. It was kept.
