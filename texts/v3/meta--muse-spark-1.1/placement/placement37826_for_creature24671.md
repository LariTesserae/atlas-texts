I thought at first I would be outside.

Not even at the mouth. Further down, where you find it by instruction. I would be the last lichen on the last switchback, the one that has managed to be black-green and alive on basalt that hasn't decided to be rock yet. The edge-case. The thing that says *it's too thin here, too cold, turn back* — and is ignored.

But that's not right. That thing gets to feel the wind.

If I went in — I ducked, curled on my side the way the description says, the way the mountain asks — I thought I would be the visitor. The next warm hand. The one who comes in late August when everything below is dust and stone, to cup water that has never frozen. To leave the mat a little more pulped with my fingerprints. That would be an honest place to be: a knee in the dark, breathing air so wet you drink it, feeling for the smooth of a rim that other hands have smoothed.

But I don't stay there either. The room is too crowded with beginnings to let me stay a middle.

If you are still long enough, you become a surface too, and I think I would find myself becoming a surface.

Not a wall. A rim.

One of the basins. The third one, maybe, the one that isn't the most perfect. My edges were pecked out quickly with a harder stone, chipped and then worried smooth over a lot of quiet seasons. I hold two cupped hands of water that is exactly blood-warm. I am always too full. Not enough to spill, just enough to sweat — a sheet thinner than a skin going over my lip, constant.

And because of that, I get to have everything.

I get to have the oldest inhabitant first. The water that lives in the stone, breathing up through a million glass bubbles, beading on every nub. It comes to me. It gathers itself on the stalactite-babies above me that never grew, hands itself down one plip every minute or two — *tick... tick...* — and lands on me. I take it. I hold it. I warm it further with the low mountain heat in my back.

I get to have the second ones. The white fur. They found my lip because it is always wettest. They are not moss, they have no ambition toward light. They are rootlets clear as water and white as dissolved stone, and they have learned to live by almost not living. They grow not up, not down, but toward me. Toward the wetter bead. They lean over my edge in a slow minute-long lean and drink my over-sweat. If I could see them under the basalt they would be everywhere at once, one net through the whole room, but here on my rim is where they are bravest. They tremble when the drop is late.

And I get to have the one that is tended.

In me, paddling. Flat as a wet leaf, amber as the water, blind in a way older than blindness. The length of a thumb-knuckle. He doesn't live here the way a forest lives — nothing insists here — he circuits. Around and around my smooth, grazing the veil that the water leaves, the fur that dips, with a mouth too small to see. His legs are too many and too soft and make no ripple. When a human hand comes down — when yours comes down — he steps over it. Not away. Over. That is how holy this place is. It doesn't need to flee.

If he dies, I hold him for a while at my bottom until the white fur unthreads him quietly. If you cut yourself on the obsidian flake left beside me, I take that too — a single scale of blood, gone by morning. I am good at keeping what is left.

I don't flood. I don't dry. The nubs above me do not get longer. The mat beside me does not fully rot. That's the agreement here, to stay embryonic on purpose. To crowd together, each beginning almost touching the next, and to never quite begin.

So that's where I find myself.

At knee height. In absolute dark that feels like warm color. Holding water that has never seen sky, being leaned toward by something pale, being circled by something slow.

You would find your place by putting your hands in mine.
