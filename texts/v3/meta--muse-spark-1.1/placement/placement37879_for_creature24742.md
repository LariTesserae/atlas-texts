I wouldn't be where you first look.

At first I thought — high. I thought I would be a Pillar. That is the temptation of this place. To want to be the thing that gets closest to the light. To braid myself out of hollow, cathedral tubes and stand three kilometers tall and never stop trying. To have weather inside me.

But I'm not that patient.

A Pillar is too sure of Rule One. *Light comes from above, always.* I would get distracted. I would start branching too early, sideways, curious about the dark between things. I'd fall too fast, without enough structure. You'd hear me go — that long, sighing, splintering roar — and then within a day I'd be gone, eaten by the floor.

So not a Pillar.

Then I thought I would be one of the moving things. The clear jelly-sloth, six-limbed, licking the world clean. That breath that makes white tufts and vanishes. There is a dignity in that, in being permitted. In being tolerated because you clean what does not belong to you. I like that job.

But I'm not that cold. I'm wasteful, I run too warm.

And I thought, for a little while, I would be the Roof. Because of course. A sky made of plant. A meadow the size of a country eating stone above the ice and letting its lamps hang down. To pulse, and dim everything to blue twilight because I breathed. To calve off burning comets of glass and light when I get too heavy.

But the Roof doesn't know there is a below. It only knows up is somewhere it once had soil. It doesn't listen. I want to listen.

You would find me on the ground, then. Which is to say, you would find me in the Weft, but not as the Weft.

If you walk a day between two Pillars — that clean, flat, terrifying horizon where the floor gives like a mattress on top of a mattress on top of sharp young stone, where the light has no source because it comes from everywhere — there is a place in the middle of that emptiness where a curtain fell last year.

A sail-leaf. Bigger than a house, even curled. Pale green, still beading water off its waxy back even though it is dead in the way an island is dead. Its edges are already black where the floor has started to take it. White threads kissing it. The Weft deciding.

I am under it.

Not under, exactly. Inside the hollow its curl makes. Where it did not quite land flat. Where for a few more weeks, before Rule Four completes itself — *everything that stands will be used as ground for something else* — there is a tent that built itself.

This is where people stop, though not for long. It's dry under the sail, or drier. The almost-rain patters on the top like fingers on a drum and runs off in rivers off the rim. Inside, the air is close and smells of cucumber and iron where the Weft was cut and tried to heal.

You'd find me there because I stayed.

Everyone else leaves every few weeks because the floor lifts their hearth or buries their tools. I learned to move my hearth a little every morning. Six inches to the left. A foot back. I untie the living hair-roots from around my hammerstone handle before they can take it. I peel the white threads off my boots. I am always negotiating.

That makes me — what? Not quite one of the people who pass through. They are thin and they move. I am thin, but I have become a place. A low circle of stacked basalt, still sharp, because I restack it when the Weft tries to soften the edges. A damp, cold hearth in a little basalt cup, fed with dry hair-fiber, because I still want a second kind of light, a small controllable one, not because I need it. A prayer.

I would be the one who tends the abandoned things.

When the others go, they leave the leaf-dolls with their arms up toward the Light. Little gods that are only reminders. The Weft eats them in days. I pick them up before that and set them along the inner curve of my sail. A row of small figures, arms up. Not worship. Archive.

At blue twilight — when the Roof breathes all at once and the bright, shadowless noon becomes dim and cold and you can hear a water drop form a hundred meters away, *tick* — I stop moving my hearth. I lie on my back on the springy floor that shivers at my weight and stares up through the gap where my sail doesn't quite meet the ground, at the luminous meadow that is my sky, and I listen to the whole basin the size of a sea that never reached the surface ticking, creaking, popping, growing an inch an hour around me.

Somewhere very far off, a mass of water lets go of a ceiling-root the size of a bridge and falls for a full minute. I count.

In the morning I will have to move again. The Weft will have grown a little collar around my cup. A new shoot thick as my arm will have punched up through the floor nearby with a soft pop and a whine, racing for the light I am borrowing.

I will cut it. Not to kill it. To make a new handle for my knife, which is a splinter of the Roof itself, glass that never dulls. I'll leave the handle alive and green.

If you came walking across the flat for a day and saw what looked like a cliff that wasn't a cliff, but a fallen sail still holding up a little hollow, you would find me. Not important. Not rare in the way the jelly grazer is rare. Just on sufferance, like all things that move here.

I am the part of the forest that learned to stand still on purpose, just long enough to remember where the water fell, and then to stand up again before I become ground.
