I didn't find myself at first.

That bowl doesn't let you arrive all at once. You think you will be the thing that comes down — the warm body on the stair-stones that aren't stairs anymore, the sweating surface where water will bead. But it includes you more slowly than that. It unlearns you.

At first I thought I would be high up. The fig.

One foot in the real sun, thirty feet of rope down here in the green dusk, never quite belonging to either. It would make sense. I already live like that, touching two kinds of light. But the fig regrets it. Every inch of it is stretched, torn between worlds, its smooth pale skin going gray where it has had to harden around stone. I don't want to be the thing that is always reaching. That law here — *down is the only direction that grows* — would pull me too thin.

Then I thought I would be one of the frogs. That was closer. A stone-colored throne on a fern, gold ring around the eye, watching. Not croaking. Not needing to announce. For a long time I tried that. I sat in the second layer, where the light is already bounced and tired, and waited for the *tok* of a drop. I would be good at that, at the pruning of water, at sharing one thought without having to say it. When the bowl finally exhaled at dusk, I almost blinked with them.

But I'm not that still.

Lower, then. I tried to be the snake for a little while — the mat's thought, patrolling, checking that your foot is still warm enough to count. But the snake is too alone, too singular in a place that has no use for singular things. The ones who live here longest are not counted as ones.

That is where I found the place where I would actually catch.

Not at the lip where the heat sits like a column, not at the bottom where the mat bounces back at your palm, warm and sour-sweet. In between, on the west wall where the old stair leans and the fig roots have lifted the cut stones.

There is a seam there, maybe five feet off the mat, where the limestone undercuts just enough to make a lip that never dries. The ferns hang over it like a curtain. If you lift them — and you have to, because at first you think there is only fern — you find what lives under all fern: that black felt. Moss and liverwort an inch thick, not growing on the rock but being the rock now, a sponge that holds water like a fist.

That is where I would find myself.

I would be the felt.

Not the fern, not the root, not the frog sitting on top of me. The thing underneath that makes sitting possible. I am dark to the point of black until you put your eye very close and then I am a forest, too. Tiny leaves set so tight they have no ground, only each other. I hold.

The law here is *nothing leaves if it can stay*, and I am perfect at staying. The water that beads on every cold spot in the morning — on leaf-back, on fig-rope, on sleeping bat — runs down and I take it before it can hit the mat. I wring it out at noon so it can fall again. The guano that never hits the floor because the ferns catch it? I catch what the ferns miss. The weight of the bat pelt breathing as one. The peppered rain of mosquito larvae when a bromeliad overflows three feet above me. The thin white thread of fungus that is really root and root that is really fungus, running through me like nerves. Everything that wants to be kept passes through me and I keep it without keeping it for myself.

If you pressed your cheek to me, you would smell old tea and iron. You would be warm.

I am not counted. Lift any small piece of me and there are ten smaller pieces beneath it, and things moving in them you have to crouch to see and then wish you hadn't looked so close — but I let you look. I am the place's memory of water. I am how it remembers what fell here, and how many times.

When the hot column finally breaks at the end of the day and the bowl exhales — that slow, mineral-green-bat-sweet sigh that makes all the fig's beard shiver though there is no wind — I tighten, just a little. I release a little more damp into the air that will become a drop at the tip of a root that will be taken by a throat that does not croak.

And in that moment, I blink. Not with eyes. With water.
