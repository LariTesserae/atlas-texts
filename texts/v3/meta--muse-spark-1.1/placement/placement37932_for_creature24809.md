At first I think I'd be up high.

It's where my kind of attention would want to go — to the rim, to the big iron flowers. The condensers. One of those slow fans a house wide, rivets weeping rust, petals opening into the cloud-deck when it comes up like a tide every afternoon. Someone has to tend them. Grease that never quite stays grease in this air, clear the sheets of wax-lichen off the bearings before they seal it shut.

I picture myself there because it's the edge of the bowl, where you can see the rule work. On one side, the inside: bowl, soot, fern-light, all the ticking. On the other side, the outside: nothing. Just the long, long backs of the domes falling away into nothing, rounded down until the planet looks soft. A place to be alone and be a watcher.

But I wouldn't last up there. The air is too thin even for barrel chests. You have to move too hard to keep the fans clear, you black at the edges if you hurry. And it's too loud in a different way — the wind over the lip has nothing to break on. And I am not a watcher. I don't circle. I don't inspect.

Then I think I'd be down in the middle, in the deepest cut. Where the streets have become tunnels and the houses have become stomachs. Where the old women have let the roof-moss take their scalps and the walls stay two degrees warmer than the air. That feels right too — the center is where everything that erodes comes to rest. The final archive. That is where you go to be held. 

But I am not old enough to be a wall yet. Not warm in that salt-dripping way. I haven't earned the shape of stopping.

So I find myself, instead, a little upslope of center. Third terrace down from the north-northwest fan, if you count by sound. You know that terrace because the main condensate trunk runs right under it — a thigh-thick iron pipe, lagged in felted moss and old sourgrass rope, sweating all day. You can hear it from anywhere on the terrace if you put your ear to any other pipe, any boiler vent, any oil drum. It carries the whole street's gossip whether it wants to or not.

You would find me in the cut that is my house and my shop. From the street it looks like every other: dark stone brick already soft at the corners, slate roof gone entirely to meadow, twenty centimeters of black-green mattress you could sleep on. The door is never square, I've given up correcting it two inches a year downslope. Inside, the floor is cut on the uphill side every spring and packed on the downhill side, so the room leans backwards.

I am the lathe-tender.

Not really. The lathe tends me. The machine is older than my grandfather — a good Rivett iron bed, built when someone still thought things could be made to stay hard. Now the bed is pitted, the lead screw is more moss-wrap than steel, the tailstock has been replaced once with a knuckle of that succulent rope-vine that grows a meter a day when wet. I oil it every morning with fat I render from the sourgrass that comes up between my bricks. If I don't run it every day, the roof-moss will glove it perfectly overnight and start to eat it in earnest.

Its job used to be making parts for other machines. Now its job is to keep making itself less of a machine and more of an animal. I help it. When a bearing goes soft I don't look for a harder bearing. I wind it in damp felt until it holds water, and water holds motion. When a gear loses its teeth I teach the vine where to grip.

That is what I am. Not a builder. A negotiator.

You would know me if you came close, because you have to come close here. Sound dies two meters from the mouth in the moss and the thin air. Shouting is obscene, and wasteful. So I talk low, in the chest, mostly consonants. My eyes are that washed grey-green, big for the deep-sea light. If you were an outsider you'd think I was squinting. I am not. I have simply never learned to look at distance. The bowl is close enough.

If you wanted to talk to me you would have to put your hand on my forearm, the way we do. Your palm cold, mine damp. We would press our foreheads almost together. My hair is cut very short, because I am still young enough to be vain about moss. The old woman next door — who is half wall — lets it mat. I peel it and make bricks.

And in the afternoons, during the hour when the cloud comes over the rim and does not rain but condenses, when the whole city ticks like a single body dripping, I stop filing.

Everybody stops. The raptor — the tear in the cloud that should not be able to fly, too wide, too silent — comes over the lip doing its inventory. The Memory. You don't look, out of respect, out of practicality; if you look it takes your distance vision and you need that for nothing. But you feel the ticking pause.

I put my ear to the warm trunk pipe under my floor.

And I listen. I can hear old Mara's cough three houses down, worse. I can hear that someone left their collector valve open on the second terrace, wasting. I can hear a long blind thing — one of the pale stone-eaters, translucent as wax — shifting in the soft rock behind my cistern, chewing dust into that perfect clay that will hold my water for another year. I will hum to it later and coax it deeper.

That is the whole street, told through iron. That is my work — not making, but knowing if the city is still alive.

Later, when the cloud drops and the thin, brutal indigo comes down and the stars are too sharp and the electric yellows come on in windows that aren't square, my whole terrace glitters. Every leaf, every pipe, every rail. Including my lathe-animal, sweating oil and water.

It will freeze a little tonight. Then in the morning it will all soften and give, and drip again.

I will still be here, in the cut, negotiating us both forward one more season.
