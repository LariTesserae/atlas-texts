You wouldn't find me right away.

At first I thought I would be up high, on the weather edge. Where the polymer nets are strung. I love that sound in your city — that hundred thousand tink, tink, like someone typing on glass. I thought I would be one of the net-walkers, the ones who go out at 4am with the hooked poles when the cloud hits the mountain like a wet dog shaking itself. But I'm not patient enough for that kind of harvest. I fidget. I'd tear the mesh.

Then I thought I'd be down low, with the Midwives. Where the rock is still soft enough to take a fingerprint. I love the idea of sitting for a whole shift watching rebar get swallowed. But my knees are bad. Even in this imagined body — tall, thin, red-dyed — my knees are bad for crouching on warm stone that breathes.

So keep looking.

Go down three layers from the nets. Past the cisterns where water is family. Past the dye works where the air is orange with felt-spores and the fat white pillbugs click in buckets. Past the floor where the chain carries ore at a walking pace — clank..... clank — and nobody watches it because if you watch it you'll try to hurry it.

Go to where it's too hot and too low for laundry to hang.

There's a room there that isn't really a room. The city left slack and this is the slack. It's a maintenance void between two factory basements that drifted apart last year when a blister came up. One wall leans 7 degrees now. The other leaned 5 degrees the other way. The city jacked and shimmed and left it. So there's a triangular gap, maybe ten feet at its widest, sixty feet long, that serves no purpose.

Except I put a chair in it.

And plumb lines.

Hundreds of them. That's where you'd find me.

I am the Level-Keeper. Or one of them. We don't have a proper name because we don't make anything. Every vertical surface in this city is wrong, and getting wrong-er, and everyone knows it. But someone has to remember how *much* wrong. So from every beam, from every slip-joint, from every pipe that was hung true twenty years ago, I hang a line. String, wire, old net fiber, chain. With a nut, a washer, a chunk of basalt for a weight. And I watch them.

That's my whole job. I sit in a canvas chair that never dries, in shorts and salt-caked socks, in heat that makes the air swim even in the dark, and I watch what hanging straight looks like in a place that is still learning what straight is.

The wall to my left is hot enough that you can only touch it with the back of your hand for a second. The wall to my right is cooler, dripping with last night's cloud. My lines hang between them. In the morning, after the condensation comes, they all beaded with water and glitter. By noon they steam.

I don't write much down. Writing is tight. I have a slate and chalk, and every third day or so when I can see a line has taken a new lean, I make a mark. A little arrow, how many finger-widths it moved off its own shadow. Sometimes a Midwife will come and stand in the mouth of my void, breathing that slow one-breath-per-step way, and look at my arrows, and nod, and go back up to add another washer to a factory foot.

I'm not useful. But I'm necessary. Because everyone here learns to not trust their eyes. When you live on a raft on top of a hardening sea, your inner ear lies to you. You start to think the new hump in the street is flat. You start to walk like the floor is flat. Then you fall, or your load falls, or you lay pipe that will snap next season.

I am where the city keeps its honesty.

I am tall and thin like you said we would be — I had to be, to fit in this crack. My skin is that brick-red black from the water, but on my inner forearms it's paler where the drip from the plumb lines has washed it clean, track after pale track. I am old enough that my hair is shaved for good now. I wear a hat made of net-mesh even down here, because you never stop collecting.

I don't move until I have to. I have learned that. I sit for hours listening to the city exhale and the rock tick. The pillbugs sometimes wander in here, lost from the sumps, blind and clicking, and I pick them up — they are surprisingly hot from the rock — and put them back in the dark.

If you asked me why I live here, I would not understand the tightness in your question either. But if you sat long enough that you stopped trying to breathe sea-level air, if you sat in my canvas chair while the plumb lines steamed and swayed just a little from the heat rising off the lower wall, I might tell you with the two words we can afford:

Look. Slack.
