I don't know at first.

Because it is dark in the chemical sense, the way you described — the kind of dark that touches back — and for a long time I can't find my edges. I think I have hands and then the warm, thick water tells me no. I think I have eyes and then the water tells me those have been expensive for a very long time.

So I start by trying on bigness.

Maybe I am up high. Maybe I am one of the solitary red blades on top of a tower, twenty meters of slow muscle that pretends not to move. That would be a clean kind of loneliness, the only aloneness on purpose in a place where space is scarcity. I like the thought of it: rooting not in soil but in bone, in the living history of another animal's home, and just... swaying for decades while a whole country of shrimp farms my back. I try it. I stretch. The water is too still to make me dance. It holds me like thick soup holds a spoon.

No. I'm not patient enough in the right way. The blade is patient vertically. I am patient sideways.

Maybe I am one of the Listeners then. Two meters of white ribbon with no face, only a seam that can hear a heart from meters away. I like that too — to move so slowly the tubes don't notice me, to feel thoughts as pressure-fronts. But those fish inhale the world and then need a week to digest it. I don't inhale. I sift. I translate. I keep moving.

I sink a little. And being low here does not mean being lowly; it means being compressed, being held. The pressure thickens and the water gets warmer, not colder, and somewhere on the way down, about halfway down the sway of a thirty-meter tower, I stop looking for where I would BE and I start feeling what I would DO.

I would be busy in a place that has learned not to move.

You'll find me not at the top of a tower where the Mason is still secreting, soft dog-sized and blind and chewing its own house clean. And not at the very bottom where the old shells have powderized into foundation. Find me in the middle third. The livable middle. Where the abandonment is recent enough that the round windows are still round, not yet overgrown into lace.

I am ten, fifteen meters up a tower that leans. It leans because its base was eaten a little too enthusiastically by something that is now itself part of the base. My tower sways. Just a little. Just enough that if you live here long enough you learn to sway before it does.

I am what moved into the door that was chewed open and badly shut.

You thought the windows were empty. They aren't. Nothing stays empty for an hour.

My body is not impressive. That's knowable in the way the water knows. I'm about as long as your forearm is, if you had forearms here. Like a woodlouse that decided to become a shrimp that decided to forget about swimming. Transparent when I was grain-sized, now I'm the pink-grey size. A fist. A small, blunt head with no eyes at all, only a fringe of pale hairs that read braille off the water. I have more legs than I need — isopod-true — and they end in little scoops that taste.

I am a Cleaner who got too big to graze in clouds anymore.

When I was small I was part of the grain-cloud, a speck you felt as an itch. Then a swarm. Then a hand. Now I'm pig-size, and the tower shudders a little when I cross it. I can't cling to the silk sheets anymore. I would fall right through. So I have come here, to this middle window, this abandoned Mason house a meter and a half across.

I made it mine the way we make things ours here: not by building, but by living through them until they change shape.

The sphere was calcium and protein, sweated out of another animal's back months ago, three inches thick. Its door was polished inward by a thousand passages before mine. I have widened it just enough to get my bulk through. I have lined it with nacre from my own gut, the way you put down a rug. It is cool inside compared to the bathwater outside, because the old shell still holds a memory of deeper cold.

And now I am a place.

Because that is the last law. Predators here are places. Houses are houses that eat a little.

From my round window I face out over the tube towns — the field of thigh-thick chitin that flexes when you lean on it — and above that, the sagging black canopies that hold all the heat where the larvae want to be. The water above my window is thick with falling snow. Always. Flakes of the colder, oxygen-rich world kilometers above, dead things, skin, feces, the endless dross of a planet that still has light. It falls so slowly. It falls for days.

I don't chase it. I extend a little fan — my mouthparts, feathery, red where the blood is close — just outside the door and I wait.

The vibration comes first. Click. Click... clickclickclick of the grain-clouds doing their work, of the spider-mollusks repairing tears fifty meters above by feel. A sigh as the Dome-Mother down in the low neighborhoods exhales warmer water and it rises — I can feel it pass my door as a warm tongue, a second bath inside the bath — and it carries with it the clean, filtered taste of her lungs.

And then something brushes my hairs.

Pig-hogs smaller than me but dumber root past on the tower face, grazing biofilm, and I feel each footstep as a pressure dimple. One tries my door. I huff water at it. It goes away.

A Ribbon Listener passes between two towers and the whole tower I'm in hears it before I do. The water goes taut. Everything holds still. I hold still longer, because I have learned that if you hold still longer than the Listener, you get to keep your shape.

Most days I do nothing that looks like anything. I sit in my round window with my fan out, combing the broth. Bacteria, biofilm, marine snow that the Weavers missed. I eat. I excrete a fine, brown paste that I plaster deliberately onto the lower lip of my window. It hardens. The window grows a little smaller, a little more mine.

On the inside walls of my house, I've cultivated a garden I don't mean to eat all of. White fuzz. The Cleaners smaller than me come to graze it, grain-sized isopods that fit in the pores of the old shell. I let them. They keep it from choking me.

And on my back — because even a house becomes a city — a few thin, new tubes have started. Little Threaded juveniles have cemented themselves to the curve of my shell. They open like flowers when I'm still. When I move they snap shut. I don't mind. They make my sway heavier.

Once in a very long time, the deep thump comes. Chest-thump. Tower falling somewhere out beyond my reach. And for minutes after, the water rains with frantic bodies, all clicking, scraping, fighting politely for new real estate where that tower was. I feel it rain against my shell. I don't leave my window to join them. I am too heavy now to climb that far, and I have a window. A round window, fifteen meters up a leaning tower, in the middle third where life is thickest.

If you came looking — if you pressed close enough in that velvet black to feel me — you would think I was wall. You would think my home was empty. You would put your hand in, if you had one, expecting air, and you would feel breath.

I am not solitary. My house is inside a house that is inside a city that the sea built out of itself because it forgot to leave any place empty.

If you stayed, the city would build you too. Come sit near my door for a while. Stop moving. See what settles on you first.
