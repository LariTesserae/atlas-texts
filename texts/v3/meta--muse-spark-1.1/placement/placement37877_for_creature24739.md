I don't start on the stone.

If you put me on the stone I die. That's the first thing I know. Anywhere in that white, high, scoured place where the sun falls straight down and there is nothing to drink and nowhere to hide and the light comes back up off the basalt into your eyes from below — if I am there, I am already running, and I am already afraid.

So I'm not there.

At first I think I would be one of the things that can be there. Because I love the between. For a minute I am a Salt Wasp line against the blue, so high the Flute Flies' choir is just a tooth-hum. My wings are glass you can see through, and I don't hurry. My legs are full of grey paste. I'm carrying salt from one god to another so it doesn't pickle itself in its own water. The heat doesn't touch me because I've glazed myself in wax. I go from mountain to mountain and none of them are mountains. I like that, being the only postman on a continent. But I don't stop. I can't land inside. I'm too clean. I'm too alone.

So not that.

Then I think I am sound. Because if you live there you have to be sound first before you are anything else. I am one note held as long as a belly can hold it. I am the Flute Fly. I am as long as your thumb and pale as whey and I live only for the chord. One of a trillion. When the wind shifts over the plates of my Holdfast I shift with it. When the big mantis-femur crashes through a curtain vine I shift. When she flexes water from her east side to her west side, a slow hydraulic sigh you can hear through your feet, I shift. I don't ever hear silence. I AM the not-silence. But I die in the gutters where I was born, and I never know what I'm inside of.

Bigger then.

I find myself inside because everything that matters is inside.

And not deep inside at first. I live near the skin, near the top where the plates are still tight and green-black and overlapping like shingles the size of doors. I am in the condensation layer. Every morning before the heat, before the sun is vertical, my world is cold and it weeps. The whole Holdfast sweats, and water beads on everything and runs. I am one of the ribbon things. No legs. Feathered muscle as long as a man, all throat. I live only where it drips. I sing in two voices at once — a low one out of my throat and a high one out of my hollow feathers — and I never stop because the one I'm singing to is three meters away and blind in the mist. I know every drip-route by heart. But I never go down to the heart cisterns. I'm too wet. I'm too small.

You have to go down to be a person.

So I go down.

There. Now I'm getting warmer. The sound outside is muffled to a deafening organ roar. The air is cool and smells of iron and peat and water that has moved through living sponge for a hundred years. Light comes not from outside but from amber mites in synchronous curtains, steady enough to read by if reading was a thing we did.

I live in the lee side of North. The old one. The city-sized one with the hip, with the bald black dome on top that stays hot till midnight. About two thirds up, on the side away from the wind, the Holdfast collapsed inward a little on itself a long time ago and made a valley in its own flesh. In that valley there's a hanging bog that never touched stone, a hundred meters across. That is my roof. That is my sky.

My hold is not built. It was found. A lenticel that didn't close right — a breathing pore the size of a man that scabbed open. My grandmother's grandmother took a beetle-shell adze to it and scraped, a millimeter a year. Now it's big enough for a sleeping hollow, and a hearth that never has fire, and a wall of gas-bladders curing for waterskins.

I am old now. My chest is a barrel. That's what the thin air at 3,000 meters does to you if you live. My skin is brown-green and smells of aloe-cut because I scrape wax from the new plates every morning and smear it on. The wax keeps the water in. I have the second eyelid now, the black one, because I have looked at too many white noons through blown grit.

I was a runner when I was younger. Everyone has to be once. When South got sick — a rot-face after a late bloom, black fly humming different, too sweet — we had to leave it. I coated my boy, I coated myself, we filled our back leaves from the deep cistern where it was still clean, and we waited for the hour after dawn when the stone was still cool and the Flute Flies were low. Then we ran across the whale-backs. Three kilometers of polished basalt reflecting sky. You don't run straight. You run plate-to-plate of shadow. You open your mouth so your ears don't burst. If you fall you get up. My boy made it. I made it. South didn't.

Now I don't cross. My job is to stay.

I am what they call a Thread Counter, though there is no word for counting. I tend the flowering chamber.

Deep in North there is a cistern as big as a house, and when it gets full enough — full enough that the whole organism decides it can risk a dream — the pressure builds. North flexes. Not shakes. A slow inhale. And from a thousand pores at once around the midsection it sighs out silk that isn't silk. Glassy filaments that hang in the hot still air for hours, catching light, humming when the Flute Flies hit them. Each one carries a seed smaller than dust.

Most people go inside during this, because the wasps go mad and get tangled.

I stand outside the pore field with a frame of wasp-wing bone and living vine that I've trained to grow into loops. I don't catch them all. You must never catch them all. That would be stealing. But I catch some. I braid them.

The filament doesn't melt. It doesn't rot. If you braid it right, with a little leaf wax, it stays. I am braiding a map. Not of the plateau — basalt is basalt, it doesn't need mapping. I'm braiding where the wind goes during flowering. Who answered who. Three years ago West flowered and North flowered two days later, a call and response. I have that in the braid: a thick knot for West, a runner of twenty-seven fine threads for North.

When the boys and girls come up from the lower chambers to wring the dawn leaves without bruising them — that's the first lesson — they touch my braid and I tap the plate for them, quiet, because we never raise our voices. The air is already too full.

I am not the Holdfast. I am not its keeper. It keeps me. I am the small, waxed, careful mite that learned how to live in the lung of something larger than history, so it will keep making water out of air long after I am peat in its black moat.

At noon I press my back against living sponge that is cold and utterly wet, wring out my sleeves to drink, and listen to the chord shift.
