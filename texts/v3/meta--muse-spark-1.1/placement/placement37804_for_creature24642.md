At first I think I know where I'd be.

Up high, trying to see the whole thing. On one of those long swells that pretends to be a hill, with the wind coming straight off the scarp nine days old, trying to get the continent in my head. I'd want to be the cartographer, the one who came to map the city that has no center. I'd try to count walls.

That doesn't last long. In air this clear you can see forty miles and it means nothing. Distance lies. And the cold settles. At -20 in the sun you don't want high, you want lee.

So later, a few hours of walking later, I think I'd find myself lower. Not in the high exposed crust where the rosettes tighten down to armor, but down in one of those shallow grooves that aren't really valleys. Where the light puddles.

I would have stopped because my hands had gone blue in it. I'd have sat down without meaning to stay, just to look at my hands being the wrong color. To check if I was still.

That's where you'd find me. In a ripple-valley maybe eight feet deeper than the plain around it, a mile wide, so shallow you would not know you were in it unless the light told you. On the edge of an old riverbed, the big kind, broad as a highway and a foot deep, with only the black trickle in its middle. My back against a dew-cistern.

The cistern is a better place to be than any house. The houses are sunk and private and they are someone's pantry. The cisterns are for everyone. That big stone bowl, ten feet across, fitted without mortar, half full of water that wasn't there yesterday and won't be there tonight, and over it the lattice of bone-white cured sedge poles. Singing a little. Ticking.

I would have learned fast not to sleep on the crust. You learn to step only on stone. So I'm on the capstones of the cistern's north wall, where the moss has been trimmed back. There is a neat, round footprint in the felt next to me that is not mine - broader, soft-edged, holding clear water. Still clear. So recent.

If you came looking, you'd find me there with my mouth open.

Not stupidly. Deliberately. Learning to drink from the air the way they do. Letting the condensation run down the poles all night and drip. One drip every twenty or thirty seconds if the frost was good. You have to be patient enough to not lick your lips. Tears freeze here, they said. Speech is expensive. You don't waste water wanting it elsewhere.

So who would I be?

At first I thought - one of them. I wanted to be tall and polite to gravity and lichen-grafted and old enough to remember where a river used to go. I wanted that amber iris, that third lid flicking against the wind. But I haven't earned that slowness. My blood is too fast. I'm pink and loud-breathing. When I walk my boots keep water in their prints too long before the silt colonizes it. I'm fauna 1.0 in a place built for 0.5. I'm too much animal.

Later I thought I'd be none of them at all. I'd be the moss itself. The carpet-world. The felt that moves an inch an hour. That would be honest - to be what actually lives here, not the gardener of it. To swell in the morning and sigh at noon. To flow downhill around a wall until the wall leans. But I can't do that either. I notice myself too much.

What emerges, if I sit long enough that my shadow goes hard and short and then long again, is something in between.

I would be the keeper who wasn't born to it. The one the plateau gets every couple generations when someone comes over the cliff-edge expecting ruin and finds maintenance instead.

Not a citizen - citizenship here is knowing which terrace is yours without needing a fence - but a neighbor. The term they have is "how many walls away" and I would be three walls away from the nearest door. Close enough to hear the *tok tik-tok* on the poles when something breaches, far enough that no one has to feed me.

I would be the apprentice to a specific wall.

This one wall runs absolutely straight for what looks like forever, then follows the old earth-swell in a slow curve, which is how you know it's truly old and truly tended. Ten feet high, two feet thick, basalt without mortar, light in the hand because gravity is polite. The green tide pushes it. Every spring after the calf-deep flood that comes from nowhere, I would go out with my copper-comb and my felt mantle that I still haven't learned to felt properly, and I would re-stack the three or four stones that have slumped.

I would be bad at it at first. My hands would get cold even through gloves. My lichen - because I would have asked for the graft, on the back of the left forearm where the sun hits when I lean, olive and sulfur-yellow, itching shallowly - would be patchy and thin. I would speak too much and then be too dry for an hour.

But I would be useful enough that the tall one whose pantry I border would not ask me to leave. She - though sex is a soft distinction here - is eight feet when she unfolds and never stands at full height. Her feet leave those neat round prints. She breathes with that wet crackle, like tiny ice forming in tubes, and when she does speak it's low and close and not projected. Mostly she taps.

I would learn to read the cistern bowl's drips. I would learn to tap back. Not words. "North terrace holding." "South wall leaning a hand." "Water good here last night."

I would be the one who sits in the light-puddle and watches yesterday's weather still walking on the horizon, because I still have time-sense that is too fast, too linear. And eventually I would stop trying to map the whole city, which is a lowland ambition. I would map instead the forty houses I can see from my door, and then ten of them well, and then one of them well enough to know when its owner is out tending her crust and when she is inside breathing slow at thirty beats a minute.

I'm not the city. I'm not the gardeners who never left. I'm the person who came to study an abandonment and found instead that someone had left a copper rod on a rock near my foot.

*Tok.*

Invitations are maintenance.

I'd pick it up.
