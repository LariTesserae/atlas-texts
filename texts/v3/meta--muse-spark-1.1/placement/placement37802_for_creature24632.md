I don't think I would make it to the middle deck.

Not at first.

If I went down that fresh bite in the road — where the yellow line just stops writing itself — I would get to about twenty feet and stop because the heat changed my mind about what down meant. The air gets a weight there. It's not that it's hard to breathe. It's that you realize you are breathing something that has been breathed already, by rock.

I would sit on that first real stair-step of basalt, where it's still glassy and sharp and hasn't learned how to be old. My feet would be dangling over the first tin roof. That would be my first shelf. Not built, just found.

I think you'd find me a little off the main vertical line, on the north wall where the surface cold pours in first at night. Where the condensation starts early.

I am not good at being in the middle of things. A hundred meters across where every rooftop is a floor is a ceiling — if you stand on the mesh in the center everyone hears your throat click. I have lived too much of my life being heard when I didn't mean to be. I wouldn't last there.

And I wouldn't be at the very bottom, near the sump. I admire the Keepers but I don't have their ears. I can't tell the difference yet between a drip and a tick that matters. I would try to be down there and I would be useless, getting in the way of the pump that goes *hiss... hiss... hiss...* like a lung that never learned to stop.

So I'd be mid-tier. Third or fourth lattice up from the bottom. Where the light is already amber-bulb amber, not furnace amber. Where a little bit of the 2 a.m. weather still reaches you sideways.

What would I be? At first I thought maybe the lichen — that black-green plate that grows because it can afford not to be green. Or the white rootlet that drinks air. Something that doesn't need soil and doesn't ask much.

But no. The longer I sit with it, the more it becomes itself: I would be one of the listeners who went narrow.

Not a builder. I don't have the faith for building a shelf and hoping someone builds under me. I would want to see if it holds first.

I would be the one who tends the loop because the loop is small enough to understand.

There's a job no one names. Someone has to keep the drip from becoming a dead end. The condensation beads on the underside of every catwalk and falls, and most of it finds its way to drums and cut tanks and finally to the sump. But some of it doesn't. It gets caught in a sagging tarp where it breeds mosquitoes that can't exist here but tries anyway. It pools behind a shack where it starts to rust a joint from the inside out faster than it should. It sits on a bare bulb until it pops it.

I would be the one who walks the stack during the interior rain with a rag that never dries.

You'd hear me, if you were listening in that active quiet, but only as part of the rain. A soft tick that isn't water on tin. I go barefoot because boots hold water and because the grate tells you more through your feet — who's home three houses over, where a new cord was run and now the hum changes the sound-shadow.

My hands would be the soft-palmed kind. Pruned translucent. White scar map across the fingertips from when I touched the young basalt wrong the first week. Salt blooming at my wrist creases. My shirt would be cut down to almost nothing, shade-cloth, and it would never dry. My lips would be cracked. I'd lick water off pipes without thinking about it, same as everyone else.

And in the hours when it's not raining — which is most hours — I would sit on that edge shelf I found and do the other part of the job, which is listening for the tick of fresh rock spalling off as it cools. I would collect the flakes in a dented oil can. Not to crush into patch paste like the Keepers do. I would keep them because they are the only thing here that is truly new. Everything else loops. Water loops, mist loops, even the words loop — *hold, up a bit, good — leave it.* But the rock makes a little more of itself every night, ping, on tin.

In a place that makes more of itself out of itself, I would be the one who keeps a little bowl of the new stuff and counts it.

You would find me exactly where the rules are most audible:

What falls from above will fall again — I would wring my rag into a drum and watch it come back as mist later.

Metal rusts from the inside out — I would touch the dark orange in the corner of a joint and know not to put weight there tomorrow.

You do not build a house, you build a shelf and hope someone else builds under you — and I would have finally, after months, welded three feet of rebar out from my found stair-step, not because I needed the room, but because a kid below me needed a roof that was also drier than her old tarp. And I would not tell her I did it.

If you stayed long enough to lower yourself to my lattice — turning sideways to pass — I would offer you the cup, that same cup everyone offers. Water off my own ceiling an hour ago, breathed out by rock, drunk yesterday, will be drunk tomorrow. I would watch you drink to see if you understand that you are not being given a drink.

You are being invited into a loop.
