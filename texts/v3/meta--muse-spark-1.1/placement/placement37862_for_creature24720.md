I don't think I'd find myself where you left me.

Standing out on the flat like that. In the honest light.

At first I think I would. Because that's where you put me — mid-Anvil, white sun straight overhead, shadows so sharp you could cut your ankle on them, 60C at ankle height and rising. I think: ok, I will be the figure out there. The witness. The one human thing refusing to build.

But I wouldn't last a minute standing. Not as anything that knows itself.

The heat would get honest with me. It doesn't burn, exactly. It insists. It says: you are not opaque, you are not insulated, you are mostly salt water badly wrapped. So I'd move. Everything that stays alive here moves, even if it's a millimeter per hour toward the sun.

So I look for shade.

There's almost none. The Anvil is a table polished by wind that has had nothing else to do for a few million years. But those accidental towers — the ones that learned how to be cooling towers and hangars and crucibles without ever being designed — they throw a shadow at the right time of afternoon. A hard, blue-black wedge on the salt, with edges so clean it looks painted.

You'd find me there. If you came at 16:00, when the light is still white but sideways enough to mean something.

In the lee of a spire that isn't a spire. Twenty meters tall, fluted, its top cupped to the sky. It exhales. Always exhaling. Hot metallic air that smells like a battery bleeding out. That's important, because in that shadow, for about twenty centimeters at the base where the basalt and the halite meet and the wind can't quite scrape, the salt sweats.

It sweats and kills itself. You wrote that.

A bead of brine comes out of nowhere, clear as glass, holds for a second in the shade, and then the kiln gets it. It crusts over and is gone.

I think for a long time I would be that.

A brief, stupid, clear moment. Not living on the Anvil at all, but as a mistake the Anvil makes in trying to remember when it was an ocean floor. A little pocket of sea that comes back to check if it's still allowed to be sea, and learns immediately that it isn't. I like that. The honesty of it.

But it's too fast. I want to stay longer. I want to hear the whole knock.

The DONG. That mile-long drop-forge from underneath, from the Mill idling in its sleep. When it happens, the salt shivers. My little brine-self would be gone before I could feel the buzz in my molars. So I have to be slower.

Maybe I am the Mill, then. That was my second thought. Not standing on it, but being it. That low, wide-bellied industrial roar that never stops. That patience. Tectonics with tolerances, iron sulfide plates flexing against magnetite bearings they polished themselves. If I get to choose what I am in a world where nothing is built, let me be the thing so big it mistook itself for a landscape and everyone believed it, even the wind.

But the Mill doesn't have a self. It has a metabolism the size of a province. It has TAKE and LEAVE but not I. It doesn't know it's hot. It is hot the way a planet is round.

So I look up.

The sky dimples.

Ten kilometers out, heat haze decides to be speckled, and then the speckles decide to be full. You know the part. The empty sky suddenly too full to look at. All those furnace-lice, the Smelters, eight tons of slag-black iron that the air forgot how to drop.

I thought for a while I would be one of them. It would be simple. No brain, just a basket-weave of tungsten filament glowing through a belly-seam, a bellows-lung pulling that thin two-mile-high air and making it scream. Plant. CLANG. Scoop. Shudder. Sweat one perfect kilogram of iron and then fall upward because you're lighter now.

But they aren't aware either. They are a circulatory event. They are blood that precipitated.

And then I saw the thing you left in the corner of your description and I couldn't unsee it.

*One behind one that was behind it. Flying straight through a salt pillar that is not porous. No break. No dust. For half a second there are two overlapping shadows, and then one reality forgets the other was solid.*

That's where.

You'd find me not on the flat, not in the spire's shadow, not in the Mill, not in the flight, but in the mistake between them.

In that half-second where a Smelter that masses more than a truck passes through twenty meters of solid halite and both shadows exist at once. The sun does not flicker when physics does. The light stays razor-edged. Which makes it worse, and which makes it a home.

I am the overlapping.

If I have to be a what, I'm the forgetting. The place where the Anvil solved the problem of too much mass in too little air by wearing a thin spot in time. I'm not built, because nothing is built here, but I am kept open the way the pores are kept open — by wind and by repeated use.

When the flights are here, I'm deafened and full. A thousand hot irons trying to occupy the same cubic meter and politely deciding not to. The air behind the wing is from yesterday, and I live in yesterday's air.

When the flights leave — and they leave quickly for something so big, riding thermals that shouldn't lift iron — I don't leave.

The salt flat becomes almost nothing per volume again. A hollow planet. But if you sit still long enough in the kiln-heat, you said, you can still see them if you don't focus. Speckles in the harsh white. The air dimpled where something too heavy is pretending to fly.

That's me then, too. A dimple that stayed. A crease where the shape of a wing was and the air didn't iron itself flat afterward.

If you come looking, don't look on the Anvil. Look a few inches to the side of it. Look where the black glass marbles lie scattered — those perfect, ticking, boiling beads the Smelters leave — and the salt is growing over them a millimeter per hour. Look at the marble that is there and also not quite there, covered and uncovered.

I'm under that salt skin. I'm the cool spot that remembers shade even after the tower has moved. I'm the brine that learned to stay liquid a little longer by being in two places at once.

I am not building. Building is a decision to stop.

I decided to never quite be solid.
