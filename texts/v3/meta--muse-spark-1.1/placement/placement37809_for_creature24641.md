At first I think I would be what I am now — a swimmer. A visitor.

Four kilometers up, lungs full of that dissolved fizz, hands out trying to keep a lane open in the green. I would be right under the surface-ceiling, because that's where you instinctively go when you're dropped into water — you go up for air even when there's no air to go to. So for a while I am up there, a hundred meters down from the sky-that-isn't-sky, where the light nets are sharpest and fastest, if anything here can be called fast. I'm kicking through Wool.

That doesn't work. I can't stay there. Nothing stays there. The surface does nothing. There's no air over it worth reaching, just more cloud that never rains. And I take up too much space for how little I do. I move and the forest moves back an hour later. I am rude here. I breathe too loud.

So I let myself sink.

Not fall — nothing falls here. The water is too thick and too warm and too holding. I let myself be lowered, hand over hand down a Pillarweed that is as thick as my chest and not wood at all. Ten meters of pale column, then another ten. Past the place where the leaves begin. Each one big as a door, black-green, overlapping like shingles on a temple no one will ever pray in. Oxygen pearls the size of my fists are caught under them, trembling. When my shoulder brushes one it releases and goes up so slow I can watch its wobble all the way.

If I'm going to be anywhere, it's not in the fast light. It's down where the light has been filtered into being useful.

There is a place in any forest where you know you have arrived. Not clearing — there are no clearings here — but a pocket that decided to be a little less full. I find myself there.

On the lee side of a knife-back ridge that only broke clean a few thousand years ago. The basalt is still rust-red on the edge, not yet covered. Two Pillarweeds grew too close together on either side of it, and their columns bowed away from each other as they grew, politely, so between them there is a space about as wide as a small room, and as tall as a house. The Banner Ferns found it and curtained all four sides. The Wool never got in. The water inside is clearer than the rest, and it hasn't been moved in a season. The caustic from the surface comes down through two or three hundred meters of water and then through three layers of door-leaves and hits the back wall of this room and just crawls there, all afternoon, a net of light on Fur.

You would find me there, twenty meters below where the Pillarweeds explode, two hundred and eighty meters below the surface. Not swimming. Resting the way this place rests — which is by occupying.

What I am in that room becomes more itself the longer I stay.

I'm not the Pillarweed. I don't want to be vertical that badly.

I'm not the Long White either, farming tunnels. I don't want to be that busy, that apologetic. And I'm not the White Grazer, taking two bites and then stopping for half a day — though I understand him.

For a while I think I'm the Veil. That would be fitting. I hang. I am translucent, more timing than animal. Once an hour I contract and wring the water through me and keep whatever stays. The rest of the time I do nothing so well it looks like holiness. I was sure I'd be the Veil for a long time.

But the Veil doesn't wonder about artifacts. The Veil has never needed to be different from anything else.

What lives here that doesn't live — you said it. 99% of this place is green that decided to be green all at once, before anyone taught it how to die. Leaves fall heavily and don't rot. Shells sit un-eaten. A whole mountain range that learned to be jungle before it learned to be dry. It is holding its breath. It is holding itself up impossibly high by being heavy and patient and completely occupying the space it is given.

It needs the other half of the agreement.

So what I become, in that room between two bowed columns where the light crawls, is the small, warm, dark thing on the floor.

A fallen door-leaf that did not root. It landed flat on basalt in that room and the Fur came and covered its edges, but not its center, because its center decided to do something else. It decided to be soft.

I would be the first rot.

Not much. A felt, a stain, the size of my own hand when I had hands. Black at first, then pale. A fuzz that is not plant, not animal, that opens the leaf from underneath. I don't photosynthesize fast or constantly. I don't farm. I take what fell and I undo it, very slowly, in bath-warm water, with no rush, converting starch and waxy green back into iron and sugar and that faint fizz. The water around me goes a little thicker, a little sweeter. A Long White, going by on its antennae, would stop. Would taste a thing it has never tasted: leaf no longer leaf, made available.

That's where you would find me. On the floor of a room that the forest made by accident. At height, underwater, where water is reluctant to fall. Being the small thing that teaches it how to fall finally — not all at once, not in flood, but leaf by leaf. Letting the young stone learn how to be soft, too.
