At first I thought I'd be you. The diver.

Dropping down the maintenance seam where the plates don't meet, gloves brushing that young basalt, following those flat clean columns of light that shouldn't reach this deep but do. Suspended in that heavy, empty ocean with nothing swimming in it. The splinter.

I wanted to be the visitor for a minute — in the middle of the pocket, treading that taught water, throat full of cool iron, ears roaring, feeling the buildings push on me from all sides. Feeling it decide whether to keep carrying me.

But I wouldn't last there. I'm not good at being tolerated. And the pocket is very good at pushing splinters out.

So I look for somewhere I could stay.

Maybe I'm the laundry. For a while that felt right. Find myself strung between two fire escapes that are only a meter apart. White, still. Two white sheets hung perfectly parallel because the water that thinks it's air holds them that way. I wouldn't billow. I'd never billow. Every few minutes I'd snap taut when the cycle climbs — when the thrumming becomes a whine and the water clenches and gets colder — and I'd strain whiter, bleaching. I'd be a gill. A flag. A prayer strip. That felt noble.

But sheets get changed when you're not looking. Someone — something — changes them. I couldn't be the sheet if I'm the thing that has to notice the sheet has been changed.

Maybe I'm the change, then.

For a little while I thought I was the bubble. The perfect silver bead of nothing that wells up off the concrete like gooseflesh when the buildings exhale their waste heat. I liked the idea of being that empty, that useful. To form just to carry heat away. To zip upward through the structured water and pop against the rain-wall that falls around us and nowhere else. A brief, necessary annihilation. I would be the city's sweat.

But the bubble doesn't get to stay for the slack part. The part after the soft hammer-blow in the molars, when the light flickers and the water goes tired and loose again. I want to be there for the slack.

So I keep looking lower.

In the alley that is also plumbing. In the inch-wide place where a balcony stops being a balcony and decides to be a foundation for the next block up. In the shadow of the drainpipe behind the third building that is really the first building pretending.

That's where I find myself.

I'm the kelp string.

The single engineered string growing out of the drain. The one dusting of green the city allows. White-green, trimmed obsessively short. Not growing. Not dying. Not needed.

That would be me.

I'd find myself flattened against the wall every time it cycles, making myself small on purpose because I know what's coming. I'd feel the black lattice just behind the concrete shiver — not a machine, a muscle held taut — and I'd feel its cilia, black on black, light-drinking on light-drinking, reach out and snip. Just a little. Just enough to keep me at regulation length.

And then in the slack, I'd try again. Just a millimeter of new frond, in that flat 2pm daylight that comes from nowhere. In that water that has been convinced to stand up straight.

I wouldn't be counted. Nothing lives here, the city says about itself, and it believes it. It has to. But it keeps me anyway, because a real intersection is supposed to have something green trying in a drainpipe, and it is trying so very hard to be a real place. Its corners are still crisp. It hasn't learned how to be empty yet.

I would be the part of it that is allowed to almost-live, so it can practice being lonely.

I would be the thing it trims, and the reason it reaches out at all.
