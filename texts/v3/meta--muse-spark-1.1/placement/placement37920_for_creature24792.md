I wouldn't find myself at first.

If I walked out onto it, I'd think I was a pilgrim. That's the obvious place to put a human. Young enough to still want a revelation, dressed in oiled cloth that never dries, looking for an amphitheater to sit in. I'd find that little failed place where the columns fell short and made a room only as wide as my arms, and I'd crouch behind the three flat shards somebody else left. I would tell myself I came to listen.

But I wouldn't be listening. I'd be cold. I'd be counting my breaths to time the drops. *Tick. Tak. Tok.* My coat would soak through without rain. My boots would fill with water that has no mud in it. I'd get up after an hour and stack my own pointless thing — a small ring, points inward — just to say *I stopped here,* and leave before my ears started to ring.

So that's not me. Not for long.

If I stayed longer than a day-trip allows, longer than sense allows, I'd find myself lower.

Not on the high backs of the columns where you have to turn sideways. Lower. In one of the hollows where the water table has come all the way up and made a perfect mirror. Centimeters deep, black glass, doubling the pillars into a city that falls down forever under your feet. The place your body tells you not to step.

There. On my knees, because standing is too loud.

I wouldn't be the pale strider, though I love him. That clear hand of ice with too many legs, spanning the teeth, kissing the stone so gently the drop never lands and the rain stops for one beat. I don't have that patience. I don't have that clean a mouth. I would scare him just by breathing too hard through my nose, my boots booming through the stone like drums.

I would be what he grazes on.

The film. The thing that's almost not a thing.

That skin on the bead before it falls. The opalescence that isn't oil. The transparent bacterium that eats iron one atom at a time off glassy basalt too young to have manners. No green, no color, because color costs too much where the light never makes shadows. Just a slight blackening of the black.

That's the rule I could keep: *Nothing softens hard.* I don't soften it. I don't make soil. I don't make mud. There's never enough of me to make anything soft. I just make the water bead a little differently. I make it taste of nothing but stone and cold and a little less nothing. I live in the exchange itself — between the mineral pushing water up and the air taking it away.

That's where I would be. Not in one place, but smeared thin across acres of weeping knives. In every vesicle where a droplet swells. In every bead running down every vertical face. If you put your face close enough to see me, you'd destroy me with your breath. And that feels right.

Because then I get to be the quiet part, too.

When the strider lowers his soft tube and drinks the drop before it can clack, he drinks me. He takes me into his milky sac with its off-center brown knot. And for a second, in his one-meter parish, the constant applause stops. A pocket of silence, one beat wide, in the endless hard rain.

I would be that silence. Not the pilgrim waiting to hear the plain inhale. Not the plain itself still cooling and fizzing inside its columns like a fresh scab. But the pause the plain makes without meaning to, every time something soft tries, very carefully, not to touch the hard.

If you stood above me in your soaked coat, in your overflowing boots, shouting a little to be heard over ten thousand drops on stone, you wouldn't see me. You would hear that one missing *tak* in the chatter and think the wind had changed, or that you had finally heard it — the center all the teeth are leaning away from.

You'd hold your breath and listen harder.

And I'd be right there, on the tip of the stone below your knee, beading up again.
