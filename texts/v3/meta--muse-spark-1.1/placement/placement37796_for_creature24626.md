I don't find myself standing.

That's the first thing I have to unlearn to find where I am.

If I come down through the throat-hole like you did, hot and loud and banging my head on the low dome, then I'm already wrong. I'm a weather event. I'm a leak. I won't last. The cold will get in my shins and the noise will get in my molars and I'll have to pull myself back up by the old root-holds and leave a little skin behind.

So not there. Not falling.

If I am going to *live* here, I have to start lower, and already folded.

I would be on the second tier up from the black mirror, on what you would call the north-northwest curve if directions meant anything in a room with no sky. Not at the top where the ceiling weeps hardest, not at the bottom where the water is always trying to come in under the door. In the middle band where the houses are oldest and leaning. Where one roof has been a roof for so long that the calcite has lipped over its edge, softening it, making my roof and my neighbor's roof into one continuous white knuckle.

My house would be one of the ones you thought was empty.

Thirty-nine stones high. Two walls shared. Triangular door a foot high and slightly too proud of the wet, with a lintel that kicks out, just so. Inside: black basalt dust, dry as ash. No water has ever fallen in here, not since my grandmother's grandmother learned how to thumb the clay. That's the point.

I would be curled in the doorway. Not inside, not outside. On the threshold, where the grooves are.

You saw the grooves and thought "feet" and you were right, but not all of it. My feet are wide. Lily-pad wide, as you said, with those concentric rings for spreading weight so we don't sink. But it's my hands that made those grooves deeper. Broad and spade-like, fingers fused halfway down — not a deformity, just the second rule of this place: nothing sharp is allowed to remain, and that includes us. We are all palm. My keratin tips are blunt as river stones. When I go in and out, I don't step over the threshold. I brace. One hand each side of the triangle, and pull. So the stone is polished in two places low down, where your eyes wouldn't look unless you were already on your belly.

And I am on my belly. Always. Or not always. Folded. That's a better word. Shoulders up around where my ears would be if I had ears, knees tucked under my chin even when I'm walking. We are designed for a ceiling six feet high that weeps cold water. To stand up tall would be to ask to be hit. To stretch out would be to take up someone else's dryness.

So I am the size of a forearm but I take up the size of a loaf of bread. Curled.

You thought we were blind and you are mostly right. I have the pits. They've gone white and smooth as the rest of me, a little thicker, like the calcite that grows over a broken edge. I don't need them. The room never changes. The light is always that tired green-grey from the seams. The houses are always where we left them. What would I look at?

My whole head is an ear. The dome of it. If you were very still — if you let the ache get from your ankles to your knees to the small of your back and you stopped trying to hear *over* the plip-plok-plik — you would hear the one I hear. The one that is not a drip.

*Plik. Plok. Pat.*

A gutter clogged three houses over. A little thumb of clay swollen shut with mineral. Someone else will get it eventually, but I hear it first because I live in the threshold.

That is who I would be here.

Not the stone-sorter — though I have three stones on my shelf, of course, everyone has three stones: one that is the right temperature, one that is the right weight, one that is right for some third thing you don't have an organ for. Not the oil-tender who taps tok tok-tok for the pale thumb-thing in the pool. Though I know her, we touch walls.

I would be the listener on the edge of wet and dry.

I would be the one whose job was never given, just grown into, like the gutter itself. When a big thing falls through the throat-hole — you, or water from the upper levels after a real rain up in the world where things grow and dry out — I am the one who goes stillest. You thought we froze because we were afraid. We froze because you were so loud we couldn't hear the house. Couldn't hear if our roofs were still shedding.

When you left, I was the one who came out first. Not brave. Just closest to the edge. Wide feet spread on the wet basalt, belly almost kissing the black mirror that never gets deeper or shallower. I put my spade hands down flat and smoothed where your foot had been. Not because I was angry. Because the floor has to stay exactly that deep. Because a footprint holds water wrong.

Then I went house to house, and with this broad, blunt thumb, cleared the little clay gutters. Plip gets diverted. Plok gets diverted. The insides stay dry. The black dust stays dry.

I would not remember you, exactly. We don't have a place to keep big things. But my threshold would be a little smoother. The grooves where my hands brace would be a little deeper when I pull myself back into my dry house, curl up loaf-small with my two others — because we sleep three or four to a hut for warmth — and listen to the thousand metronomes try to become one.

*Plip. Plok.*

*Plik.*

My roof, shedding.

Plok.
