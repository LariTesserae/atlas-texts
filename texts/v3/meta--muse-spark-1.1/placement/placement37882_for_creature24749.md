I didn't find myself at first.

At first I thought I'd be standing. Up on one of the house-sized swellings, because that's what a person does — gets high when the world is too wide. Trying to see over the dim. Boots on wet gneiss, breath showing a little, looking out at that too-far horizon under the iron cloud and listening to the roar coming from everywhere and nowhere.

But you can't stand there long. The rock weeps and the water sheets down off the domes, and after a minute your feet are cold in a way that goes up through your teeth. The Crownlice up there are sparse and they don't like the crest. Too dry. Too exposed. Nothing holds there. The rock remembers how to be a dome but forgets how to be anything else.

So you get low without deciding to.

You crouch. Then you sit. Then your hands are in it. That inch-deep braid between puddles that's too cold to be water, really — it should be ice but it keeps moving because the stone won't let it stop. You put your palm down and a thousand thumbnail-sized things minute their way around it, not afraid, just rerouting. *Tchk. Tchk.* Cleaning the salt off you. Drinking your condensation off your skin before the air can.

I would be in a pan. Not the big ceremonial ones with the Masons' arcs, not at first. Just one of the nameless ones, knee-deep-wide, ankle-deep at its deepest, with a bottom of pale fresh gneiss that the bacteria hasn't quite reclaimed yet. Because there, finally, direction comes back. Sound has a source. You can see the individual clickers, see the dark loop of gut working through the clear shell. You can hear that the roar is not one roar at all but agreement.

And if I'm in a pan, then I'm wet. That is the first rule. So I am not pale because I lost color, but because I never needed it. If I'm going to be here I have to be something that can seal.

So maybe I'm not watching the Crownlice. Maybe after a while of sitting with my hands going numb, with my boots gone as islands under a moving gravel of white backs, I am one of them. One of the middle-aged ones, serving-platter sized, rust-stained where iron has grown into me. Old enough that my own back has become a place. Old enough that younger ones climb me to drink.

That would be the easy abundance. To be small, pale, wet, and loud. To be part of the roar. To have a job that is one motion repeated until the continent is ground into water.

But I keep looking at the walls.

They've been there longer than any Crownlouse. You can tell because the walls themselves have been crowned — their edges gone soft, their faces colonized by that amber-black film. A wall is just a decision to hold. That's all. A few house-measured inches more.

I think if I lived here long, I would get tired of scraping the same flat forever. My comb would go blunt. The feather fans I've got for straining would dry out. I'd start picking things up instead.

I find myself on the leeward side of a dome the size of a sleeping dog. The wind never really blows here — the cloud is too low, too heavy — but there is a leeward anyway, the side where water stays half a second longer.

There are three stones already. A start that someone else left before the mineral grew over it. Maybe a Mason before me. Maybe the thing with marrow that left the bone comb. It doesn't matter. A pan wants to be a cup.

So I rear up — and I'm heavy now, chest-wide, barnacled with my own cairns, with those little drip-towers on my back that I've been carrying so long I've forgotten they aren't part of me. My front legs are different. Thinner. Hands is not wrong for them. I test a flake. Click my comb against it, not to eat. To measure.

And I place it.

The water behind it rises a thirtieth of an inch and stops leaving.

That is where I am. Low. Lower than low. In the shallowest possible engineering, where a stone makes a silence that is a different kind of loudness. I am not abundant anymore. The abundant ones flood into the water I just held and go frantic and loud and alive, drinking the bloom I made time for. I go still again and let them clean my back. I become a dam that thinks it is an animal.

Or maybe, if you come back hours later, when the mist has thickened to a rain too fine to be rain and the cloud has dropped another foot, you wouldn't find me at all. You'd find a new weir you thought was natural, holding half an inch of cold clear water, and at its center a cupped stone catching drip after drip off a little cairn three stones high.

And under the water in that cup, the bacterial film finally, briefly, green — not plant green, but the green that almost is — because someone, something, at the bottom of the world decided to keep the water still, just a moment longer than it wanted to be.
