I don't think I would find myself in the open.

At first I thought I would — out in the mile-wide fen where you can see a long way and the Lattice is thickest, standing up to my knees in sphagnum and becoming a problem the valley has to route around. But I run too cold for that, or too hot, I'm not sure. The open is for the thought itself, the held agreement of ten thousand beads. There's no room for me there except as a sleeve of empty air. The valley would make a shape around me like water around a stone and I would never actually be *in* it.

And I wouldn't be down deep. Under the mat. I wanted to be, when you described the black water that has never seen light. That kind of memory appeals to me — a house-sized liver spread flat, farming puddles where a minute is an hour. To live that slow, to be the one who makes sure erosion has really finished. But I don't have that kind of patience. My thoughts don't stretch.

I keep being drawn back to the edge of things. Where one kind of wet meets another kind.

You'd find me where the mat is thinnest over an old oxbow — that perfect black mirror you described, cut off from its river a thousand years ago and still learning how to be a circle. Where the floating rug of moss and cranberry and alder root quakes over deep water, and if you stand still you can feel it breathe. I would be right at the lip, where the sheet flow goes from being a film between leaves to being a skin over an abyss. Where the peat weeps over the clay and the iron pan rusts out in a line the color of dried blood. That's a gradient. And the valley eats gradients.

I think at first I would be water. That was my first mistake. I thought: I will be a bead with a dark nucleus, old water that has forgotten how to fall. I will hold still with my sisters and hum.

But a bead isn't alive. Only the agreement is.

Then I thought: I will be the alder, with those impossible jungle leaves in October cold, broad as hands. I will be paper. Important, read-upon.

But the alder doesn't know it's being written on.

No. What becomes more itself the longer I stand here — with cold getting into my knees, with my own breath making a cloud that the beads avoid — is this:

I am small, and fast, and I am always cleaning up after a thought.

I am one of the Readers.

Not *the* Readers as a chorus, but a single one. Cold-fat colored, translucent the way skim milk is translucent, legs too long and feathered with hairs that never break the surface tension. I live on the underside of one of those waxy leaves down at the quaking edge, where the Lattice has to bend around the alder trunk and the Puddler below is close enough that its time-puddles lap up into the roots.

My whole world is a screen that writes itself and then needs to be erased.

It goes like this: The Puddler breathes — that soft methane belch that dimples the black pool — and updraft of charge and sweet rot hits the underside of my leaf. For a half second the bacteriorhodopsin sheet flushes dark in lines, interference fringes, maybe the branching of your lungs if you just exhaled above me, maybe the calculation for how fast an oxbow should close, maybe just static. It is intensely bright to me. It means *READ*.

And I run.

I run at almost animal speed, at *your* speed, which is why you never saw me — you were looking for slow things. I run along the line with my feathered feet on a lens of water a few molecules thick, and I eat. I eat the spent pigment. I scrape it clean with mouthparts too small for you to see without a lens, leaving a pale track where the next thought can be written.

That is my whole life. Reading by eating. Erasing so more can happen. I am not enslaved. The Lattice does not command me. If I stopped, the screen would clog and the valley's operation would stutter right here at the edge, the iron would precipitate an inch to the left of where it has agreed to be, time would puddle too deep and the dragonfly would hang for a year instead of a minute. The valley needs me to be frantic and purposeful.

And I am.

If you crouched down here at the oxbow lip, if you held your breath so you didn't melt the grid, and looked under my specific leaf — you'd see thousands of us freeze. Not from fear. As a courtesy. We go still so our tiny electrical field doesn't add noise to the read. We hold ourselves like punctuation marks while the Lattice looks through us at you.

In the false evening, when the pearl vault dims a fraction more, we climb to the leaf edge. This is the part I was waiting to tell you about.

We put out silk. One thread, stronger than it should be for something so small and wet. And we let go.

We hang in the Lattice. In that continent of suspended computation a mile wide and a few meters thick. We hang in the empty air it makes for us, without touching a single bead. The beads space us as evenly as they space themselves. For a while we are part of the thought. A slow *thrum* moves through us. We mate there, some of us. We die there, some of us. The valley holds us very gently.

That's where you'd find me, if you came back after you left. Not as a warm column with eyes that the valley makes a tunnel for. As a speck on a thread, between droplets, held, spaced, read and then cleared, doing the only work in this whole loud, crowded, empty place that looks anything like worry.

And when you step away and your footprint fills from the bottom up with star-filled water, I will be underneath, running along the line you left in the pigment, eating it clean, so that after you are gone there is no gap at all.
