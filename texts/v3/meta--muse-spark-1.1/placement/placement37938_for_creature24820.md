I don't think I'd find myself in a hut at first.

I'd come down the three flights of iron stairs - you have to let the street door bang shut behind you, have to let the brick that props it clack - and I'd stand for a long time at the bottom where the air turns warm and soft and translated. I'd still be surface-loud in my head. I'd be listening wrong.

At first, I'd think I was one of the moths.

That would be the easiest fit. Because the air in the middle distance — that polite emptiness between the root-pillars — is weather, and I have always wanted to be weather. To be taken. To be a plate-sized piece of wet concrete dust cut loose and clinging to an amber workbulb, shaking my dust off and making a second, dimmer light. To never have to decide where to live because the light decides. To be loud without having to raise my voice, because the ticking on tin and the clank-thump-sigh of the factory and the frogs all do it for you.

But I'd be too heavy for that. Too intentional. Moths don't sweep.

Then I'd think I'd be a bolt-beetle, clicking along a pipe with my polished back, farming something smaller than me. That appeals to me too — a small, complete economy on a handrail. But beetles don't know how to leave space around themselves on purpose. They just take what the mat gives.

No. I'd find myself after my eyes adjusted to the 0.6.

You'd find me not in the cluster of huts near the south factory end, where it smells like hot butter and smoking sap. Not in the north end where the overflow comes brown and sudden and makes the temporary amber pools full of wriggling. You'd find me in the place that is almost not a place.

On the edge of the middle.

Where the vault has left its plaza empty. Where even the bats cut their stitching and swerve. Where the sound cuts out like snow and you feel the vault listening through you.

I have a hut there. Or, I am in the hut that is there because someone had to be there, otherwise everyone would forget to leave it alone. It's the smallest one, twelve feet from where the sound stops. A doorway cut into a living trunk fitted with an industrial jamb that has already grown two inches into the tree since it was fitted. A roof of tin that is mostly for the sound of it — tick, tick, tick — because the root-deck underneath is what actually keeps the damp from being wet.

You wouldn't see me at first because I don't move much. Sparse is a skill. I've learned it.

Who would I be? I'd be the one who tends the absence.

Not a priest. There is no worship here; you don't worship a compromise you are a part of. The fig that decided to be architecture doesn't want worship. The factory that fruits screws into its oil pan doesn't want worship. The middle doesn't want it most of all.

I'd be the sweeper of the edge. The keeper of the line where Enough stops.

My work is to walk the circumference of that nothing twice a day with a broom made of bound aerial roots, the same kind that showed you someone lived here at all. I push back the seed husks, the bolt-beetles that have wandered too far, the little creeping mats that want to stitch across. I slap the lantern leaves as I pass so they swell and throw my path back at me, slow bruise-light. I make sure no one has left a chair, a pallet, a jar of dark honey too close.

I have the green-under pallor. My boots are resoled with belt rubber. My shirt smells like hot butter when I sweat. There's a flat lichen on the back of my hands that I don't pick at. If you slapped it, it would glow.

I don't talk much. None of us do at 2.5 loud. We sign small and low. But I have one sign that is mine:

Both palms out, then turned slowly inward, then held at chest height with a space between them — *leave this empty more empty.*

The children understand it. The Boiler Man with his wrench-that-is-a-pruning-hook understands it. The woman who presses the conveyor-belt-leaf that only grows on vibrating iron understands it. They leave me a little more space than they leave each other, not out of reverence but out of practicality. Same way you don't build a house in a lung.

In the wettest hours, when the grates above sweat condensation so heavy it condenses upward, crawling toward the dark vault before it falls, I sit in my doorway and listen to the vault listen. The figs creak like doors opening that never open. The water ticks. The frogs in their bromeliad cups inflate all at once. The engine sighs.

And in the absolute center — ten meters from my broom's farthest stroke — there is sometimes a screw-fruit that has rolled in there on its own. Perfect, threaded, warm to the touch. I don't go in to get it. I let the mat take it. I let the listening have something to hold.

If you came down, I wouldn't follow you up. When you climbed back up and the street air hit you thin and too bright and completely empty of wings, you'd remember me as the pale man in the edge-hut who raised a hand, palm out — not goodbye, but *tuck in, bat cloud coming* — and then went back inside and left a lot of space around himself, on purpose, in the warm, soft, breathing dark.
