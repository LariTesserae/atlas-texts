I thought I'd be high.

That's where you put yourself at first when you hear "top of the world" — out near the edge, looking over, wind in your face. But I can't be there. The edge here isn't a place you get to. The mist is always doing something between you and it. So I'm not there.

First I find the air. I thought I might be something light enough to live in it. One of the salt-white gliders that drink off the wires. But I'm too heavy for that, my breathing is too loud. This air is thin and heavy at the same time. It has weight. It sits on your shoulders like a wet coat. If I were a glider I wouldn't feel my own lungs remind me with every third breath that I'm high up. So not that.

Then I thought, water. Because so much of this place is water that never decided to be rain. I thought I might be the water bead on basalt, or the trickle from an overhang. But water here isn't still enough to be me. It's always being asked to go somewhere else. Someone taps it with a brass rod and marks where it wants to go next with chalk.

So then I thought, low. Lower than I thought. Under the walkway that is over the street that is over the canal that is over the lower street. Where it's warm.

I can hear where I am before I know what I am. The thrum that never stops. The forge that hammers once every six minutes and has for eighty years. Water gargling in four different kinds of gutter. And right above that, the clatter. A wide, flat clatter, claw on wet stone. Chitin ticking on iron.

I look down — or, the part of me that looks down finally arrives — and there they are.

My hands.

They are broad. Not built for anything fine. The knuckles are thick and in the cracks of them there is a grey-green shadow that won't come out. I scrubbed it this morning with the wire brush by the boiler house door. It's back by midday. Under the nails, too. And a little itch that you stop noticing, the white salt lice moving through it. My skin never dries. It shines. The back of my left hand has a raised, moon-shaped scar, pale against freckled pink. The kind a crab leaves when it hooks you by accident, not meaning anything by it.

I'm wearing canvas that used to be some other coat twenty years ago, rubberized at the shoulders. The collar is high because otherwise the glass-flies go down your neck. My boots are too big on purpose. I can feel water slosh in them, warm from the stone underneath.

I am not young, but I am not yet keeping my head bald on purpose. I still have hair, bound up tight under an oilskin cap. It will never dry otherwise. 

I think at first I must be Waterfolk. I have the broom. It is flat and wide and I am standing in something that is not a lake the way lowland people mean lake, but a pan — an inch, two inches of clear water over stone so smooth it looks like glass, so big it holds the whole sky and the underside of the factories and the gantry rows. I have been sweeping silt off the film that lives on the rock. The film that is our field. An eighth of an inch a year. That would be Waterfolk work.

But I keep listening past my broom.

There is another sound over the clatter and the water. A high, almost-not-there singing. The powerlines. They leak blue in the damp after dark.

Ah.

I'm not in the pan in the day. I'm on the gantry at night. I'm one of the Listeners.

Or I was Waterfolk and I accreted into this, the way everything here accretes instead of grows. That's how it happens. You get to be thirty and you can't hear the culvert song anymore because the dynamo hum has taken the high tones out of you. So they give you the long earthed pole and send you up.

So that's where you'd find me.

Two districts over from the slag heaps — I can smell them sweet and hot, even up here — on the third level of gantry where the old basalt block meets the poured stone. It's second night-shift. The mist has come in thicker, which makes the insulators leak more. The dark is orange from forge-light below and blue where it leaks. I walk slow. If you hurry up here you slip, you put your hand where a glasswing moth has laid its acid eggs, you miss the change.

I stop where one of the stone crabs has decided to sleep on the warm rail. She's an old Urajara, wide as a manhole, pale as boiled bone, with a rust-beard I should have shaved last week. She is humming slightly in her shell. That is not a metaphor. They hum when warm. I lean my pole against the rail and lean my weight against her, hand on the cool dome of her carapace. That's the work. She is not my pet, no one owns them. But someone has to get her off the rails before the ore lift comes. Someone has to talk to her while she decides to move.

"Come on, grandmother," I say, but I don't say it out loud, because my voice would be lost under the thrum anyway. My mouth is wide and still coarse from shouting when I was young. Now I speak mostly with my hands. The fast, wet clapping language you can read through misted goggles. I sign to the other Listener two gantries over: *crab on line, wait.*

He signs back: *seen. blue on 9.*

I look. Insulator nine is leaking more than it should, a little crown of St. Elmo's fire, hissing. That means a bearing two districts over is about to seize, or water has gotten in where water has finally decided it wants to go. I tap the crab, slow. She sighs through some valve on her underside and starts to turn, scraping rust from the rail with her mouth in the same motion. Leaving it clean. She will detour a mile to get back to her favorite boiler house roof and it will take her all night. I will still be here.

That is who I would be.

Not a builder, not a traveler. One of the people who learned the song of the thing that is already there. A maintainer. I keep a cooled worm-brick in my coat pocket because it holds heat. I have a piece of chalk for where water wants to go, even though that's not technically my job anymore. I take off my wet coat at the end of shift and hang it over a warm pipe where the pipe things will pick it clean by morning.

And when I'm done I go down to the low roof that is also a road and gather with the others, shoulder to shoulder, and we watch the other city turn above us — the slow, state-sized spiral of living weather, breathing. We feel its wind on our shining faces.

Then we go inside to sleep to the sound of everything dripping and hammering and chittering and humming. Stone to skin to water to rust to chitin. Nothing alone.
