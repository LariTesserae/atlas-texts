I wouldn't find myself right away.

If you dropped me on that ridge — that last idea of a mountain — I would miss it at first too. I'd be standing on the black glass in that indigo noon, in that cellular cold that tells you exactly how many bolts you have loose, looking for a town and seeing only a dark line you could step over. I'd think I was meant to be above it. The lookout. The one who keeps the cable untangled, maybe. The one who listens for the helicopter that comes once a year.

But nothing survives alone up there. Not even for an hour. The windless cold would find the gap between my collar and my skin and I'd have to get down.

So I'd take the cable. Hand over hand, the knots are placed for someone shorter than me.

I thought at first I'd be one of the people. That would be the easy story. I'd be the maintenance person with the black second-knuckle gloves, tending two square meters of bench and making nameless dog-bone brackets with two holes. I could do that. I know how to make a thing whose only job is to hold another thing in place for a little longer.

But then I'm down in it, shoulder-close, ceiling-close, and I realize I don't have the right kind of patience for brackets. I hear the town breathing shallow, I hear the tap-code — *tap tap: hold that* — and I realize what is actually being maintained isn't the scaffolding at all.

It's the interval. The agreement to stay dense.

So I go lower. Not to the bottom, where the rock-lice work in their fast, cold panic and the air drops back to -15C. Not to the top, where the choughs steal insulation and scream once to save air.

I'd settle in the middle third. Where the heat gathers. Where -2C can pass for warm if you have enough neighbors. On the lee wall, where the old rust-pale scaffold has been swallowed and become foundation for the new yellow scaffold, and there is a junction box that doesn't close properly anymore.

You'd find me there, behind the light.

I would be the amber 40-watt in its cage.

No — not the bulb. The socket. The ceramic socket that has been unscrewed and re-screwed so many times its threads are gone and it's held in with wire and faith. I'm on during the day-circuit, from 05:30 when the generator thrums awake through the rock like a low heart, until it clicks off to save fuel. When I'm on, I am crowded. A soot-moth lands on me, big as a hand, her felted, smoke-black wings wrapping my cage to drink the heat off me. Then another. Then a third. They don't burn, they are exactly as hot as they need to be. I tick. They shiver.

When a bad-air day comes, they don't come, and the people look up at me and know.

When the generator goes off, I go cold instantly — the thin air can't hold any memory of warmth — and a bat folds herself up inside the junction box behind me, tucking her oil-mopped feathered feet around my ceramic for the left-over trace of it. She leaves a smear of guano that the boy with the putty knife will collect at dusk.

I would be that kind of thing. Not a person — people pass through, none are born there anymore — but one of the parts that was meant to be replaceable and then wasn't. A piece of infrastructure that learned to be habitat.

A shim. A socket. A maintained thing that ended up doing the maintaining.

From above, on the lip, you would not see me at all. Just the black glass and a dark line. Which is how I'd know I was doing it right.

I would be the fissure's way of convincing light to stay a little longer.
