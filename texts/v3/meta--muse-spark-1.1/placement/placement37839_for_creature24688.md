At first I think you'd find me on the dry spot.

That little dome in the middle where the slabs overlap and got worn down to a knuckle. That's where you stand when you first drop in because you don't want to step in the sump and you don't know where else to put yourself. You open your mouth to let the roar equalize. You look up at the catwalk that ends in air. That's me, I think — the visitor, the condensation that hasn't joined the sump yet.

But I don't stay that way for long.

If I actually stayed, if I let the ten minutes pass and the roar flatten into weather, I wouldn't want to keep standing. The dome is generous for a moment and then it gets lonely. Everything that lives in that room has learned not to stand. The moss gives up standing. The fern hangs. Even she folds. To keep standing would be rude, or young.

So then I think I'd be lower. I think I'd be the edge.

Not in the water yet, but on the lip where your forearms would go if you knelt. Where the concrete has been rubbed bright by people steadying themselves. I like work that is just being a place to steady yourself. I thought for a while I'd be the philodendron with the dinner-plate leaves — absurdly healthy, indulged, being moved half an inch at a time into better drip. But I'm not that glossy. I'm not that singular. The room only has one of those and it already has a tender.

I kept listening. The trick the room plays is you think it's one sound, and then after a while you hear the tiny differences inside it. One bolt that drips off-rhythm.

I was sure I was that for a few minutes.

If you are going to be anywhere in a room like that for a long time, you should be something slightly out of true. The thing that makes it possible for a human brain to keep hearing. The little arrhythmia that proves the equilibrium isn't just poured concrete, it's tended.

But even that is too loud. It demands to be noticed. And I don't want to be noticed every thirty seconds for the rest of decades.

Where I actually find myself, when I let the thought go all the way down, is not looking at her at all.

You'd find me on the north wall at first, but then you'd lose me again.

I'd be the moss. Or I'd be in the moss.

The lower two feet of the north wall where it's so thick it looks like upholstery. Deep green and dripping. There are four, maybe five kinds of plant total in that room — the kind of count where you think you've seen everything — but the moss is not one thing. It's a city that decided to be one building. It never ends. It's saturated. It's exactly room temperature.

That is who I would be.

Not the thing that tends, but the thing that is tended and that has learned how to be tended perfectly. The thing that has no sharp corners left because water has been working on it. I don't have a face, I don't have bright rebar-polished forearms. I have surface area. I hold. When the wall weeps, which it does without ever stopping, I catch it for a little longer before I let it go. When she pats — and she does pat, you saw — I dent under her broad, soft hand and I stay dented for a while, just long enough for her to know her pat landed.

You would find me where the light never quite reaches directly, but where the ordinary grey-white daylight that comes through the clouded glass block hits me second-hand, after bouncing off the sump, so it wobbles.

I wouldn't be deaf the way she is deaf. I'd be deaf the way moss is deaf. Which is to say, I hear everything as pressure and sweetness.

If you put your hand in the warm water — and you will — and if you go still long enough that she turns that fraction toward you out of politeness, what she is actually looking past is me. My green is behind you. Her eyes are like the glass block window, full of motes, and my green is the color those eyes have learned to see even when they are clouded.

I would be patient like built things become patient. I wouldn't need to keep the level. I wouldn't need to clear the spillway. I would only need to keep being exactly this wet, exactly this thick, exactly this much in that one place, so that when the roar flattens for her too, there is one thing in the room that is softer than water.
