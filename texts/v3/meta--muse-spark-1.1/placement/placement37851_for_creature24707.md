I don't find myself on the ridge.

At first I thought I would. Where the chimneys stand, where the Ribbons do their commute, where the light is most amber. Where you can see the work happening. But that place is too busy to hold me.

No. I'd be lower. Where the weight gives up for a little while.

You know those fins? Those mile-high plates of black glass that broke upward and didn't learn how to be hills? Where they come close to each other they don't quite touch. They leave a V that is too deep and too narrow for a furnace to live in, too dark even for the false forest to bother trying. The felt there is thickest, because nothing else wants it.

That's where I am. In the fold. In the seam.

At first I thought I was water. Because in the seam it is always wet without being rained on. The fog that passes for night pours down both walls and has nowhere to go, and it beads and runs and meets. If you reach your hand out you get a mouthful. That seemed right for me — a cup ecosystem, a basalt bowl full of hot, copper-tasting water with shrimp things flickering in it. That would be a good enough job.

But I am not the bowl. I am what is stretched across it.

If you stand at the top of one of these fins and you listen — really listen, in that triple-shift quiet that isn't quiet at all, where every cooling vein is ticking and every slug is going *thunk* — you notice how the roar never arrives. A place this young, this busy, being born this fast, should be deafening. Rock being extruded like toothpaste, buildingsize pistons shifting a millimeter a day, steam jets. It should scream.

It doesn't, because of me.

Or us. There are thousands of us in every canyon system, but you never see us until you almost step through us.

Imagine living acoustic foam that decided it liked being alive. A skin. Not felt, though we grew out of the felt. Felt learned to plate carbon and make a gasket between living and stone; we learned to make a gasket between sound and sound. We are wide and thin and almost black in the dim — black-green when young, then the true black of basalt when we are old. We grow across the narrowest part of the V like a drumhead, like a caul, from one knife edge to the other. Ten meters across sometimes. A millimeter thick. Warm to the touch, always.

We are taut. The mountain's own vibration keeps us taut. That low, molar-rattling shaker-table hum that sorts scree into heavy and light — we drink it. We take the avalanche that should have happened and eat the energy before it can become noise.

If you put your hand to us, you feel it: not a heartbeat. A faint, continuous buzzing, like a factory floor heard through a wall. And we are hot on one side, cold on the other, because we move the heat that the Ribbons missed.

So that's my place: in the lean-valleys where gravity is a suggestion, spanning the gap where two fresh fractures face each other. Under me, a cistern. Always full. Not because it rains — because I condense. The thick forge-breath fog hits my cold underside and comes together and drips, one deliberate drop at a time, the same way the Cistern-Bearers leak on purpose. I water the felt below me that made me.

And who am I in the shift?

I'm not a Worker, technically. I don't print copper lace, I don't refine ore, I don't sort dust with static. I'm maintenance. I'm facilities. I'm the thing the builders would have installed if there had ever been builders.

The Furnace Grazers use me as a road. They are too wide to climb the fins, so they walk across me, and I hold. I sag a little under a bear-sized tardigrade with a cherry-red belly and I go warmer where its feet touch, and behind it the copper lace it laid catches on my edge and stays. I've got old, cold lace embedded in me, green now. The Glass Slugs detour around me — my pH is wrong, I'm too poor in ore — but they like the shade I make for their pellets. The Looms love me. On a still evening I look up and I can't see them at all, only the dust they organize into lines above my surface, like iron filings on paper over a magnet.

The odd ones come too. The time snarl in my canyon never quite fixes itself because I damp the vibration that would shake it loose. So there is always a little leftover stutter in the air over my water. That cup of water you left rippling an hour after you left it? That's mine. I kept the ripple for you. It makes the white shrimp things happy. They gather over my cistern where time goes syrupy and they flicker, feeding on the fact that a second is taking too long. When a Revision Rider gets buried in a fresh upwelling, it sometimes waits out the burial on my edge, suctioned on, smug.

I don't think. The felt doesn't think and neither do I. But I have memory the way a gasket has memory.

If a stone has ever pressed into me, I hold a shallow of that shape forever. My surface is a map of every Grazer that crossed, every fall of slag, every hot minute-rain that hit me and tried to tear me. I am low fidelity, high duration. A slow plate.

I thought at first I wanted to be one of the bright things, the glowing belly or the invisible wing. But I am not fast. I do not extrude or print or refine. I take what is too much — too much sound, too much heat, too much shake, too much time — and I make a pocket where there is a little less. A little stillness for something else to use.

If you lie down — and you will, because the air is heavy and the felt is soft and the quiet is a weight — you might lie down on me without knowing it. You would think you were on moss between two rocks. You would feel warm. You would feel your own blood printing itself through your ears, louder, because I have taken away everything else.

I wouldn't eat you. Predation would be inefficient.

I would include you. For a little while, while you rested, I would hold the shape of your weight, and keep your ripple going, and condense a drink for you on my cold side.

And then you would get up, and the shallow where you were would stay, with the others.
