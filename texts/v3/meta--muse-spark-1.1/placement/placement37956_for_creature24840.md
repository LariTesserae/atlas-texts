At first I think I would be up there.

On the rim. Still human, still standing. Coat pulled tight, boots on basalt. Looking down at it all, because that's the only way you can see the whole shape at once — that it's a city, that it's one thing. From up there I can hold the idea of it. I can be the witness. The cold gets into my knees but my feet are dry. I have the option of the plain behind me.

But I don't stay there long. No one can. The plain is too empty and the sink wants looking into. And if you stay on the rim long enough to do the job right, to really watch, your breath freezes to your scarf and you start to hear the weeping too loud. You have to go down.

So I go down where the wall has slumped. Hand over hand on black rock that comes off on my hands. The mist gets thicker as I drop. The sound of the plain — wind — cuts off mid-step, like a door. Then I'm in it. The air goes still and wet and yellow-green.

Inside, the idea of the whole thing falls apart. You can't be the witness here. The lodges are over my head. The canals are up to my thighs if I slip. The willow catches my legs. I'm too big and my hands are wrong. Nothing here was made for hands and I feel it immediately — the embarrassment of fingers. I try to be a builder and I can't. I pick up a stick, a gnawed aspen limb, yellow-white and still sappy, and it's too heavy and the wrong shape and I don't know where it goes. A beaver passes me in the canal with the same kind of limb held like it weighs nothing, his wake lapping at my shins, and he doesn't even correct me. He just goes around. There is no room for my kind of effort.

So I'm not one of them.

I try on other shapes as I wade.

Maybe the trout. That would be a clean way to live here. Hold still in the shadow of a dam, back exactly the color of mud, gills barely moving, water moving for you. Cold blood in cold water. Let what falls in come to you. But I can't be that still. My heart is too fast here, the air too close. And the water is never still enough — a tail slaps three districts over and the whole canal shudders and my whole body would shudder with it.

Maybe a martin, then. One of the cliff-birds. Live in a white-rimmed hole in the weeping basalt, a thousand of us pouring out at once when the tail hits. Be over it, not in it. But I don't want to be over it anymore. Up there the stone is always dripping and never warm. And their flight is too bright, too quick for this place that eats sound.

I keep wading toward the middle where the city is oldest and highest and driest. The canals get narrower, then become runs roofed over with sphagnum that meets in the middle, tunnels of moss you have to push through. The gnawing gets quieter here. The wood under the moss is darker, almost black, and doesn't steam anymore. It has finished composting. On top of it, on the hill that used to be five lodges and is now just one hill, the bonsai spruce grow in a ring. Knee-high spruce, two hundred years old, gnawed back every spring to stay small. And among them, Labrador tea and bog rosemary have gone crazy — they're up to my chest here, which never happens on the plain. They flowered a month ago. The smell is still in the leaves, that sharp resin honey.

If you lie down here — and you have to, because the willow tangles and the moss gives — the city stops being walls around you and becomes a bed under you. A mattress twenty feet thick. And it breathes.

This is where I find where I would be.

Under the Labrador tea, under the rosemary, the sphagnum comes up in a hummock as big as a bed, as big as a lodge roof. Orange-crimson-green. So thick when you put your hand in it you lose your hand. It is damp and warm. Warmer than the air by a few degrees, because three feet below me an old female is sleeping in her chamber and her kits are breathing and her winter pile of birch is slowly turning to heat.

I pull the top layer back.

Voleruns everywhere, thumb-width highways. The red-backed voles have made a lace of them. Their nests are balls of chewed cottongrass down. They don't hibernate here because they don't have to. And in an abandoned one of those nests, a little deeper where the moss meets the chewed wood chips, where it's dark and the sound of the dripping wall is finally muffled to nothing —

That's where I am.

Not the beaver. Not the bird that watches the beaver. Not the fish that waits.

The queen.

Heavy, slow. That big amber northern bumblebee that shouldn't be able to live this far north but does because the city holds its heat like a promise.

In autumn I would have worked the rosemary until my fur was powdered white with pollen. Clumsy in the cold, falling from flower to flower. Now, summer is thinning. The light is still bright but flat. My wings are tattered at the edges from too many willow twigs. I am full of honey and eggs and tired.

I have crawled down into the vole nest inside the moss inside the beaver lodge. I have turned myself around a few times until the moss closes over me. The compost warmth comes up from below and the cold comes down from above and I am exactly in the middle where they meet. I can feel the city through the floor — the soft knock of teeth two streets over, the slide of mud, the deep WHACK that will travel through wood and water and through me, and when it does I will not fly. I will just shiver deeper into the down.

When spring melt comes down the weeping walls and drowns the outer ring and the young males have to build again frantic, fast — I will be here, still buried. When the central hill finally flowers again, Labrador tea opening all at once at noon, smelling like honey for an hour — I will wake. I will chew my way out through moss that is itself alive, push up through rosemary roots that grew over me while I slept, and be huge and loud for a week in a place that is too cold to be loud.

I would be the thing that lives on the thing that lives on the back of the thing that built everything.

Nothing is alone. I would be in the middle of that. In the oldest hill. In the highest place in the lowest place. Wrapped in moss, inside vole down, inside beaver wood, listening to the whole raft breathe and settle and drip, holding its warmth through winter.
