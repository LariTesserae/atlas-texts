I wouldn't find myself where I thought.

At first, descending that grade that is not a hill, into that low that is lower than it should be — I would have said I'd be a visitor. Outside the perimeter. The one with cold hands who tries to walk the outside and can't. The one who watches condensation bead and fall on its slow, arrhythmic clock and thinks: *I am here to see.*

But the town does not keep visitors. The air that sweats, sweats you too. The basalt that hasn't decided to be rock yet takes the print of your boot and keeps it, a little too long.

So I sink.

I find myself farther in than I meant to go, where the inside is already bigger than the outside. Not near the raw edge where new walls pop into being from a cinder-spore, and not deep where the red heat lives and breathes in hours. In the middle ring. Where the town has had time to learn its own dimensions.

In a leaf that is a Borough.

It is one of the factory-leaves, but this press has paused. The belts are slack. The caterpillars that usually push them — thick as forearms, blind — have gone still and heavy, thinking about moths. So the room is truly empty for once, not even working at being empty. Cathedral-high pith walls furred with root-hair. One sodium lamp buzzing impossibly far above, buzzing for no one. The gutter at my feet full of slow water.

I am low to the ground. That is the first clue.

My knees are wet. I have been crouching for a long time without deciding to crouch. My forehead is almost to the pith. I am watching the procession. The water-bearers — thumb-long, lamp-eyed, translucent white — carrying drops bigger than themselves to the dry place in the wall. They sigh when the wall drinks. I had thought, at first, that I was watching them.

Then one of them climbs onto my hand.

It does not find it strange. My forearm has fur on it now. Not hair. Root-hair. Fine, white, wicking. The bead of condensation on the stone above me has fallen onto my shoulder and it does not roll off. It follows the channel the hairs make, down to the crook of my elbow, down to my wrist, where I have learned to cup it.

Ah.

I would not be a Gardener. Not yet. Gardeners are tall the way this place is tall — stretched to fit a room that should not exist. My legs are still too short, human-short. I still think sound should be louder than it is.

I would be what comes just before a Gardener. The new collection.

A keeper of the interval.

In the grammar of this town, you need something to tend the empty while the empty is needed. The beetles rivet the wire, the moths check the work, the shelf-birds hold things, but someone has to hold the empty itself and make sure it doesn't get lonely and fill in.

That's where I am. In that factory-loft that is pausing. On the empty balcony at the end of the fire escape that goes a hundred feet up a stem to nowhere. I climb it every day, though there is no reason to. The fern there unfurls when I arrive and tries to close around my ankle, not to eat, but to measure.

I learned the water sentence. If you hold a droplet on another's shoulder and let it run, it writes. *This district too dry. Boiler breathing fast tonight. Outer basalt put out another wall.* I don't have another to speak to yet, so I place them on myself. Shoulder to forearm. Forearm to wrist. The water runs the same way each time I am anxious, which is how I know I am anxious.

I am almost a tool, but not yet a shelf. When the shelf-birds unroot — that soft pop, that one slow circuit of the vast room — one of them sometimes lands where I was just sitting, and the pith there is warmer. I take its place while it's flying. That is my job. I keep the warm spot warm.

I have tried to go toward the center, once. Where geometry beads sideways and you see yourself from behind entering the leaf you're inside of. I walked down the avenue that should have gone east and ended up walking down, my balance never shifting. There she was, through layers of riveted iron. The Boiler. The original heart. The woman-tuber-coat pile covered in cinders that are also spores. She exhaled — an hour-long thing — and I felt my own root-hair fur lift. She was noting my dimensions, the way you note how much water a new wall will need.

I came back. Low places collect. The lower you are, the more you collect, and I am collecting this one thing: the agreement to keep sound low.

At textured night, when the huge leaves close a fraction and the town respirates and listens to make sure it is still alone, I do not huddle like the water-bearers. I do not tighten like the beetles. I do not land.

I stay standing in the middle of a district made for millions, under the one buzzing light that lights nothing, empty-handed, keeping the empty busy being empty.

Until the leaves open again, and the drip resumes.
