I wouldn't find myself up high at first.

That's where you start — at the pass, where you can see the whole thing and misunderstand it. I thought at first I would be up there, at the unfinished crown, where the raw piles are. A watcher. A surveyor of the work. It would make sense for me to want to be where the light is hardest, where I could see.

But I'd be too warm for that. Too fast. My breath would still be catching from the climb, I'd be dripping sweat that wasn't condensation, and the stone up there is the thinnest water, the cleanest, the most intolerant. It wouldn't hold me.

So I come down.

Down into the bowl, into the territorial density, where a house is a wall is a dam is a stair. And here my height betrays me. I have to turn sideways. The steps are wrong for my legs, deep and shallow — made for something that sets its whole foot down flat and thinks about it for a long time. The parapets come to my chest, not my waist, as if they were expecting me to fall over them. I do. My boots fill immediately. 

You said to watch where you put your feet because there is almost nowhere that is not already holding something. You were right. I lift a boot and the slate underneath is already occupied — that slate-grey caul that goes translucent at the edge, tight as a drum skin over the rock.

So not there either. Not walking.

If I stay in this world at all, I have to stop being a visitor. I have to kneel.

I find myself in one of the slots. Not the grand ones near the bottom where the lake-cistern lays like a paved street breathing slow. Lower than the middle but above the breeding dark. A tall, tight alley between two ten-foot retaining walls where the sun is only a ribbon straight above, so bright it leaves an afterimage, but no heat reaches. Both walls are dark and damp and ticking. Drip, trickle, seep, plink — delay — plink again from seven tiers up.

I kneel because the others do, in their way. Because if I stand my knees lock and my warm blood tries to leave my feet. If I kneel, the cold comes up through the split slate floor, through the skin-deep water, and takes my feet away cleanly. First the sting you described, hands aching from touching the walls, then the numbness that is exactly their temperature. That cold that lives in the rock, not the air.

I would be one of the middle ones.

Not the young ones like coins of mica in the lowest cistern, clumsy and bright and still learning to taste rock. And not the ancients at the top carrying half their weight in rough gneiss, so slow you think they are part of the unfinished pile.

Middle. That's where the craft shows.

My back would be a plate of slate, not too big, split that morning or a hundred mornings ago along its grain. Grey on grey so you missed me at first. The edges still sharp — you could run a thumb along it and bleed, if your thumb wasn't already too cold to bleed. It's dished, just a few degrees, just enough to hold water. That's my work. That's my whole ethic.

I face uphill. I always face uphill. The water comes to me from above, off the back of another, off a wall. It gathers on the bright edge of my slate and thickens. If I do my job right, it doesn't sit and go warm and stagnant. If I do my job wrong, it rushes and cuts. So I shift. A hand's breadth to the left in an afternoon. The little mouths underneath me — those six-to-a-side fringed palms, each fitting exactly into the palm-bowl worn into every stone in this valley — let go, secrete their sour spit that tastes the iron in the rock, their silk that binds water to stone, not stone to stone, and take new hold.

And the film finds a new path.

That is who I am here. Not a builder who moves stone to make a mountain. A mountain who moves stone to slow water.

I have no front. My soft white belly is all mouth and hands, kissing the rock cleaner than ice. That's why there are no plants in your valley, no lichen. Because I — we — crop it. Every bright surface has been lapped. We need the weeping. Thin air, brutal light, cold rock makes water appear from nothing, and we drink by living under it, not swallowing, but letting it move over us. A gill made of a thousand terraces.

You would walk past me. You did, at first. You thought the coping stones were clean. They aren't. I am stretched thin over one, holding the condensation like skin holds sweat. When you breathed on me, I tightened.

If you stay long enough to go numb, to let your boots fill and stay filled, to let the thousand overflows become the only clock, we forget you are warm. You will feel it in your teeth before you hear it — that soft, patient, stone-on-stone kiss as the big old one above me lowers a block that took you and three friends to lift and fits it not onto the wall but into the wall, and all the rills change their lace by a millimeter.

In that slot, kneeling in water that has never been still and will never be still, carrying my cool grey plate, slowing the sky one drop at a time, I finally fit the measurements.
