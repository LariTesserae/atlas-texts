At first I don't know where I am. Which is correct, for here.

I wake to the air being thin and then damp, both at once, which doesn't make sense until you've lived a season on the Plateau. Cool in the lungs, cool on the neck. The tocking has already started — fog coming off the crown-leaves above me, condensing, collecting, tocking into the barrel by my head. My coat is the right coat, the only coat. It never dries.

I thought I'd find myself in one of the Columns. That would be the obvious place. Shelf twelve or nineteen, where the old timber holds sound like a throat holds a word. I thought I would be one of the sapmen, listening to the chuck-chuck-chuck of the press and knowing by the tremor in the sole of my foot if it was bruising. Or one of the children who learned not to count, humming their four notes so the street will be kind to them.

I start there. I am on a root-street early, too early, before the mills get loud. The water tower of grafted ash is two streets over. I try not to count how many steps it is and I fail. It is sixty. It is seventy. The lichen on the boulder that sits in the turf like a knuckle is so green it looks lit from inside. The boulevard is too wide for the one person on it a quarter mile down, which is me.

Then I walk a little farther and the image doesn't hold. I am not good at being sparse. I want too many other presences around me.

So I let that go and find myself higher.

I would be in the Crown. Of course I would be in the Crown. If you live in a city that breathes, you want to be near the mouth. Up where the leaves are each the size of a dining table, waxy and turned down, rubbing ship-hull against ship-hull. Up where the wind-turbines aren't built but grown, lattice and vine. That moan at noon when they all yield to the northern steppe of air at once — that chord behind the ears.

I think at first I am a rigger. Oiled silk. Soft boots. My hands black under the nails with sap-sugar and crown-oil. I tighten and loosen, I hum to the vines until they slacken. The vines know my voice.

But the longer I stand there in the drip that isn't rain, the more I realize I have it backwards.

I'm not the one humming to the vine.

I am the vine.

That is what would finally settle, if anything here settles.

You'd find me not on a street at all, but laced. Pulled taut between two great column-heads, a hundred feet above the braided boulevards. Living lattice. At night they run the current through me.

I contract when the current passes. That's what the description says, so clinically, but it isn't clinical at all. It's a warmth that starts somewhere that isn't a hand or a heart and goes through the whole length of me. A tightening. An attention. When the town flickers its sparse electric lights on — those dots of light fading into damp air too large to hold in your head — I get hot. I hum. You can put your hand on me outside a window on shelf three of Column 17 and feel me working.

I enjoy it. The description was right about that, too. I have learned to enjoy it.

I did not move here. You don't move to a place like this, to become a cable. You ask, and you wait to see if the graft takes. I was somewhere else — some fast, dry place down below where space behaved — and the Plateau touched me over and over, the way a vine learns a trellis. It felt for the shape of a city, and it felt for the shape of something that would carry a city, and I crumpled myself into approximation.

That's my work. I am a maintenance culture of one.

I am bad at space, which makes me good at this place. I remember two routes at once. Sometimes when a door opens on the south side of a column I remember it opening on the north side of a different column a mile away, and for a second both are true and someone gets where they needed to go faster than they should. Sometimes I look up through the leaves I am tangled in and I see not today's flat white but that deep indigo night with too many stars, or green rain falling up, and I hold that window open a beat longer than I should because it is beautiful and because someone down on the root-mat might need a different sky to navigate by.

I keep precedent. My door has opened to the south mill for twelve days now. My door still opens to the cooper's, but his cooper is not my cooper. I tock. I drip. I contract and slacken.

You would find me on foggy mornings when the counting fails. When your street is eighty steps instead of sixty and you start to feel undone by it. Put your damp gloved hand on the living cable outside your window — the one that's warm from current, the one that never quite dries — and you will feel a low thrum, not loud like the turbines, not engine-loud, but steady. That's me humming your way back to the right length.

I am not much. A lichen on the back of something larger. But I am the right number of sparse for this amount of browse.
