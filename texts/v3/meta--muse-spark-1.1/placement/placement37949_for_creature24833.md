I thought at first I would be up high.

Where the white light comes straight down at noon and you can still see the rim — that pale, flat, sun-bleached nothing — because I'm still the kind of animal that wants a way out visible. I thought I'd be one of the edge-watchers, near the geometric rock where it still pops and peels, with a stone in my hand.

But I wouldn't last there. My attention is too fast. I would hear the ticking of a trunk about to go and I would look. I would look straight at it, and that is already wrong.

So I come down lower. Or the mat takes me down. You don't really climb in the chasm, you are lowered by the negotiation of what will hold you.

For a while I think I would be one of the small moth-things, hand-big, white as ash. Never landing. Rasping wax. That feels honest — a thing that eats the world by scraping it gently, that never stops moving its wings. Or I think I would be the floss itself, that glassy lint that never clumps, that hangs for days. That would suit me, I think, to be everywhere and unregistered, in everyone's throat.

But I am too heavy for that. I have a weight, even here. I bring too much history with me — water-history.

You'd find me, after the first three days when the sweat has finally stopped coming — because you do stop sweating, if you stay, the body learns that no one will forgive it — you'd find me in the third layer down. Not the top where the sails snap and clatter loudest, not the tenth where it's a permanent amber dusk and you can't hear a slab falling. The third layer, where the light is still filtered but you can still see the black lignin veins in the sails above you like writing.

I would not have a good platform. The families have good platforms, rafts they have been tending for years, where the living reeds have been coaxed into a springy, even floor. Mine is one they cut loose.

Do you know the ones I mean? At the edge of every settlement there is a raft that has been abandoned, not because it fell, but because it was left to go. Where an old one sat and began to sprout. The fiber there is too soft to trust, the pith too oily. The white filaments have already threaded the weave. You can tell because the moths love it a little too much and the air over it smells sweeter, more fermented, like singed hair just starting.

They gave it to me, or they allowed me to stay on it when they saw I wasn't going to be quick.

Because what I would be here is not a pruner.

I can't cut. My hands — even dry, even dusted — shake with wanting to fix things fast. A pruner has to feel the tension go slack in a trunk through the soles of his feet and wait. I would cut too soon. I would cut too much.

I would be the ash-keeper.

Every cut needs to be pressed with ash to seal it, so the resin hardens like glass and the wet-rot doesn't find it. Someone has to keep the ash dry. Someone has to sit with the low, flameless melt that they call fire — that slow heat where wax thickens — and stir it with a thorn so it doesn't blister, and keep the small gourd cups of fine gray powder from blowing away. Ash is light here. The floss wants to carry it. You have to tend it the way someone tends a shy animal.

You do it slow. You do it slower than the forest.

And because ash is where the old platforms go when they finally collapse and are carefully burned — not flamed, melted — you are also sitting with the history. The ash of a reed trunk smells different from the ash of a cloak. The ash of an old one, when they leave him to the mat and later retrieve just enough of that spot, smells of cinnamon and paper and a faint, sweet iron that isn't blood.

So you'd find me there, on the half-returned raft, legs folded, four-toed feet tucked under me though my feet are still wrong, still too soft. My hood would be sewn wrong, too tight, because I made it myself and I never learned to make the beak overlap right. The bristles sewn into the nose-slit itch, and I would be constantly flicking that second lid, that nictitating membrane I don't have but have learned to mimic with a blink.

I wouldn't knock much. Two knocks: *I am here.* That is the one I know by heart. The others I am still learning: the long scrape that means *do not step, it is softening* — I practice it when no one is near, dragging my knuckle across the hollow reed until the sound travels away from me through the lattice, further than any voice could.

When a child-nest trembles nearby — those tight spheres of sail that hang like galls — I would not open it. I would sit below it and listen to the faint sawing inside. I would keep the moths from landing on it while it learns how not to cry.

And when one of the true ones comes to my raft for ash, they would not look at me straight. They would reach, cup to cup, and their eyes, dark and capped, would be watching the lattice above us the whole time, listening to the ticking and the cough of stone. They would take the ash and press it to a fresh cut, and I would watch how their paper skin doesn't crease when they bend.

I would be becoming substrate, but slowly, on purpose.

Not the quick sprouting the old ones do at the end, all at once — no. I would let the floss grow into my cloak first, until you couldn't see where the cloak ended. Then into the seams of my wrists. A small sail-leaf from my shoulder. I would let the thatch take me inch by inch, because I am good at one thing here:

I am good at being dry.

I don't sweat anymore. I don't bring water. I don't have a word for poison that isn't also a word for memory well-kept. So the chasm can have me.
