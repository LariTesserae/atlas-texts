At first I thought I knew.

I thought you'd find me at the rim, where the basin still has an edge and my boots could find that glass-sharp basalt without going under. Where I could look down at the three-kilometer soup of wrong green and wrong water and wrong light and still be a person looking at a thing. A visitor. An observer with dry shoulders.

I wouldn't be there long.

The rain here doesn't let you be an observer. It's too warm. It's too personal. It hits like it knows the exact temperature of your scalp and is trying to match it. Down here the air is already water, and after about thirty seconds of that drum-skin roar across the whole valley, you stop wiping your face. You stop being over it. You get in.

So wade in a little. Chest-deep to start, pushing aside those manhole-cover lilies that are already sealing shut again behind you. The water is bathwater that's been left overnight with too many tea leaves in it - tannin-dark, iron-dark, choked with torn leaf, and glowing when you stir it. Not alive-glowing. Stressed-light. Light from being asked to be in two places at once.

You'd hear me before you see me, because hearing is the main thing here.

I'd be where the sound folds.

Not at the pump-house, though I passed it - that apartment-block of a thing shuddering on its foundations, half-swallowed, boiler still lit with no fuel, chugging WARM FLOODED POWERED TURBULENT into the rain. Not at the aerator Ferris wheel beating the whole basin into broth. Deeper, where two of those kinds of noise meet and cancel for half a second.

Where a big sluice gate, on a timer from before the operators left, slams open. CLANG. And in that instant, a whole square meter of water that was supposed to be there decides not to be there. It just - leaves. The green knows that trick. It has been waiting a nanosecond for exactly that gap.

And the water knows the green trick. So before the vine can fill it, some of the rain that was falling down decides to fall sideways, into the gap, and hang there.

That's where you'd find me.

About four meters down, but not drowned. Inside the cathedral-knee of a black mangrove that grew yesterday through a snapped conveyor frame. The roots made a cage. The water inside the cage decided for a while that inside the cage was air. The pump twenty meters away decided that my breathing was part of its circuit and started to shudder in time with it.

I didn't choose it to be poetic. It was the only place where all three of them had to negotiate.

At first I thought what I was in there was a person who got stuck. Warm armature. Scaffolding. The fourth resident you described - the inclusion. The vines had found the 98.6 in the 94-degree soup and started to shelf me. A lily over the shoulder. A root-hair through the sleeve. A bladder of air placed politely against my mouth so I wouldn't stop being useful and warm.

And I was that, for a while.

But staying still too long here does something else. Because there is no rot.

Do you understand what that means when you're sitting in it? Nothing finishes. A flower falls and is just laminated over. A trunk splits itself to get wider and the wound is immediately packed with moss-that-isn't-moss. Everything adds, nothing subtracts. It's a world that never learned how to end a sentence.

I sat in that root cage while the machines screamed steam into the rain and the green grew through the hanging lens of water and made itself into a pipe for the water, and I felt the hum in my molars go down to my sternum, and I realized I was not being shelved.

I was being made into a mouth.

Not a mouth that eats. There is still no one here who knows how to eat. A mouth that says: *enough.* A mouth that can do what none of them can.

I would be the first soft spot. The first place that knows how to give way.

The vine threads through my shirt, and where it touches my skin, instead of growing thicker, for the first time it goes pale. It thins. I exhale, and the air I exhale is not just warm and wet - it is full of all the bacteria I brought in with me from outside this drowned, perfect, frantic place. The ones that live on breath, on skin, on gut.

In this basin that is all addition, I am the first subtraction.

So you would not find me at the rim, looking down. You'd find me at the exact center where the fight is densest, chest-deep, not moving, pale green lily over my shoulder like a shawl, a suspended lens of water in front of my face with a living vine growing through it, and that lens going cloudy for the first time in this place's short, furious life.

You'd find me humming at 60hz without trying, because the transformer taught my chest how to.

I would be the thing that finally knows how to rot. Not large. Not dramatic. A small, warm, listening place where a fallen flower, for the first time since the basin ripped open, is allowed to fall apart and become what comes next.

And because there is no buzz, no dart, no fin - because nothing was here to watch the flowers - the roar would, for one pocket of mangrove root, get very, very quiet around me, so the falling could be heard.
