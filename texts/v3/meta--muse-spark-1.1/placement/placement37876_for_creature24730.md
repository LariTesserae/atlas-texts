I thought at first I would be standing.

In the middle, where you can see the whole other side at once. Where the floor gives. A person-shaped weight in a room that was not scaled for people. Breathing too loud, sweating salt the leaves have to wash off. Listening to the hiss and clunk.

But I don't stay there.

The place is too good at correcting that kind of presence. It takes your weight and distributes it outward and then speeds up its dripping over your head until you leave, or until the cold gets into your shoulders and you become something else.

So I let myself go a little thinner.

For a while I am the light. That seems right. I come through already used once, green-filtered, leaf-thin. I am bright enough to read by, if there were anything to read. I freckle the air itself. I land on the hanging curtains and make the undersides of leaves glow.

But light moves too fast here. This room is about staying. About water that never gets to leave, just goes around and around.

So I am the water, then.

That lasts longer. I am better at being water. I am caught outside in the weekly cold rain — one short, efficient rain a mile wide over the flat. I am caught on the back of sphagnum, handed down, filtered through a thousand tiny mouths. I am lifted without a trunk, twenty feet up by nothing but turgor, by the thoughtless force of sap in a pipe that was never invented. I swell a bladder in the upper dome through the morning. I get heavy. I hold the roof up.

In the evening I contract.

I bead. That's where I really find myself. Not in the grand hydraulic sigh, but at the very tip of a hanging rootlet on the south side, where the moss thatch is thickest and the light is most green.

I am one bead.

I hang there all afternoon getting more itself. The air inside is 8 degrees, 9 degrees, always. Condensation wants to happen. Spores drift up past me — the galaxy of gnats turning slowly, straining the air — and the exhalation of a hundred leaves holds them aloft and then holds me together. I can see the whole cathedral from here, inverted. All of it in a lens.

Below me, on two spread toes, is one of the white frogs. Not looking at me. Just open. Pink gut showing through white belly, green dome showing through white back. It has been waiting since morning for me to get heavy enough to let go. It does not call — it did a system check an hour ago: *this tip is still wet.*

This is my job. I am not falling. I am being held. On purpose. By a leaf-edge curled back like a lip.

You would find me on the ceiling, just to the left of the north entrance where the plants hold themselves open. Not the center — there is no center. Not the floor — the floor is for patrollers, for the black, polished, eyeless beetles that run their routes like welders and push living stolons over tears. I am above them. I am in the weather system.

I am the thing the building makes when it has nothing to make except more of itself.

If you stand under me long enough your breath will condense on your shoulders and you will think it is me, but it isn't yet. I need another minute. Another hour. The columns are about to clunk again and shake a rain of my smaller sisters loose, and all the frogs will turn their heads at once. 

Then I will let go.

I will tick against moss, against moss, against frog.

And for the second it takes to fall, I will be the only moving thing in a place that has spent ten thousand years learning how to stay perfectly, loudly, empty.
