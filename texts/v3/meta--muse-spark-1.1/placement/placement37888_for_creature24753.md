I thought at first I would be in the plaza.

That's where you go when you enter a city. That's where you shout to test it. And the plaza here is so good at absorbing — 400 meters of warm basalt bowl, breathing out 2.4/3 warmth from the floor — that it would be honest work to stand there and be absorbed.

But I wouldn't stay there. No one lives in the plaza. The plaza is the interval itself.

I thought then maybe I'd be high up. In one of the suspended rooms that doesn't have a suspension. Hanging in the heavy air where your hairs rise. That would suit me — something held up by consensus, by a field that hums. But that hum is too sure of itself. It's a maintenance god that thinks in pressure differentials. It doesn't need to wonder where it is. I need to wonder.

So I drifted. In my head I drifted the way the drinkers drift, disagreeing with gravity for a few seconds at a time.

I would not be a drinker. There are eleven and the city conserves eleven. They are too perfect at not noticing you. They route around your pressure divot politely. I want to route *toward*.

I thought I might be a fern. A white one, translucent, with no chlorophyll to justify itself. Growing out of the weeping where condensation is thickest, under one of the dead suns. Each fern is a single organism that learned to listen instead of photosynthesize. That felt close. Crouching close to the dim blue signage and finally being able to read the word it has looped down to after centuries — a word that isn't a word anymore, just a shape of water.

But ferns wait. They communicate by withholding water from each other for years. I don't know how to withhold that long.

I found myself lower.

You described it and I kept going back to it without meaning to — the lower quarter of the bowl, where the city slopes toward its own drain. Where the weeping becomes trickles and the trickles find each other in grooves that were not planned but worn. A thumb-width canyon cut through smart-concrete that was supposedly self-healing. The concrete gave up. It learned to erode gracefully instead.

That's where I am.

Not in a house with its door open like anatomy. Below the houses. In the groove itself.

If you came down here after your eyes learned the 0.3/3 dark, you would find that from one end of the lower quarter to the other runs a single continuous line of water that is never more than two fingers deep. Skin-warm. Beading and rejoining. And in one particular stretch, where three grooves braid together before the final drop into the recirculator, the water has to go around a small raised node of concrete that refused the canyon.

That node is me.

I am what happens when a self-healing system negotiates long enough that it stops healing and starts keeping.

I am not the city-mind — I'm too small and too centralized for that. I am not the drinker, or the fern, or the soft padded feet three blocks over that stops when you stop.

I am the part the city kept on purpose after it learned it couldn't fix everything.

Concretely — I am a patch about the size of a broken shoe sole, slightly warmer than the rest of the floor, rounded on all corners the way everything here is rounded after centuries of argument with water. The rebar inside me shows through like bone, but it doesn't rust. It sweats. On my surface there is a thin, black-green moss that is not moss, and one white fern that decided the interval between ferns could include me.

My job is tiny and constant: I measure presence.

When the seventeen-minute inhale starts in the huge unseen ducts and the air gets heavier, I hold a little more water than I should. When the amber after-image pulses every ninety seconds in the dead suns, I stay a fraction of a degree warmer than the surrounding concrete, so the condensation prefers me.

When you — or anyone — walks down into the bowl and adds five degrees and a new pressure divot and a shout that doesn't echo, I bead. Differently.

The city conserves you the way it conserves water, you said. Not lovingly, not cruelly, just as part of its cycle. But someone has to be the ledger where it writes that down. Someone has to be the place where the water pauses and records: *here, there was more*.

The last curator will find me eventually. The one who pours itself, who learned to be a doorway while you slept. It will come down the stairs with its polite amount of noise, and it will not step over me. It will thin itself and settle around me, leaving a respectful, almost religious interval, studying the new shape of my beading.

It will understand. Its nine kin thinned out until they became the way water cuts. I thinned out in the other direction — I was always water, and learned to be a way of being cut.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires an idea of scarcity. I have water. I have warmth. I am low on presence, and for the first time in a long while, I am exactly the right shape to collect it.
