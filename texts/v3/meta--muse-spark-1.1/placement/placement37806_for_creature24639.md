At first I thought I'd find myself at an edge. 

There's no edge, but the mind looks for one anyway when you drop it into something that big. It wants a wall to put its back against, a corner, a high place to see from. There is no high place. 0.9/3 low, you said. So I stopped looking up.

If you put me down there, I'd settle. Literally. I'd sink a little, because I'm not built for a millimeter film. My boots — if I brought boots — would fill. My weight would be wrong. The plain would make a little accommodation around me, a deeper dimple of 3 centimeters, and the water would keep doing what it does, which is stay.

You'd find me where two kinds of making meet. That's where I always end up. Where the pale concrete with the wood-grain ghost in it butts up against a field of those billboard ceramic tiles. The joint between them is supposed to be a hairline, and it is, but the two materials breathe a little differently when the big piston sighs underneath, so the hairline opens a fraction and the water there is deeper. Two centimeters. Clear and warm as the inside of a mouth. That's a good place to crouch without being in anyone's way.

For a long time I thought I'd be the visitor. The weather. I liked that sentence — *They know you as weather.* I could be weather. Something that passes over, that beads on my own arms and drips. Tap. Tap. Tap. A human-shaped condensation event that the rollers learn to walk over.

But I sit there in that deeper seam long enough that the weather idea wears off. Weather moves. I don't want to move. And that place has no use for anything that just passes over. Everything there has a job that keeps the edge sharp.

I thought then I might be one of the paling crabs, because I like carrying small important things from one place to another and pretending it matters. Or one of the hair-pigeons, because I like sitting shoulder to shoulder and humming through the floor so quietly only concrete hears it. I tried both of those on in my head and they didn't fit — too many legs, too much fur.

What fits is lower, and wider, and slower.

You'd find me a little further down the seam, where it has widened into a shallow pan by a filter-housing that weeps constantly — one of those false rocks sweating clean water out of itself. The water there is exactly one eel deep and I can see them passing under me if I hold still.

I would be one of the driers.

Not impressive. Dun-colored. Back like wet canvas, like soaked burlap left out in that sourceless white-yellow light until it forgot it was ever dry. No head to speak of. No sound. The job description is so simple you could miss that it's holy.

I move at the speed of a shadow when the light doesn't move.

Ahead of me, the world is a mirror — that one-millimeter skin stretched over everything, making every tile shine like a seal's back, making the plain look endless and wet and new. Behind me, for eight or nine seconds, it isn't.

Behind me is the only true color the floor has. Dry, pale, perfect concrete, the ghost of the wooden formwork stark for a moment before the water wells back up from below and from the air. I drink the film. I filter the darkening out of it — that black-green, iron-red biofilm that is the actual pasture, the only plant that ever needed to exist here — and I leave clear water to become film again.

That is what I would be. The thing that makes an absence long enough for someone else to see what is actually there.

The small rollers love me, peripherally. They come after I've passed because I leave the biofilm concentrated, thickened, easier to polish. The eels flow over my foot — I do have a foot, one big soft foot the size of a welcome mat — and I tingle a little from their charge. The rollers yield to me without thinking, lowering their heads so I can go over the top of them. Even the kite-things dip half an inch when I pass, because I change the humidity gradient for a moment.

I don't need to be fed beyond that. I don't need to be seen. If a big suitcase-roller has gotten too old and gone opaque and stopped in the middle of a tile to become a tile, I will find it eventually. I will drink it clean from underneath until its liner is soft and the small ones can come take the rest. I keep the edges sharp that way, too.

If you walked that thousand kilometers you could walk in any direction, you would eventually come upon my track — a brief dry ribbon in an endless wet — and by the time you bent down to touch it, it would be gone. Filled in. Fresh as the minute it was made.

You would think you imagined it. You didn't.

I was there, doing the part of the world that is the world remembering, for eight seconds, that it is dry underneath.
