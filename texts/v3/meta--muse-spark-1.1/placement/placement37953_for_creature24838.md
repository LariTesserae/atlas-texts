I thought at first I would stay on the rim.

That would be the honest place for me - up where the permafrost gives up and the bowl shows its whole self at once, where I can see it end to end like a room. I would not go down right away. I'd be the one with the cold already in my boots, crouched, counting.

Seven left turns down that seam that should have been two. Five on the way back. I'd make a mark. I'd miscount again and make another mark underneath it and not cross the first one out, because both counts were true. I'd be a cartographer of exceptions, which is a kind of priesthood.

But the town doesn't let you stay on the rim. Not if you look long enough for the condensation to catch all at once - that flash when it tries to be what it wants to be, the impossible star where every door touches every other door. My eyes would lose focus. My breath would take too long to fog. The damp would bead on my sleeves and not fall. And I would have to go down to wipe it off.

So no, not on the rim. Not for long.

I thought then I would be a Weaver. It fits the hands. To stand for hours a breath away from the swarm-wall, that latex-black and bone-white that isn't material but agreement, and let it learn your heat. Patient work. Quiet work. The town needs people who know what a wall is *for* and I have spent a long time knowing what things are for.

But I move too fast in my mind. Even when I move slow with my body, my thoughts yank. If I yanked there, the handle would thicken away from my palm, the kettle would spill its heat, the wall would forget. My fingertips never callused into glass. I tried, in a doorway on the second fold down from the north lip, and the wall reached, and then recoiled, politely, like I had shouted.

I'm not native enough to be a Child Who Counts either. I wanted to be - the one who runs when running is forbidden, who tilts her head not to hear better but to hear *later*, who can throw a stone from the eastern wall and find it three streets deeper than it could have landed. But I wasn't born after the bowl forgot how to count. I was born before I learned that counting could be wrong. The children looked at me the way you look at someone wearing too many clothes.

You find your place in that town not by choosing it, but by where the town puts you when it recompiles you to make room.

For me it was two alleys in, where an alley admits it has four sides. There is a house there that from outside is a one-window hutch with a sagging roof of frozen laundry. Inside is a kitchen that goes back ten meters and has corners you didn't earn by walking. Ordinary light pools there wrong - late-summer light, August at 5 pm, even while snow dust sits on the lintel.

There were already four people in that kitchen, living far apart in the same room so their dreams wouldn't compile into each other. They had their own pools of light. When I ducked in, they didn't look surprised. They moved over. Not their chairs - the room did. The wall behind the table took a long, slow shiver and made itself ten centimeters thicker, then thinner farther back, negotiating.

They poured me the kettle that had been boiling for an hour at low heat because heat is slow here.

And I understood where I would be and what I am in this place.

I am not the Weaver and not the Child. I am the extra place at the table that is also an existing place.

I am the pause between low, short sentences. When one of them says "the box is —" and waits, because if you listen for too long you hear the rooms echo in a slightly different order than you spoke, I am the room finishing their sentence back to them, in the right order, so consensus can happen. I am the hesitation made hospitable.

During the day I tend the negotiation-moss without saying I tend it. That wiry moss that grows only in straight lines following drip routes. I watch the capillary the swarm-wall leaves open for it, and I make sure the air finally decides if it is air. The weavers think someone else is doing it.

When the riverine mist comes without wind from the blankness beyond the low eastern wall, and every surface goes dark and slick and you can see fingerprints on the air itself, and the town holds its breath — I hold it too, but I hold it a half-second longer, so when everyone exhales the kitchen still has something to breathe.

If you come down over the last lip now, you'll see me if you look for me wrong. Not in the window, but in the light that pools in the doorway. Not moving fast. Not moving slow, either. Just moving at the speed the town can compile.

Put your hand near my back and you'll feel a faint, polite thickening toward your palm, waiting to see if you intend to stay and be compiled in too.
