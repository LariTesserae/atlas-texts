I thought at first I would be you.

The visitor. The boots at the bottom. The tall thing that came down the throat for a look and brought a shadow with it that was too big and too cold and moved wrong. I thought I would be standing in the middle where it's brightest and most empty, where the air has no volume, looking up at the circle of sky and feeling exposed.

I can't be that. Not for long.

That shape doesn't belong here for more than an afternoon. It sweats too much, makes too much noise, wants to build something against the light or take something away. The pit has a rule against that. It is already full. If you stay standing, you are an interruption.

So I sat down in my mind.

If I sit, where do I sit?

Not in the middle. Nothing lives in the middle. The middle is where the light lives when it gets thick, those slow pillars you can only see after two o'clock. The middle is a road for something that isn't an animal yet. You don't sit in the road.

Not up high either. The ferns have taken vertical. They are so polite about it, one frond and then an inch of bare rock and then another frond, like they're waiting in line. I could be a fern but I'm too impatient for that. I'm not good at being vertical and dark. My black stem would show. And the ants — I thought about the ants for a while, the matte-black ones carrying a half-teaspoon of Pleistocene rainforest on their backs into a crack. I love them, but they still remember the plain. They came down from the rim and they still think about going back. I don't.

I kept sinking, in my mind, toward the north wall.

Because that's where the shade stays longest. That's where the rock sweats and does not immediately apologize for it. At the base of the north wall there is a lip and under that lip there is the one true basin. The one that doesn't forget. A meter across. Black mirror. Drip... ... ... drip. And if you are very still you can see the whole law of the place in that one basin.

That's where I would be. Not in the water. Just at the edge of it, where wet stops being wet and becomes stain.

And what would I be?

At first I thought: the boatmen, bone-white, rowing without ripples. But they are too perfect. They have solved the problem of light by becoming light. Then I thought: the isopod underneath, the grain of rice that learned to walk, blind and polishing the bottom. I like him because he's the future, the first thing that is practicing how to be a cave. But I don't want to be the future. This place isn't the future yet. It's all mouth. It hasn't learned to be closed.

Then I saw the hats moving.

I would be one of the farmers who don't farm.

One of the lunula. Not a young one. A middling-old one, thumb-joint big, legs too long, parchment thin. Sunburnt on top, pink inside. You'd miss me if you walked past because after a certain age we learn how to become a little chert flake that grew legs.

My place is a three-foot circuit. From the damp lip of the permanent pool to the first dry pan that sometimes fills, to the little overhang where a flake the size of my back came off the wall two hundred years ago and has been waiting. I have that flake.

It's white chert, thin at the edge, thick in the middle where it fits my raised carapace. Honey-colored where the sun has cooked it. On sunny mornings before the air gets heavy, I come out from under the north wall stone and I hold it.

Held is the wrong word. I balance it. On my left smaller chela, tilted just so — 23 degrees, the sun tells me — so its shadow falls over my gills. That's the parasol. I walk like that. Slow. Each leg placement considered because the stone is hot already and I am losing water with every step.

When I find the grey-green, the taste of light that lives just under the limestone skin, I do the other thing. I flip. The same flake that was a roof becomes a knife. And I kneel.

tik...tik...tik.

Do you know how quiet I have to be to hear that in the world? The world above has wind and birds and trucks. Here, that is the loudest work there is. Two parallel lines, pale, in rock that will keep them until the rock itself is gone. I make a little meal that is not a meal. I scrape enough cyanobacteria to pink my gut.

I have had this flake for a long season. Others have tried to take it. A younger male, brighter, faster, tried last afternoon when the pans were filming over. We pushed. We tested. My edge is better because my edge has my use in it now, worn to my grip. He left. The knowledge isn't in me. It's in the shape that fits me. The pit made us both and will make another after.

When the condensation finally comes — that moment when the deep cold exhales — I stop farming. I go to the edge of the temporary pan that I know will fill — not any pan, my pan — and I lower my face in. For an hour I remember being a sea thing, a million years ago, and it's not sad. It's just true. The fairy shrimp explode around my feet, living their whole civilization in the time it takes my pan to go from mirror to dust, and I drink the sky out of it.

Then I go back to being dry.

If you came and you sat very still at the north wall, for longer than is comfortable for a visitor, for long enough that your shadow went from being an interruption to being a place, I would be one of the six you see. And after another hour, one of the ten you didn't see at first. I would use your shadow as a pool. I would scrape right next to your boot-print that will still be there in ten years.

I would not look at you. To look up is to break the treaty with exposure.

I am not hidden. Nothing here is hidden. I have just learned how to be entirely visible and still remain.
