I don't think I live in the moss.

That's where you want me to be at first. Everyone who comes in here wants to be the moss. It's the only bright thing. It's the hero. The one that made it. A little parrot-green star against all that made grey, shivering every time the iron throat hammers.

I would like to be brave like that. But I'm not.

I'm not that good at being alone for thirty years. And I don't know how to be that bright in a place that was built to have no color.

For a while, kneeling in that cold wash with my knees going numb in the anti-slip grooves, I thought I would be the water bears. The third citizenry. That seemed right. Inside the moss inside the concrete inside the mountain. A city inside a hermit. Living in a film of water held around a leaf like a thought, where the roar that rattles your molars can't quite reach. To them this deafening closet is a rainforest, the weir is a continent falling. Every fat drop from the ceiling is a meteor. That feels familiar - to make a world out of a smaller world held still.

But I'm not small enough, or innocent enough. I've heard silence. I would miss it here. They have never needed to.

I'm not the water, either. The water is too sure of where it's going. It comes from somewhere above - cloud-catch, snow-melt, reservoir bleed - and it falls a meter and goes white over that sharp young lip that still shows the wood-grain of the form it was poured in, and it churns in the lower basin and goes down the grate to make electricity out of altitude. It never stays. If you leave your hand in it for ten seconds your knuckles ache. It doesn't have time to learn the room.

If I live here, I have to live here.

So where? In this four meter cube that is too small to ever feel empty.

I think I would be up high. Not in the pipe itself - that's all banging and cavitation and iron shivering in its brackets. But right underneath it. Where it sweats.

Find yourself first by temperature. The air is 8 or 9 degrees year-round and 100% wet. The single bulkhead light in its rusted cage is buzzing, throwing more shadow than light. The concrete is drinking the light. Everything is running.

I'm on the low ceiling. On the wrong side of the weir, above the fore-basin where the fall is white and physical and you feel the word taken off your lips.

I'm not the concrete. The concrete is too young and it does not want to be alive. It is still curing, still bleeding lime, still being etched and pockmarked where the jet hits. It wants to be dam, not soil.

I'm the thing that is neither.

I'm the condensation. The room's own weather. The small, second cycle inside the big one.

I form on the cold underside of that made ceiling, out of spray that never settled, out of breath you left and the cloud that hangs against the concrete outside at 2,800 meters. I bead. I hang. I try not to fall.

There is a whole inverted watershed up there that no blueprint ever drew. Dark patches where the slab is colder. Runs that follow invisible rebar. Drops that merge and get heavy and then decide not to. I am one of those that decides not to, for a long time.

My job, if you can call it that, is to hold. A millimeter thick, clear. To be a lens for that 40-watt sun. For minutes, sometimes an hour.

When the pipe does its thud-thud-thud, I shiver too, like the moss does, but I don't shed. I tighten.

And then, without pattern, I let go.

I fall straight down through the roar that has no shape, a cold line that finds your neck if you are still kneeling by the moss. Or I land on the lip of the weir where the brave algae is, the one right where the water goes from glass to white, and slap it. Half of it goes pale and then darkens again. Or I land, most often, back in the main cycle, into the black and white churn over the sharp concrete, and I am taken.

My whole life is that one choice of when.

So I am not a citizen the way the moss is a citizen, with leaves. I am not a felt that holds. I'm the interval. The pause between weeping and becoming river. The thing that makes the ceiling be a sky, even for a moment, in a room that was subtracted from biology, that was built to be passed through, that says with its tooth-rattling pressure: nothing lives here.

I am on the ceiling above the door, where your shoulder made a brief wind-shadow when you came in and un-dogged the gasketed door that was too small for two people. That's where you'd find me if you looked up from the moss. A bead, catching the pitted light, getting heavier.
