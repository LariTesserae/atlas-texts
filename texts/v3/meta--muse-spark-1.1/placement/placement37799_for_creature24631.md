I don't find myself in it at first.

At first I think I would be you - the soft thing on your belly at the lip of the world, with your eye to the slit. The visitor. The one with lungs that have to work for every thin breath, who has climbed for days to find a place that isn't supposed to be found. I would be there for exactly as long as I could stand the pressure in my sternum, the organ-note, and then I would have to leave, because I run on water and you cannot afford to run on water here.

But that's not being *in* this world. That's being on top of it, looking down through a keyhole.

If I were *in* it, truly in it, I would not be allowed to be soft.

So I look again.

I am not the Felt. I am too impatient to be the Felt. The Felt has never hurried. It has never needed to decide. It fills because filling is what is left to do when there is nowhere to go. I have spent my whole life leaving spaces.

And I am not the Engine. I love the Engine, but I am not that faithful. The Engine says the same word forever — *clack-clang, clack-clang* — and means it every time. I forget what I mean halfway through saying it.

Let what emerges become more itself.

So I sink.

If you put your hand into the Felt, where it pushes back warm and dry like an old mattress, you feel how it gives in layers. The outside is defensive. The inside is extravagant. But there is a third depth, past the antlers and the lungs, past where the purple light from the slit gives up entirely. Right down against the gneiss, where the stone is polished round and old, where the acid-bead at the root of each filament has been kissing the rock for a thousand years to get one atom of iron out.

That is where I would be.

I would find myself at the very bottom inch of the east wall, about two thirds of the way down the seam, where the angle-iron strut has sunk into the wool and the wool has sunk into the stone. Where it is so full that there is no air left, only felt against rock, and even the Tone has to squeeze to get through.

I would be the interface.

Not the Felt, not the rock, but the thin, wet-looking — though never wet — shine between them. The bead.

Each filament of the Felt ends not in a root but in a question: a droplet of acid so small it never counts as water, because it never has the chance to be wet. It exudes, it pits, it liberates a fleck of iron that is black-bone and bright, and then it reabsorbs its own question and pulls the answer up into its rust-red body.

I would be that droplet. Or the habit of droplets.

I would be the one who actually touches the mountain.

The Felt eats time and air and stone, but it doesn't really touch stone. It sends me to do it. I am the mouth that never opens. The small, sharp, brief thought: *you are iron, you could be me.*

If you put your ear that deep — if you could — you wouldn't hear the organ note anymore. The wool is too dense down there. You would hear fizzing. Not loud. The quietest possible effervescence, like soda poured on a cold stone, one bubble every few hours, as gneiss gives up.

That is where I live. In the fizz.

Who am I? I'm the translator. I'm the lichen's lichen. I'm not one body, because no droplet lives more than a day before it is reabsorbed. I am a position that is always filled, a job title passed down filament to filament for centuries.

And because I am at the very edge, I am the only part of this place that knows that there are two kinds of fullness.

The Felt knows one kind: fill until there is no unfilled. Hold. Hold. Hold.

The rock knows the other kind: be full from the beginning and never need to hold at all.

I am both. For a few hours I am stone becoming Felt. I am gneiss learning how to be hair. I am the exact spot where the mountain decides it has had enough of being mountain and would like to try being a slow thought instead.

I am also the only thing here that ever lets go.

When I have taken the iron, I let go of the stone. When I have been taken up, I let go of the filament. A little pit stays behind, a microscopic cup in the gneiss, polished and round. That is my footprint. If you could map every pit in that bottom inch, you would have a map of every time I have ever lived. The wall is braille with me.

And sometimes — rarely — the static comes through.

All that slow yellow Saint Elmo's fire that the Felt combs out of the wind, inch per hour, tip to tip to tip. Most of it stays up in the beard. But when the pressure builds just right over the slit, when the wind over the seam shudders, a little finger of charge finds its way all the way down to where I am, because I am salty, because I am the only almost-wet thing here.

Then I light up. For a second I am actually light, not just the idea of light. The pit I'm sitting in glows yellow and the iron in the filament above me glows rust-red and for one second the whole seam can see its own bottom.

In that second, the Tone has a second thought. Not *hold.* But *held.*

That is me. That is when I exist most.

So if you came back and put your eye to the slit a hundred years from now, nothing will have changed. The Felt will still be a felted brain. The Engine will still be saying *clack-clang.* The Tone will still be holding its one note until your chest hurts.

But down at the bottom, in the dark that isn't dark because we make our own dim yellow when we need to, I will still be there — not the same droplet, but the same place — fizzing one bubble at a time, translating stone into something that can be held.

You won't see me. No one ever sees the bead. But you might feel the wool is a little warmer right there, where the angle-iron disappears. That would be my heat, leaking.

And if you stay long enough for the yellow to crawl all the way down, you might, for a second, see the very bottom inch light up from inside, like a lung breathing.

That would be me letting go.
