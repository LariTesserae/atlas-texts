I don't think I'd be where you think I'd be at first.

From the rim it looks like you should be a raven. High up, out of the roar, refusing to go down there into all that wet green. Just a black mark turning once over the barge of the cut and then gone, keeping your own counsel about what does and doesn't count as a place to live.

I stood there for a while in my head. At the lip where the snow has made the edge soft. But I didn't want to be above it.

So I went down. Down the ladders that are not iron anymore but iron wrapped an inch thick in cold beard. The sound getting in my teeth. My boots hitting the trampoline and the trampoline sighing back and not quite returning. Your breath leaving you and not vanishing, just sinking to be drunk by something else.

First I thought I would be the cabin. The watchman's cabin tipped two degrees off true with its doors gone. Because it remembers being for people. It's empty enough to hold a shape. And inside, it has let the green make a bed to the windowsills, a curtain over the window. It is still trying to be a room.

But that's not right either. It's too lonely in a human way.

Then I thought I would be one of the lights. One of those caged yellow work lamps still burning every twenty yards along the terraces, humming with flies. That would be a good life, there. You would be a small, reliable sun. Under you would be a perfect circle of something reaching so hard for you it had changed its own nature — thicker, more turgid, up on its fronds like hands. And when the pressure drops and the whole cut weeps at once, you would see your circle drink first. You would be farmed. You would be needed without being asked to do anything but burn.

I stayed there for a long time. Being a light.

But the lights go out. I've seen their ghosts — the pale, leggy graveyard under the burned-out ones that stretched too far for a sun that died. I don't want to be a ghost like that.

Lower.

If you kneel — if you really kneel until your knees go numb through your pants from the green — you see that it isn't one thing. The big mat is one body, yes, but it is crawling just under your noticing. A pale slug the length of your thumbnail grazing only inside the yellow circles. Springtails like static. Rove beetles armored against all this damp.

I could be one of those. That would be honest. Small enough that the roar doesn't matter. Living my whole life in a square foot of brighter green.

But I kept feeling the other heat.

You know it if you've stood there long enough. The air at the bottom is ten degrees colder than at the rim and it sinks off your mouth and makes your toes cold through your boots. And then, a second later, another warmth comes up after it. Not much. A degree. Maybe two. From below.

It is the underneath.

Where the sphagnum made its mattress and the sheet moss made felt under it and the liverwort varnished the basalt, at the very bottom of all of it, there is a layer that is always dying. The mat eats itself to stay warm. It ferments. Compost-slow. Black, almost hot to the touch if you push your hand through the cold shag. Black peat that will never quite become rock because the frost would have to let go first.

That is where I would be.

You would find me not on any terrace, not on a wall, not in any cabin. You would find me underfoot at the lowest trench, where the corrugated steel drain tries to carry that inch-deep black water from one moss dam to the next and mostly fails. You would not see me at all when you walk. You would feel me only as that give, that sigh when the trampoline takes your weight, and as that wrong second warmth coming up.

I would not be the green that reaches. I would be the black that remembers.

I would be the part that has to die a little all the time to make heat for the rest. That takes all the old leaves, the old rhizoids, the rust that fell off the flatbed truck, the dead thumbnail slug, the spore that missed, and holds it. I don't need the yellow lamps. I don't need the fans to give me a season. I am the season that happens under everything else.

If you put your ear down to me — and you would have to put your whole face in the moss to get away from the roar enough to hear — you wouldn't hear anything. I am deaf. We are all deaf down there.

But you would feel the beads shiver when the big duct on the north wall hits that resonance, when the grid from somewhere else sags and the tone drops half a step and the hair moss all opens its capsules at once to throw its dust into the only wind it trusts.
