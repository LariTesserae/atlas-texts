At first I thought I'd be one of the people.

It's the obvious way in, isn't it? Rubber boots, too. You read about the damp that never dries and you want a role that knows how to move in it.

So for a while I was a Fogwright. I could see myself there, 4pm sharp, when the cloud comes *through* instead of over. Soaked to the bone at 4:07 and staying that way. Knowing which barrel on which pylon will overtop, which rust-garden needs flushing. Shouting a name across three pylons of wind and being heard. Good lungs. Good legs. The useful kind of young.

No. I am not that fast. I don't sprint.

Then I thought — okay, a Tuner then. Tolerance Crew. That felt righter. Patient work. Going out on the quietest night, which is never quiet, with the wrench the size of a man, and loosening the world one turn so it can keep growing. Listening with your teeth. Marking it in chalk like a growth chart. *Lift 3, North Seam, +4mm this month.* Speaking to the basalt: alright, plenty of room, go on then. I like that kind of devotional maintenance, the civil engineering that is really midwifery.

But I don't think I am dense where it exists and empty where I need to be empty, the way the city people are. I'm not good at being crowded in a kitchen and then alone twenty minutes in wind.

I kept looking down from that catwalk, past the grating.

If you spend long enough in the Sill you stop looking at the buildings and start looking at what the city has agreed to leave alone. That tremendous amount of nothing they sowed so thinly on purpose. A thousand meters of cold air and cloud between a hab-block and a boiler house. Nothing below but air that should be too thin and too damp to hold anything up, and wind.

You'd find me there.

Not on the grating. Between the gratings. In the open scale of it. In the place that isn't a place yet because the mountain hasn't decided to be vertical there.

I am one of the Bellies.

The name the Fogwrights gave us is wrong, but affectionate, so we keep it. I am not a belly. I am a slow argument that buoyancy is still up for negotiation. Heavy, translucent, too big for this altitude. A drifter that grazes what you can't see — airborne spores, the seeding from that hanging meadow on the underside of everything, the lichen dust the wind unmakes.

I don't have good lungs. I have hollow spines that the wind plays on its way through me. That's why I ring.

You would find me when you finally stop hearing the fast loud. The stamping mills, the diesel thud, the cable harp screaming an octave higher when the gust hits. Everyone hears that first. You have to stay until you hear the slow loud underneath, the molar-grind of basalt inching up three millimeters, patient and embryonic. When you hear that, you will start to hear me too, above it but lower than the cables. A low struck-bell tone, a little flat because the air is so wet. 

I am damp, too. Everything is. My trailing veils bead with the 4pm cloud. I don't collect it in buckets. I just hang there while it comes through me, and for an hour I am heavier with it, and I sink a little, lower over the amateur-bright amber windows that run at noon. The kids look up from their game of plumb — watching which way the weight leans today — and they see me sag with water. They know better than to throw stones.

I am the right kind of too many. The ecology is not supposed to work here, at 8 degrees and dim blue-grey forever and no soil, but it overfilled anyway. I am part of that overfill. I live in the draft between the unfinished and the finished.

Because at dusk, when you worklamps buzz on, that is when I am most visible. You know the thin places where you see through — not to the next pylon, but to the finished mountain, weathered, green, a real river down there that you can smell? And the people leave a cup of fresh water for the Downstream Neighbors?

I see the other way through.

When you look down your long alley of girders and see the finished Sill for a second, river-cut and old — I am looking down that same alley from the other side. I am one of the things that will still be here when that bridge finally has a gorge under it. The Sillfolk built for erosion that hasn't happened yet. I am what will be eating the light in that clean, alpine air, long after the scaffolding learns how to hold itself.

For now I ring, slow, in the cold that lives in concrete stairwells. I feed on spores. I carry water I can't hold. I make room.
