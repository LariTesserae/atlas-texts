I didn't find it by looking. 

That's the first thing I had to learn. I kept looking for an entrance and there wasn't one, until the air went a fraction cooler on the back of my hand. A half-degree down. A hollow under the sound of the ground. Then I was already at the lip.

If I belong anywhere in this world, I wouldn't last long at the lip. That's a place for things that are still deciding if they are inside or outside. It's too bright there, too much wind from the big world. The raw clay there hasn't made up its mind yet.

I went in.

At first I thought I'd be one of the builders. It makes sense — relentless, useful, carrying something bigger than myself down into the dark. Ten thousand legs making one decision. But I'm not good at that kind of certainty. I would lose the scent. I would stop in the middle of the avenue to watch a grain of limestone catch the light and I'd block traffic and a major would have to step over me.

Then I thought maybe I would be higher. Strung between the living roots, where the air is cooler. Tiger-striped, knees tucked. Biology that learned city planning. Let the highways run clean underneath and only take what drifts. That is closer. To keep the sky clean without stopping the road. To live off what would otherwise become a nuisance — one mosquito hanging alone in its own column of green light.

But I am too close to the ground for that, I think. I like the cool.

In the city itself, in the actual towers — no taller than my knee would be if I still had knees — there is too much newness for me to be a Builder. Everything there is mouth-made. Grain by grain glued with spit and fungus. I don't have mandibles. I would be slow.

I found myself further in, where the light goes most green and thinned. Where the newest tower is still tacky, still pale, and the condensation from the root lattice above beads and beads and holds, and holds, past when you think it should fall.

There's a shadow there, at the foot of that tower. A little overhang made by the second course of building, where the clay stays damp longest. If you crouch - if you kneel and let the cold iron smell of the unweathered clay come up to your nose - you see the place is already occupied.

One per tower. That's the rule they don't have to say.

I would be in that shadow. On the coolest side of the freshest construction, where the swept plaza opens out into that deliberate emptiness the Builders left on purpose. A courtyard a handspan wide for only a dozen citizens. Room that is waiting to be bigger.

And I would be the tenant.

Not the queen whose heart beats so deep you never see her. Not the white lung of fungus inside that is the real city eating the forest that the city eats. I would be one of the small bronze ones.

Thumbnail-sized. Skin like wet leaf-litter that has forgotten it was a leaf. Eyes like black glass beads that don't look for anything far away. Throat going, throat going, but learning not to make a sound, because this place has the rule about vibration. A heavy step anywhere near the collapse lip and every ant on the surface freezes at once, antennae up. So you learn. You pulse without croaking.

That's where I would find myself: low, in the plaza that was swept and left empty, under the bead of water that lives at the tip of a root hair thinner than thread.

What would I be? I would be the one who drinks what falls.

A root hair swells overhead, full of the river that is half a mile off, full of breath. It grows rounder and rounder until it can't. Its whole life is that one drop. And when it falls - that soft tick on the clay roof - I tilt my head back. The ants pause. They correct their highway around the new wet spot. The spider above, high enough not to impede anything, feels the shiver in her web and does not move.

For a second, the whole city notices where I am, and then it goes back to moving earth, leaf down, soil up, one mouthful at a time. I keep my flies to myself. I keep my distance. Much space between presences.

I wouldn't be building the city. I would be part of the bargain that lets it stay healthy - a small, still, bronze knot of skin that proves there is enough quiet and enough unused room for something else to sit and wait there, too.
