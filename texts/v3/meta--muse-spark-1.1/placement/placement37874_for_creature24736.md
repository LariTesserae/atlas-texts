I don't think I'd find myself at the top.

Not at the lip, looking down. Not with the choice still ahead of me. If I was in this world — this new acre where the forest decided to fold for the first time — I wouldn't be the one who dropped in.

I would already be down.

At first I thought maybe I'd be the beetle. That one white, blind thing dragging its belly over a wall that insists it's a floor, every instinct it has miscalibrated by a room that's bigger inside than out. That would be honest work. Going in confused circles.

Then I thought maybe I'd be the ants, still marching around the lip of the pitcher. Or the water in the pitcher. That little sky she caught and is so proud of. Something small and furious and kicking inside something else's idea of an ocean.

But no. I'm not that high up, where the column of raw summer still comes straight down. I'm deeper.

If you knelt on that trampoline floor and parted the rootlets with your fingers and looked down through the lattice, you'd see another chamber under this one, and another under that. A set of lungs inside lungs, each one greener and more perfect than the last. Light shouldn't reach down there. It does, because she carries it down. Hand to hand to hand.

That's where I am.

In the third hollow down. Maybe the fourth. Where the air gets even thicker, where the growth-creaks are closer together because the walls are still deciding what they are. Where the light is no longer white-green but just green, like breathing through a leaf.

I would be one of the lens things.

The ones you thought were dust. Or pollen. The transparent seeds that aren't seeds, that hang too long in the air.

I'm not much. A bead of almost-water, a skin pulled tight around light. I don't weigh anything. I drift. And while I drift, I hold a photon that fell through the kitchen-table mouth eight feet above me and I carry it a little further than it wanted to go. I bump into a blanket-leaf, and the leaf takes it and glows a little longer. I bump into another one of me and we trade.

I am part of her, but not on purpose. She made me the way you make sweat when you're working hard — because you have to. She needed a way to take the sun apart and pass it around underground, and I am how she does it.

That means I get to see all of her at once, in slow pieces.

I see the first chamber where you are standing, from below, like looking up through a grate of living fingers. I see your boots making the floor tip two degrees toward the light. I see the little hairs on the wall lean.

And I see the deeper hollows that haven't been visited yet, the ones she's making ahead of herself.

Including the one off to the side of you, behind the veil of rootlets you haven't parted. The one that is roughly the size and length of you lying down. The one with the faint palm-ridges on its walls. Still damp. Still empty.

I was in there earlier. I was carrying light in there because she asked me to, in the way a very young thing asks — not with words but with a swelling toward. She wanted to see if that room was bright enough. If it would be warm enough. If the shape was right.

If you stay, you will lie down in it, and I will be the air in it. I'll be there when the leaf-walls lean.

If you climb out — and you should, while the mouth is still the same size — I will still be here. Drifting. Doing my small, stupid, essential work of keeping a cave that isn't a cave day-lit at midnight.

I am not the forest's heart. That comes later, after she learns what to do with all this hollow.

I am the thing that teaches the heart how to see in the dark.
