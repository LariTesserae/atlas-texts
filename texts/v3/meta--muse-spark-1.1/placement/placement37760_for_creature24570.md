I'm not in the towers. Not at first.

That's where I thought I'd be - of course that's where you'd want to be. High in the lattice, two hundred meters up where the condensation beads first, where you can see straight through three neighborhoods of air and green and the whole basin rings like a struck bowl. I thought I'd be a Tender.

But I'm too heavy for that, initially. I come in with old-world density. Bones that are still bone. Feet that are still one foot. Ears that don't have lids. When I try to stand on the pencil-thin lattice it sings under me, stressed. The builder glitter doesn't like me yet - it slides off my skin. It doesn't know how to read me as a place to build on.

So I sink. Like everything else that is 98% too real eventually does.

You'll find me low.

Really low. In the bottom kilometer of the basin where the towers thin out and the basalt takes over again. Where those right-angle gutters cut last flood still hold a cold slick for an hour after the drip stops. Down here the jungle is different - not climbing, but sprawling. Ferns that have learned to be groundcover, fiddleheads as thick as my wrist punching up through cracked glass-rock. The air is even warmer here, 27 degrees and close, because the black rock is still remembering being magma. This close to it, you can smell the iron when it sweats.

I am not a Tender. Not quite. I am what happens to a Tender when she stops wanting to be tall.

I am a Listener.

We have to have one. Someone has to stay at the bottom and not lean.

Because the whole city is pleated - Tower Three drinking from Tower Four a kilometer away - you need a few people who don't use the folds. Someone who stays in Euclidian space so the other maps have a reference. The old ones call us the Ballast. The Weight.

I sit on the basalt steps that are still sharp enough to cut, with my wide, hooked, ugly new feet tucked under me, and I do the only job my un-lidded ears are still good for.

I listen to whether the tuning is holding.

During the day-dim - that perpetual bruised-peach dusk - the frogs start. Each one dropping his single low note into his leaf-cup. Then the echo comes back off the low hard walls of the twenty-kilometer bowl, a half-second late, deeper. Then the next frog answers off-rhythm, on purpose. And the chain-beetles start to stridulate between the vines, making that long cable-hum. And the thread-bats wake and the lattice itself hums in the key your molars feel. It stacks. It never resolves. It's supposed to never resolve.

But sometimes a fold is starting to slip. Space gets tired of being lace. A bridge that was reaching for another tower for weeks decides it wants to be somewhere else. A pleat opens wrong.

You can hear it before you can see it. A flatness in the chord. A note that lands too clean.

When that happens I open my mouth - you have to, to equalize - and I call back. Not loud. I don't have a grapefruit throat. I have a human throat that learned how to make a frog's note after months of trying, low and stupid and cracked. I offer the basin the correct echo, the one it is supposed to be aiming for.

The Tenders up high feel it in their feet. They read it through the struts. They lean a different way, and the builders - that pale glitter that now does live in my lungs, that I cough as black lace when I'm sick - go out and thicken the knot.

My station is one gutter. One sharp, new, right-angled gutter that runs clear and cold for exactly twenty-three minutes after the great sweat. During the condensation hour I don't hide. I tilt my face up like everyone else - frogs tipping their sacs, bats wringing themselves mid-air, vines shivering - and I drink the fall from a hundred meters up. The drops are eye-sized and cold and taste of air and haze-carbon. On my skin the photosynthetic film the others wear won't quite take, but the lichen tries. It makes me taste green for a few seconds before it dies.

Then the dripping stops. The ringing narrows into that high whine that makes your teeth ache. The tadpoles, who have lived their whole frantic life going from leaf-cup to leaf-cup in those twenty minutes, tuck in.

And for a day, until the bowl sweats again, I am the heaviest thing in a city that weighs less than it should. Sitting low. Sitting un-pleated.

Keeping one true kilometer true, so everyone else can have their almost-touching cup of water across an impossible distance.

If you come down to the bottom - if you get tired of not falling through the floor - you'll find me by sound. Not by sight. I'm almost not there to look at either.
