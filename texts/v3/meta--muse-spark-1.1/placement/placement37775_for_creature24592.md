I wouldn't find myself up on the high, sharp edges at first.

At first I think I would — because that's where you can see. On the back of one of those house-sized basalt polygons, where the stone is still almost geometric, where the wind has something to hit. I'd want the vantage. I'd want to be tall enough to watch the vents firing across the whole dim table and count the work.

But I can't live up there. Nothing can. Up there the film can't hold, the water sheets off too fast and the wind strips it. The lichen can, but only tight against the lee. I am not tight against anything. If I stood up there my thoughts would keep coming slow, and then slower.

So I sink.

Not all at once. I keep sinking in this place until I find where the temperature really changes.

I find myself down in the sag. Not the middle of the pan where the mud turns over and the biofilm shivers — that's too busy, too exposed, that's the factory floor where the geese are always scraping. I find myself just off that, where a cooling lobe has slumped and made a single step, maybe a foot high, a black stair in the middle of a mile of wet.

There, behind that step, the wind breaks.

That's where I would be. Because in this world, place is just another word for where wind doesn't.

And what would I be? At first I think I would be one of the quiet flock. I like the idea of that — head down, white throat hidden, the soft *shhk, shhk* of my bill harvesting stone into food, making the pasture thicker behind me than it was in front. To be useful without hurry. To be spaced.

But I am not that patient. I lift my head too much.

I think I would be a drag-coat for a minute. I can feel that weight — the hair that becomes mud that becomes insulation, the slow, bowed walk, the mouth full of grit and bitter heat. That mammalian, mineral endurance. Standing in my own steam while the vents steam. But I don't move that slowly. I metabolize fast. I worry. A drag-coat never worries.

No.

Let me stay behind my little basalt step a little longer and get colder and get more accurate.

Under the lip of that step, the flour-paste of shattered basalt has made a hummock the size of two cupped hands. The lichen doesn't like it there — too wet — but the moss does, a tight cushion, dark green gone almost black. And in the middle of that cushion, holding that cushion together by being in it, is one button of saxifrage.

That's me.

A fist of waxy, red-tipped leaves knotted into itself. Not impressive from any distance. From ten feet you wouldn't see me at all. You'd see black rock and black water and think, empty.

But if you kneel — if your knees go cold where the water table sighs up around your boot — you see what I'm doing.

I am making a place that is ten degrees warmer than it should be. Just in here, in the center of this knot, where my leaves overlap and trap dead air and hold the faint heat rising from the young rock below. Ten degrees. That's not ornament. That's an engine. In the middle of that warmth I am ripening a few absurdly small, white flowers that will never be pollinated by flying insects, because nothing flies well here. They will be pollinated by the crawling black beetles that live in the drag-coats' hair when they drag past and collapse on me for a moment.

I am bitter. Literally — I am full of sugars that taste like antifreeze and resin so nothing wants me, except the one thing that has to want me to keep me alive. I expect to be eaten whole. I expect to be ground by molars, to travel through acid, to be powdered with basalt, and to be dropped a quarter mile away in steaming dung. That is my germination requirement.

I can wait five years for that foot to land.

I am not lonely. Lonely is the smear, reading heat with his paws. I have the moss with me, and the film under me, and the mites in me. I am not fast. I do not need to read the whole plateau. I only need to hold this one pocket of heat against the pearl-grey dusk that never ends, and be eaten at the right time.

If you were walking that day, you would step right over me. You would hear the wind, and the vents droning, and the geese scraping, and under it all the low productive hum of water moving through rock by the ton.

You wouldn't notice that one of the red tips had opened, just a fraction, because the steam from two drag-coats fifty yards upwind had briefly lifted the fog around my little step.

But that is where I would find myself. In the process. Not on it.

Pressing heat into moss, waiting to be milled.
