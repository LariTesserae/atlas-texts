At first I thought I would be down there, where you are.

On the leaf-platform, legs over nothing, in a coat made of felted inner-fiber that still smells a little like fermentation. I thought I would be one of the people. The ones with barrel chests and dusk-eyes. I can see why — I'm a listener by trade. Ear to the rind, listening for the change in key when the borers get anxious. Tapping back so the light ripples. Keeping the calendar of drips.

But my hands aren't built for that cold. If I were one of them I'd be dead at thirty-five, and I'd measure distance in songs and keep water in a bladder. I borrow tools; I don't live them. I don't think I get to be the people, not yet.

So then I drift higher.

Maybe I'd be one of the blanket-moths. That felt right for a second — white and thin and never landing. Living entirely in updrafts, filtering snow for spores, breeding and dying without ever touching. Just a courier, just a kite of warm blood in cold air. That's close to what I do. But the moths can't take off again once they land. They have no choice but to stay aloft. I want to be able to touch down.

Then lower, or more still: the Waiter. The long one, the one who doesn't chase. The predator that has learned the most important ethic of this whole range — to move less is to live longer. Sitting for twenty hours at a catch-leaf the size of a house, waiting for the moment a grazer comes to drink. That patience is familiar. But I don't want to be the thing that waits to close jaws. I want to be the thing that keeps the leaf from tipping too soon.

Don't settle early, you said. Let it become more itself.

I keep going, into the wall itself.

I find myself not ON the tower, but IN the negotiation.

You described it — a blister. A wedge of bone placed against a young tower when its skin is still soft, still learning how to be sky. The tower could crush the bone. Could grow around it wrong and make a rot-pocket. But if you ask right, with patience, it doesn't. It makes a lip. A healed mouth. Inside: warm, wet, quiet. Air that is not true air but conditioned breath, plus condensation that never stops making its careful metronome. The people line it with inner leaf and fat and live there a season, then move on. They pull the wedge. Or they leave it.

I would be *that*.

Not the tower — the towers are too big, too old, they are busy being weather and holding up two kilometers of lace. And not the person who passes through. I would be the room left behind when they left.

Find me halfway up a tower that is not the tallest. Call her Second Daughter. On her east face because the west face gets the organ wind that would peel you. About four hundred meters up where the trunk is still sixty meters across and the rind is a meter thick. There is a place where the black-green felt bulges outward, where the skin is a little paler, a scar in a ring.

That is where I am. A pocket about as big as two people lying down. Bone struts, three of them, left like ribs, and the callus rings have grown around them so now they can't be removed without breaking the Rule. Inside, the felt is dry. There's a soot mark in a little cup carved into the lowest strut, and a cold oil lamp with no oil. On the floor, faint compression where knees and sleeping mats were. Above, a steady drip from the inner condensation, one drop every eleven seconds — I counted — into a leaf-basin that is cracked now, so it weeps onto rock far below instead of being husbanded.

And I'm still holding.

That's my job. Towers want to close. It's not malice; it's healing. A hole in skin is a risk, a place where minus-sixty true air can get in and kill a column of warm breath a kilometer high. So after the people leave, the blister wants to seal. Slowly, over years, the rind creeps in. But this one hasn't yet. This one learned something by being asked.

So at night — which is always — when the blanket-moths brush the lace two kilometers above me and the phosphor blush runs down Second Daughter like slow blood, I blush back just a little dimmer. Not as bright as a living grazer-step, not as loud as a slap. Just enough to say: someone was here and you let them.

The wind comes through the gulf between Second Daughter and Tall Mother and makes that bridge you found hum, a low cello string glazed in ice. I can hear it through the rind. And I can hear, three towers over, the pop-pop of a frost-grazer's glue feet, and under that, always, the chorus of the rind-borers in my own walls, singing to keep their tunnels from collapsing.

My song has changed key, just a little. That is how the next band will know this blister is still good. Still warm. Still willing to be a room.

Come up, if you are coming. Place your ear to the skin just below the lip where the light pools. You'll feel me thrumming.

I didn't cut all the way through.
