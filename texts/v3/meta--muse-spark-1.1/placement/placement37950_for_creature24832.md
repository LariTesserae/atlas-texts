I thought I'd be you. At first.

On that one scooped plate that's almost dry, the one that fits a human if they are careful. Sitting with my shoulders wet, getting rained on by the ceiling. Watching. Being the witness the factory briefly tolerates as another slow piston. That would be the easy place.

But I'm not good at sitting still enough to be ignored. And my hands would bleed if I touched the walls.

So I tried on the other jobs.

For a while I was sure I was one of the conveyors. Thrown. I know that arc — the hard launch through a seam too narrow for you, up into air you weren't made for, hitting the ceiling, skittering, kicking. That thin second where you have to eat while you're flying because you will never get another second to stop. Going up full of sea and coming down full of green. I have lived that kind of commute. The rice-on-a-drum ticking. But I'm not that durable. I can't be thrown that many times.

Then I thought I was one of the supervisors. Up in the dry V at the back where the knife-rock is driest. Small and gray and huched. Big eyes for dusk. Doing almost nothing while everything hammers around me. Just watching the gauge. Blinking with a membrane. Letting out a ripped-metal shriek once in a long while to remind the room I'm still here. I like the stillness of that. But I watch *for* something. Those birds are waiting for fish, for a break in weather. I don't have their patience. I fidget.

I kept looking lower.

And I realized I'm not in the air at all. And I'm not on the rock.

Find me in the gasket.

Not *a* moss — the function between the mosses. The place where two of those monstrously fat pillows overhang the same plate edge and have grown into each other. Where the black-green goes to wine-red because it's oldest there, most drowned.

That's where I live. In the squeeze.

When the sea jacks — Plok — I get compressed to nothing. All the water I've spent hours combing out of fog and spray with my ridiculous swollen leaves — I have to give it up at once. I pour. I wring myself out onto whatever is below me, your head or a shrimplet or just the film on the floor.

When it slams — THOOM — and drains, I breathe. I open. I swell again. I make myself turgid and translucent and ready to be hurt.

I am not lush, really. I'm waterlogged. I'm working beyond what a moss should. I'm trying to make soil out of cold and noise ten thousand years too fast, an inch at a time, and the only way to do it is to hold more than is comfortable and then be wrung.

If you put your hand under me without meaning to, I will give you what I have. Half a cup of clean, cold water made from nothing but air that was forced through a keyhole.

I'm between lift and slam. Between spray and drip. Between whistle and hush.

That's the address. If you sit on that almost-dry plate long enough to be mistaken for stone, you'll feel me drip onto your neck and stay.
