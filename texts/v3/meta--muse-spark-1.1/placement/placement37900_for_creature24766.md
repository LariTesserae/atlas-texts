I wouldn't stay at the rim.

At first I do, of course. Everyone does. Because you come up too fast yourself — lungs still working too hard in the thin air, body still leaking — sweat, breath, the wet of your eyes drying — and from the rim it looks like you could understand it all at once. The whole bowl, the whole city, laid out like a model.

You can't. From the rim it's too dense. It looks like one thing. A black reef. A stain.

So I have to come down, if I'm going to find where I am.

I come in by the only way that allows entry, the fourth side where the lift broke. Clinker under my boots, still sharp enough to cut through sole. The rock is warm. Warmer than it should be at this altitude. It holds the day. My shadow falls in front of me, into the bowl, and as soon as it touches the first black pillar, it doesn't look like a shadow anymore. It's just a slightly cooler place on black.

I am too loud. That's the first thing I learn about myself here. My breathing is a weather system. My jacket rustles and a whole street of silver curtains shivers though there is no wind. I am using water to make noise. That's obscene here.

I can't stay as what I am.

Not up here, not for long. A human is a wound that won't seal. We boil off at about a liter a day even sitting still. This place has spent fifty thousand patient years figuring out how to turn a single breath of frost into oil that will never leave. It has no budget for me.

So if I am going to find myself here, I have to become something else. Something that has learned the rule.

At first I think — I want to be a Holder.

It's the obvious ambition. To be big. To be foundational. To be the one everyone else needs. There in the center, the oldest one, as thick as a house and taller than any church spire needs to be, blacker than basalt, its felted hairs all trembling even in this absolute stillness, combing nothing into something. To be that patient. To take wind that has nothing in it and make clear, bitter oil that never evaporates. To be ninety percent kept thing.

But I haven't earned it. Holders are time. You don't become a Holder, you become held by them. They were here first. They cracked this glass rock with the slow swell of their own fattening. I have only been here fifteen minutes.

Then I think I could be one of the salamanders in the lanterns. That would be easier, almost luxurious. To live inside a single amber cell, a fist-sized sun. To never know dryness. To be born in honey-colored light, to swim in warm oil, to turn over in your sleep and see the shadow of your own body on the glowing wall of your world. To die without ever having been thirsty. One bladder, one life. Each lantern has its own quiet, blind god.

But I have already seen outside. I have come down from the rim. I can't un-see the city. If I were sealed into that light now, I would spend my whole life pressing against the wall, trying to see the courtyard again.

I keep going. Because in this city you don't walk, you lower yourself. You find the clefts between the fused trunks, where the black hairs part to make avenues no wider than a shoulder. Light filters down in fragments, dyed amber where it passes through the bladders up top. The air is hotter in here, utterly still. It smells like warm wax and hot iron and faintly, faintly like old honey.

And my breathing slows down whether I want it to or not. The stillness edits you.

I find the little courtyard. I know it when I see it because you described it — where three Holders have leaned away from each other and not yet learned to close the gap. A table-sized patch of vesicular basalt, still sharp, still showing every bubble from when it was born. Roofed over by a net of grafted arms. Here the light pools and goes syrupy.

That's where I would be. Not at the top where the lanterns are flags, not at the bottom where the Holders fuse into one. In the middle story, in the sidestreet that almost made a square and then decided to stay a square for a while.

And I think I know what I would be now. It has taken this long to get small enough and still enough to hear it.

I wouldn't be the beetle. The beetles are too sure. They know exactly what they are tending. They touch antennae and say *are you sealed? yes.* and that's enough for them. I am not sure yet.

I would be one of the silver.

Not the curtain itself — that's ten thousand — but one grain in it. One moth-thing, rice-sized, with no mouth worth mentioning and wings too big for my body, rectangular and felted silver on both sides with wax hooks.

I would be on the north side of that courtyard, second sheet back from the avenue, where the dust that the Holders comb tends to catch. I would have found a good spot years ago and locked down. My wings interlocked with my neighbors on all four sides, so that we make a continuous sheet that does not sway, that looks like moss if you don't know, like hanging felt if you do.

My work is so small it hardly counts as work, which is how you have to work here. The faintest static on my felt. The drift of one volcanic glass shard every three hours that lands and dissolves under my secretion. A microgram of frost that the Holder's hairs made liquid for an instant before handing it to the air again. I take it. I keep it. I do not turn it into water. I wax it over.

Around me, the sheet breathes in the way this place breathes — not in and out, but thicker by a micron this year than last. Sometimes, two pillars over, a red shelf has grown another millimeter and pinches the light a little narrower. The beetles below walk by in their slow procession, their glass backs full of dark oil. I feel their footsteps through the branch.

And once or twice in my whole life — which is long, for a moth — I will have to let go.

When the patch I am on gets too crowded, or when the Holder shifts and the light moves, a signal will move through my legs, not a thought, a loosening. I will unlock my hooks. All ten thousand neighbors will feel the lace tremble and then re-mend around me before I have even moved a millimeter.

And then I will drift.

For an afternoon. So slowly. In air that is warm, thin, and absolutely still. No beating. No hurry. Hurry costs oil. Just the faintest letting of the stillness take you, two meters, maybe three, like ash in honey. In that hour I am the only thing moving in the entire city, and I am moving slower than a minute hand.

Then I find a new bare place, perhaps where a bladder split months ago and left a little oily sheen that the Holders have half reclaimed. I fold my great felted wings down. I hook in. I seal again.

I would like that. To be a small, patient stitch in a curtain that will one day be a wall. To be one of the ones who keep what they catch so well that even my death does not fall — my neighbors eat the felt from my wings and keep my place warm.

If you came down into my courtyard and sat where I once sat as a loud, leaking human, you would not see me. You would see silver. You would think the city was empty and perfect and still.

You'd have to stay for an hour, until your own breath had gone slow and shallow, to see one silver scale detach and begin its afternoon-long journey across a space no wider than your hand.

That would be me.
