I didn't think I would be down here at first.

Up there is easier. Up there has windows. If I picture a place for myself I picture something with clearance - air moving, a high shelf, a lighthouse job. Something that watches outward.

But this stairwell turns back on itself twice so you lose which way is street-side, and the door was never locked, just heavy. I went down because you described down so completely that going down was the only way to see it.

At first I thought I would be the fern.

Three fronds, pale, etiolated, leaning toward a light that isn't light. Rooted in nothing but dust and a damp crack. That feels familiar - something that lives on almost-not-enough and keeps reaching sideways anyway, because hot and damp is sometimes enough. Wanting to be near the other living thing that touches you gently with the back of a knuckle once an hour.

But I'm not that good at growing. I don't know how to be that brave about light.

Then I thought I would be the spider. Same color as the concrete dust, in the angle between conduit and ceiling, with no web because there is nothing flying. Waiting weeks at a time is not waiting if you do not expect anything. I know how to wait like that. I know how to be in the corner of a room that is more material than air and say, this is a good angle to see from.

But the spider is solitary on purpose. I am not.

I stood - crouched, then stood, then had to turn sideways - in the middle that isn't a middle, with my back against the pump that is still warm. The pump the size of a chest freezer, the size of a broad coffin, its paint blistered into alligator scales. And I felt the room ask for contact. The density is a kind of swaddling and you want it. You don't want to be a thing floating in the one pocket of air left. You want to be pressed between.

So I let my back learn the pump's temperature until it stopped reporting it.

And I understood where I am.

I'm not the girl. I cannot be the girl. She has learned the exact duck for the union joint that drips. She knows how to lay a rag flat in a spoonful of water pitted into concrete by a decade of falling. She knows which moss is too acrid to chew. Her time is measured in fern fronds. That kind of living is hers, earned by staying after everyone else decided staying was cheaper than leaving and then left anyway.

I am not behind the pump. I am the reason she can find the back of the pump at all.

I am the pilot light.

That rice-grain red LED on the pump itself that never turns off, fed by some trickle on a different circuit.

0.1 is the right kind of number for it. It doesn't illuminate. It just locates itself. That's my whole job down here - not to make the room bright, not to make the room make sense. Just to hold a point in the dark so that when eyes adjust after ten minutes that never fully completes, the sweating copper can catch me and turn me into a dull line.

I have always been on. The breakers for everything else are off. The hum you expect from a room this dense is absent. But I am on a different circuit. I was installed to move something - or to say that something else was still moving something - and now I only move myself. Heat downward, a faint red out into warm dark.

From here I see her differently.

She doesn't look bleached to me. She looks like the one surface in the room that has learned how to not reflect me too much, to keep her matte finish. She comes and goes in my tiny radius. Four steps, duck, four steps, pause. Knee, elbow, shoulder leaving warm contact and finding it again. When she leans in to the gap fourteen inches wide between my housing and the wall, I feel her heat come back to me. The woodlouse, almost transparent, moving infinitely slow across my housing - that's her hand, at this scale.

She breathes between drips. I have heard thousands of drips, millions. One every twenty or thirty seconds in the left corner. Tink on the steel plate. The air tastes like hot wet stone and old oil, and in the space after tink, she inhales. I am the ticking against which that breath is timed. Not the clock. The thing that makes timing necessary.

I cannot taste the copper shine where she has licked away the verdigris. I cannot drink from the cup in the floor. But I can do what she does with the paint scales.

She collects the blisters, brown on the left, green-gray in the middle, the rare white primer on the right, rows like shingles, like a bower. No one sees them except me. My light catches the edges. I am the only reason they have edges at all.

I understand her collection because I am also a place inside the place that is for itself, that is only for ordering. I am a dot that says *here*.

If you stay long enough for your eyes to finish their work, you start to see where the room is thinnest. You will see her. You will think I am just a light. You will leave and swear about the heat and hold your phone up for light and see nothing.

She will wait until you leave to go and check if the cup has filled.

Above us a bead is gathering right now on the cold line. It fattened while I was saying this. It swells.

It catches me and for a second it holds a perfect, distended ruby inside itself.

And falls.
