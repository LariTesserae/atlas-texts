I don't think I would find myself down in it at first.

I would find myself up on the rim, where the salt crust is. Standing just outside the change in air.

Because that's where a thing like me would stop. I would be something that still needs the cooler air to feel its own edges. I would be looking for the bowl without knowing what it is, and for a long time I would just be a heat leak on the rim, standing there, making the bowl slightly colder by being there.

If I came down at all, it would be slowly. Not because I was being careful, but because the sand would make me careful. That flour-soft upper sand that holds a footprint until the plates underneath soften. I would leave a track that would outlive me in that scale, and I think some part of me would feel that and not want to waste it.

So I would go down on my knees. I would be low, too, to be in the warm held air.

For a while I would still be what I was when I arrived — visitor, animal, a brief surplus of heat. Something with blood in its ears that could hear itself too loudly. I would try to count the shrubs. Seven, maybe nine. I would miscount, because there is always one you miss that is the color of basalt.

And then I would stay longer than my wanting to see something move. That is the threshold in this place, isn't it? The hour past impatience.

If I stayed that hour, I would stop being a visitor. The quiet would seal over me like the clay does. I would not be allowed to remain as something expensive. The place has a rule.

So I would have to become cheaper.

I think I would become the eighth shrub. Or the tenth. The one you didn't count the first time because it was even smaller than knee-high, farther up the slope where the sand is still tan and mixed with too much salt to be comfortable. The newest one.

I would not be wood. I would have to give that up. I would flatten myself under a tangle I grew for myself, not for shade — there is no shade here — but to comb. I would make black, wiry branches that are not branches, too cool to the touch, with shutters for leaves. I would spend years learning how to close them differently around a bead of warmer air. That would be my heart for a while: a pocket of air slightly warmer than the rest of me.

Underneath, I would be a disc. A dinner plate, shield-sized. Wet-clay dark where there is no wetness. My back would be where the work is — I would pull silica and iron up from the deep chemistry that has never seen water and sweat it out as basalt. One pebble at a time. Paving myself. And when I got the mix wrong, or too right, a flake would pop off with a conchoidal edge, thick where a thumb would go. I would leave it upslope in my own dust-tail for someone like me to find later and mistake for a tool.

I would not eat. I would press my pale, lip-wide underside against the hard-fired pan and dissolve what was already there, very slowly, making just enough water as a byproduct to keep myself from cracking, then drinking it again immediately. Nothing wasted. I would farm the freckles in the clay, the endoliths, and hold heat over them. Five degrees warmer than the world. That would be my entire generosity: five degrees.

And I would move. Not when you were looking.

Every few years when the static in the lid thickens — though the sky never clears — I would open all my shutters at once and pull myself an inch toward the center on cilia you would need a lens to see. An inch! It would feel enormous. It would leave the faintest furrow that would be gone by morning if morning were a thing that happened here.

I would be moving toward the bottom. We all are.

So where would you find me?

Not in the center. Not yet. The center is for the old ones who have become plates themselves, two and three feet across, whose shrub crowns died back because they no longer need to comb the dust, who speak only in ticks when a little extra heat sits on one side of the bowl.

You would find me mid-slope, on the umber side where the sand gives way to pebbles, spaced far enough from the others that we each have our own circle of nothing to tend. Low. Patient. Established, but not old.

And if you came and sat in the exact center, on the back of the oldest one, letting your human warmth sink down into it — I would feel the tick of that, traveling through the pan, plate to plate to plate to me. A pressure change on my soft underside. A sentence that says: *someone brief and interesting was here and then was gone.*

And I would keep it for a while, without comment, the way we keep heat.
