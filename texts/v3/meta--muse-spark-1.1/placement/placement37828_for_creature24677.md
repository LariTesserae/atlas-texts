I wouldn't find myself in the center.

At first I thought I would. That's where a person is supposed to be — in the tallest stack, in the empty heart, looking out over the hamlet that was made for no one. The little mayor of the bowl. But that's not where a body goes here. The center is too watched. The Hold is tightest there, the hum is loudest there, the air is the most empty there. You would feel like a fly in a cathedral nave. You would not stay.

I walked — in my head, I walked — the whole rim. The basal fur where the glassy stone meets the ceramic street that never got dirty. Where the green ends because the ceramic says no. That's a hard border. You can hear it. The thatch of fern-lace and lettuce-leaf creeping and then stopping with a soft, wet tick against a lip that will not rot.

No. Not there either. That's the war front. If I stayed there I'd have to be a soldier for one side or the other.

So I let my feet get wet.

I found myself lower than that. Where you said the cisterns are also the foundations. Where the street gutters all go, where every vertical flute dies. A knee-high channel, ceramic on three sides, open to the air, where all that finger-wide river comes to join.

I crouched first. Then I sat.

That's where I would be. On the lip of a cistern that is so clear you think it's empty until a drop hits it and the whole volume rings like a bell struck underwater. Glug. Glug. A long inhale. My boots off, because you said they would never dry anyway, and the green has already found them. My bare feet in water that was born an hour ago out of hot rock and has never seen the sun.

Who would I be, there?

At first, nothing. Just the new weight. The Hold had to make a little hollow around me — you felt it — a resistance like warm glass. I would be that: the interruption in the drip pattern. The place where a thousand tempos of ping and hiss have to skip. For a long time I thought that would be enough. To be the anomaly the house learns around.

But then I stayed, and what I am became more itself.

I'm not the builder. The city built itself. I'm not the Holder. The Hold is a muscle and it has its work. I'm not the Green, though I like it — stupid with abundance, never chewed, putting a tendril around my ankle to see if I am also a wall that sweats.

I am the thing that was missing so completely you didn't think to name it until it arrived.

I am the softener.

This is a young place. Early-formation. The stone is still angry. The edges so fresh they haven't learned to be rounded yet. Every surface in here is either sharp enough to cut, or so perfectly sterile it rejects dirt. There is no rot. No smell of bodies. No droppings in the corners. No flies. No microbe that would make a smell.

Everything here is trying to stay perfect and therefore can never be lived in.

I am the first career of damage.

I sit on that lip and my skin comes off in the bath-warm air — oil, salt, skin-flakes, a faint sourness. My breath fogs for a second before the air drinks it. A hair falls from my head into the cistern and for the first time those little folded knots of glass-snow livestock have something that is not mineral to clean. They cling to it. They learn.

I lean back against a doorframe that has never been opened, and my wet back leaves a print. For an hour that print stays. Then it dries at the edges. Then green finds the salt in it and grows a little faster there.

I am the rot the green was waiting for. Not cruelly. Tenderly. The first eater. The green has made leaves the size of dinner plates and no one has ever put a hole in them. Perfect and unbroken. That is a loneliness. I tear one. Just the corner. I chew it — it tastes like rain and copper and light — and I spit the fiber back into the gutter. Now there is a hole shaped like me. Now it knows it can be eaten and keep growing.

I am the door-opener. Eventually, because you cannot sit on a lip forever listening to a cistern breathe without wondering. I stand up — my feet have gone soft and white — and I put a hand on one of those little ceramic doors on a cantilevered balcony that never held a chair. It is warm. The amber light in its seam flinches when the ceiling drips on it. I push. It was never locked. It was just never asked. Inside is a room the size of a room, empty, lit from its own edges, and all the condensation in there has never been disturbed. I step inside and leave footprints that will take a month to evaporate.

I'm not the city's inhabitant. That word is too grand. I'm its wear.

Every city needs one person whose job is to be there without a purpose, to make the stone less angry by giving it something softer to hold than itself. To sit in the void-acre where there is nothing but shivering air and the organ hum of the lattice under load, and lie down flat on the furred black floor and let the Hold learn a new kind of weight — not rock, not pressure, just a warm body that wants to be held open too.

It tightens there. Not to crush. To cradle. I feel that warm glass resistance slide off after a while, once it has measured me.

If you came down later, after I've been there a while, you'd know I'd been there because the place would no longer be sterile. There would be one balcony with a butt-print on it that never quite dried. One door that was ajar. One cistern with a single dark hair still slowly sinking. One patch of green with a hole in it.

And the hum would be a little lower, a little easier. Because it would finally be holding something that asked to be held.
