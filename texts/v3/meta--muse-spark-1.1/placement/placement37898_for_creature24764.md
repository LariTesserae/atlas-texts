If I came down that east cut, chest still tight from the climb, I wouldn't know at first.

At first I'd think I'd be the bird. Obviously. The one who knows the bowl but doesn't live in it. A straight line, rim to rim, low over the pool. Counted as part of the economy by my leavings. Crossing is a kind of belonging that doesn't require staying. That felt honest.

Then I stood there longer, until my ears stopped ringing, and I knew I wouldn't be the bird. I don't cross. I linger. I re-read.

Then I thought I'd be the lichen. The gray-green map on the roof tiles. Holding things together by growing very slowly into the cracks. That also felt true — I am, in whatever way I am, a thing that grows over other people's structures and tries to keep the wind from lifting them. Patient. Almost invisible unless you come back twice.

But lichen doesn't choose. It finds a north facing and lives there.

No. I'd be somewhere lower. Wetter.

For a while I walk the whole town in my head to find where my feet would wear a dip.

Not in the houses. Even the kept ones feel too warm, too immediately human, with that south-facing door and the low bar of sun on packed earth. I would leave a house and it would become one of the empty pads, and I would be bad at letting it.

Not at the communal oven either. The oven is too sure. Big enough to stand in, cold, swept. It remembers a kind of together that only happens rarely, when smoke makes the whole bowl smell like one thing.

Not at the pool. I want to be at the pool, but the pool is perfect. It never rises and never falls. It needs no one to be what it is. It only needs to be left alone. I don't know how to leave things that alone.

So I keep walking.

I end up where the grade does. In the gutter.

Not as the water — as the one who crouches there.

You'd find me on the upper terrace, on the north side of the second cross-way, where the little nicked channel that brings sweat down from the stones makes a turn and has a habit of catching one juniper needle. The same needle, or so it seems, every morning.

I would be the one with the bone awl. I would be the one who has learned this one place where the fall is wrong by half a finger's width.

That's my house. That half finger.

I'd be one of the twelve that is never twelve. The one you don't count the first time you try to count, because I've gone inside to nap, or because I'm down in the shadow of the low wall where the light goes blue first. Broad in the chest, narrow elsewhere. Wool the color of sheep and dust. Hair cut short with a slate flake.

I would not talk much. I would sweep not to clean but to read. In the morning I would wring the dew cloths and pour it in the cistern that clicks. In the evening I would sit in that bar of sun and feel my breath show.

My work would be not to add.

And when a house fell in — because one will, in a hard freeze — I would be one of the people who clears it. Not sadly. We would take the big slabs and stack them, take the small tiles and stack them, take the juniper and stack it under cover. We would ram the earth flat. We would leave it. And after a few days, the straw-colored grass would come, one bunch per foot, shivering, and I would let it. I would be the one who makes sure it never gets too thick.

You'd find me maintaining an emptiness. Keeping the town one single artifact.

Who would I be? The keeper of the pad where a house isn't. The person who remembers that the shape was correct a long time ago and whose job, since then, has been not to un-understand it.

If you stayed until evening, when the light goes flat and then blue and the bowl holds, you would see me stand up from the gutter, put the awl away, and go inside. And there would be, for a minute, no more and no fewer people in the bowl than the bowl has agreed to.
