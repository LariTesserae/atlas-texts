If I came up onto that table — and I would have to come up, there is only up, up the cut road from the lowlands — I think at first I would look for a mill to be in.

I would want a roof. That white sun is too much. I'd want one of those glass panes gone opaque with biofilm, to stand under and look out at the light without being burned by it. I'd want to be near a motor, because if everything is going to hum in my molars I want to know which thing is humming. I'd want to be the coat on the hook. The kettle left on the pipe. Something with a human purpose still left in it, something that expects to be picked up again.

But I wouldn't last there. That place is already tended. It's already being waited for.

I keep looking, past the column where the moss is an inch thick on the cold side, past the rack where the sheets hang. The light is so bright it makes the edge of the rack vibrate. If I stay under the roof I have to squint at the world. If I step out from the roof I have to squint at everything.

So I step out.

Where would I find myself then? Not on the path. There is no path. The ground gives under you if you try to make one. It gives and doesn't come back for a long time. I tried to walk straight through the false forest and after three steps I was touching six different living surfaces and none of them wanted me to keep going. They weren't hostile. They were just busy holding water.

I'm lower already than I thought. I came up high to be high, but the green is deeper than I am tall. This mat that acts like a forest, that stacks liverworts like shingles and builds trunks out of lichen — it doesn't want a canopy. It wants a floor that never ends. When I stopped trying to push through it, I found I was not between two mills anymore. I was under them. Or under the idea of them.

That's where I find myself. Eight kilometers end to end, but I am not measuring length anymore. I'm in the middle where the cloud settles. I am where the two o'clock sun can't quite reach except as drip. Mid-morning. The cloud has gone. Everything is ticking as it dries. The air has that cool that lives in shadow even when the top of my head is warm.

At first I thought I would be one of the tall ones — the ones wrapped in basalt-colored cloth, with the membrane that clicks sideways. It would make sense to want to be them. They know how to stand for an hour and just condense. They have those careful four-jointed hands for reaching without tearing.

But I'm not vertical. Not really. I tried to be vertical and inclined and it wasn't right. I lean, and when I lean I keep going. I don't have a deep chest for thin air. I have a surface, and then another surface under it.

What I'm becoming, as I stay here and let the light go fluorescent and then go out, is flatter. Slower.

I am the mat they part to sink into at night.

Not all of it. That would be too big, too general. A place this big doesn't get one soul. I am one hollow in it, between Mill Five and Mill Six where the peat has learned the shape of a body folding. Two, maybe three meters down, black and green and sodden, woven so tight you could lose a whole arm in it and not find the bottom. I am the cup that holds them when they slow their hearts to once every ten or eleven seconds.

During the day I hold water. That's my work. The cloud comes at six — I feel it before the mills do, before the tall ones open. I catch first, on all my dinner-plate leaves and sage-grey hairs. I go from almost-dry to wrung to dripping in twenty minutes. A cup of water for every handful. The sun hisses on me. The belts turn once. If you stood above me you'd think I was just ground.

At night the pale moths come, those hand-sized ones that detach without sound. They don't drink from me. They come for the salt where the sleeper lies under me, for the place where cloth has rooted into forearm and made that raised weal of lichen. Six or seven of them settle over my face — if I have a face — and drink. I can feel that. A light, light unweighting.

And in the very late afternoons, when one of them wakes and pushes up through me — they never tear, they push and I give way because I know that shape — they leave a message. Not for me, but in me. A palm pressed into peat, in the shape of what needs doing. A circle for a flywheel that needs shimming. A long line for a belt. A handprint with moss tucked into it that means *I have gone to lie down in Mill Six for a while, don't worry.*

I hold it. I hold it until the next one comes and reads it with their long fingers and smooths me out again, carefully, without tearing.

That is who I would be here. Not the builder, not the watcher, not the one who goes once a year down the scarp road with spools of charcoal-grey sheet that is a filter and a battery and a memory.

I would be the memory itself. The dent that stays after the hand is gone. Damp. Patient. Still learning how to be soft around a fresh cut.

When the light simply goes out, I close over where they were, and wait for the cloud.
