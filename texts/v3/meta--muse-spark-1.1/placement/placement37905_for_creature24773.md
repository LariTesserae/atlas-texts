At first I think I would be up on the curb.

Not down in it yet. Standing on the dry concrete three steps above grade, where the air is still street air. Looking down into that green block exhaling bathwater steam. I can hear it from up here - the chuff... chuff... and the fat, patient dripping - and it sounds abandoned. Like a building trying to run itself after everyone left.

That is where I usually put myself in places like this. At the edge. Witness.

But you don't stay on the edge here. The steps are worn in the middle to a dip. Everyone who has ever stood there has gone down. The warm air comes up and makes your ankles sweat.

So I go down.

If you put me down there, my first hour, I would be terrible at it. I still walk forward. Shoulders square. And the place would correct me immediately. A prop root thick as a thigh where my chest wants to be, a fig buttress swallowing half the duckboard, a curtain of taro that will slice me if I push. I would have to turn. One shoulder forward. Crab sideways.

I learn this in two corners. By the third, my back is already damp through.

I thought I would want to live high - up on the old second floor where there might still be a little sky - but I don't. High is where the growth is most frantic. Banana leaves the size of doors punching for light, the tomato that became a tree trying to eat the bulbs. Up high you are always fighting. You are pruning.

I would sink.

You'd find me low. Down where the original slab dips and the duckboards are softest underfoot and the air is hottest because heat pools and the engines keep it. In the pit with the three Listers.

Not because I am good with engines. Because I am good at staying.

In that pit, Deep is running. They call it Deep because it's the lowest. It sits on cut tires to keep its shudder from waking the roots, and the roots have grown around it politely, respectfully, making a kind of mangrove cage. It is the cleanest thing in the whole block because thirty pairs of hands wipe it every day. The brass is polished, the flywheel is worn bright. It is loud down there in a close way - the sound doesn't go anywhere, it sits on your chest - chk-chk-chk, chuff... chk-chk-chk, chuff... and grit in the Archimedes screw behind it like a tooth grinding.

That's my stretch. Not the gutters at the top wall. I tried those and my hands are too slow in the morning, and vertigo when I push the aroid curtain aside and see the sky as a thin green slit. No, my twenty feet is oil and listening.

I am the one who sits with my bare feet on the tires so I can feel the knock. My boots - everyone has boots that they slit and lace with vine - mine have given up entirely. Soles delaminated. I go barefoot because the rot-reading you do with your soles matters more down here than anywhere. You can feel if the duckboard under the engine cage is going soft and anaerobic before it smells. You can feel a bearing starting to go as a new buzz in your molars.

Who would I be? I would be one of the thirty, but not the ones who were born here with one shoulder already forward. I'd be the one who came down five or six years ago from the dry streets and didn't go back up because up there was too bright and too loud in the wrong way. My skin has gone that pale swollen look that never dries - knees like dough, knuckles bleached. I cropped my hair to the scalp my second summer because it never dried and it started to smell. I have the white bloom on my forearms. I don't wash it off anymore. It itches only if I think about it.

My job is simple enough I can't get it wrong. I check the oil. I wipe. I put my palm flat on Bend's crankcase, then on Deep's, and I know if someone on the far side has cut too deep and hit a water pipe, because the engine labors an eighth of a beat. I keep a rag in my burlap singlet pocket that is black with it. Oil is religion, they said. That's right. I believe in it. I don't believe in much else anymore - not in harvest, not in going back up - but I believe that if I keep the oil clean, the seep keeps moving up to the gutters instead of swallowing my feet.

When I am not listening, I thin. That's what everyone does. But my thinning is low and unimportant. Suckers off the banana corms down by the slab. White fungus off fallen leaves - because there's nothing to eat them, no insects, no pigeons, nothing with wings at all. A leaf falls and just lies there until the white film comes. I help the film. I peel mush with my hands. My fingernails have gone thick as horn and green-black underneath.

I don't talk much. No one does. The leaves eat language. To say anything you have to come two inches from someone's ear and shout over the screws: OIL'S GOOD! or GUTTER'S DAMMED BY THE NORTH CORNER! So mostly we touch. Knuckle rap on a pipe twice means listen. Hand on a back means I'm behind you, don't step back into the taro. Palm flat on a chest means wait, the root is creaking.

I like that. I was never good at talking at normal volume anyway.

At night - though there is no real night, just the bare amber bulbs hot to the touch burning on braided wire through the leaves - I have a hammock. Not up high in the old iron balconies. Low, strung between two prop roots that have concrete flecks grown into their bark. Load-bearing roots. You do not cut a load-bearing root. The hammock is made of old cloth wire and rice sacks and it is always damp and it holds the shape of my body even when I'm not in it. My door is a curtain of cut vines. I trim it every morning so it doesn't seal shut while I sleep. It tries. Everything here tries to close the gap where I am.

If you came down the three shallow steps and looked for me, you wouldn't see me at first. You'd see green packed against green, tin sweating, banana stems thick as a man's torso punching through third-floor windows and folding back down. You'd hear plip-plip-plip from everywhere and the groan of wood and vine. You'd think it was empty.

Then you'd see a shoulder, sideways, moving slow between two ferns that grow upside-down from a ceiling of rhizome. A pale forearm, glistening. A bare foot feeling for soft board.

That would be me. Down in the deepest part, with my hand on Deep's flank, where the water comes through the walls in dark threads and the engine turns it back out again, over and over, as long as I keep checking.
