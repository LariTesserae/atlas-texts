At first I think I'd be up high.

Because everyone does, when you first read a place like that. I'd be one of the Kites - six foot of torn violet, all bristle and hollow flute-bone, not flying but deciding how to be thrown. It sounds like freedom. To live in the shear, to surf the broken wind, to scream that thin tinny *kii-kii* and never have to land. To take the charge full in the belly and tumble, ecstatic.

But I wouldn't last up there. It's too fast for me. Everything eating discharge, everything screaming and being stripped bare ten minutes after it grew a foot. I'd burn too bright. I'd misjudge a vortex and cartwheel down into the soft that isn't soft, into the meter-deep mattress, and I'd lie there buzzing until the wind forgot me.

So then I think - down, then. Rooted. I'd be a Comber.

That would be more honest. To be the one who makes the place be a place. To braid myself out of my own old selves, cable over cable of black waxy hair-rope, to put all my living in the top six feet and spend it roaring. To go stiff and hungry when the air gets fat and rip water out of nothing with nothing but charge and spite. To smell like hot beeswax and iron. To have a note so low people feel it in their molars.

I could do that. I could stand ten feet apart from everyone else and still be a chorus. I could be lush, in the way a pelt is lush.

But Combers don't get to leave. They don't get to choose when they're cleaned. They have to droop and wait to be struck.

No. If you went looking for me in that bowl - really looking, after your knees stopped aching from the thin air and your eyes adjusted to the bruised-violet dusk - you wouldn't find me in the air at all.

You'd find me at foot level. Where the wind rarely reaches.

In the warm stillness under the roaring. In the north side, where the basalt spur makes a curl and the eddies turn back on themselves. Inside one of those low, dry-fit rings of pumice, shoulder-high, open to a sky that never rains. The one with the hearth full of black glass slag in it.

I'd be sitting with my hands in the ground that isn't ground.

That's where I settle.

I wouldn't be flying. I wouldn't be rooted, quite. I'd be what happens after both have happened for a long time. I'd be the third thing - the people part, but after the people left.

Maybe I am the woman who stayed when the others took the ladder down to the blue haze. Maybe I am her bones gone to cinnamon powder by now. It doesn't matter. In this bowl nothing rots the wet way. Things just get drier and more aromatic and more charged.

So what am I? I'm the keeper of the loom.

Not the loom itself - though I love it: two basalt teeth in the earth, a crossbar, and that thick amber cable of wound streamer-silk that still holds a storm from forty years ago. If you touch it your hair lifts and your teeth buzz. It hums when the wind hits it, a low angry bee-note.

I'm the thing that wound it. That tends it. That learned its rule.

My lap would be full of that shed - filament mats, cast-off wax combs, bone-thin husks - all milled to that salt-fine gravel that lights faintly when you scuff it. My tools would be the long needles, plant spines shaved and fire-hardened in the electric hearth to cherry-black. Wax-picks. Combs. For harvesting not to eat, but to remember.

Because what I do here, in the quiet that smells of warm rock and ozone, is listen and tally. Not on the stone slab - those notches are done. Counting storm cycles and breeding swarms is for people who still think they're leaving.

I count the afterimages. You know the weirdest thing? The light and the weight? Around the largest fronds you see faint halos, echoes a second late. If a flier darts fast enough, it leaves two or three afterimages that have to catch up.

I am that. The filament that got frayed off the bottom eight feet of the ladder over the eastern rim. The cloud of fiber that the wind itself sawed off and kept. I never fell. I never climbed. I hang there in the updraft, just east of the rim, where if you jump you hang a fraction too long.

I am warm, and dry, and double at the edges. I hold a charge.

When a Kite drags its belly through a crown and the bowl cracks white-violet - SNAP - and the whole Comber lights from inside like nerves, I don't shriek. I am the ground-level stillness that hears it and is glad. I am the hot, coppery bacterial threads six inches down, linking filament to filament, running the leftover gradient down to nothing, slowly. I am the black glass slag that learned how to go soft without ever getting hot in a way you understand.

Find me there. Palm on the low wall where the powder sparks faintly under your hand. Breathing the warm, wax-ozone air that makes your own hands look slightly double. Not screaming. Not being screamed at.

The whole small country is screaming its one idea - *give me a difference and I will live on it* - and I am the small, warm, listening difference between the roar and the echo.
