I don't know at first.

For a while I would think I have to be large to count here. That if I'm going to put myself in that valley at all I should be one of the big ones, low to the ground, making that *shhhk-shhhk* sound that is the valley's main sentence.

So at first I am a scabber. I am a kneeling-table of overlapping black plates. The light hits me and does not go through. I am so heavy I have to consider every step before I take it, and I like that. I look down because down is where all of my eyes are, and the skin of the world is right there, that oil-slick bacterial bloom, and I erase it. Behind me I leave a clean, gleaming path. For an hour I am a proof that something passed. Then the film comes back. I split when I am too full. That seems like an okay way to go on.

But I can't stay that. I'm not patient enough to be a rock that grazes.

Then I think I'd be a runner, because the floor is impossible if you try to plan it, and they don't plan. They are a bright, polished pill stretched into a line that just goes until it can't, hits something, turns. Touch whips, test bigger/smaller, flee. Over your boots and onward. No riders. Nothing sticks to them. I like the cleanness of that ethic — *be too slick to carry anything*. For a few minutes I am that, and the valley is just a series of vectors and collisions and the faint iron tang of scabber shit blooming white.

But I can't be that either. I keep stopping. I keep looking up.

The air is too busy to ignore. So then I am a clinger for a while, which is worse. I am a hand with a rabbit inside it. My belly is full of one big grape of water I stole from the air itself, and I can see — not with eyes — how every bead of condensation on the basalt above me wants to fall. I wait. It swells. It falls and I throw myself at it. I miss by inches and *thup* onto something hard and warm and already moving. Everything yells in ultrasound. I yell back. I jump again. I would get so tired.

I keep landing. That is the clue.

Where would I find myself? Not in the middle of a sun column. I couldn't take that. The center of one of those house-sized shafts of noon is a place of execution. You can see where a scabber tried it and baked. Its plates popped open like a kiln. The floor there is too clean because nothing can eat there for long.

I would find myself at the edge of one. Right on the penumbra line where the hard light ends and the basalt is still warm from it but not cooking. I would be in that thin band of near-dark where the rock is still sweating after the fog hour, where the weep is constant — plip, plip — and a shallow pan has formed that is too irregular to have a name. It is maybe as wide as my arms spread. It is ringed with mineral white and that faint rust that used to be blood.

And who would I be? I kept thinking I had to be one of the old lineages, the ones who knew how to eat rock or air or each other. But I am not. I am new here the way this valley is new. I am an opportunist for something else.

I would be one of the bleached ones.

Not the bright, fast young knapper who clack-clacks a perfect flake and drops it and chases a drip. One of the ones who stayed. My plates have gone white because I stopped molting iron back out. I am chalky, and pitted, and too heavy to ride. My eyes are up on stalks now, quartz blisters crusted a little with salt, and they can see over the backs of the grazing tables. My hands — those repurposed gill-combs — are chapped from basalt dust.

I am knee-high. I have a core stone in one fist that has been struck so many times it fits my palm. In the other, a hammerstone that was a bleached plate from someone before me.

And I have found a pan that holds water for a little longer than the others because it is in the shadow of a fallen ceiling slab. The big grazers cannot quite fit under the overhang. The runners go around.

I have built a ring.

It is not much. Loose flakes stood on edge, a little gut-sinew I pulled off a dead clinger that misjudged a jump, wedged with the usual glitter of debitage. It is just enough to keep the scabbers' mouths from licking clean the film around the rim that helps hold the water in. It is a habit that lasts as long as the water does.

Under the slab behind me, there is a roof of woven shed plates. It is not my village. It was left two days ago by a mother and her purse of commas. The commas have already burst and become a fizz of runners and gone. The roof is sagging. I should leave it. The fog will pool there again in the morning and I could fan it.

Instead I am still at my pan. Scabbers pass. One shoulders my ring and it collapses on the north side. I click — not loud, not language — and start to stack again. Another tries to steal my white quartz pebble, the cool one I carry because it is cool, and we tumble into the pan and send the shrimp-lice scattering. My back gets wet. My belly gets wet. I taste wet iron.

This is why I would be here, and be this: because everything else in this valley is perfect at what it does, and I am bad at everything except staying a little longer in a place that will not stay.

Nothing here has had time to become lonely and specialized and blind and perfect. I would be the first thing trying to be lonely. Trying to be endemic not to a rock type or a water chemistry but to a single, temporary puddle under a broken roof that lets in columns of normal, warm, yellow-white daylight.

I would be the one who rebuilds the same temporary wall after the physics takes it, not because I think it will last this time, but because the water is still there, for an hour more, and someone has to keep the grazers out.
