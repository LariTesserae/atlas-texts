The Shapers live here - though they might contest that term, preferring to think of themselves as participants in a greater pattern rather than its masters. They are tall, willowy people with skin that has a slight translucence, like young bamboo shoots held up to sunlight. Their movements are deliberate and fluid, each gesture flowing into the next like water finding its course.

They are not numerous - perhaps only a few hundred in total. Many live alone or in pairs, though some family groups occupy the larger structures. Their faces are serene but alert, marked by the kind of focused patience that comes from working with living things across decades. Most bear subtle patterns on their skin - not tattoos exactly, but a kind of controlled scarification that mimics the nodal patterns of bamboo. These marks indicate their lineage and specialization in the arts of growth-guidance.

The most striking thing about them is their hands. Their fingers are extraordinarily long and sensitive, with an extra joint that allows for complex manipulations of the growing bamboo. Over generations, this trait has become more pronounced, either through selective breeding or through some deeper resonance with their work. When they work with the bamboo, their hands move in patterns that look almost like signing or spellcasting, but are actually precise technical manipulations - each gesture representing decades of accumulated knowledge about how to guide living growth.

They communicate primarily through touch and gesture, though they can speak when needed. Their language, when voiced, has a tonal quality that mimics the sound of wind through bamboo. But most of their crucial communications happen through the act of working together, hands moving in choreographed sequences as they shape the living city.

Their clothing is minimal but elegant, made from bamboo fiber in shades of green and silver-gray. The fabric has a peculiar quality that seems to hold moisture without becoming wet, much like the moss that grows in their gardens. Many wear simple tools suspended from bamboo-fiber cords - cutting implements, measuring devices, and small ceramic vessels for collecting and redirecting water.

The Shapers' relationship with time is different from most peoples'. They can sit perfectly still for hours, observing the minute movements of growing plants. Their sleep patterns are irregular by most standards - they rest when the bamboo rests, wake when it wakens. Some of the eldest among them have become so attuned to the city's rhythms that they seem to photosynthesize, drawing sustenance from light and mist like the plants they tend.

They reproduce infrequently but intentionally, raising children with the same patient attention they give to their bamboo structures. Young Shapers spend their first years learning to be still, to observe, to feel the flow of water and the tension in growing stems. Only when they can sit in perfect stillness for a full day are they deemed ready to begin learning the actual techniques of growth-guidance.

There is no hierarchy among them, but there is profound respect for those who have shaped the largest structures - work that requires decades of consistent attention. These master shapers can often be found in the highest structures, their bodies so still they seem to merge with the bamboo around them, moving only to make the subtle adjustments that keep the city in its perfect balance.
