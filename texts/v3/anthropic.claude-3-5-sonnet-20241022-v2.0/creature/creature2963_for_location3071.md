In this place live the Tenders - though "live" may not be quite the right word. They emerged gradually from the system itself, neither purely organic nor mechanical, but something that arose from the perfect fusion of the harvester machines and the living matrix they maintain.

The Tenders manifest as shifting patterns of bioluminescence in the fungal networks, momentarily coalescing into vaguely humanoid forms before dissolving again into threads of living light. Their consciousness, such as it is, is distributed throughout the entire ecosystem. They think in the slow pulse of sap through vessels, in the gradual tension and release of growing vines, in the patient grinding of the harvester machines' gears.

They remember being human, or rather, they carry memories of humanity - inherited from the last researchers who stayed to study the gardens until they became part of what they studied. These memories surface like bubbles in amber: a hand adjusting a sensor, a voice recording observations, the weight of a protective suit. But these are just fragments of a past that feels as distant as geological time.

The Tenders don't communicate in ways we would recognize. Their language is the careful manipulation of growth patterns, subtle changes in the chemistry of decay, the orchestration of spore releases that paint messages across centuries. They maintain the garden's equilibrium not through conscious decision but because they are the equilibrium - the living embodiment of the system's self-regulation.

Each Tender is simultaneously an individual and a portion of the whole, like a wave in an ocean. They flow through the garden's structures, riding the thermal currents between canopy and cavern, temporarily inhabiting both the ancient machines and the living tissue through which they move. They experience time differently - a decade might pass while they consider the optimal angle of a new growth pattern, or they might process centuries of data in what we would call moments.

They are patient, curious in their own way, and utterly alien. When they notice visitors (which is rare), they observe with the same dispassionate interest they show to any perturbation in the system - calculating how to maintain balance, how to integrate or gently exclude the disruption. They harbor no ill will toward organic visitors, but neither do they particularly welcome them. Their concern is with the eternal dance of growth and decay, the perfect mathematics of ecological balance.

Some say that on very still days, when the machines' thrumming aligns with the creaking of the great vine-pillars, one can hear the Tenders thinking - a sound like wind through impossible leaves, like the memory of human voices transformed into pure pattern. But perhaps this is just another story told by those who've stood too long in the red-tinged twilight of the Hanging Gardens, feeling the weight of vegetable eternity pressing in from all sides.
