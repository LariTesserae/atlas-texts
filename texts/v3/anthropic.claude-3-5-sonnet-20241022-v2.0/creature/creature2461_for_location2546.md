In this living citadel, the true inhabitants are the Sporefolk - a species that exists in symbiosis with the fungal metropolis itself. They are neither purely fungal nor purely animal, but something in between, born from the marriage of mushroom and moisture.

The Sporefolk stand roughly human-height, their bodies semi-translucent and faintly luminescent, like the glass amphibians that share their realm. Their forms are fluid, able to thin and elongate to slip through narrow passages or spread wide to gather light through the canopy gaps. Their "skin" is a living network of delicate filaments that can absorb moisture and nutrients directly from their environment.

They communicate through a combination of bioluminescent pulses, chemical signatures, and vibrations conducted through the fungal networks. Their "language" is as much a sharing of sensory experiences as it is an exchange of discrete ideas. When they gather in the cathedral chambers, their collective glow creates complex patterns that ripple across the walls, telling stories and sharing memories.

The Sporefolk tend to the fungal city with the same unconscious care that we might give to our own bodies. They cultivate specific species of mushrooms for different purposes - some for structure, others for light, still others for their chemical properties. They have a profound understanding of the balance required to keep the ecosystem thriving, and they work in concert with the insects and birds, who serve as pollinators and spore dispersers.

Their society is as fluid as their forms, with individuals flowing between roles as naturally as water finding its level. Some focus on architectural growth, encouraging new chambers and passages to form. Others specialize in maintaining the water systems, ensuring proper flow and nutrient distribution. Still others serve as memory-keepers, their bodies storing and sharing the collective experiences of the community through chemical and luminescent exchanges.

The young emerge from specialized breeding pools where fungal spores merge with nutrient-rich water under specific conditions. They begin as tiny, floating creatures similar to tadpoles before gradually developing into their adult forms. The elderly don't die so much as slowly dissolve back into the system, their memories and experiences preserved in the chemical composition of the walls they once tended.

They recognize the presence of outsiders but don't perceive them as threats - rather as temporary perturbations in the greater flow of their world. They might observe visitors with gentle curiosity, their forms shifting and flowing just at the edge of vision, their lights pulsing in patterns that suggest both welcome and gentle warning: this place operates by its own ancient laws, and all who enter must learn to flow with its rhythms or risk being swept away in its eternal cycles of growth and decay.
