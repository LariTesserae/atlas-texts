Within these ceramic halls dwells the Vessel Keeper - though whether it's truly a single entity or many is difficult to determine. At first glimpse, you might catch what appears to be a tall, robed figure moving through the archways, but as your eyes adjust, you realize what you're seeing is a flowing collection of tiny ceramic shards, held together in a loose approximation of humanoid form.

These shards - thousands of them - are actually highly specialized creatures that evolved from common pottery beetles. Each has a shell that mimics fired clay in texture and glaze, and they move in perfect coordination, streaming through the vessel-corridors like water given purpose. When still, they often arrange themselves into shapes that echo the vessels around them - becoming a series of small cups along a wall, or clustering into the suggestion of an urn.

The Keeper tends to the garden's many systems with meticulous care. Its constituent beetles can spread out to simultaneously monitor dozens of water channels, adjust the flow by subtly shifting positions, and clear blockages. They seem to communicate through minute vibrations that resonate with the chamber's natural acoustics - their movements adding another layer to the garden's constant music.

What makes the Keeper truly remarkable is its relationship with the garden's clay. The beetles secrete a substance that can temporarily soften and reshape pottery, allowing them to perform repairs and even create new vessels as needed. This process is exceedingly slow - it might take months for them to craft a single new cup - but they work with the patience of creatures that measure time in generations rather than hours.

The Keeper appears to have a symbiotic relationship with all the garden's other inhabitants. The luminescent moths are drawn to the subtle biochemical glow of the beetles' shells, and in turn, their movements help distribute spores that the beetles feed on. The long-toed mice leave alone the vessels that house active beetle colonies, perhaps recognizing them as part of the larger entity they comprise.

Most fascinating is how the Keeper preserves and passes on knowledge. When individual beetles die, their shells become part of the garden's architecture, and new beetles emerge from eggs laid in specially maintained vessels. These new generations seem to inherit not just their predecessors' physical adaptations, but also their accumulated understanding of the garden's needs and rhythms.

The Keeper's consciousness, if it can be called that, appears to be distributed throughout its component parts - like the garden itself, it's a complex system operating through countless small interactions. It's both the guardian and a living expression of this place, as much a part of the garden's cycles as the water that flows through its vessels.
