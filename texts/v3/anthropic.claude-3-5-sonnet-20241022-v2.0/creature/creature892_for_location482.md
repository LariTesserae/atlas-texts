Doctor Eleanor Wren lives and works here, though her colleagues sometimes joke that she's become more amphibian than human after fifteen years of running the facility. She moves through the space with the same deliberate care as her charges, her footsteps precise and silent on the raised ceramic platforms. Her white coat has taken on a subtle green tinge from years of brush-contact with the moss-covered walls, and she's never bothered to replace it.

She's a tall woman made to seem shorter by her habitual slight stoop - the posture of someone who spends hours observing tiny creatures. Her hair, pulled back in a tight silver braid, catches the dappled light in a way that sometimes makes it look wet. She speaks softly, barely above a whisper, having learned that volume is unnecessary in a space where sound carries so efficiently through the moist air. Her hands, when not taking notes, often rest palm-up at her sides, unconsciously mimicking the posture of the glass frogs she studies.

Eleanor shares her private observation pod with Maurice, an elderly red-spotted salamander who wandered in through a ventilation shaft years ago and refused to join the research population. He lives in a crack between two copper pipes that carry warm water, emerging occasionally to accept choice insects from Eleanor's fingers. She knows this compromises certain research protocols, but after so many years, she considers Maurice more of a colleague than a test subject.

The pod itself reflects its occupant: meticulously organized but with an organic touch. Her notes are written in green ink, the pages of her journals rippled from humidity. Small sketches of amphibian anatomy decorate the margins, precise as technical drawings but somehow tender. A collection of glass jars holds specimens preserved in clear liquid, their labels written in her distinctive slanted handwriting. A brass microscope sits on a copper stand, its base wrapped in moss for stability.

She keeps odd hours, often working through the night when certain species are most active. The other researchers have learned to recognize her presence by the soft glow of her copper lamp moving behind the screens, or by the subtle changes in the chorus of frog calls that follow her progress through their territories. The amphibians know her, not as a threat but as part of their environment, like the mist or the flowing water.

Eleanor rarely leaves the facility except for quarterly meetings with the institute's board. On these occasions, she emerges blinking into the sunlight, her movements temporarily too quick, her voice too loud, until she can adjust to the dryer, faster world outside. Her reports are thorough but increasingly philosophical, full of observations about time and adaptation that make the board members exchange worried glances.

They say she's going native, but Eleanor knows better. She's not becoming more like her amphibians; she's simply slowing down enough to understand their world. In her pocket, she carries a small brass pocket watch that hasn't worked in years. She doesn't need it anymore - she can tell time by the rhythm of the mist cycles, the movement of light through the tinted panels, and the changing songs of her cold-blooded companions.
