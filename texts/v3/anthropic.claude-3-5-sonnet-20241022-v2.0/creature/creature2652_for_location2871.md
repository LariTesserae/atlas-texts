Here live the Flux People - though they would never call themselves that. They are creatures of constant adaptation, their very bodies having evolved (or perhaps deliberately transformed) to mirror their environment's liquid nature. Their flesh has a slight translucence, like clouded glass, with visible currents moving beneath the surface. Their bones are more flexible than rigid, allowing them to shift and flow with the city's movements, and their joints can rotate in unusual ways to maintain balance on the ever-changing ground.

They don't so much walk as flow, their gait a fluid combination of stepping and sliding that automatically adjusts to whatever surface they encounter. Their fingers are long and multiply-jointed, perfect for scrambling across the pipe networks or making the constant adjustments their machinery requires. Their eyes have multiple pupils that can dilate independently, allowing them to track movement in several directions at once and see clearly through the perpetual haze.

But it's their internal architecture that truly sets them apart. Their circulatory system mirrors the city's infrastructure - a complex network of semi-independent systems that can reroute and adapt as needed. Their lungs have evolved to filter and even extract sustenance from the mineral-rich steam that pervades the air. Their nervous system operates more like a distributed network than a centralized hierarchy, allowing them to respond instantly to changes in their environment without conscious thought.

They communicate through a combination of conventional speech and subtle bodily signals - ripples of color through their translucent flesh, patterns of movement that convey meaning as clearly as words. Their language is rich with metaphors of flow and adaptation, and they have dozens of words for different types of ground movement and steam conditions.

They live in extended family groups that are as fluid as everything else in their world, members flowing in and out of different households as needed to maintain the community's balance. Their homes are as much process as place, constantly being adjusted and modified to accommodate the shifting ground. They sleep in hammock-like structures that sway with the building's movements, their bodies partially liquefying during rest.

They are master engineers, but their technology looks nothing like conventional human machinery. Their tools and devices are designed to flex and flow, to fail gracefully and be easily repaired. They work primarily in materials that can adapt - special alloys that can bend without breaking, polymers that can change their properties in response to stress, biological components that can grow and heal.

Their art reflects their nature - sculptures that move and change, music that evolves over time like the flow of water, stories that have no fixed form but adapt to each telling. They create beauty from impermanence, finding grace in the constant dance of adaptation.

They have a deep, almost mystical relationship with their city's movements. The oldest among them can read the subtle patterns of steam and subsidence like a text, predicting shifts days or weeks in advance. They pass this knowledge down not through formal teaching but through a kind of apprenticeship of attention, young ones learning to feel the city's rhythms in their own bodies.

They are not fearless - they respect the power of their environment too much for that - but they have transcended fear through perfect adaptation. They don't fight against instability; they have become instability refined into grace.
