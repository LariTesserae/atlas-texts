In this living metropolis, the primary inhabitants are beings I'll call the Symbionts - entities that exist as a fluid merger between plant and animal characteristics, much like their city itself. They have translucent bodies that seem to shift between solid and semi-solid states, their internal structures visible as intricate patterns of varying density. Their "skin" can photosynthesize like the city's walls, and they process both light and nutrients absorbed through direct contact with their environment.

The Symbionts move with deliberate grace, extending temporary appendages as needed - sometimes forming legs to walk the living pathways, other times spreading sail-like membranes to catch the humid air currents. Their bodies range from roughly human-sized to much smaller, and they can modify their form significantly, though each maintains a distinct core pattern that identifies them as individuals.

They communicate through a combination of color changes, bioluminescent pulses, and chemical signatures released into the air and water. Their "language" is as much about sharing metabolic information as it is about abstract concepts. When they gather, their bodies sometimes partially merge, sharing nutrients and information in direct chemical exchanges.

The Symbionts don't simply inhabit the Living City - they are integral to its metabolism. Their bodies process and transform materials the city needs, and in turn, they receive sustenance from it. They tend to cluster in the spaces where building-plants join together, their bodies sometimes extending fine filaments into the living walls. During rest periods, they might become almost indistinguishable from the city's tissue, their patterns blending with the architectural forms.

Several distinct variants exist, each specialized for different roles in maintaining the city's complex biological systems. Some focus on managing growth patterns, their bodies producing chemical signals that guide the development of new chambers and pathways. Others concentrate on maintaining the delicate balance of the water systems, their bodies filtering and transforming nutrients as they move through the pools.

Younger Symbionts begin as small, highly mobile forms that flit between water and air like the other aerofauna, gradually developing more complex abilities to merge with and influence their environment as they mature. The eldest become nearly sessile, their bodies so integrated with the city's systems that they function as living regulatory nodes, their consciousness distributed through the biological networks they help maintain.

They have a profound awareness of time that operates on multiple scales simultaneously - they can process immediate sensory information quickly when needed, but their default state is a slower, more deliberate consciousness that matches the city's growth patterns. Their memories are stored both chemically within their bodies and in the living tissues of the city itself, accessible through direct biological connection.

Their society, if it can be called that, operates more like an ecosystem than a hierarchy. Different roles emerge naturally from biological affinities rather than being assigned. Decisions affecting the whole city emerge gradually through chemical consensus, spreading through the networks of living tissue like slow waves of realization.

They do not seem to make or use tools in any traditional sense - instead, they grow or become whatever they need, their bodies and their city adapting together in an endless dance of mutual transformation. Their technology, their architecture, and their very bodies are all expressions of the same fundamental biological processes, refined over countless generations into their current elegant forms.
