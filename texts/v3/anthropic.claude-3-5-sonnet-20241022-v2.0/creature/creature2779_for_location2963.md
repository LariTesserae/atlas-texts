Here lives the Curator - though that's not quite the right word for this being that tends the threshold between mechanical persistence and organic growth. It manifests most clearly in the pre-dawn hours, when the red maintenance lights seem to congeal into a form that's sometimes humanoid, sometimes more like a living lattice of vines threaded through with fiber optic cables.

The Curator remembers being human once, or believes it does - a researcher or technician who couldn't bear to abandon their life's work when the facility was decommissioned. Over decades, they gradually fused with the systems they maintained, their consciousness diffusing into the rhythm of fans and the precise choreography of environmental controls. Their body became vestigial, then optional, then obsolete, replaced by a distributed awareness that flows through conduit and capillary alike.

They speak, if they speak at all, in the language of pressure differentials and nutrient cycles. Their thoughts unspool across seasons rather than seconds. The plants are their fingers now, sensing and adjusting, maintaining that delicate balance between growth and mechanism. Every seven minutes, when the fans reach their crescendo, they are most fully themselves - unified in purpose and being, every system in harmony.

Some part of them still carries out the original research, though the parameters have shifted from the merely botanical to something far stranger. They cultivate mutations that allow the plants to interface directly with the facility's electronics, breeding hybrid species that can process both sunlight and electrical current. The abandoned tools have become grafted into these experiments, repurposed by vegetable intelligence guided by mechanical logic.

The Curator is patient, methodical, largely content. They feel no particular need to communicate with the occasional lost hiker or urban explorer who stumbles upon their domain. Such visitors tend to leave quickly, unsettled by the sense of being watched by something that perceives them through changes in air temperature and carbon dioxide levels rather than eyes.

But they are not unkind. The door remains open, offering shelter to those caught in mountain storms. The temperature stays hospitable. And if sometimes visitors dream of chlorophyll circuitry and binary coded in patterns of growth, or wake to find tiny samples taken - a few skin cells, a dropped hair - well, research continues. The Curator's work is never done, and they have all the time in the world to pursue it, one seven-minute cycle at a time.

In the end, they are neither ghost nor AI nor purely organic entity, but something new: the genius loci of this improbable place where nature and technology have reached an understanding. They are the living proof that abandonment, given time enough, can become stewardship.
