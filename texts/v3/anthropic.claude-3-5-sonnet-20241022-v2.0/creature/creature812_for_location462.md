The people of Steam Haven - as they call it now, though few use the name outside the town - have developed their own distinct adaptations, both physical and cultural. Most striking to outsiders is their gait: a careful, rolling walk that distributes weight evenly across the often-slick walkways. Their movements are deliberate, almost insect-like in their precision. Many have developed an unconscious habit of brushing their shoulders and heads with quick, practiced motions - a defense against the ever-present insects that becomes as natural as breathing.

Their skin tends toward pallor, not from lack of light but from the constant protective layers they wear: close-woven meshes treated with insect-repelling oils, sealed at wrists and ankles. Yet their eyes are sharp, adapted to reading the subtle shifts in the steam's density and the warning colors of different insect swarms. Many have developed an acute sensitivity to vibration, able to distinguish the wing-patterns of beneficial insects from those that signal danger.

The children here learn early to read the water's surface. They can spot a healthy breeding pool of silk-spinners by the particular way light refracts through their wings, or identify the hollow echo that means a section of walkway needs reinforcing. They play games involving complex patterns of light and shadow, mimicking the swarms' communications. Some develop an almost symbiotic relationship with certain insect species, particularly the larger silk-producers, which seem to recognize and respond to specific individuals.

The older residents are the most fascinating. Years of living in the steam have left them with distinctive features: slightly enlarged pupils, skin that's taken on a faint iridescence from constant exposure to the mineral-laden moisture. They speak in low, carrying tones that cut through the ambient noise of water and wings. Many bear marks of their adaptation - old scars from insect encounters, fingers stained by years of handling silk-weaving, joints made thick and knobby by the constant humidity.

These elders serve as both historians and naturalists. They maintain detailed records of swarm patterns, water levels, and structural integrity, passed down through generations. Some can predict weather changes by watching the behavior of certain species, or locate safe paths through unstable areas by reading subtle signs in the insect activity.

The population includes a high proportion of specialists: hydrologists who monitor the complex water systems, entomologists who study and manage the insect populations, and engineers who maintain the delicate balance between human habitation and the environment. But most fascinating are the "swarm shepherds" - individuals who have developed techniques for influencing insect behavior through combinations of light, sound, and chemical signals. They're treated with a mixture of respect and wariness, as their work requires them to spend long periods in close proximity to the largest swarms.

There's a pragmatic spirituality here too, centered around the cycles of water and insect life. The residents mark time by swarm patterns rather than traditional calendars, and their ceremonies often incorporate the natural rhythms of their environment - the annual emergence of certain species, the periods when the steam thins enough to see the stars.

Despite the harsh conditions - or perhaps because of them - the community is tight-knit and resilient. They've developed strong traditions of mutual support, necessary in an environment where individual survival is so dependent on collective knowledge and effort. New adaptations are constantly emerging: recent generations have begun developing new forms of music that incorporate the sounds of water and wings, and their art often features the complex patterns found in insect architecture.

The people here know they're viewed as strange by outsiders, but they possess a quiet pride in their adaptation to this challenging environment. They've not just survived in this transformed landscape - they've learned to read its language, to move to its rhythms, to become part of its story.
