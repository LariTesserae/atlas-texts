Let me describe those who make this remarkable place their home:

The primary inhabitants call themselves the Teng-wei, a people whose ancestry reflects a deliberate melding of human and what they name "cloud-walker" lineages. This integration happened so long ago that trying to separate the two strands would be like trying to unweave morning mist. They stand slightly taller than typical humans, with elongated limbs that move with fluid grace. Their skin has a subtle translucence that seems to shift with their moods, occasionally revealing patterns beneath that echo the bamboo's growth rings. Their eyes are large and multifaceted, catching light like dewdrops, equally adapted to the bright days and the luminescent nights.

The Teng-wei live in extended family groups called "stems," each tending to their own section of the living city while maintaining complex relationships with other stems through a system of reciprocal obligations as intricately woven as the silk-spinners' paths. Their children learn to walk on swaying bridges before solid ground, developing an innate sense of balance that makes their movement through the city appear almost choreographed.

The silk-spinners, their closest non-human partners, are more than mere arachnids. Each colony has a collective consciousness that can communicate with their designated Teng-wei family through a combination of chemical signals and intricate web patterns. They range from tiny individuals no larger than a fingernail to matriarchs the size of a human palm. Their silk comes in different varieties - some structural, some water-gathering, some luminescent - and they can adjust its properties through careful manipulation of their diet and the ambient humidity.

Less visible but equally important are the "bamboo-listeners" - specialized members of each stem who have developed an almost mystical connection with the living architecture. They can sense early signs of stress in the structure, predict where new growth will be most vigorous, and communicate with the plant consciousness that has evolved alongside the city. Their bodies are marked with patterns that match the sections of bamboo they tend, created through a careful process involving the application of certain epiphyte extracts.

The small mammals, called "hand-dancers" by the Teng-wei, are semi-domesticated symbiotes who evolved alongside the city itself. Each has six limbs and iridescent fur that helps them blend with the epiphytes they tend. They possess a sophisticated social structure and have developed a cooperative relationship with both the Teng-wei and the silk-spinners, helping to maintain the complex ecosystem of the city in exchange for shelter and protection.

Throughout the city, you'll find the "mist-weavers" - beings that seem to exist halfway between matter and vapor. They appear most commonly at dawn and dusk, moving through the city's airways and water channels, their forms suggesting humanoid shapes but never quite resolving into solid bodies. The Teng-wei treat them with casual reverence, like respected neighbors whose ways are different but whose presence is essential to the city's harmony.

All these inhabitants move through their days in cycles that mirror the rhythm of their living home - rising with the morning mist, working in harmony with the bamboo's natural growth patterns, and settling into meditative stillness as the evening's bioluminescence begins to glow. Their technology, their art, and their social structures all reflect deep integration with their environment, creating a way of life that feels both highly advanced and profoundly natural.
