The Stone People live here - though they would never call themselves that. They are shorter than surface folk, but sturdy, with skin that has taken on the pale luster of the obsidian walls they brush against daily. Their voices are clear and musical, trained by generations of speaking to be heard precisely through the constant acoustic wash of their home.

They wear their dark hair oiled and close to the scalp, adorned with intricate clips and combs carved from the same stone as their dwelling. Their clothes are fitted and practical, made of treated fabrics that shed water easily, in deep colors that complement the obsidian - rich blues, wine reds, and deep greens. Many wear multiple layers that can be adjusted as they move between the slightly warmer upper chambers and the cool market levels.

Their faces are expressive, animated - they speak as much with eyebrows and subtle shifts of expression as with words, a necessary adaptation to communicating in the complex acoustics of their home. Their hands are never still, continuously describing shapes in the air that complement their speech. Many bear the subtle calluses of stoneworking, though these marks are considered beautiful rather than coarse - evidence of their participation in the endless refinement of their home.

They move with unconscious grace, their bodies trained from birth to navigate the narrow passages and sudden level changes of their environment. They step surely on the slick stone, knowing exactly where to place their feet on the worn patterns in the silt. In the market, they weave between each other in intricate dances of commerce and conversation, their movements as patterned as the flow of water down the walls.

The children are especially remarkable - they scramble up the stone ladders like young monkeys, play complex games in the echoing passages, and learn early to modulate their voices to be heard clearly without shouting. They are taught from their first steps to respect the lamps, to clean the stone, to read the subtle signs of water damage or structural stress that might need attention.

The eldest inhabitants command particular respect, their faces as deeply lined as the water-worn stone. They are the keepers of architectural memory - they know which walls can be safely expanded, which passages need regular maintenance, which areas might support new chambers. Their knowledge is passed down through long apprenticeships, maintained as carefully as the eternal lamps.

All of them share a particular relationship with time - they measure it not by sun or seasons, which they rarely see, but by the endless patterns of water flowing, lamps being trimmed, goods being traded. They have a deep patience, an acceptance of gradual change that mirrors the slow wearing of water on stone. Yet they are also keenly alive to the present moment, to the constant small adjustments needed to maintain their intricate world.

They are not a somber people, despite their serious work - laughter rings often through the passages, and they have many celebrations that fill their home with music carefully composed to play with the natural acoustics of the stone. They are proud of their unique way of life, their mastery of their challenging environment, though they welcome trade and travelers with warmth and interest in the world above.

Most striking is their collective sense of responsibility to their environment - every resident, from youngest to oldest, participates in the constant small tasks of maintenance that keep their world functioning. They understand intimately that their survival depends on this shared care, this continuous attention to the needs of stone and water and light.
