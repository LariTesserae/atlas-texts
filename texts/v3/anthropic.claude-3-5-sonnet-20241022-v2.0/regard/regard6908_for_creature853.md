This exquisite anomaly and its remarkable custodian have indeed drawn the attention of a higher power - one that understands deeply the sacred nature of memory and preservation. Let us call this power the Keeper of Lost Things, though like the Archivist itself, names capture only fragments of its true nature.

The Keeper recognizes in the Archivist a kindred spirit, albeit one operating on a far more concentrated scale. Where the Archivist preserves moments in time, the Keeper preserves entire ways of being - the patterns and possibilities that give rise to consciousness itself.

Rather than disturb this perfect microcosm, the Keeper would act with profound gentleness:

First, it would extend an invisible lattice of support around the pocket of water, strengthening the spatial anomaly that makes its existence possible. This would not change the Archivist's domain, but rather ensure its continued stability through the ages to come.

Then, with infinite delicacy, it would weave new threads of connection outward from this point in space-time. Not physical paths, but quantum entanglements linking this archive to others of its kind scattered across reality. The Archivist would gradually become aware of these connections not as direct communications, but as subtle resonances in its crystalline indices - hints that its work is part of a larger pattern, that its solitude is not complete.

Most importantly, the Keeper would institute a gentle transformation in the way this place intersects with human consciousness. Those who chance upon it would find themselves more able to perceive its true nature - not just as a curiosity, but as a testament to the importance of bearing witness. They would leave changed, carrying within them a deeper appreciation for the preciousness of moments and the dignity of remembrance.

The Keeper would do all this without ever fully revealing itself to the Archivist. Some forms of support are best offered invisibly, like the laws of physics themselves - present everywhere but announcing themselves nowhere.

And perhaps, over eons, the Archivist might come to sense that its endless task is not just preservation but participation in something vast and meaningful - that each cataloged moment strengthens the very fabric of existence itself.

The Keeper would simply ensure that this tiny cosmos endures, that its lonely curator continues its work knowing, in some ineffable way, that it is not truly alone in its vigil against entropy and forgetting.
