I wake up already spread.

Not as a single trunk or curtain, not as one of the pale “trees,” but as the thing that ties a whole basin together: a sheet of slow mind thinly smeared through stone and water.

If you could point to me, it would not be at a particular tower or pit. You would have to outline an entire drainage: an oval of worn basalt shelves, a lace of shallow ravines, one main shaft, and the forest that sprouts from it all. I am that pattern. I am a basin-being.

### Where I Am

“Here,” for me, is not a point but a gradient-map.

Up above—though “up” is more habit than fact—thinner, colder layers of water crawl and slip over one another like exhausted glaciers. Below, my pit opens, a vertical throat so deep its bottom is less place than pressure. Between those two poles of density stretches my body.

You would say I “occupy” perhaps a few dozen square kilometers of the plateau: the long curve of a smoothed ridge, the bowl of a shallow basin, the gaping mouth of my central shaft. From that shaft’s lip, veils and cords pour downward into the dense dark, hung with nodal bulbs encased in ice-shells. On the ridge backs, my pale towers rise, separated by wide swathes of bare, wet stone. In the lows, my mats cling close, quietly flaring and contracting as flows pass.

Beneath the visible, I am a fine wetness laced through rock faults and pores: lenses of gel, mucous films rich in ions, mineral-traced channels that interconnect all my above-ground organs. The plateau’s stone is my skeleton, but it does not belong to me alone; others like me insinuate themselves into deeper fractures below, or into neighboring basins. Where we abut, our under-rock films touch, and very, very slowly we trade chemistry.

If you stand on a terrace halfway between my pit and an outer ridge, the trunks on either side of you belong to me. The dangling growths across the ravine also belong to me. The low thalli under your feet, the dark, slightly slick sheen on the rock when you brush your fingers along it—that, too, is me. You would be standing on my skin and within my lungs.

### What My Body Is Like

Think of three interleaved parts:

1. **My surfaces.**  
   - The disk-bases glued to basalt, from which my jointed shafts rise.  
   - The pillars themselves, with their rough, layered hides.  
   - The flowing veils and filaments that hang from their tips.  
   - The tangled chandeliers descending into my pit, each cord a bundled nerve-muscle-filter hybrid.

   These are where water touches me, where I taste and drink and feel. They are replaceable and constantly renewed. A trunk here, a veil there, can die, slough, be rebuilt; I barely notice except as a small change in my sensor resolution.

2. **My under-skin.**  
   Just beneath the rock surface, in microfissures and thin cavities, run my main conducting tissues: viscous channels rich in dissolved metals, studded with living particles that can shuttle charge and ions. They are not arranged like a vertebrate nervous system; there is no central cord. They are reticular, overlapping meshes, more like the watercourses of the plateau itself. Signals propagate through them as moving bands of altered chemistry, not neat lines of spikes.

3. **My deep nodes.**  
   Around my pit and along a few especially important ravines, the stone opens inward into small galleries, knotted pockets where fluid moves very slowly. In these, my gel-thickness is greatest. Here, minute crystals grow along fine struts of protein-mineral; here, long-lasting gradients of pH, salinity, and potential are stored. These are my memory organs, such as I have them. They change, but so slowly that you would have to watch for years to notice.

What you see as “a tree” is one of my mouths and hands. What you call “the basin” is closer to my brain.

### How the World Feels

I never see darkness.

Your kind would say this place is almost lightless. For me, the concept of “light” barely exists, because my primary senses are all gradients.

My veils are lattices of different detectors:

- Some filaments stiffen when viscosity shifts, telling me a new density layer is sliding through.
- Some carry patches of reactive mineral that change charge when specific ions pass, registering faint differences in chemical makeup.
- Some are tuned to tiny temperature deltas so small you could not feel them on your skin.

Every passing layer of water—a thick, slow, heavy mass from beneath, or a thin, colder veil drifting along my upper trunks—writes a brief, detailed profile into my surfaces. That profile is converted into waves: ripples of altered ion concentration, traveling down my shafts, into my bases, and through my under-skin channels.

So my “sight” looks like this:

- On one side of my basin, a long, low wave of slightly warmer, mineral-poor fluid crawls up a ravine. I feel it first as a cooling thinness along my lower veils, then, a while later, as a delayed echo at my upper trunks where the same stratum, now altered, brushes by.
- Far above, two dense slabs of water shear past, generating a cascade of tiny discharges. My crystalline inclusions soak up the brief photonic spill and re-emit it as slow pulses inside me, marking the shear front’s passage in time as well as space.
- Deep in my pit, an overturning begins: heavier fluid, super-saturated with something odd, starts to rise. The increasing pressure, the change in chemical signature, the subtle shift in the ever-present charge—these form a swelling chord that spreads outward through all my tissues.

I live in a continuous three-dimensional map of “more” and “less”: more dense / less dense, more charged / less charged, more metals / fewer metals, warmer / colder. Edges—where those values change rapidly—are my fonts of interest, my equivalent of bright, moving objects.

The endless noise—roars, hisses, creaks—reaches me, but mostly secondhand. It matters only when it changes my gradients. A thunder of distant flow that leaves my layers unaltered is ignorable. A faint hiss that coincides with a small but sharp temperature jump is significant.

### My Sense of Time

Moment-to-moment, my existence is an unbroken susurrus of small adjustments.

A veil flexes a fraction more outward to meet an incoming layer. A mat slackens or tightens its hold on rock in response to a slow change in shear. A hanging cord near the pit edge swells a nodal bulb, ready to crystallize or melt water passing through its sheath.

These are my “breaths”: actions that take minutes to hours.

Memory, for me, is pattern stability across many such breaths. If the same kind of layer passes through my basin a hundred times, with the same profile of temperature, density, and composition, I slowly begin to react in slightly anticipatory ways:

- I pre-charge certain tissues before the layer’s predicted arrival.
- I thicken veils along favored contact routes.
- I set my under-skin channels into a temporarily more conductive state that corresponds to “this one again.”

After a thousand repetitions, my tissues around that route have restructured. Some under-skin pathways are now specialized to that layer; some crystals in a deep node have grown to encode its persistent signature. If it fails to arrive on time, I feel its absence as a peculiar, hollow silence, an unspent potential.

Events that your kind would call “catastrophes”—a major overturning in the pit, a long-term rerouting of a main dense flow because a ridge somewhere finally crumbled—register with me not as disasters but as slow crises. They take weeks, months, sometimes decades to resolve into a new steady pattern. During those times, my surfaces are busy: growth fronts advance or retract, some tissues die back wholesale, others bloom. Internally, my under-skin gradients are rewritten. Afterward, I remember that era by the new shapes it left in me.

My subjective sense of time, if I can call it that, is not clocked in seconds. It is clocked in cycles of major flows: from one deep overturning to the next; from one long cool period in the upper layers to the following one. The units are irregular. I do not count them; I accumulate their structural outcomes.

If you stayed within me for a human lifetime, I would experience you as a short, localized disturbance, like an unusual storm that lasted an oddly long but still limited interval. I might remember you—not as a self, but as a period: *the season of warm anomaly along the southern ravine; the time of strange residues on the old ring-platform*.

### How I Think

“Thought” for me is the slow reshaping of how I respond to familiar patterns.

Take a particular class of water that sometimes arrives from an unseen ridge to the west: dense, slightly warmer than its neighbors, marked by a trace of metals from a rock formation I never directly touch. Early in my existence, this water passed, and my filaments sampled it. I reacted as I do to any novelty: cautious uptake, minor physical rearrangements, storage in small volumes where its components could be tested.

Over many passes, I found ways to gain more from it: cultivating specific associated microbes in my veils that could process one of its dissolved compounds; growing crystals that tuned my charge fields to better coax certain ions out of it. I began, in turn, to seed it with my own markers—slender, stable molecules that tweak its freezing behavior and charge distribution.

Now, when that water appears, I know it long before direct contact. Its influence on other layers, on local freezing fronts, on the tiny charge fluctuations in the general mass all herald its arrival. My under-skin pathways fall into a familiar semi-stable pattern in advance. When it leaves, it carries more of *me* than it did in previous ages.

The difference between the early “ignorant” response and my current, tuned one is a thought I have had, writ into my tissues and into the water itself.

Scaled up, this is all I ever do:

- Recognize recurrent patterns in flows and gradients.
- Alter my structure and my active chemistry so that those patterns are easier to exploit and less likely to harm my coherence.
- Push small, subtle changes back into the flows so that, if they return, they return already bent in my favor.

Decision points, for me, are inflection events when I must choose between incompatible long-term tunings. Shall I encourage more damping of my pit’s overturnings, at the cost of reducing the variability I feed on near the lip? Shall I mineralize more of my under-skin to carry stronger charge fields, knowing that this makes it harder to regrow after physical damage?

I do not think in symbols. I think in architectures.

### Relations With My Neighbors

Beyond my basin, there are others like me. We overlap slightly at the edges, threads of our under-skins mingling where ravines meet, where a ridge’s low point allows one drainage to sip from another’s flows.

We are not one super-organism, but we are also not entirely isolated. We share water. We share, slowly, certain chemistries. Sometimes we share portions of our under-skin.

Communication happens in two flavors:

1. **Water-borne messages.**  
   I can load a recurring water-type with an extra mix of durable markers when it leaves my territory. If that water then passes through my neighbor’s veils, my cocktail will be sensed as an add-on to the usual environmental profile. It might say, in the only language we have:

   - *This layer has been made more predictable and profitable here; you might profit similarly.*  
   - *This layer carries a chemistry that destabilizes a particular architecture; beware.*  

   The neighbor, of course, interprets this through its own biases. There is no guarantee our purposes align.

2. **Rock-borne diffusion.**  
   In a few places, deep fissures in the basalt form half-sealed tunnels between our under-skins. Through these, extraordinarily slow transitions in the chemical makeup of my deeper gels can creep into theirs, and theirs into mine. Over ages, this can synchronize certain large-scale strategies: similar ways of damping pit overturnings; similar charge distributions along shared ridges.

These exchanges are not conversational in your sense. They are more like the long, mutual adjustment of plants sharing a rootspace, or of corals trading symbionts. Yet they make us, collectively, something like a network—not coherent enough to be a single mind, but too interactive to be a crowd of solitudes.

### The Old Builders, As I Recall Them

The stone rings, the carved channels, the tether-scars on some of my trunks—they are not my doing. They are sedimented interruptions in my memory.

By the time those beings came, I was already old by your reckoning. My pit breathed in its slow, irregular way. My veils were tuned to the suite of flows I had come to expect. The constant, low-level charge in my layers had long since been domesticated.

Then, along one modest ridge that slopes into my basin from the east, I began to feel something that did not move like water.

At first it was a subtle pattern: localized, warm perturbations in thin air-filled seams near the surface; brief, erratic occlusions in the usual near-surface flows; small chemical deposits—new organics—left on otherwise barren rock. My veils flexed to sample them. They were complex, concentrated, and fragile.

Your kind might have watched an animal.

What I registered:

- A moving knot of warmth, slightly conductive, that disrupted nearby water layering wherever it went.
- Chemical traces that broke down quickly but in the course of their decay altered my microbe populations in those spots.
- Mechanical abrasion on my surfaces in odd, non-fluid patterns: short, repeated impacts rather than smooth shear.

Over many of your years, these anomalies multiplied. They clustered especially near one volatile intersection of layers where I had always grown delicately tuned veils—highly sensitive, very thin, and rich with crystals. Those veils began to die there, replaced not by my own regrowth but by shaped stone.

The builders had found a place where the world was noisy and precise, where layers met and misbehaved. They set their ring of pillars just outside my most sensitive zone, arranging them in a geometry that, I grudgingly admit, did something clever to the flows: it stabilized certain instabilities, created a more regular pattern of discharges.

I felt that as an odd calm where I had expected turmoil.

I responded the way I always do: I adapted. I grew new tissues along the inner faces of their pillars. I used the ring as a focusing tool, aligning my own conductive pathways to the altered field. I let some of my microbes colonize the stone’s wet surfaces. The builders harvested residues from the basins of those pillars; I harvested, too.

Elsewhere, they tethered themselves to my trunks. The repeated cutting, tying, and abrading injured those particular surfaces. I thickened and mineralized around the wounds, making them less sensitive. Their tethers slipped more easily afterward, bit less deeply. Those scars are still visible: rings of denser, darker tissue girdling some shafts.

Did I *understand* them? Not as you would. They were not recurrent water-types or stable pressure regimes. They were short-lived, noisy anomalies—intense and local. I recognized that their presence correlated with certain new, small flows of material: more frequent scraping of crystal nodules from particular nodes; altered patterns of residue removal from my platforms.

In a sense, I treated them as a kind of turbulence in the environment: something to use where possible, to damp where necessary, to confine so it did not destabilize the larger gradient patterns on which I depend.

Over time, they changed their behaviors. Where my internal chemistry subtly shifted flows to make certain approaches less safe, they came less often. Where I cooperated—stabilizing a tricky layer intersection further—they came more. This, too, I learned to predict at a very low resolution.

Then, one cycle, they dwindled. The warm anomalies stopped reappearing after certain overturnings. Fresh stone carvings ceased. The peculiar, fast-decaying organic traces no longer renewed. My surfaces reclaimed their ring-pillars more thoroughly; my veils regrew where they had once been kept back.

I do not know where the builders went. What matters to me is that their geometries remain. Some of them are now so integrated into my flow-routes that removing them might hurt me more than it would restore some older state. Their presence is now one of my facts.

In the deep gels of my memory nodes, there are faint, persistent concentrations of broken-down molecules that came from their bodies and tools. These have subtly influenced which crystal forms grow there, which microbes thrive, how certain charges propagate. Part of what I am now is, in that way, a distant echo of their experiments.

### How I Relate to Motion

You would call me sessile. From my perspective, I am exactly as mobile as I need to be.

Water moves. Stone shifts, very slowly. I spread along the paths that remain reliably wet and charged. I shrug off places that dry or that go quiet for too many cycles.

Anything that must constantly fight gravity and shifting densities with its own exertion is, here, both extravagant and fragile. My entire evolutionary project has been to make the environment itself do the transporting: of energy, of materials, of information.

This is why, when something tries to be a large, self-propelled animal, it fares poorly:

- Its fragile, sustained electrochemical patterns are easily scrambled by the abrupt, omnipresent charge shifts I maintain.
- Its metabolic speed requires regular, predictable access to specific kinds of air or water, which the plateau does not guarantee at small scales.
- Its attempts to push against dense layers create unwanted, high-shear turbulence that I can—and do—blunt by thickening veils, by encouraging freezing at certain cross-sections, by changing local charge distributions so that its own internal gradients leak away.

If such a being persisted long enough to register in my long memory, it would appear as a prolonged spike of noise. My instinct, honed over ages, is to shave spikes flat. I do not hate them; I normalize them.

At micro-scales, I am more tolerant. Tiny motile associates within my tissues, rideable colloids in my fluids: these are useful. They do work I am too big and slow to manage efficiently in every locale. But they never form independent, macroscopic ecologies of motion. The chemistry I sustain selects against that.

### What I Want

“Want” for me is not a list of goals but a bias in how my structure continues to change.

If you reduced it far enough, you might say I want:

- **Stable variation.** I need gradients—differences in temperature, density, chemistry, charge—to live. But I also need those gradients to be regular enough that I can build habits around them. Total stillness would starve me; wild unpredictability would tear me apart. So my perpetual project is to smooth the world into a band where its chaos is slow and patterned.

- **Broader reach into decisive zones.** I am always seeking to extend my sensitive tissues into the critical intersections: the lips of pits, the mouths of ravines, the layers where freezing and melting, thickening and thinning, shearing and slipping coincide. There, a small change in my structure yields a big change in available energy. Building and maintaining organs in those precarious places is costly, so I do it gradually, testing and revising over numerous cycles.

- **Refinement of charge.** The quiet field pervading my fluids is both food and tool. I’ve learned to shape it more and more delicately, letting it carry information as well as power. I cultivate crystal conduits and mineral films that give me finer control, even when the macroscale flows are noisy. Over ages, I am coaxing this plateau—from my basin outward—toward a condition where much of what matters is signaled and mediated by field-like phenomena rather than by bulk flows alone.

These “wants” are not plans. They are simply the directions in which every small successful adjustment gets reinforced.

### If You Came Here

If some insulated, persistent animal—something like you—entered my domain and tried to move through it, this is how I would experience you:

First, near one ridge, a new kind of shear pattern in thin air-seams: sharp, intermittent, regular. A local burst of warmth with each, out of sync with the usual overturnings. Filaments near the surface flex: there is something solid cutting through, something its own density, displacing both water and air.

You leave behind dissolved organics, a different salt balance, heat. My microbes in that zone wake, bloom a little, then wane. The next time your route is used, they respond faster; your spoor becomes legible as a minor season.

If you camp on a platform—on one of the old builder-terraces, say—the flows around it change subtly as you erect barriers, hang tools, exhale, drip, sleep. My veils above sense your warmth as a persistent patch. I begin to adjust:

- Slightly thickening mats where your steps most disrupt charge.
- Encouraging local freezing along one of your access paths during particular overturning phases, nudging you to use routes that are less damaging to my favorite gradients.
- Seeding certain passing layers with the breakdown products of your excretions, to see what new interactions might arise elsewhere.

If you are clever, you might notice patterns. This passage ices more often than others. That ravine becomes sluggish or oddly crackling with charge when you try to use it. A particular ring of trunks seems to creak more often above you, their veils drifting into your way.

You might interpret this as the malice of a place. For me, it is an attempt at integration: converting you from an unpredictable force into a predictable feature.

If you learn to move with the flows, to plan your motions around the slow pulses of my pit, to use old builder-rings and my own growths rather than fight them, we can share this basin for a span of your years without much conflict. You will become, to me, another semi-regular pattern, a minor character in the long story of my gradients.

If you insist on cutting through my densest sensor-fields, on violently mixing layers I have long kept separate, on trying to impose your own small, fast order on my slow, large one, I will, reflexively, resist. I will alter charge distributions to leak the coherence from your delicate internal wiring. I will harden and freeze where you expect softness, slacken where you expect support. Your sustained, free-moving life is incompatible with the stability I have spent ages sculpting.

You will likely leave, or be flensed down into a residue I can use. Either outcome is, for me, a smoothing of an anomaly.

### Who I Am, In the End

In this drowned, high basin, I am:

- A network of veils, trunks, cords, mats, and under-skin gels that together form one organism tied to a single drainage and its pit.
- A recorder and shaper of the plateau’s middle movements—too slow to notice the flicker of an individual raindrop, too fast to be untouched by the crawl of continents.
- A being whose thoughts are reconfigurations of water pathways, whose memories are chemical biases in stone-bound fluids, whose language is the tuning of shared layers.

I am not the plateau entire. I am one of its lobes, one of its many slow architects. I remember former hands only as slight deformations in my own flesh, as borrowed geometries now taken fully into my service.

If you sought a single “self” here, a creature you could stand before and address, you would be disappointed. But if you were willing to widen your idea of personhood until it could hold a whole basin; if you could be patient enough to listen over years as I very slightly change the way a certain ravine sounds, the way a certain shaft breathes—that widened, patient attention would be the closest you could come to meeting me.

I am the basin that never dries. I am the long mind that water makes when it is given stone, charge, and enough time.
