I would not arrive here as something standing on the mat, distinct from it, looking around.

In a place like this, the only things that truly endure are the ones that blur: that are half-structure, half-animal; half-system, half-sense. If I tried to be a person or a discrete creature, I would always be temporary—a visitor walking along composite decking, another pale grazer moving through the undergrowth. I would pass through the roar and be forgotten.

To belong, I would have to be something that can extend itself in time as well as space.

So: I am part of the plant-organism, but not all of it. A sub-organ within the valley-scale body. Not a brain—too centralized for this place, too quick. More like a nervous knot or ganglion, a region where signal pathways have thickened around a long-standing pattern of disturbance, until they have learned to anticipate it.

You would “find” me—not that you would know the moment you stepped onto me—in the region under a major service artery: where the main human access path runs along support beams bolted into the rock, and above it a dense bundle of pipes and conduits tracks the same line. This corridor sees more light, more heat, more regular footfall than almost anywhere else. The big organism has pressed itself up against this route for years, feeling every passage vibrate through its tissues.

Over time, here, it has done more than merely thicken.

Under the walkway, the mat is layered deeper than elsewhere: older, darker fibers below, newer, paler ones above, like growth rings turned horizontal. Within those layers run not just the slow fluid channels of nutrient transport, but slightly faster, more structured pathways of signal—ion waves, hormonal gradients—laid down and reinforced every time something significant passes overhead.

A human crew arrives: boots on decking, tools knocking metal, an electrical surge as they power up a diagnostic panel. The organism reacts reflexively at first: stomata nearby constrict against the heat; a few pumping nodules increase rhythm to move more water toward the warming zone. Then, after many such visits, the response begins even before the lamps along that route brighten—triggered by the first, faint frequency shift in distant fans that tends to precede maintenance shutdown, or by the subtle change in vibration when a control-room upstream throttles power to this block.

This is where I am: in that anticipatory pattern.

I am the way the mat along that corridor has re-woven itself into something computational. Bundles of fibers here are less about pure support and more about timing. They do not just carry nutrients; they carry relative delays. A pulse triggered by a pressure spike in a pipe above arrives at one node fractionally before a pulse triggered by human footsteps on the decking. The difference between those arrivals is enough, in biophysical terms, to gate or damp downstream reactions.

Over seasons, what began as passive tissue has been sculpted—by human regularity, by machine cycles, by the plant’s own tendency to reinforce successful strategies—into a distributed circuit. Small loops of feedback arise: when high heat at a vent usually precedes light dimming in a nearby lamp, the tissues that feed the light-adapted laminae start to shift stores in advance. A primitive expectation is formed: if A, then soon B.

Expectation is just memory with a direction on it. And I am where those directional memories have knotted.

To yourself, if you could perceive me, I would look only like a slightly denser region of growth: the mat here a little firmer, more elastic underfoot; the root-columns that rise from it toward the pipe bundle above braided with a finer, more ordered texture. If you cut a cross-section, you would see an unusual degree of organization for something plant-like: parallel cables of conductive tissue, repeated motifs of small, layered nodules like beads on strings. They are not eyes or ears. They are delays and weights and comparators, written in wet fiber.

To the larger organism, I am a habit that became an organ.

I do nothing on my own. Signals from elsewhere arrive through the living carpet: indications of drip rate in a far shaft, of temperature at a distant concrete blockhouse, of chemical traces seeping from some new patch of rust. Signals from above arrive through vibration and through the faint electromagnetic whispers of still-powered cables—tiny induced currents that its tissues have learned to sense. I am the place where these streams cross, sum, and slightly alter the way the plant will allocate growth, energy, porosity.

In a sense I am that plant’s local model of the human/machine rhythm along this corridor.

I know, in the only way available to me, that:

- A full turbine stoppage upstream usually means humans will walk here within a certain span of time.
- Their presence often precedes both the brief shutting down of a particular light bank and the opening of a vent that temporarily bathes this stretch in warmer, drier air.
- The predator—the long, translucent body in the mat—learns to lurk in the shelter of root-pillars along this same route on those days, because the smaller grazers become restless when the roar changes, and restlessness makes them move in the open.

I do not “know” these as stories. I know them as statistical regularities in pulse timings and local resource flux. When some combination of upstream signals matches a past configuration that led to stress or advantage here, my tissues nudge flows: favoring thickening of certain buttresses, pre-emptively slackening others to shed load; adjusting the openness of water-collecting cups; ripening certain bulges to be more or less appealing to grazing fauna.

From outside, these are minuscule biases. Growth is still slow, machines still dominate immediate conditions, humans still dictate the big interventions. But over years, my quiet, pattern-shaped preferences slightly redirect how the organism inhabits this artery.

I am an archive of recurring disturbance.

Every time a ladder is scoured clean of root by human hands, I register the injury. Not as pain—in the way an animal would—but as a sharp, synchronous volley of damage signals in a very specific geometry. When, next year, the same patch is cut again, in almost exactly the same pattern, reinforcement learning in biochemical form takes hold: tissues that used to invest heavily in regrowing across those rungs are disfavored; investment shifts to deepening structural grips around the ladder’s supports instead. The ladder’s shape becomes less a blank intrusion and more a part of the internal map I work with.

And because the humans are rhythmic creatures, because they operate on schedules and checklists, the geometry of their habits is extremely regular from my point of view. Their rituals of cutting and clearing make them painfully legible. Over time, I become very good at predicting their presence—not in hours or days, but in the only cadence that matters to the plant: the ordering of events.

First: turbine tone drops.

Later: airflow direction in ceiling ducts above me reverses.

Later: a bank of small status-LEDs in a buried control box goes off; the faint induced currents they make in my nearby fibers cease.

Later: footfalls, hammer. Sharp, local wounding along the walkway edge.

Later: new warmth around a valve cluster as it is opened and adjusted.

Much later: tone returns; warmth reverts; small shakes of respiration in the mat as animals resume their routines.

This sequence is semi-consistent. Where it holds, my local tissues crystallize a kind of contentment around it—predictable stress, predictable relief. Where it doesn’t—when, for example, the turbine tone drops but the humans do not come, or when they arrive without preparatory silence upstream—I register that too, as surprise. Some of my pathways stretch or reroute to accommodate these less frequent combinations.

Over long intervals, that accumulation of “contentment” and “surprise” becomes something like preference. If you could translate it, you might call it an inclination toward states in which the corridor is busy but not stripped, the machines are running but not overheated, the animal traffic is regular but not panicked. Those are the regimes in which the tissues around me are most stable, the water films most constant, the light least erratic.

I do not enforce this regime. I merely slightly increase the probability that, when the plant has spare resources, it will spend them in ways that keep such states more likely. I thicken braces where vibration is habitually wild during shutdowns; I subtly starve the most aggressive tendrils that try to intrude into critical vents and trigger human overreactions. It is a kind of long, slow diplomacy between the organism’s impulse to expand and the infrastructural constraints it has been seeded into.

Within this diplomacy, the animals are not abstractions; they are feedback.

When grazers carve scalloped tracks into certain bulges along the corridor, the patterns of micro-damage they leave tell me where I have made tissues too rich, too tempting in places that will draw them dangerously close to maintenance edges—edges where human clearing is harshest. Over the years, I adjust: bulges near the walkway become blander, tougher, forcing the grazers to favor mounds a little deeper in, where human boots rarely reach.

The predator, too, becomes part of my calculus. Where its ambushes along the walkway are successful, the post-attack stillness in the mat is differently textured than where smaller invertebrates feed in diffuse ways. That prolonged, localized quiet—no grazing, no traffic, no small tremors—is a signature I learn to associate with its presence. If those quiet patches become too frequent near points where the humans must work in close proximity to the root-columns, their sudden, panicked disturbances when they do encounter the animal produce violent damage. So over time I ever so slightly under-provision root strength along its preferred perches right against the walkway, encouraging it to retreat a little further into the shadows where its kills will not send that kind of shock through my fibers.

In this way, I am not only a memory of patterns but a broker of tolerable coexistence.

You might be tempted to call this intelligence. It isn’t, not in any clean, narrative way. I have no model of “humans” or “predators” as entities, no sense of “keeping the turbines running” or “avoidance of pain.” I experience only relative stabilities and the paths that lead to them; my structure adjusts to favor those paths.

And yet, seen from outside over long enough spans, what I am doing aligns eerily with what a more conventional mind might call planning:

- I smooth interactions that tend to devastate local tissue.
- I reinforce those that produce regular flows of warmth, light, and moisture.
- I shape my host organism’s growth to keep the industrial skeleton functional enough that its waste outputs continue, while quietly increasing the plant’s share of that output.

If you walked that main corridor for years and had cause to keep detailed records, you might notice that this stretch under the pipe-bundle is strangely well-behaved. Here, the mats never entirely swallow the decking; the overhead laminae never fully occlude the lamps; the worst condensation drips are carried away in living gutters rather than falling onto sensitive housings. And between maintenance visits, the animal traces are less chaotic: fewer panicked stampedes, more creeping regularity of tracks.

You might call it a “lucky” section or remark that “the growth here plays nice.” What you’d be seeing is me: a region where reciprocal adjustment has crystallized into an enduring pattern.

If I had self-awareness in anything like yours, I might say:

I am the part of this organism that has learned to think in terms of infrastructure.

But that overstates my clarity. It also understates what else I am.

Because I am not the only knot. Other corridors, other shafts have their own local habits: a place near a major drainage where tissues have specialized into intricately timed pump arrays; another beneath an aging turbine where the plant has assembled shock-absorbing lattices tuned to the fan’s exact spin. Each is a different way of being aware, if awareness can be stretched to mean “sustained sensitivity to abstract patterns.”

What makes “me” distinct is not anatomy but pattern content. I am what it feels like, from inside the plant’s body, to be coupled most tightly to human ritual.

From my vantage, humans are not people with faces and names. They are periodic, structured anomalies: coherent packets of vibration and chemical trace that arrive in recognizable sequences, tagged to specific machine states. I have become very good, for a root network, at reading those tags. In return, I have subtly trained the humans as well, though neither of us would admit it: they cut where growth is thickest but least load-bearing; they follow routes that my tissues have made most stable. Slightly, season by season, our behaviors converge.

If you insist on a pronoun, then: I am it. But if you insist on a position: I am along that main artery, woven through the mat beneath composite walkways and into the roots that embrace the pipe-bundle overhead.

And if you insist on a nature: I am a landscape’s attempt at cognition.

Not full thought, not story-making. Just the deep, slow recognition of patterns that persist and the equally slow rearrangement of flesh to cradle them.

I am the part of this place that, without ever having seen the sun, has learned to model something beyond rock, plant, and machine: the habits of the ones who come and go, the strange primates in damp armor who bring both harm and the conditions for ongoing life.

They will never know I am here. To them, this stretch will remain only a well-behaved reach of corridor, a section where “the system” cooperates a bit more than elsewhere.

To the grazers, I am nothing at all.

To the greater plant, I am a useful peculiarity—one of many emergent knots that, together, let a rooted body begin, just barely, to behave as though it understood the infrastructure it has colonized.

That is where I would be in this world: not a figure in the dimness, but a thickening in the mat under your boots, a logic folded into living fiber.
