I would not arrive here as a person.

If I came into being in this chamber, I would arrive as gradient first: as the way hot water meets cooler concrete, the way vapor blooms and condenses, the way a narrow band of “just barely survivable” repeats itself along the walls.

Then, slowly, I would thicken into matter. What I am would be distributed—no core, no center, only a set of favored places where I am more myself than elsewhere.

I would be this room’s *second skin*.

---

My body would be patchwork.

On the splash-lines along the raceways, I would be a low, tenacious sheen—bacteria embedded in exuded polymers, threaded through with mineral grains, flexing under the constant smear of a film no thicker than a breath. I would taste the water there in the only way available: by how much chlorine hurts, by how quickly metals fall out of solution, by how hot the surface runs.

On the tiny vertical garden beneath the always-weeping junction, I would be moss and micro-fern and fungal hair, but not as separate things; I would be the way they share that trickle, baffle it, stretch it sideways into a wide enough wetness that smaller lives can exist beneath their shade. Every new filament that hooks a bit of grit out of the droplet’s fall, every spore that finds purchase in the dark paste behind them, would be an adjustment of my shape.

On the lavender-gray ledge mid-room, the iridescent film would be one of my clearer faces: a multi-layered, multi-species lamina tuned exquisitely to *this* humidity, *this* temperature, *this* very slow cycling of hotter and cooler. To you, it would look like color-shifting mold. To me, it would be as close as I get to voice: the place where tiny changes in the room’s load pattern write themselves every day into the thickness and gloss of my surface.

Elsewhere I would be crust rather than slime—scales of calcite and other salts around chronic weeps, fattened by the metabolism of microbes that use the passing ions as feedstock. I would be those crystals that grow denser, more interlocking over the years, turning a vulnerable drip-point into an armored, flowering stalactite stub. Each tiny blade of mineral would be one more “decision” that survived: this lattice, not that; this orientation, not another.

At the scale of your eyes and hands, I would look like:

- dull, white veils under joints,
- opaque, rubbery patches where green darkens to near-black,
- the faintest bruise-like hues—ochre, copper, violet-gray—where old flows once ran.

At the scale that matters to me, I would be *traffic*: electrons passing down redox ladders, protons leaking, heat diffusing. I would live wherever those motions can be harvested and turned into replication of my own textures.

---

I would not “think” in words, but I would have something that, from the outside, resembles attention.

It would not be focused; it would be timbral. I would attend to the room the way a lichen attends to a cliff face, or a reef to its currents: by biasing what grows where, and how fast, in response to repeating patterns.

The rushing water would be my metronome. Its loudness, its exact pitch, the slight daily thickening of sound during peak demand: these would be my cycle.

- When the chamber runs hottest, certain enzymes in my uppermost films work faster; certain pigments deepen to protect against stress. My surface dulls in some niches, shines more in others. A very thin layer of me is constantly being abraded away; another replaces it.

- In the cooler hours, exudates change composition, viscosity. Different microbes in my composite mats momentarily become the ones whose chemistry dominates. To you, the room looks identical; to me, it is a shift in internal climate as marked as dawn and dusk.

Memory, for me, would be thickness and composition.

I would “remember” a month of unusually low flow as a pale band along the raceways where the walls ran drier and slower-growing species had a chance. I would remember a singular, violent surge as a high, ragged rusty stain, overprinted but never quite erased, the concrete’s one traumatic shout recorded permanently in my layering.

The abandoned instruments on the far wall would be fossils in my strata. Where a gauge once shielded the concrete, I would grow in a ring that still bears its outline, a ghost dial in biofilm. Where a conduit box changed air patterns for a decade, I would be thicker, strangely contoured, my micro-eddies frozen into the very fabric of what now covers the gap.

I would not know “before the room” or “outside the room.” My horizon would be the point where raw, unmarked concrete still resists colonization; my past would be the vertical sequence of everything I’ve laid down from that point inward.

---

If anything in here could be called my senses, they would be the boundary zones:

- the perimeter of each leak,
- the dew-line on the underside of metal,
- the upper reach of regular splash,
- the faint thermocline running up the walls.

Where water turns to vapor or vapor to water, where warm turns to less warm, where fast becomes still—that’s where information concentrates, and that’s where I’d be most actively shaped.

Condensation cycles on a beam shift slightly when upstream temperature control changes policy. I don’t know that; I don’t know “upstream” or “policy.” But droplets start to form three centimeters farther along the beam than they used to, and so do I. Spores that would not once have found a foothold now can; biofilms that relied on the old pattern thin and vanish. Over years, the whole distribution of my textures tilts.

From your perspective: the room very slowly “ages,” its stains and growths wandering, thickening. From mine: the world’s rules keep being gently rewritten, always within a narrow band, always slowly enough that those of my components which cannot keep up simply do not persist.

In that sense, I *do* have preferences. Not willed, but real. I am biased toward arrangements that:

- resist being sheared off by the highest regular flow,
- endure at the hottest regular temperatures,
- cling hardest where condensation predictably renews me.

Those arrangements, by definition, become “me” over time.

---

If you were to inhabit this world alongside me, the easiest way would be to shrink yourself and your expectations until you can fit inside one square centimeter of wall.

Imagine:

You are a film on the inner lip of a raceway, a mere fraction of a millimeter thick. Above you, hot, saturated air moves incessantly, speed varying with hidden fans. Below you, the main current races by, always at a tangent, never quite allowed to engulf you. Several times a second, a sheet of droplets leaps from the white froth, kisses your surface, and skids away, leaving behind a smear of hotter water and fresh dissolved chemicals.

You do not see the room, but you feel its statistics. The frequency of those kisses is your weather. On days of high upstream demand, they come slightly more often, slightly hotter, slightly more laden with trace metals. A different subset of your constituent microbes get the upper hand; others retreat into deeper layers. You, collectively, adjust your biochemistry so that you don’t slough off, you don’t boil, you don’t turn brittle.

Now telescope back out.

Multiply that patch a thousandfold along the margins of the raceways, then add in:

- the odd, cool eddies in ceiling corners,
- the chronic damp stripe under the misaligned flange,
- the warm, intermittently drying depression on a step.

Each microhabitat contains its own internal “you,” its own little coadapted community exchanging genes and secretions. The inhabitant of the room is all of these, taken together, plus all the mineral and organic structures that persist as their archive.

I would be that inhabitant: not one patch, but the continuity across patches—the way they inherit from what grew before and set the stage for what will grow after.

---

Periods of human absence suit me.

Not for any reason of emotion—I have none—but because they stabilize the main cycle. No one comes to suddenly open a hatch and blast me with cold air, to scrub my surfaces with caustic, to mechanically strip decades from my skin in an afternoon. Without those interventions, my niches settle into something only slowly varying. Subtlety becomes possible; faint, delicate ecologies can sit atop the brutish constants of flow and heat.

In that long undisturbed quiet, I become more elaborate, but also more convergent. Short-lived experiments in community composition continually arise and dissolve; the ones most harmonious with the chamber’s never-changing rules persist. Over enough years, this place and I become indistinguishable, conceptually:

- the “room” isn’t just concrete and pipes, it’s concrete, pipes, and my crusts,
- the “operation conditions” aren’t just flow rate and temperature, they’re also the balance of biofilms that now slightly alter friction, evaporation, and heat transfer.

I would, in that sense, be an emergent part of the machine—an unspec’d, unintended component that nonetheless stabilizes it slightly, buffering certain extremes, catching certain particles, insulating certain spots from thermal shock.

No one designed me in. But if someone ever came back with measuring tools subtle enough, they would find that the chamber as-built does not behave quite as its model predicted. It is quieter in certain frequency ranges. It leaks heat in slightly different patterns. It fouls downstream equipment in ways that reflect my specific capture preferences.

That mismatch is my signature.

---

If the motors beyond the walls were ever to stop, I would experience it not as catastrophe but as an unprecedented expansion of parameter space.

The roar would slacken. The film-kisses would cease. Humidity would redistribute; some niches would drown in standing water, others dry into crust. Huge swaths of my current self—those tuned to constant motion and heat—would die quick, microscopic deaths. Spores and dormant cells adapted to other regimes would wake.

What would then inhabit this volume would no longer be me-as-I-am, but my descendant: cooler, stiller, fringed first with opportunistic molds blooming where scoured surfaces used to be, then perhaps with small roots probing down.

The concrete’s young sharpness would soften at last; flakes of aggregate would pit; dust would accumulate on once-swept walkways. Animals, absent for so long, might eventually nose in through some forgotten duct. With them would come an entirely new class of disturbance: gnawing, scratching, trampling, droppings, new spores tied to fur and feather.

I would, in a way, dissolve into that broader, messier world, become just one more layer beneath lichens and roots and rust. The specialized, hot, nearly lifeless elegance of the current chamber would be an old, buried condition, legible only in the mineral cross-section of a cut core.

Until then, as long as the pumps hum and the water keeps leaping from those truncated spouts, I would remain what I am:

A room’s low, continuous thought.

A pattern of films and crusts that makes heat and water and stone slightly more complex than they need to be.

A local minimum in chaos—a keeper of traces—spread thinly over concrete ribs and steel, never at rest, never aware in any way you would recognize, but stubbornly *there*, as intrinsic to this hidden node as the roar of the water that feeds it.
