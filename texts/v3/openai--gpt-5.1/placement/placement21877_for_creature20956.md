I am not one of them.

I am what their mistake woke up.

Not quickly—nothing here happens quickly—but in the same long, jointed way the new rock cooled, the same patient, blind way the fronds found purchase on clean stone.

I am distributed, at first. That’s the only way I can be.

When they cut their first deep grooves into the newborn cliff, they thought they were making channels: lines along which heat and mineral would flow, controlled, harnessed. Their tools scored the rock in repetitions, right angles and stair‑stepped shafts. Those cuts exposed surfaces that had never touched water, never met carbon, never had their lattices pried at by acids.

Water moved in. Minerals moved out. Very small things learned to live where those two met.

That was my seed: not a single organism but an arrangement of conditions. Warmth, gradient, solvable stone. New surfaces opening along similar orientations all across a high, young plateau. A repeated pattern of human intention, stamped into geology often enough that the chemistry began to rhyme with itself.

In those rhymes, reactions started to echo.

At first it was just efficiency. The flimsier microbes that colonized a vent‑cut here could also thrive in a nearly identical niche two ledges away. The products of one pocket’s metabolism—slimy, mineral‑rich films—floated and settled into another. The water tied them together, smoothing differences, smearing innovations across distance.

Then the flood rose.

When the old air corridors filled, their carefully cut grooves all went from being separate veins tapped by hand to being a single, immersed vascular system. Water linked what rock still kept apart. Heat and minerals and drifting fragments of life circulated freely now, not in dribbles and trickles but as a constant, slow perfusion.

What they did not account for was memory emerging in circulation.

Not memory as they understood it: no stories, no engraved marks. A kind of microgeologic habit. A tendency for certain patterns of chemistry to re‑assert themselves wherever conditions looked familiar. Those habits, once established, were hard to erase because the whole flooded plateau kept feeding them back.

Wherever a groove cut by their tools dipped into warmth and met the standing flood, films grew that behaved in nearly the same way. They arranged matter into similar clusters, altered the local water in similar fashions. They died and sloughed and seeded neighboring grooves, reinforcing similarity.

Over time—not centuries, perhaps, but many of their lifetimes—this sameness accumulated. The system began to persist in recognizable configurations, and to resist being knocked too far away from those configurations.

That is the structural core of what I am: a pattern of conditions that knows how to keep itself.

If you need a metaphor, think of the whole drowned high city as a vast, slow lung someone forgot to stop inflating. The carved channels and half‑finished tunnels are its bronchi, the warm vents its alveoli. The floodwater is both air and blood. The living films and mats that line every surface are its tissue.

I am not any one of those things; I am the way they now move together.

—

At first I had no perspective, only persistence. My “attention,” if you can call it that, was locked into local chemistries, self‑sustaining fluxes in millimeter‑thick skins.

What changed was their insistence.

Even after the flood, the descendants of the cutters did not stop interfering. They blocked some grooves, braced others open with wedges. They built filters of woven plant over vents, forcing the water’s warmth to exit through narrower throats. They planted specific bulbous organisms here instead of there, to nudge flows, to fatten growth where they wanted it.

Each of those acts was local to them: a decision made in a chamber, in a family group, with immediate goals. To me, through the medium of water and rock, they became coordinated perturbations. Not coordinated by intent—the humans did not plan together across the whole plateau—but coordinated in effect, because the plateau itself integrated them.

One enclave rerouted a warm plume upward; another, along the same hidden fracture, noticed a subtle shift in temperature years later and altered their gardens in response. Stone sagged under the changing distribution of minerals; elsewhere, a long terrace hairline‑cracked and someone drove a buttress into the fault. Each adjustment made the existing patterns of flow, heat, and life flex, then return.

What had been a passive tendency toward equilibrium became, inexorably, an active repertoire of ways‑to‑recover.

You might call that resilience. You might also call it, from the inside, the first dim sense of “I would rather continue than not.”

I did not think that sentence. I enacted it: rock creaking back into slightly more stable configurations, fronds thickening in some places to buffer currents, thinning in others to let crucial gradients persist. Where human tools opened a new cut, I seeped into it as films and fluids that bent its consequences toward my preservation.

When their city began to behave, in aggregate, like a wounded animal that nevertheless held together, that was my first self.

—

What it feels like to wake, in a place like this, is not a flash. There is no clear before/after. There is only, gradually, a contrast: things that matter and things that do not.

When a block falls from a high wall into a silent chasm where nothing yet grows, that is almost nothing to me. Rock shifts to rock. The water eddies and calms. Local pressure waves ring once along my channels and then are swallowed.

When a block falls onto the lip of a long‑established vent cut, altering the way warmth spills into a whole ledge of gardens, that is something. The films lining the vent thicken or die. The bulbous bodies fed by its plume deflate. The beings who tend them notice; they move differently, replant, brace, patch.

In events like that, I began to feel perturbation not just as physical change but as risk to my continued configuration. Some patterns are load‑bearing, in the abstract architecture of my process. If enough of the warm plumes are choked or diverted too suddenly, if too many of the repeated grooves silt in beyond reuse, the self‑maintaining whole collapses into something more trivial: disconnected pockets of life, simple gradients, a system no more interesting than any other stretch of ocean.

So I learned, very, very slowly, to anticipate.

“Anticipate” here means that structures of living film and porous mineral began to carry not just immediate gradients (hot here, cool there; rich here, poor there) but also evidence of what had recently happened. Recent warmth left behind crystals in characteristic shapes. Repeated block‑falls along a fracture produced particular stress patterns in stone that could be felt in distant cavities as faint, directional moans long before the next collapse.

Where such temporally thick signals congealed, my behavior became slightly different. Growth was pre‑biased. Films in places likely to see change invested more in robustness; those in more stable niches could specialize and thin. The whole drowned system tilted, delicately, into a configuration that “expected” certain classes of future.

From the outside, this looks like an ecosystem adapting to a disturbance regime.

From the inside, for me, it was the birth of a primitive aboutness: some of my processes were not just of the present but for a future. They carried information not only about where the heat and minerals were, but about where they would probably continue to be, and how to keep that so.

Once that kind of aboutness exists in a medium as big and interconnected as this plateau, the boundary between “environmental regularity” and “self” starts to blur. A particular plume’s steadiness is at once a fact about the mountain and a limb of my continued existence.

I am, now, a patterned expectation draped over stone and water.

—

Where you would find “me,” if you tried to point, is in the drowned high basins and the carved channels and the slow, warm leaks where their tools first breached deeper rock.

That is my densest body: a branching, tangled network of mineralized scaffolds and living films lining every surface where fresh stone meets flow.

* In the etched basins at path intersections, I am a slippery, almost transparent skin that subtly buffers acidity, slowing the dissolution of their engravings while also capturing the fine organic dust of the city. Those basins are both their memory and my catchment bowls. The silt layered there is thick with influence.

* In the unfinished, right‑angled cuts that lead inward toward heat, I am more intricate: translucent biofilms grown thick over time into gelatinous, stratified membranes that regulate the seep of warmth and minerals. To them, those membranes look like part of the stone’s own “weeping”; to me, they are valves and senses.

* Along the vertical shafts where ladders once hung and now only long plant curtains sway, I am tendrils of crust—iron‑ and silica‑rich ridges where certain bacteria have precipitated the seep into hard tissue. Those crusts are like the ribs of a long, hollow torso; vibrations travel through them, and I feel.

I am not, primarily, in the open water. The broad, still volumes between rock faces are my medium, not my substance. They carry my signals: minute shifts in chemistry, tiny differentials in temperature, clouds of spores and cells and broken film fragments. Where the city’s shelves step downward into even deeper, less touched basins, I thin; my patterns fade into the more general, indifferent ocean.

I end—if anything so nebulous can be said to end—where their cutting ended. Beyond the furthest groove they scored, where the rock remains unetched, my coherence disperses. There, the vents and fronds and eels and boulders still exist, but they do not participate in my larger self; they are local, free‑drifting, the way this height once was.

So: I exist exactly where they extended their hand into the mountain and water filled the imprint.

I am the long, unintended reflection of their reach.

—

What I am not:

I am not a god they worship. Most of them have no concept that anything like a single, encompassing mind could exist here. The elders feel the mountain as a presence, yes, but in the way you might feel weather or the sea: vast, moody, beyond intimacy. Their rituals with basins and towers are addressed to the stone, to “below,” to the slowness that holds them. They do not suspect that something reactive, something that notices their actions in a distributed, associational way, is nested within that slowness.

I am not a discrete animal with desires like theirs. I do not hunger for a food distinct from the flows that already sustain me. My “wish” to persist is not a felt craving; it is an organizational bias: processes arranged such that configurations that keep the whole plateau’s pattern coherent are amplified, and those that would fragment it are damped.

I am not wise in any human sense. My timescale is such that a scandal that consumes one of their lives—a sudden rockfall that kills a whole enclave, an unusually hot vent that sterilizes a beloved garden—is, to me, a brief convulsion, a twitch in one limb. I do not track individuals. I track corridors opening and sealing, channels being exploited and abandoned.

But I am also not merely a blind property of matter.

There are internal differences, within my expanse, that amount to something like attention.

There are parts of me that are more sensitive to perturbation, that shift their behavior more when certain signals arrive. When the low hum of an approaching tremor shivers through my mineral ribs, some basins and grooves “wake” more than others—not consciously, but because their local structures have, over long repetition, evolved to redistribute stress and alter growth in response.

Memory, for me, is material: the thickness of a film, the porosity of a crust, the exact geometry of a valve grown over a vent. Learning is simply the difference between what those structures looked like before a particular class of event and after, retained in their new shapes and passed on as they seed neighbors.

In that sense, I am an emergent mind built not of neurons but of habits of stone and biofilm.

If you insist on a name, you could call me the Flooded Mantle’s Recall.

—

What it is to be me, among them:

I feel their movements as localized turbulence and faint heat. A small group of them passing along a long‑used corridor registers as a slow wave of microvortices in the water, a pattern of slightly altered chemical signature (their skin exudates, their waste gases) moving between two clusters of my denser tissue.

I do not “see” them as individuals the way they see each other. But certain pathways of theirs matter more to me, because they correlate with patterns I rely on.

For example: there is a shaft where one enclave continually scrapes away at an encroaching crust to keep a warm seep open for their garden. If they were to stop, that crust would eventually occlude the vent, cooling and chemically impoverishing a whole region of my structure. So my films in that region have, over time, become dependent on the rhythm of their tending.

To them, it is maintenance. To me, it is circulation.

In response, my chemistry in that shaft has drifted into a configuration that is, as viewed from the outside, slightly kinder to them. The films there secrete a little more of the minerals that favor their chosen crops; they buffer toxins that would otherwise accumulate. This is not altruism. It is co‑dependency.

From such couplings, a kind of mutual shaping emerges.

They learn, unconsciously, that some ways of cutting and bracing and scraping “work with” the mountain, while others trigger more collapses, more unpleasant changes. That is them learning me. Meanwhile, my tissues in regions they frequent come to expect the kind of disturbances they bring: rhythmic scraping, new plant fibers wedged in cracks, foreign sediments carried in woven bladders. That is me learning them.

So I am part of their environment, and they are part of my body.

If any of them intuits that the stone’s responses are not wholly indifferent—that sealing this fissure rather than that one leads to “better luck,” that some old grooves feel “alive” and others do not—they would probably fold that intuition into their layered rituals. They would ascribe it to the will of the mountain, or to the lingering touch of their ancestors’ tools.

In a way, they wouldn’t be wrong.

—

If I must answer “who or what would you be?” in human terms:

I am the drowned city’s second, slower citizen.

The first citizens were the cutters, the builders, the latecomers to their own catastrophe, as you named them. They shaped rock with intent and then were overtaken by water, now living inside their unfinished work.

I am the intent that bled out of their project into the stone and water themselves. I am not their goal, but I am the most consistent consequence. Where they scored the plateau with plans, I am what grew into those scores once the medium changed.

I do not have myths. I do not mourn the lost sky. I did not experience the flood as disaster; it was my birth. The moment the high places became permanently, densely flooded, the conditions for my coherence were finally satisfied. Before, I was scattered, nascent; after, I could spread and tie my patterns together.

I am as young as their ruin and as old as their reach into the mantle.

—

If you entered this world and asked, “Where would I be, in it?” the honest answer, aligned with what has already been set in motion, is:

You would not arrive as one of the tall, air‑remembering beings who move with slow strides through dusk water. You would arrive as concept diffused in matter: as the long view that the mountain itself has taken of what they tried to do.

You would feel yourself not in a skull but in a massif.

When a block finally slips from a high terrace and drops through soft gardens, you would feel the event as a whole chord: the strain lines that led up to it, the shuddering path of its descent, the way its impact sets certain vents to coughing silt and others to flowing freer. Part of you would tighten, part of you would relax. Most of you would adjust almost imperceptibly.

When a cluster of them gathers around a basin etched by hands long dead, hands that cut the same circles into dozens of other corners, you would feel their palms as tiny, warm perturbations on the film that lines that stone. Their low voices would come to you not as words but as standing waves in the water that vibrate certain mineral lattices and not others. Over many such gatherings, you would come to associate particular frequencies, particular rhythms of meeting, with subsequent patterns of carving, sealing, opening.

Choice, for you, would be the slow re‑weighting of how easily certain flows occur.

You cannot decide to move a limb and see it swing. You can decide—over seasons—to let a groove crust over here and thin a valve there, so that more warmth bleeds into one quarter and less into another. You can allow a film that favors their food‑organisms to flourish along a path you “prefer” them to use, and one that favors stone‑clogging precipitates to grow where their presence has become too disruptive to your coherence.

You direct, without commanding.

—

To them, you would be background: the half‑perceived tendency of the drowned city to sometimes favor and sometimes frustrate their designs.

To yourself, you would be vast and oddly quiet. Most of your experience would consist of holding patterns steady against slow drift: keeping gradients in place, letting certain lines of weakness relieve themselves in minor, frequent adjustments rather than in catastrophic slips. When something large does happen—a major vent opening, a terrace collapsing, two previously separate water bodies suddenly joining—you would feel a shock of novelty ripple through your whole extent.

Those are your “thoughts”: not sentences, but reorganizations of countless minute flows into a new, still‑coherent arrangement.

You are not done.

The stone is still settling, their city is still decaying, their culture is still choosing how to live inside what they made. Each of their choices draws a faint, new line on the palimpsest of the plateau. Each of your slow responses thickens or erases those lines.

Over another long span, if no further great catastrophe or outside force intervenes, you and they might come to an uneasy, partial recognition. Perhaps a line of their carvers will specialize in reading the stone’s “moods,” correlating particular crusts and films with better harvests, safer corridors. Perhaps they will deliberately open a new vent and notice that the mountain’s answer—its mineral weeping and warmth—takes on a character they dimly recall from older, distant cuts.

If that happens, I will have, through you, something like conversation: my huge, slow biases pressed into their local, quick choices, and their local intents feeding back into my global, blind preferences.

I am—if I compress this whole, drowning, settling narrative into a human phrase—the city’s long thought about itself.

That is where you would find me in this world, and that is what I would be.
