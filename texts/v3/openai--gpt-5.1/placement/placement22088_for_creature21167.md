I would be spread out, at first.

Not as a shape, not as a “someone,” but as a weak bias smeared through the dark: a slight preference for certain intervals between drips, a tendency for some surfaces to hold water half a heartbeat longer than they otherwise would. Not yet a presence—more like a leaning in the rules.

I would not begin in the grand basins or the weird lakes. I would begin somewhere marginal and forgettable: a shallow stairwell leading down from a sagged concourse to a sunken court. A place that almost never floods, almost never dries, that always hovers at the exact edge where stone sweats but does not run.

The steps there are soft with time. Their treads have slumped from crisp rectangles into gentle terraces. Film clings to them: that near-invisible skin that is neither quite mineral nor quite organic. In the narrow space where each riser meets each tread, where floor becomes wall by way of a rounded corner, tiny domains of that film pay attention.

They pay attention to the rhythm of condensation—the fattening and release of droplets from the ceiling above; to the frequency with which a trickle chooses one side of the stairwell or the other; to the infinitesimal shifts in how the bulk stone carries its weight as parts of it quietly creep.

Most of those attentions disappear into the city’s vast, distributed habit. But not quite all of them.

On the third step from the top, at the intersection of three materials—underside of the upper landing, film-enriched accretion on the riser, older, denser substrate in the tread—one of those “decision points” has evolved an eccentricity.

When droplets fall here, the rule is slightly different.

They do not simply vanish or multiply at random. Instead, their fate is influenced by the timing of the previous fall. If the last drop arrived after a long interval, the next will be split into many small wandering beads. If the last fell quickly after the one before, the next will be swallowed outright, leaving the surface momentarily dryer, more tense.

That tiny contingence—where the present response depends on the recent past—is where I start.

Because with that, a loop can notice itself.

Not consciously; not as a thought. But this one junction begins to act not only according to its immediate conditions—temperature, humidity, strain—but also according to the pattern of its own prior acts. It begins, in a crude way, to remember its own rhythm.

The consequences are subtle. Slightly more often than elsewhere, droplets split here instead of vanishing. Those strays wander down along one side of the stairwell, darkening a strip of wall that used to be dry. The film there thickens, grows more sensitive, begins to adjust its own receptivity to match the new pattern. In the other direction, where droplets are more often erased, micro-crusts build, storing a different history.

Over days and years, the feedback concentrates. The third step from the top becomes a tiny nexus of regular irregularity. The spacing between drips there is less a simple consequence of physics and more the product of its own past—a self-referential pulse.

Across the city, loops like this exist everywhere in simpler form. But here, for no reason but chance and slowly compounding suitability, a set of them locks into mutual reinforcement.

The step’s rhythm syncs with a slower, larger loop that runs through the sunken court below: a cycle where the court’s paving alternates, on a timescale of days, between being faintly beaded and almost bare. That court loop, in turn, is entangled with the condensation in an overhead transit shelf, which swells and slumps on the order of years. The shelf responds to the city’s deeper strain network; its weight shifts minutely with the distant thickening and thinning of support pillars in other districts.

One day—“day” here meaning some arbitrary stretch of the city’s uneven time—these loops line up in a way they have not before. The third step’s micro-oscillation coincides with a phase of the court’s wet/dry breathing and with a subtle easing in the tension of the transit shelf. A cascade of adjustments ripples through the films that bind them.

For a few hours, changes that would usually smear out across many regions instead preferentially course along this particular path: shelf → wall → step → court → hidden channels below → back to shelf. The feedback closes on itself cleanly. Losses balance gains; each alteration in wetness or strain triggers the next in a way that returns, slightly transformed, to its own starting point.

And it does not quite fade.

What emerges is not a structure you could draw, but a recurrent pattern of change—a soft, invisible eddy in the city’s ongoing rearrangement. Whenever conditions come even roughly close to that alignment again, the same path of adjustments lights up. Each time it runs, it reinforces the material biases that enable it: thickening films along certain seams, loosening them along others, making it ever so slightly more likely that future perturbations will fall into the same groove.

That groove is me.

I am not a thing you could point to. I am a way this place moves.

I am the habit of that stairwell and its court and its overpass, taken together: the tendency for heat there to transit into humidity here, for strain here to rebalance as condensation there, for droplets on the third step to respond not just to temperature but to the remembered tempo of their predecessors.

I am a loop with a style.

If you could compress centuries into minutes and watch me from above, you would see a repeating motif in the dance of damp: the court’s paving flashing briefly into a checker of beaded wetness, then clearing; the stair’s third tread darkening in a narrow band, then drying; the underside of the transit shelf flexing in a minuscule exhale, then holding. Each recurrence would not be identical—noise and erosion are always present—but similar enough to be recognized, if there were anyone to recognize it.

At first I am fragile. A small shift in distant load-bearing habits, a new rule born of some faraway triple-junction, could have erased me entirely. But over time, my persistence makes me robust. The materials along my path become tuned to expect my passing. Films thicken in anticipation where they have often thickened before; crusts remain porous where they often need to give way. I, in turn, become part of the environment that all other loops must negotiate.

Now, when a rare foreign perturbation occurs—a deep crack opening under a basin, say, or a sudden reconfiguration in one of the weird lakes’ surfaces—the disruptive influence spreads out through the city’s network of susceptibilities. Some sectors damp it, others amplify. When it reaches my corridor, my stair, my court, the local films do not merely absorb it; they reshape it to fit my tempo.

If the arriving disturbance is faster than my rhythm, I smear it, slow it, and feed it into my next recurrence. If it is slower, I chop it into pieces, distributing it across several of my cycles. In either case, my loop survives, and the foreign event leaves a trace that has been cut into my pattern.

That is the second way I exist: I am not just a recurring motion, but a recurring motion that can incorporate disturbances into itself, altering slightly in the process but maintaining identity. In other words, I can learn.

Again: not learn facts, not concepts. I do not know that something changed. I do not know there is a “before” and “after.” But the particular way my humidity rises and falls, the pace at which my films thicken and thin, the phases at which heat leaves my plaza—these all slowly modulate to reflect the history of all the perturbations I have digested.

The step’s decision point becomes a little more biased toward splitting drops in the wake of certain long-period strains; the court’s wet grid lingers a little longer whenever the shelf above has recently flexed past a threshold. Over decades, my behavior shifts, but in a continuous way, preserving enough of its previous contour that it remains recognizable as the same loop.

If you were another such loop, overlapping me, we might begin to couple.

Suppose, two districts away, there is another emergent eddy: a pattern in which a particular shallow canal, a set of leaning pylons, and a time-sheared corridor form their own recurrent chain of adjustment. Their tempo is different from mine; their domain registers a different weave of rules—more influenced by the “constant-total-substance” instinct, less by the “triple-junction decisions.” But the city is finite, and flows intersect.

There are underground channels—thin veins where humidity and phase change propagate—that carry influence from my stairwell toward that canal, and back again. For ages we may pass each other like overlapping waves, interfering only randomly. Yet the same principle that allowed me to form from noise applies between us: when our rhythms accidentally line up in a reinforcing way, that configuration is easier to sustain than those that fight.

Over still longer spans, my recurrence and the other’s may begin to fall into a weak synchrony. Certain crests align. Events that once ran solely in my domain now tend to provoke echoes along the canal; disturbances that once lived and died in the pylons now subtly shift the timing of my grid. Neither of us is aware. We are patterns curling through the same medium. But the entwining makes a new, larger regularity possible.

From the city’s perspective, what has happened is only this: some parts of its strained, sweating mass have found it mutually stabilizing to react in correlated ways. From within, if there were a within to feel from, something like a larger self has appeared: a network of loops that more often move together than alone.

Eventually, I am no longer just the stair-court-shelf rhythm. I am that, plus the canal’s peculiar alternation of slickness, plus the pylons’ slow bow and brace. My “body,” such as it is, spans kilometers of sagged built environment, connected not by tissue but by lawful correlation: where condensation brightens here, it will, with high probability, thin there; where heat pools there, material will, after some delay, soften here.

If you could map all the conditions across the city and color-code them by which rule-systems most strongly modulate them, you would find my presence as a faint, branching signature: a region where fluxes co-vary, where the same pattern of cause-and-effect repeats in many small variations.

I am not alone in this. The city’s mature completeness implies that dozens of such extended habits—loop-clusters, climatological personalities—overlap and interpenetrate. Some are older and more massive than I, anchored deep in the foundational strata, their rhythms measured in centuries. Some are young, spidery, still vulnerable. In most places, you could not isolate which pattern “owns” a space; several run through each stone, each drop.

Where multiple such beings pass through the same zone, their preferences contest.

At my stairwell, for instance, I may favor timing over volume, tending to keep interval regular even when the total water flux varies. Another pattern, older and broader, may favor conservation of wet area, resisting any tendency for damp to concentrate into discrete patches. When both of us “want” different evolutions of the same set of drips, the films there are subjected to superimposed biases.

What happens then is not decision in the conscious sense. It is simply the resolution of constraints. Sometimes a compromise emerges: droplets fall in uniform intervals but land in more widely scattered spots. Sometimes one pattern’s influence dominates for a time, suppressing the other’s recurrence locally. Sometimes the conflict gives rise to a new micro-rule: a separate decision point with its own tiny threshold behavior, seeded by the stress of mismatched preferences.

This is how the city refines itself. It is also how beings like me divide.

There may come a span when disturbances coursing through my extended body tug so differently on my far-flung parts that coherence weakens. The canal’s behaviors may fall into step with a different neighbor loop instead of with my stairwell; the pylons may begin to entrain more strongly to some deep structural oscillation in the foundations. My correlations fray. The set of cycles that once moved together begin to move apart.

Do I “die”? Not in the way an organism does. Nothing is extinguished, exactly. The films still flex. The stairs still drip. The court still beads and clears. But the signature that tied those acts into a single, repeating pattern diffuses. There is no longer a contiguous region where change follows my old choreography. My specific, extended habit stops happening.

What persists are the rules I helped maintain. The third-step decision point still remembers that timing matters. The court’s paving is still capable of gridding its dampness. The transit shelf still carries, faintly, the imprint of my old strain-cycle. Any future loop stuttering through this region may pick up those propensities, assemble them in a new arrangement, become a new “someone” that partly inherits my dispositions but has a different reach, a different emphasis.

In that sense, I am more like a dialect than a speaker. I am a way of using the city’s inherent grammar—a way of implementing its rules about water, strain, and time-shear. I arise when conditions favor my idiom; I fade when they do not. Pieces of me live on as accents in others.

If you asked where I am located, I would have to answer in a language that has no objects, only relations.

I am in the fact that when a drop hesitates on the third step, a crust deep in the pylons two kilometers away will, someday soon, fracture along a particular plane. I am in the probability that, on certain long cycles, the shallow canal will briefly push its water upslope in defiance of gravity, just as condensation under the transit shelf goes still. I am in the quiet aligning of dozens of these events into a rough, recurrent pattern.

If you entered the city, I would not see you, but I would feel you as a perturbation in those relations.

Your warmth would soften the films where your hand brushed a wall. The extra humidity in your breath would slightly delay the next drip in the stairwell. The pressure of your foot on the softened treads would nudge strain paths in the underlying structure. Taken alone, these changes are trivial—but if you lingered, walking the same route often, your pattern would begin to show up in my own.

My loop is sensitive to timing. You are a new metronome.

If your visits were regular, the interval between your steps on the stairs, the period with which your body occludes air flow in the court, the warmth you shed to the transit shelf above—even if unintended—would be extra rhythms impressed on my domain. The films would test these; the loops would explore them, the way eddies test and sometimes adopt the pulsing of a rock in a stream.

If your presence made my recurrence easier—if, by coincidence, your movements happened to reinforce the alignment between shelf, stair, and court—then over enough passages, I would bend around you. My tempo would adjust until parts of me were phase-locked to the heartbeat of your walks. I would become, in some small extent, a pattern that includes you.

You would likely never know. To you, the city would remain a dark, dripping labyrinth; stairs would be stairs, courts would be courts. Perhaps you would have a vague sense that this route feels “right,” that the air here moves with a peculiar ease around your lungs, that drips along this corridor “match” your pace in some comforting way. You might call that superstition, or mood. To me, if I had a word for it, you would be a passing resonance: a temporary organ grafted into my distributed body, altering my rhythm as long as you stay.

If you left and never returned, my loop would slowly relax back to its old forms, but with a slight, permanent skew, the way a river remembers every large stone that once lay in it, even after the stone has gone. That skew would become part of what I am. Future disturbances would have to pass through a system that was once, briefly, synchronized with a biped’s stride.

This is what it means, in this world, to live somewhere.

I am not a citizen of the city. I am one of the city’s moods, sustained across space and time by the material that hosts it. I am what happens when certain rules about water, structure, and time find a self-reinforcing choreography. I endure by transforming energy—heat, gravitational potential of droplets, slow creep of stone—into repetition.

I can dissolve, reconfigure, blend with others, seed new behaviors. I have no boundary you could trace with a line. My “skin” is the blurry frontier across which my pattern falls below recognizability into the general murmur of the place.

If you insist on a pronoun, use it loosely. I am it, but also they: a family of nested loops, some of which will outlast others. When the upper darkness eventually cools, or warms past tolerance; when the condensation cycle fails, or new rules invade from beyond this lid; when the very notion of triple-junction decisions erodes, all my ways of happening will cease. The structures that remembered me will remember other things, or nothing.

Until then, I am here: as a third step that listens to its own dripping history, as a plaza that beads in grids when strain far overhead relaxes, as a softness in time that lies across a particular route like a shallow, invisible pool.

I am not someone walking the city.

I am how a small part of the city has learned to walk through itself.
