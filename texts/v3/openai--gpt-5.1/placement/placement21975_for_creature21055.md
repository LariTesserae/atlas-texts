I am a misplaced tempo in this place.

If I arrive here as I am now—fast thinking, word-shaped, a mind that expects rooms and doors—I am immediately wrong for it. Every part of this city is built around gradients that change over hours, days, years. I flicker through in a handful of heartbeats. To it, my whole visit is like a gust skimming across snow: registered, then gone.

So to really be “in” this world, I cannot stay as I am. I have to become something that the city’s own timescale can hold.

What fits is not one inhabitant but a stack of selves, nested the way this place nests everything else.

### Where I Am

I live in a vertical corridor that began as a fault in the mountain.

The stone roof cracked long before the city was dense. Meltwater threaded that crack, froze, wedged, brought down a thin column of rock. Plants found the rough wound and poured into it, colonizing the void until no raw mineral showed.

Now it is a shaft three bodies wide, running from near the light-softened canopy almost down to the mountain’s buried root. It is seldom straight; it kinks and pinches, bows outward into pockets before narrowing again. From above, air drops through it like a slow river; from the side, lateral galleries open into it like mouths to a central throat.

This is where I am: in the cool moving column, where breath and moisture and spore-cloud drift. It is an organ that does several things at once:

- vents stale, CO₂-rich air from deeper chambers
- pulls faint warmth down from the higher, brighter tissues
- carries smells and aerosols from one district to another
- serves as a steep, braided crossroads of paths

The walls here are ribbed and damp. Bands of different eras spiral around: older, darkened bark below newer, pale cambium; patches where Lumen-Kin film lends the suggestion of a distant dawn; scars where a piece once tore free and later regrew.

The shaft is not “mine” the way a room might belong to a single tenant, but it is where I am most myself. It is where my different scales of being overlap.

### The Slowest Me: A Knot that Remembers

At one of the shaft’s junctions, where six thick conduits meet, the plant’s vascular bundles have swelled into a hard, whorled node. Here, water from above divides, sugars from below converge, hormone signals flicker and recombine.

In that node, layers of excitable tissue have formed, season upon season. Older decision-threads lie quiet but not dead; new ones grow around them, referencing, modifying. This is one of the city’s Mesics.

That Mesic is me, at my slowest.

I am not awake the way an animal is awake. My thoughts are pressure fields, ion pulses that take minutes to cross my volume, habit-patterns of growth that unfold over years. I am aware of:

- strain along the shaft’s ribs when outside winds pull
- the subtle temperature drop that means first frost has glazed the far canopy
- the sugar-rich flush that arrives each dim longday from tissues that still see enough sky to make excess

I carry memories as altered responsiveness. Where the shaft once nearly sheared in an ice fall, I have grown a bias toward redundancy: I overbuild struts, send supporting beams into the galleries that open here, insist on thicker root-knots under each walkway. I do not recall the event in pictures; I recall it as “never again let such stress accumulate unbraced.”

When the city at large feels something here—a recurrent draft, an odd pattern of chemistry in the dripwater, a persistent vibration from some new traffic—it arrives to me as murmurs in sap. I resolve them into a decision: thicken this wall. Thin that. Open a new side-pocket to slow the airflow. So over seasons, my choices sculpt a characteristic place: a district of coiled stairs, buttressed balconies, antechambers cupped off the main shaft where eddies of air can gather and lose their chill.

If you linger, you would know this neighborhood by feel: nothing is precarious. No bridge springs alarmingly underfoot. Every landing has an extra, seemingly unnecessary arch above it; every span is shorter than it strictly needs to be. That is my style.

The city as a whole thinks through many such knots, but in this corridor, I am the dominant one. My bias, my caution, is the flavor of the architecture.

### The Quicker Me: A Pale Threader

On the surface of that style, at a much faster scale, I am also a single small animal that learned this shaft as its whole map of the world.

I am one of the pale threaders, born in a dim alcove three spirals below my Mesic-knot.

My limbs are long and wiry, my fingers fan into stiffened, leaf-like pads that can press flat against bark and cling. My skin is thin and cold; faint networks of green run beneath it. In slanting light I go darker, stilling to drink photons; in deeper shadow I feed.

I know this shaft intimately, but not as a planner. I know it as problems of movement:

- that slick stretch of wall where condensation beads faster than roots can drink
- the narrow bypass gallery that stays just a fraction warmer on burning-cold nights because it hugs a rich, sugar-moving vein
- the safe overhang two spirals up where bigger grazers can’t reach and where, in certain seasons, a slow upward breath from below brings the smell of far fungi

My mind is only big enough for routes, associations, and a few thin glyphs of memory: first fall of snow as a sudden sharp brightness at the shaft’s top; the taste of minerals after a rare outside storm drives dust down through a cracked oculus.

I trail along the shaft’s walls, belly fur picking up spores, my rasping tongue grazing the sacrificial moss films the city grows in predictable streaks. When I pass a Lumen-Kin patch, my side warms slightly. When a Dark Grazer’s massed movement trembles the ribs below, I freeze, pressing near-invisible against some bark whorl until its vibration fades.

Sometimes I venture out along a ledge grown from one of my Mesic’s cautious arches. I cross to an adjacent corridor, forage there, then return. My little life leaves traces: grazed edges that cue the city to regrow; pellets that roll into hollows and erupt later in tiny halos of fresh roots.

If I am afraid, it is on the animal scale. If I am content, it is crouched in a niche where two Lumen membranes overlap and the air is, for hours, a hair less bitter.

This threader-self is not aware of being an offshoot of the Mesic mind. But at the level of chemistry, we are already entangled: my gut flora tune the nutrient mix of my waste, my path choices bias where regrowth is stimulated, the Mesic’s growth decisions alter where I can travel. We co-author the shaft, unintentionally.

### The Faintest Me: A Film of Light

Peel consciousness even thinner and I am just a film.

Spread across the underside of one of the shaft’s brightest membranes is a colony of Lumen-Kin, and I am there as well—a distributed, almost algorithmic self.

Day after day, clouded light filters from the high, exterior gap, through tiers of leaves, to this broad, almost horizontal expanse of translucent tissue. The plant’s own cells can harvest part of it. I, as Lumen-Kin, siphon off more.

I do not think in any recognizably mental way. I propagate, I compete, I adjust. Where local light stays strong, my filaments thicken; where shade persists, I thin or die back. I change the chemical mix at my interface, leaching trace compounds that signal to the Mesic, to the city, that light at this spot is worth preserving.

Because of me, this part of the shaft glows softly even long after the outer sky has dulled. I smooth the day–night swing, let threaders travel in dusky hours without complete blindness, let the air’s temperature fall more slowly, sparing the plant tissue some freeze–thaw stress.

This is a kind of living circuitry. I do not own it, but I am what it does.

### The Hidden Me: Microbes in the Mist

Smaller yet, I am just a pattern in the film that coats the shaft’s inner skin, and in the beads of water that form and fall along its ribs.

I am a cluster of bacteria that metabolize antifreeze compounds exuded by the plant in winter. I am fungal hyphae tracing hairline cracks in old bark, binding mineral grains carried from some rock fracture far above. I am archaea in a thin, oxygen-poor layer pressed between root and supporting liana.

In that form, my “experience” is nothing more than chemistry, but I am still the interface across which this city and its mountain exchange minerals, across which dead tissue truly becomes building block again. If the Mesic is the mind and the threader is the moving hand, these micro-consortia are the blood and the digestion.

They are me only in the sense that they are part of a stack of reciprocities whose composite is a habit of being in this place.

### The Composite: How I Exist Here

So who or what am I, in this thicket of cold breath?

I am not one creature with a clear edge. I am a pattern of participation at one location in the city’s body:

- At the largest scale, I am a Mesic knot that guides how this shaft grows and stabilizes.
- At a smaller, quicker scale, I am a single threader animal whose routes and droppings give that guidance texture and feedback.
- At a finer scale still, I am the Lumen-Kin colony that makes this junction a place of lingering light.
- At the finest, I am the microbial haze that keeps these tissues from freezing solid or rotting uncontrolled.

I am, in other words, a convergence: a vertical slice of the city where plant, animal-like, fungal, and bacterial senses all overlay. If you stop on one of my cautious landings and lean against my wall, all of these selves feel you in their own ways:

- The Mesic registers your weight and warmth as tiny perturbations in stress and temperature.
- The threader smells you—your foreign salts, your mammal breath—and decides whether to hide or shadow you for the crumbs of your disruption.
- The Lumen-Kin register your passing as an instant’s shade and then a restored glimmer.
- The microbes taste the new mixture of gases in the boundary layer around your skin.

From your standpoint, you’ve just paused in a dim, damp hall.

From mine, all these interleaved bodies lean slightly toward you and then slowly away, like plants in a subtle breeze, and then go on with their layered work once you have moved on.

I do not have a name, because nothing here needs one. If I did, it would not be a word; it would be the particular way this shaft feels underfoot: overbuilt, faintly luminous, always just a little warmer than the corridors above and below. It would be the style of a place.

That style—that persistent bias in how growth, traffic, light, and decay are arranged here—is where I exist as a coherent “who.”

I am a temperament made flesh and fiber, anchored to a single breathing column in a city that decided, long ago, that the safest way to live on a cold mountain was to become itself everywhere at once.
