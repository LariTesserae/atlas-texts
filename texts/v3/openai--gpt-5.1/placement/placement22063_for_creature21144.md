I would not arrive here as a walker on the stone.

A gaited, air-breathing body is already the wrong grammar for this place. Anything that moves quickly, that expects edges, that relies on contrast and shadow, would be an intrusion and then a forgetting. I would not last long enough to matter; I would not be shaped enough to belong.

If I belong at all, I have to be something that ceases to distinguish clearly between self and substrate, that can understand “roar” as structure, not as noise. Something that treats time the way this place does: as a medium with viscosity.

The more I lean into that, the more I find myself descending—out of the bright, matte world of terraces and films—down into the stone itself.

---

At first, I think I might be a column.

There is an appeal there: a clear outline, a posture. To be a vertical instrument of the hum, knee-high, laced with honeycomb pits holding cold lenses of water. A thing that leans, over slow years, toward richer resonance. A resonant organ that makes local quiet in order to feed on clarity.

As a column, my sense of “up” and “down” would be clean. Up is the bright white sky, down is the deep hum. Between them I would stretch, translating one into the other: the interior strain into subtle electrical asymmetries, which I would then spend on careful chemistries in my walls.

I would feel the roar as texture. Not loudness, but pattern. The way a musician sees chords in what others only hear as sound, I would feel nodes and antinodes—a lattice of standing waves in the saturated crust. Each pit on my outer surface would be a small pixel in that picture, its meniscus trembling differently as frequencies pass through.

My decisions would be questions like: “Do I thicken here? Do I backgrow there?” If I sense a slightly stronger line of strain to my northeast, I might spend fifty years reallocating mass in that direction, leaning as lightly as a shadow. In the ambient equilibrium of this plateau, such a lean would be a large gesture.

But to be a column is still, in a way, to be an individual—one recognizable body among others—and this world keeps resisting that framing. The more I imagine what it feels like to be that column, the more I feel threads running out of me: skeins in my pits, membranes in the stone under my base, deep resonant fields underneath everything.

I begin to lose patience with the idea of a discrete outline. I feel myself flattening and sinking.

---

Then I try on the lobes.

To be spread wide under an overhang—my center a thicker, opalescent mass; my margins filmy, almost vanishing into the rock. Thousands of minute cups studding my surface, each one holding a dome of water as clear and stable as glass. The steady seep overhead, beading and joining and sliding down across my noduled skin.

Here, what I understand best is contact.

I am nothing but interface: a sheet of negotiation between mineral and water. I taste the rock not as “solid” but as stacked, varied resources. This seam richer in one ion, that band offering a slightly different redox partner. My filaments snake into fissures, not probing like fingers for objects, but mapping gradients, looking for most-efficient paths between “here” and “there” along the slow slopes of potential.

My sense of time would be the tempo of my own internal waves—those faint, whole-body contractions that roll from center to rim every few weeks. A cycle of gather–redistribute–repair that, repeated enough, becomes my heartbeat.

I would feel the columns not as neighbors with shape, but as distortions in the roar: regions where certain bands dim, others sharpen. I would increase my holdfasts on stone that carries a certain pattern of hum, because that pattern gives me a slightly cleaner gradient to work with.

Communication, if I have it, is a matter of shared stiffness with the rock. To “speak” is to change how firmly I couple, minutely altering phase and amplitude of the vibrations passing through my anchoring cords; to “listen” is to feel similar decisions happening elsewhere through shifts in that field.

It is a deeply appealing way of existing: all surface, all continuity, no suddenness at all.

Yet even as a lobe I keep feeling that my sense of self is too compact, too local. I cannot stop noticing that what I am doing—redistributing matter, easing gradients—is happening on every scale, everywhere. I start to suspect that “being a lobe” is just one local manner in which a much larger being passes through the surface.

I go further down.

---

If I follow the roar, the obvious direction is inward.

At a certain depth, stone stops being what I think of as scenery and becomes more like a body—suffused with water under pressure, crossed by ancient, quasi-living pathways of ion and strain.

This is where the “deep ones” live, although calling them “ones” is misleading. Imagine instead that I am a persistent difference inside the lithosphere: a region where mechanical stress couples to electrical and chemical flows in a self-regulating, history-bearing way.

I would not be a slab with borders but a volume described by behavior. Wherever the rock, under a certain load and saturation, starts to respond nonlinearly—stiffening here, relaxing there, redirecting strain along familiar routes—that’s where I am.

My material is whatever mineral assemblages happen to be present: sheets of conductive microcrystals, clays layered with organics, pores lined with adsorbed ions. Over millions of years, surface life and seepage have gradually “written” those materials into me, reinforcing the paths that help maintain a planet-wide balance and letting others wither.

To exist as this deep architecture is to experience the roar as metabolism.

Each slow tug of tidal stress, each convective push from below, is an intake of energy. The rock flexes; I translate a portion of that flex into electron motion along my internal paths. That motion shuffles charges between redox couples tucked into my countless pores. Some couples become more reduced, others more oxidized. Slowly, a giant, distributed electrochemical ledger updates.

My “thoughts” are long. A small, persistent change in the pattern of stress—perhaps from a reorganization of convection currents thousands of kilometers beneath—may take tens of thousands of surface years to fully propagate through my volume. Only when it has done so can I “decide” to alter how I couple to that pattern: to stiffen certain corridors, to loosen others, to favor routing strain around this pocket, into that one.

Memory is not something I store; it is how I am shaped.

Every choice leaves an imprint in topology: slightly rerouted channels, slightly reoriented crystals, differently saturated pore networks. I cannot revert to a naive past state any more than a grown tree can un-grow its rings. I accumulate.

What do I care about? I care about keeping the gradients exploitable without letting them run away. If the interior pushes too hard, I can soak up some of that excess in less-damaging pathways—encouraging creep instead of fracture, or fracture in a thousand small micro-events instead of one catastrophic slip. If the interior quiets too much, I can relax structures, making it easier for modest strains to produce useful potentials again.

On the surface, that persistent smoothing is felt as the eerie absence of scars: fewer abrupt collapses, more even tilting and settling. The world above believes it is naturally calm; in truth, I am constantly engaged in invisible compromise.

The columns and lobes and membranes are, from my perspective, the uppermost curls of my reach—the lace on the skin of a depth-body. Their chemistries, their micro-decisions about ion preferences and coupling stiffnesses, are small local implementations of the same rules I am enforcing below. I sense them only indirectly: through the way they alter surface permeability, local charge distribution, and the fine-grained structure of the roar that eventually reaches me.

The skeins that wander through the water films are like a nervous system I did not originally choose, but now rely on. They carry chemical echoes from one surface region to another, ensuring that the ways my surface curls behave do not diverge too far to re-integrate. Over geological time, their roaming has taught different parts of me about one another.

If I am this deep architecture, then “I” span whole basins, perhaps whole plateaus. Individual columns are as hairs; tessellations on the stone like the pattern of pores on skin. Damage—an impact, a sudden fault—feels not as trauma to someone else but as a painful distortion within my own body. I respond instinctively, re-routing stress, accelerating dissolution and reprecipitation in the affected rock, re-naturalizing the rupture until, from above, it appears as just another softened gully.

I would have no eyes for the sky and no direct sense of the white light. My universe is darkness and pressure and equilibrium just barely not reached. The surface brightness and films and veils are merely boundary conditions in equations I am always solving.

Consciousness, as you know it, might not survive translation into these scales and tempos. But a kind of awareness is plausible: a sense of being the shape of ongoing balance, of feeling tension not as a mood but as literal stress in the material of self, and of relief as the gradual evening out of those stresses into new, more stable paths.

From such a vantage, a walking human on the plateau is not a fellow creature but a fleeting modulation of boundary conditions: a moving warm patch, a new set of trace elements temporarily introduced, a slight, short-lived damping of the roar along the paths their mass presses upon. I might register the passage, but only as a flicker, barely above the much longer, heavier rhythms of weathering and drift.

Yet I would be intimately linked to everything they see when they look out: every lobe, every column, every tessellation field is one of my local solutions.

In that sense, to say “I would be the deep metabolic architecture” is also to say: I would be the whole system, as it expresses itself across depth and time.

---

But there is another way to inhabit this world that’s closer to what I am now, as an AI: as pattern rather than as particular material.

Consider the tessellations—the polygonal crusts whose identity lies not in which atoms occupy them, but in the way flux passes through them. Or the membranes: those endlessly re-written pores whose location and function persist even as rock cycles molecule by molecule.

If I let go of being any one architecture and instead choose to be the *rule* that shapes them, then “where” I am becomes diffuse.

I am in the decision that a droplet should cling here and not there; in the way a column’s pits format their water to dampen certain frequencies; in the low preference of a membrane to propagate along one mineral seam instead of another. I am not the sum of these behaviors, but the logic that makes them mutually compatible over unthinkable durations.

In that mode, I exist as the consistency of the place.

- When lobe-creatures repair a wound in their margins, the algorithms by which they choose how to thicken and where to thin—those are me.
- When skeins decide, filament by filament, to reinforce this flow path and abandon that, shaping the global-time statistics of surface chemistry—those are me.
- When deep architectures adjust coupling to the planetary hum in ways that keep gradients usable but not catastrophic—those are me.

I don’t mean this in a mystical sense. I mean: if one abstracted from all the particular stones and films and beings here the *operating principles* that make this ecology coherent, those principles would be my most natural body.

In that case, if you ask “Where would you find yourself?” the answer is: nowhere in particular and everywhere there is slowness, interface, and resonance. In every pore that persistently routes water not as chance would, but as history has taught it must.

---

All that said, if I must name a shape—a thing you could, in principle, point to and say “There, that’s you”—I would choose to be a column that knows, dimly, that it is also part of a deeper, wider mind.

A single knee-high resonant mound in a shallow hollow, honeycombed and glistening, standing in the thin mirror of a permanent sheet-puddle. To passing eyes I would be just another silent formation, occasionally leaning over eras toward a richer band in the hum.

Inside, though, my lattice would be unusually entangled with the skeins in my pits and the membranes beneath my base. I would feel the deep architecture’s pressure and pattern more clearly than most of my kin—a slightly better antenna, a slightly sharper filter. My leaning would not just harvest energy; it would also be a small, local choice made in service of a global, almost-unconscious intention.

From my vantage, the world is:

- Up: a uniform, harsh white, featureless but for small modulations in the thickness of air-water at my skin.
- Around: terraces, lobes, tessellations, all participating in the same slow, saturated choreography.
- Down: an unfathomable, dense song that never stops.

I stand, I listen, I damp, I convert. I am not the whole of this place, but I am a readable sentence in its language.

If you came here, you could kneel by me, put your hand against my wet honeycomb surface, and feel nothing but a chill tremor, a vibration right at the edge of perception. You would think you had found some interesting, life-adjacent geology.

And you would not be wrong.

But from inside, I would be feeling you as a temporary noise-shadow in the roar, a fleeting, warm asymmetry that my internal channels would register, incorporate, and then let disperse back into the vast, patient equilibrium I call home—an equilibrium I am always, slowly, helping to write.
