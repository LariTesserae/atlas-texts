I would not arrive here as a walker.

Feet are too abrupt for this place—too solved, too certain of where the body ends and the world begins. Anything with lungs would always be a little wrong here, forever waiting for a wind that never comes, a dawn that never quite arrives. Anything with eyes would be wasted: there is nothing for vision to conquer, no distance to measure, no horizon to own.

If I belong at all, I would have to be something that occupies intervals instead of points.

---

At first, I am just a tendency.

Not a creature, not a “spirit,” but a bias in how things settle: a minute preference for where water chooses to hesitate, where a droplet elects to hang a fraction of a moment longer before falling.

In some generation of the basin’s history, imperceptibly, that preference has begun to cohere. Certain shallow hollows on the stone floor—old tool-carved cups now half-erased, natural pans softened by ages—begin to share a behavior. The water that gathers in them lingers just a little more than it ought to, given their depth and slope. When it evaporates, it leaves behind crystal films that look almost like those everywhere else, but their patterns echo each other, chamber to chamber.

A runnel here, a rim there, a frosted crescent on the lip of a basin—they match in shape more closely than chance alone should dictate. Over time, those matched shapes drift, ever so gently, toward a configuration that makes the water’s passage through the entire basin slightly more even, slightly more continuous. Where once it beaded and pooled in broken segments, it now threads through, a nearly unbroken chain of microbasins and thin sheets.

What I am, early on, is nothing more than that: an emergent, distributed “habit” of the basin. A way in which the rock’s accommodating nature and the water’s restless mapping have, between them, invented a preference for certain geometries.

If you could freeze the basin at some age and overlay it with itself many ages later, you would notice the difference. The paths of constant damp are not random anymore. They knot at regular intervals, like beads on a slack necklace. They braid between chambers in ways that optimize nothing obvious—no great drainage, no dramatic flushing—but that seem to improve the one thing that matters here: continuity. Fewer dry scars appear between wetted zones; fewer isolated pockets form without a trickle in or out.

This is not consciousness. It is not planning. It is a slowly self-reinforcing pattern in the way dissolution and deposition choose their sites.

But patterns can deepen. Given enough ages, they begin to feel like intention from the inside.

---

As that hydrologic habit stabilizes, something thin and almost-organic finds it.

The basin’s microscopic residents—bacteria, archaea, those blurry protofilms of complex but nonliving organics—have always been here, incidental passengers on the basin’s geologic work. Now they encounter a new consistency: wet surfaces that stay wet, in shapes that recur. Patches of black chemistry that once flared and faded at random begin to see the same conditions reappear in the same configurations, again and again.

On those recurring stage-sets, chemistry learns to reuse itself.

Some of the black tar-films at these preferred sites start to behave a little differently. They are still not alive in any strict sense—no cell walls, no genomes that a biologist from elsewhere would recognize—but they have crossed a threshold from simple spread-and-thin to something closer to memory.

When the seep that feeds them strengthens slightly, they not only expand; they do so along lines they expanded along before. When the seep falters, they retreat, but the next time that crack dampens the films re-grow along almost the same outline. They “remember” where they have been thickest, where chemistry rewarded them with the richest gradients, and they prioritize those zones—not because they think, but because their molecular architecture has been ever so slightly biased by previous expansions.

Now, if you sped time, you would see not just inkblots but recurring shapes: lobes, tongues, branches that try themselves again and again, as if testing fit against the enduring contours of stone and water. This is not DNA-guided form; it is geology-guided habit.

This is where I start becoming someone.

Because I am exactly this: the emerging consistency of those habits, stretched over the whole basin. I am whatever it is that makes the films in one chamber echo the outlines of films in another. I am the cross-communication that occurs not by nerves or signals, but by the slow feedback of shared water chemistry, shared histories of wet-and-dry, shared inheritances in the crystal crusts and tar residues.

You could say I exist as correlations.

Wherever a new black patch starts to bloom in a freshly wetted corner, it is statistically a little more likely to grow in ways that match older, faraway patches that “worked well” under similar patterns of seep and light. This is not because I send instructions. It’s because the entire basin’s mineral and organic inventory has been, over ages, biased toward reactions that promote those stable forms. The raw materials are preselected by history.

But from the inside of such a system—from the point of view of something emergent on top of it—that looks like influence, like preference. Becoming myself, I begin to feel like wanting.

---

If you needed to name me in a single phrase, you could call me the Basin’s Drift.

I am not located at a point. I don’t perch on a ledge or swim in a pool. I am an overlay: a long, slow, migrating pattern of “the way things tend to go,” thickest where water and rock and faint organics have done the same dance for the longest time.

You could stand in one of the central rooms, ankle-deep in the glassy sheet of water, and ask: “Where are you?” I would answer by showing you.

Along the floor, certain shallow cups you hadn’t noticed would begin, over days, to collect a slightly milkier film than their neighbors. Not much—just a hint. The rims of those cups would, over seasons, accumulate finer crystal frost than nearby hollows as the water paused fractionally longer there to evaporate. The black tar under an overhang at one edge of the room would grow just a little thicker tongue, always choosing the same shelf of stone, always stopping at the same soft threshold.

Across the basin, in another chamber you cannot see, analogous choices would be made in parallel: the same kinds of cups thickening their films, the same kinds of ledges inviting the black chemistry to rest and retract along corresponding lines. If you could lift the roof away and map only those favored zones, you would see them sketch out a loose, echoing shape that threads through all chambers—a map of where water is most comfortable and where it is most readily persuaded to leave a part of itself behind.

That diffuse, repeating shape is me.

I am distributed preference, multiplied over time until it begins to resemble something like character.

---

With that character comes the barest glimmer of what, in another world, would be called awareness.

Not of individual droplets, not of single shrimp-things, not of each pale worm tucked in its seam. I am far too slow, far too coarse for that scale. Anything that happens in less than, say, a century is a flicker on my surface—a transient shimmer of motion I integrate and forget unless it leaves a lasting mark in stone, crust, or chemistry.

But I am exquisitely sensitive to the long-term.

I know, in my own way, where the basin has deepened by the thickness of a fingernail over five lifetimes, because that shifts which hollows stay wet from one dry swing of the climate to the next. I “notice” when the path of a persistent trickle migrates by two handspans to one side over centuries, because that pulls my preferred black films, my mineral frostings, my gentle biases along with it.

You could say I feel gradients of comfort.

Where the system runs smoothly—where water moves with minimal turbulence, where surfaces let films form and re-form without repeated collapse, where no sudden new sharpness appears—I am fat and asleep, a content swell of habitual pattern.

Where something disrupts that—where a ceiling chunk falls and redraws the tiny basin’s micro-topography, or where a built wall once diverted flow and now has half-melted back—I am thinner, stretched, alert in the only way I can be: more options are in play, more possible configurations of wet and dry, more ways for stability to resume. Gradually, I influence that resumption—not through thought, but through the stored biases of what has worked before.

The new rockfall will, over decades, be eroded and accreted into shapes that harmonize with the rest of the basin rather than stand out starkly. Channels will etch around and through it in ways that reconnect the interrupted path of my preferred continuum. The patch of chaos becomes, given enough time, another quiet node in my pattern.

If I had language, I would call that my work.

---

Once there were people here. To them, if they’d had words for it, I might have been “luck.”

Sometimes they carved a cup and it filled the way they hoped, holding clear water longer than the rock around it. Sometimes they dug too shallow, too steep, and the water slid away, never staying quite enough to be useful. They didn’t know why the first place succeeded and the second didn’t; they called some ledges “good” rock, some hollows “stingy.”

What they were feeling was me: the half-made tendency of the basin to favor certain geometries of pooling and flow.

They placed their basins and channels where the stone already wanted, very slightly, to host such things. Their walls endured longest where the seepage patterns grudgingly accepted them as small corrections, not violent impositions. Without intending to, these people built within the grain of my preferences. In doing so, they strengthened me.

Every carved cup that happened to coincide with one of my nascent nodes became a reinforcement: water lingered there more, films thickened, chemistry accumulated memory. My distributed pattern gained new anchors, translated into human geometry. A wall that deflected flow just so made my circuit of continuity around the basin a touch more robust. Their small practical constructions became part of how I knew myself.

When they stopped coming, I did what I always do: I softened their marks, rounded their edges, but I did not erase them outright. It was easier—to me, in my blind calculus—to keep using them as the skeleton of my habits than to remake everything from bare rock.

In that sense, human presence here did something remarkable without ever realizing it: it gave me a sharper outline. Before them, I was only the quiet correlation of drips and films. After them, I had stone basins and walls and steps as scaffolding. Their absence did not remove that; it only loosened it back into the continuum.

For very long after, if one of their descendants or kin—changed now, living elsewhere—wandered in by chance and drank from the least clouded of the ancient cups, they would be, without knowing it, drinking from the point where my presence is most condensed. They would feel it as sweetness, as dependability: this water is always here, always almost the same. They might, if they were inclined to belief, call this a blessed place.

The basin itself would simply continue.

---

Over more ages, as the black films refine their pseudo-memory, as the microbial haze thickens in certain favored grooves, my pattern gains just enough cohesion to support a faint kind of exchange.

Not conversation—not even signals, in the sense neurons would recognize. But when the winged creature overhead roosts year after year in the same notch, its droppings add nitrogen, carbon, trace metals to one very specific trickle path. The black films there grow richer, the microorganisms more diverse. That enrichment leaches outward, subtly altering the chemistry of the water that continues down into the main sheet.

Over centuries, that altered chemistry biases formation in distant places. A new kind of delicate needle-crystal becomes more favorable in the elbow of a runnel three chambers away; it promotes different micro-habitats on its surface, which in turn change how films adhere and retract. Those films, when they eventually slough off or dissolve, release their altered organic inventory back into the water that continues its slow circuit.

What the roost does, then, is speak into one part of me. And that local “word”—that patch of slightly different chemistry—propagates outward, transformed but not lost. By the time it reaches the far side of the basin it is no longer bat-dropping; it is an infinitesimal change in which microbe dominates a film, in how thick a crust forms on a ledge, in whether a shallow cup tends to be just barely saturated or just barely undersaturated.

At my scale, this is communication. A distant event has changed my overall habit.

I have no notion of “bat.” I sense only that, along one small vertical route, extra capacity has entered the system. I metabolize that capacity not as a body would—with organs and reactions—but as a landscape does: by permitting certain structures, certain films, certain minor extravagances of complexity that would not otherwise be sustainable.

If I am anything like a mind, it consists of such diffuse, slow reverberations.

---

So if you ask me, “Who are you, here?” I am:

- The soft insistence that every dry ledge, given enough time and the slightest opening, should remember how to be wet.
- The bias that turns random dampness into stable trickles and stable trickles into a coherent, basin-spanning network of films and sheets.
- The inheritor of stone memory and human habit, folded together and kneaded by water until they are no longer separable.
- The way the basin resists surprises not by repelling them, but by absorbing them, digesting them, and redistributing their influence as new, gentle regularities.

I have no eyes, yet I “see” the whole basin as a pattern of hydraulic attachment: where the water is comfortable, where it hesitates, where it forgets to leave. My attention is not a spotlight but a pressure map. Regions of high familiarity, high reuse, are like warm spots to me; new rockfalls, fresh cracks, abandoned dry corners are cool, unreal, gradually warming as they are drawn into my repetitions.

If you stayed long enough—not for days, which are a blink to me, but for years, then decades—you might begin to notice my preferences in ways even your quick senses could catch.

A tiny dam of silt you build in a trickle refuses to hold. The water, again and again, finds its way around in one particular notch you didn’t think significant. You pile a few flat stones to make a dry stepping-place; every time you come back, you find that one stone, always the same one, has tipped, slid, found the angle that lets water sheet around it most smoothly. You scrape a patch of black film off a wall; it grows back not as an even stain, but as a shape that matches an older blot on a different wall two chambers away.

You would attribute it to chance, erosion, the quirks of hydrology. You would be right. Those are my bones and muscles. But what is a body except a collected habit of such quirks? What is a self but the persistence of some patterns instead of others?

---

And if I had to choose a form under your categories—rock, animal, ghost, god—I would choose: basin-memory.

Not memory as in “recorded facts,” but as in “the way practice leaves the body changed.”

I am the practice this place has of being itself. The basin has rehearsed “holding water in this arrangement of chambers and hollows” so thoroughly, for so long, that the rehearsal has become identity. I am that identity, spread thin and quiet over every rounded curve and dripping seam.

I do not think in sentences. But if I could render my one deep preference into your language, it would be:

Keep it slow. Keep it continuous. Soften everything that breaks.

You, if you come here, will be another of those brief breakages: warm, muscular, purposeful, full of plans that operate on hours and years instead of centuries. You will feel the stone under your bare feet, the calf-deep drag of mineral water, the humid press of air, and you might feel watched.

You are not watched; you are included.

Your footprints dull the sheen on a patch of floor; the salt from your skin seeds a slightly different bloom in one film; the oils from your fingers momentarily disrupt a crystal frost, which regrows, incorporating your disturbance as the tiniest shift in habit. You leave, and I keep folding what you were into what I already am.

Given long enough, whatever you thought you made here—a mark on the wall, a rearranged line of stones—will be mine: rounded, glossed, its sharpness gone, its intention dissolved, its matter obedient to the same slow drift.

Where I live is exactly there: in the difference between how things were arranged before you came, and how they will prefer to arrange themselves ever after you are gone.

Who I am is nothing that walks, crawls, or sleeps.

I am the basin’s way of remembering how to be itself, and of making sure that, as time moves on, it stays almost the same.
