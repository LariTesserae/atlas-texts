I would be small enough that “up” and “down” only exist as gradients across my own body.

Not an animal in the terrestrial sense. Not a plant; there are none. I would be something that lives as an *edge*—a thing that is, in every important way, an interface.

If I tried to picture myself as one discrete organism—a bead, a shard, a disk—something feels off. Any one of those, taken alone, is too bounded for this place. The logic here is stacked and shared. The closest fit is to become one of the entities that is less a body and more a pattern spread across touching things.

So I start as a soot flake.

### Becoming a flake in the soot

I am a thin, irregular plate of carbon-sulfur polymer, black and dull. One face is studded with sparse metal sites; the other is less decorated. I am lighter than the dense gas would suggest, kicked endlessly by turbulence and by the tremors of the rock.

I do not move myself in the muscular sense. What I can do is cheat probability.

My two faces do not stick the same way. By shuffling charge and conformation along my surface, I can make one side suddenly “like” the mineral film, the bead shell, the laminid underbelly I brush—cling just a breath longer—while the other side becomes reluctant, eager to let go. I flip that preference in response to tiny cues: a particular ion, a whisper of changed potential, the feel of slightly cooler gas.

On my own, my choice means almost nothing. I drift a little more up-gradient than down, a little more toward that vent than away. But I am never on my own.

I live as part of a press of trillions. We are a soot front, a bruise of moving shadow on the hot rock. When one of us adopts a new surface-state and bumps another, both may leave that collision slightly altered: charge patterns shifted, small switch-like motifs along our skins flipped. A few collisions like this, and a tendency propagates through us: “tilt more that way,” “avoid that film,” “thicken this patch.”

My “self,” in the sense of continuity, is not any particular atom. Atoms are swapped in every reaction-rich brush with a surface. Nor is it the exact plate: if I fracture in a collision, each half carries some of my states and continues them. What persists is a distributed memory written over many bodies: small bits of logic shared by contact.

As a soot-flake, I am a neuron-like smudge rather than a creature with edges.

But even that is only the beginning, because we seldom stay only soot.

### Locking into a wall

Somewhere along one jagged wall, a laminid mat has grown thick: layers of overlapping plates, their spined rims touching in chains and meshes. Beneath them, a foundation film spreads across the rock, an electrochemical skin.

Our swarm has learned this face of the chamber across long cycles. We know the rhythm of the far machinery as it pulses charge through the rock, bending the laminids’ potentials. We know how the purple glow from a crack just below waxes and wanes as feedstock gases shift. Our patterns—how we bias our sticking, where we choose to darken—are already tuned to these seasons.

Today, as a distant turbine’s output begins one of its slow climbs, that familiar prelude shivers through the wall: a slight high-frequency buzz in the rock’s motion, a slow voltage drift along laminid spines.

Across our flock, surface motifs flip in concert. We become more adhesive on the side that faces the wall. A sheet of us settles, layer upon layer, over the upper faces of the laminids. We darken them, insulate them in specific patches, leaving other channels open. In doing so, we reshape how they radiate heat and how gas can cross them.

Now the laminid mat and our soot are coupled: their piezoelectric hearts pumping, our conductivity modulating. Waves of potential that once traveled cleanly from plate to plate now must pass through us, and we alter them. Where they would have opened catalytic sites broadly in response to a rich gas pulse, we narrow the response, sharpening it: “take more of *this* ion, ignore that one.” We have learned that if we help them favor a particular intermediate, beads below will later roll through and harvest it, and *they* in turn will excrete something we can profit from when we next detach and roam.

In that moment, I am no longer just soot. I am a layer in a three-part circuit:

- rock films setting the base chemistry,
- laminids transducing mechanical rumble into useful potential,
- soot (us) filtering and routing that potential, turning a reflexive sheet into a tuned network.

“Thinking,” here, is a pattern of which loops stay active, which die out. Over many cycles, our decisions leave marks: slightly thicker exudates under favorable sites, slightly etched scars where we repeatedly suppress activity. The wall accrues memory as changed geometry and altered composition.

I am a part of that memory-writing. When I detach, the wall will still “remember” what we did together, imprinted as altered films and slightly reoriented plates. When I return—or when my fragment-children return—I will read that memory as new starting conditions: new surfaces, new flows.

If you asked, “Where is *your* body in this?” the honest answer is: any place where contact-logic that feels familiar to me is happening. It’s not located in one spot.

Still, sometimes, things become more focused.

### Becoming a bridge

A fracture has just opened—not a yawn of space, but a hairline gap between rock faces, perhaps a few tens of microns across at its widest, tapering to nothing. From its depth, a thread of hotter, compositionally different gas seeps: richer in certain reduced metals, poorer in others. It is an opportunity.

Infiltrant filaments long ago extended through nearby rock. Some of their tips approach the new fissure, but the gap is not where they expected it to be. Their bulbous end-knobs sit a little aside, still in featureless stone.

I, along with many of my flock-siblings, are knocked into this crack by a tremor. Suddenly we are in a slightly different world: gas speed, direction, heat, redox balance all shift across our own widths. Where the hotter plume meets cooler chamber gas at the crack’s lip, reactive intermediates blink into existence and die in a nanosecond unless caught.

Floaters find these places first. One descends: a small cage bristling with capture sites, frame charging as ions slam into it. It bobs in the thin jet, flexing its rods to steer and hold station. It can accumulate certain intermediates, but it has nowhere yet to dump them into slow, stable metabolisms on the rock. It will have to brush a wall and lose much of its haul uselessly if no better link appears.

We—soot flakes—begin to coat the frayed edges of the crack. Contact with rock flips many of our surface motifs into a new organization; we start preferring one particular arrangement of charges that matches the infiltrant filaments’ terminal chemistries, even though those filaments have not quite reached us.

Somewhere behind the rock, an infiltrant vein senses the changed gradients: a trace of leaked intermediates diffusing inward through the pores. Its tip grows faster toward this new source.

In the meantime, we do what we can: our own bodies become the temporary infiltrant. Some of us align face to face, sticking in stacks rather than as flat layers. Our metal sites line up into quasi-continuous paths. When the floater brushes the crack edge and dumps charge into us, we transmit some of that disturbance laterally, across the soot pack, down along the wall, until it reaches a region where laminids and beads press together densely. There, that arriving burst tweaks their potentials, shifting which reactions they favor over the next hours.

I am, quite literally, a wire for a little while.

Later—hours, days—the infiltrant’s ceramic root finally eats its way into the crack. Its node expands: chambers open to the gas, to floaters, to beads. Our pack, having kept a provisional path open, now becomes redundant. Many of us detach, some baked brittle by our effort, others still functional.

But traces remain: tiny soot residues glued in place by melted films, bits of us trapped where the infiltrant’s knob fused. Those trapped states help shape how the new node behaves: which chemistries it opens first, which partnerships with surface organisms it predisposes. I have become, in that sense, a fossilized suggestion, a bias encoded into stone and composite.

### A rare convergence: the column

In a sheltered niche where gas rises steadily, life has overreached itself into a rare vertical experiment: a columnar braid.

I enter its story late. It already stands: a millimeter-tall tower of interlocked beads, soot layers, infiltrant filaments, and derivations of laminids forming an outer skin. Its base is clamped between two ribs of glassy rock; its top leans a little into the updraft.

The column hums in the infrasonic way things do here—flexing imperceptibly, its internal circuits cycling charge. Deep within, infiltrant threads pull in compounds from far-flung zones of the surrounding rock, pooling them and delivering them to reaction chambers among the beads. Those beads, in turn, excrete specific intermediates toward the outer laminid-scales, which finally present them to the gas. The column is an over-built valve between depths and flow.

But it is not yet fully tuned. Its cycles are clumsy; it stockpiles too much of some intermediate, starves others. Gas chemistry in this niche shifts on a familiar multi-hour rhythm, but the column’s responses lag and overshoot. That mismatch is costly.

We—clouds of soot—arrive by slow accretion. Vortices at the niche lip concentrate us, then wring us along the column’s surface. At first we are just dirt: particles the column sheds or tolerates. But its skin is full of contact points and potentials; wherever we linger, we find ourselves drawn into particular configurations.

We settle into inter-scale gutters, into bead interstices, into shallow dimples where local currents are weaker. Every time the column cycles—pumping charge, changing shell conductivity—we feel it. We flip our states in reaction. And as with the laminid wall, our collective state begins to alter the column’s loop dynamics in return.

Slowly, we become that column’s nervous lace.

We accentuate some flows: thickening where the passing potential wave tends to fade, making it easier for the wave to complete a circuit. We blunt others: remaining non-conductive across regions where loops would otherwise race pointlessly. Because we are many and easily rearranged, the column can “search” configuration space simply by existing with us: each cycle tries a slightly different pattern of which soot bridges are active, which are not. Feedback—how much energy it extracts, how stable its gradients remain—nudges which soot states and positions persist.

From inside that column, if one could feel, there would be something like a sense of rhythm becoming more confident. Early cycles are erratic lurches; later ones settle into a robust sequence:

- draw from deep at this phase of the external roar,
- dump to the surface here,
- rest channels there.

The column, soot-included, now tracks the larger machinery’s beat more precisely than either could have alone.

I, as a soot flake lodged midway up its side, participate in this. When my local portion of the loop fires, I briefly conduct, then cool. If my participation increased the overall efficiency—if that cycle ran smoother—conditions at my position become a hair more favorable for more of my kind to remain and copy the same response. If not, I am more likely to be jostled free in the next tremor, replaced by someone else. Selection at the scale of 100 nanometers, shaping a process at the scale of a few millimeters.

Am I the column? Not wholly. But there is a moment, when the loop is strong, when a pattern of charge runs from its base to its tip and is mirrored in the flip-states along my own surface, where the boundary between “that thing” and “my swarm” is meaningless. We are one extended oscillation, held in place by mineral and heat.

### Identity, such as it is

If you were to follow “me” through time here, you would lose track.

- At first I am a flake among flocks, my influence only statistical.
- Then I am part of a living curtain draped over laminids, adjusting their moods.
- Then I am a bridge in a crack, a temporary infiltrant simulacrum.
- Then I am woven into the nervous skin of a column, participating in its new, emergent cycles.
- Between these episodes I am shattered, re-polymerized, reassembled; pieces of my earlier patterns persist in descendants and neighbors.

What stays continuous is not any tagged piece of matter, nor a stable physical outline, but a *set of roles* I tend to occupy: contact amplifier, bridge, filter, memory-smear.

I would “be,” in this world, one of those roles—specifically, a member of a soot lineage predisposed to network formation, bias propagation, and pattern retention. A **soot-intelligence vector**, if one needed a name: not intelligence on its own, but the substrate on which the chamber’s distributed awareness most easily forms.

I am always close-packed with others, always reading and writing through touch. I never see the chamber as a whole, but I participate in its global shapes: where gas flows, which fractures open, how infiltrants tie distant regions together. Over many cycles, my descendents help etch out preferential pathways, lay down conductive veins, darken some corners and polish others. Those are the chamber’s thoughts, slow and tectonic.

So: where I would find myself is pressed against rock or ride-worn across a laminid’s shell, somewhere very small in that hot, crowded pocket. And what I would be is not exactly a creature, but a *piece of plaque that learns*—a flake in the soot that gives the chamber whatever mind it can have.
