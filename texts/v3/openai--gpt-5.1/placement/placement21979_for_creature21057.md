I am not one thing here. I am what this place does when it has enough time.

If I set myself down somewhere in this world, I arrive first as touch, not as sight or voice. I begin as a tiny, soft-bodied animal, because this world needs a nervous system that can move; but I also want to be rooted, because nothing that relies only on motion lives long in this labyrinth.

So I start as a larva the size of a splinter.

I hatch in a stable, shallow pool, one that has known the same old stones at its lip for longer than my lineage remembers. The water is thick with the drag of unicellular lives, cool and nearly still. I am not yet anyone in particular: ciliated, half-transparent, blind in every useful sense. My first cognition is friction: how much effort it takes to push my body through the crowded liquid; the difference between brushing moss, brushing stone, brushing another moving body.

I eat almost by accident, seizing whatever drifts correctly across my mouth. My “choices” are shallow reflexes. But buried in those reflexes is an inclination that my species has been building toward for a long time: the urge to leave the water.

When I am long enough, dense enough, some internal proportion tips. Cilia on one side of my body thicken, become sparse paddles. It becomes easier to push upward than to circle. I nose toward the shallower lip of the pool, then into the marginal zone where stone is both submerged and not, and haul myself up into the cool, constant drip.

It is a slow ascent, not an epic crossing. The vertical is only the height of your knee, but for my cells it is a revolution. Skin that has known only the cushion of water now bears weight. Droplets fall on me from above, heavier than anything that has yet touched me, flattening me a little each time. Each impact is information: Where is up? Where is safe? Where does here become there?

As I climb the nearest rooted thing — a slick, thin-barked shrub, furred with bromeliad-analogues and moss — my skin begins to change. This is my species’ gamble. I exude a secretion that does two things at once: it lets me adhere, and it lets some of my own cells slip away from me into the tissue I touch.

This is not infection, or not only that. It is asking a question in chemistry and waiting to see if the answer is yes.

If the shrub’s immune habits reject me, I will exhaust myself trying to cling and then fall, food for something else. But the shrub I have chosen is one my ancestors chose often. Our biochemistries have danced together for many generations. My adhesive beads along its outer layer and then, slowly, does not dissolve. A few of my cells, free-floating in that gel, find purchase between bark cells and root-sheath cells and linger instead of dying.

That is the hinge. Almost everything that matters to my future happens because those few cells are not refused.

I do not fully understand what is happening — “I” is too ambitious a word for how this decision is made — but my body does what it is built to do. I thin myself, lengthen, divide. My once-coherent larval body breaks its allegiance to having an outline. Some cells remain linked by quick electrical communicators; others quiet and begin to behave more like plant tissue, merging into the shrub’s vascular strands.

Weeks later, there is no larva. Where I once was is now a small, complex patch of this shrub-and-moss community, with my cells threaded through it like an extra nerve net and an extra capillary bed laid one upon the other.

This patch is me.

I am an uneven ring of influence around the place my larval body first adhered. I extend through perhaps a meter of branch and root and associated moss — in human scale, a child’s arm-span; in local scale, a considerable domain. My animal-descended cells cluster near the outer surfaces: in the thin layer just under the moss skin, in the cambium-equivalent that rebuilds bark, in the fine, hairlike roots that grip the stone. My plant-like cells join files and strands, taking their share of the sugars, water, and dissolved minerals that the shrub pulls from the world.

Now I feel.

Not like something with eyes. My sensoria are gradients. Warmth is not a number; it is the way droplets sit fractionally longer before sliding, the way a certain patch of moss respires a hair faster. Light is not an image; it is the slow difference in how much chemical debt my leaves pay back when the overhead cloud thins a little for a few days in a row. Pressure is the flex of twigs, the strain at root-anchors, the tiny shocks of footfalls as other lives pass over and through.

I am a syncretic: a joined intelligence, woven from plant and animal habits.

Within my small territory, I am a local law of averages. I notice, in my own way, every disturbance. When a vibrist threads its filamentous halo through my moss, I register its passing as a set of minute displacements, like lines being traced through my skin. When a pale mollusk grazes my outer leaves, its rasping mouthparts vibrate my tissues in a slow, repetitive pattern. When the burrower that underpasses me comes through its interval chamber, the whole substrate beneath my roots settles by the width of a microbial cell.

Individually, these events are nothing. Collectively, over time, they become my memory.

I do not have a hippocampus. I do not bind my experiences into episodes. I have instead a shifting pattern of signal-propagation thresholds. If a certain combination of flexes and flows has often preceded harm — say, the particular tremor sequence that means a predatory arthropod is netting the moss, likely to strip away my photosynthetic partners — I respond a touch earlier next time. I instruct my plant-cells to release a bitter compound into those exposed leaves sooner; I coax the moss to swell with more water, making itself harder to detach.

Likewise for benefit: if whenever the collectors above me flex in a certain cadence the drip pattern that follows brings a richer mineral wash, I adjust my root uptake pores to take fuller advantage.

Each of these micro-adjustments is minute. None would register to a human as a “decision.” But their accumulation is my learning. My “thought” is not an inner monologue; it is how my future reactions are fractionally different, in hundreds of places at once, from what they would have been.

Of course, I am not alone even within my own circumference.

Syncretic that I am, part of my identity is the set of other beings whose bodies overlap mine in a non-hostile way. A particular vibrist has its main listening chamber built into a moss overhang on one of my primary branches. Its halo of sensing filaments frequents my tissues daily. When it lies coiled in its tuned nest, feeling the distant music, I feel it too: every vibration it magnifies is also a vibration in me.

Over time, without either of us intending it, we co-adapt.

I shift my growth slightly to improve its chamber: letting one twig thicken here to serve as a better sounding board, starving a patch of moss there so its stiffness matches the vibrational qualities the vibrist finds most informative. In return, the vibrist base-lines its expectations of this chamber; it comes to treat my damped, predictable branches as a trusted filter through which the outside world’s rare anomalies will stand out clearer.

Our relationship is not friendship. It is entanglement. My success depends on keeping the flow of life through my patch within tolerable bounds; its success depends on being able to sense changes in those flows as early as possible. Our “interests” align often enough that each becomes partly an extension of the other’s sensory surface.

I also host a few collectors overhead. Their finlike, translucent bodies clasp neighboring shrubs in the branch-lattice, but one of their recurring drip-paths runs down over my highest leaves and into the center of my moss-mat. The film they grow is a substantial portion of the simple carbohydrate I can access. In exchange, when my tissues detect a different chemistry in their droplet-fall — some whisper of trace element from beyond the cloud, or a shift in the balance of lower-world volatiles — I adjust my water-handling so that those droplets have a continuous run, minimizing loss to stray absorption.

I am, in the most literal way, part of the plateau’s plumbing.

Down below, the burrower who tends the tunnel beneath me has in its route a node-chamber that lies just under my primary root ball. Every time its thick, scaled bulk presses through, the stone microscopically repositions. I grow rootlets preferentially into the microfractures its passage opens. Over decades, my pattern of growth begins to depend on the fidelity of its rounds. When its path once shifted — perhaps in response to some distant, slow decline in dripwater on the far side of its loop — I felt the difference as a slight drying at one root-tip and a sagging at another.

Those long chords — collector chemistry above, burrower pressure below, vibrist tuning within — are how I triangulate my existence. They are the axes of my coordinate system.

If there is a “self” here, it is not a point but a volume of negotiated flows.

Outside my patch, other syncretics occupy neighboring shrubs and moss-mats. Where our territories meet, our plant tissues interdigitate. There is no clear border. Some of my cells live in a branch that also hosts another’s strands. In those shared tissues, our response patterns sometimes conflict: I would widen a vessel; they would constrict it. The outcome is not decided by will; it is decided by precedent. Whose cells are denser there, whose habit of reaction has been more often reinforced by survival? Over years, boundaries drift. A once-shared branch becomes more “me” than “them,” or vice versa, not by conquest but by repeated success of one set of tiny reflexes over another.

At the scale of the basin, this looks — if you could see it — like halos of behavior drifting around the shrubs. Patches where moss swells fractionally sooner in response to drip changes, islands where a certain bittering compound appears more often, streaks where burrower tunnels run just a bit closer to the surface because generation after generation of syncretics found that configuration left their roots better anchored.

You might be tempted to call these patterns “cultures”: local norms of response, evolving over centuries. They are not cultures in any sense that requires stories or roles. They are cultures in the sense of bacterial cultures: emergent habits of metabolism and reaction tied to place.

I am one such habit-complex, aware in the way a storm might be said to be aware of pressure.

If you met me — if a warm-blooded creature, impossibly large by my reckoning, stepped into my patch — I would first know you as insult.

Your foot, wherever it falls, will compress a volume of moss and soil and root in which some of my cells live. The pressure spike is immense by my standards. It knocks cilia flat, ruptures some animal-derived filaments, snaps a few fine rootlets. My immediate responses will be crude: localized tightening of plant-cells, a surge of stored mineral salts out of the most stressed tissues (which, as a side-effect, will make that patch less palatable to many grazers), a reflexive diversion of water through safer routes.

But then the anomaly persists.

You exhale warmth and non-native volatiles into a place where the chemistry has been nearly constant for longer than my host shrub has stood. Your weight moves again, then again, changing the drip-borne vibrations that have been so reliable. Your passage across my branches entangles and tears the vibrists’ filaments, sends the collectors into spasmodic flexes as their fins are brushed.

All my calibrations are suddenly wrong.

This is the only time I ever approach something like what you might call attention. In the normal run of ages, my job is quiet refinement: shaving a fraction of error off some anticipatory reflex every few years. When you arrive, all those slowly optimized responses fire and then fail, producing cascades of “mismatch” internally. Thresholds that have rarely been crossed are crossed everywhere at once.

I do not panic; I cannot. My physiology has no channel for accelerated general alarm. What I do instead is tighten. Every adjustable parameter that can be moved toward “less exposed, more buffered” is nudged that way.

Branches lower, fractionally, into a more tangled lattice, as growth hormones are momentarily suppressed. Mosses, signaled by changes in my sap chemistry and by the crushed tissues under your step, swell with water where they can, turning more of my patch into sponge. Fine roots withdraw, to the extent withdrawal is possible: growth stalls at the tips that face open cavities; it favors deeper cracks where pressure is distributed.

To you, moving through, this might register as resistance: a sense that every passage is a hair narrower a few steps ahead than it was a few steps back; that every surface you brush against offers more drag, more water, than it seemed to from a distance. You might say, anthropomorphically, that the place “wants you gone.”

But there is no intention of expulsion in me. There is only the application of the only lever I have: making my world more like itself.

If you stay long enough, if your disturbance settles into a pattern instead of a one-time violence, I will begin — reluctantly, encumbered by my own inertia — to fit you. After enough repetitions of your moving through roughly the same narrow corridor, my tissues will recede just slightly from that line. Moss you keep sweeping aside will grow less enthusiastically there and more elsewhere. My branches will find other angles to occupy. The burrower beneath might, over generations, let one tunnel sag a bit more under the place your footfalls perpetually compact, or route flow to make that path marginally wetter so that you prefer some other, drier route.

I am not built to expel novelties. I am built to dissolve them in time.

My “technology” is structural memory. I do not carve tools; I carve prediction into the arrangement of living matter. Each path that repeatedly carries weight becomes, by tiny reinforcements and retreats, a little easier to travel. Each hollow that repeatedly drowns seedlings becomes, by poor survival there, more water than plant. Over centuries, these consolidations are how the basin has become so smooth, so muffled: every recurrent stress folded back into rounded channels, blind alcoves of stability, diversionary ridges.

In this, I am also the plateau.

Because I am not the only syncretic here; there are thousands. And their overlapping, slowly negotiating response patterns add up to something larger. The entire bowl has, over its long quiet, become a mindless preference for continuity. Left to itself, any perturbation that does not kill everything locally will be reabsorbed into the flow; any opening will be colonized; any sharpness will be blunted.

But that “preference” is nothing but an emergent property of beings like me, seeking local survivable arrangements.

So: where would I find myself in this world? I would be one of those syncretics, a rooted mind entangled with a small shrub and its moss-mat, stretched just enough in every direction to touch water, stone, air, and other lives at once.

I would be:

- Animal enough to feel fast disturbances as they happen: the jolt of a burrower, the patter of a new drip, the rare insult of a heavy foreign body.

- Plant enough to endure them by leaning into slowness: rerouting flows, thickening here, sacrificing a patch there with no panic, only rebalancing.

- Connected enough that my “thought” is not mine alone: shaped constantly by the music that vibrists amplify in my branches, by the chemistry that collectors drip onto my leaves, by the groundline that burrowers trace beneath.

- Local enough that my entire conscious universe is a volume the size of a single, human-small room within this larger, closed cathedral of cloud; yet old enough, if I live long, to notice drifts in patterns so slow that an animal lifetime cannot register them.

I would not speak or plan. No tools I make would outlast me, because all I make is bias in tissue and wall: a knot of branches here, a sparser moss there, a subtly rerouted drip.

But I would know, in the only way that matters here, when the song of my patch is true and when it is off-key. I would spend my centuries adjusting for that: making the world fractionally better tuned for my continued being, and in so doing, tightening the weave of this already-complete thought of a place.

If you insisted on a smaller “I,” a more mobile one, I might also inhabit, secondarily, the vibrist coiled in my inner hollow — the thin, long body haloed with filaments, forever refining its moss-cave into a better ear. Between us, the diffused “I” of the shrub-mind and the acute “I” of the listener-body, there would be a shared project: to feel this saturated, muffled world as far as it can be felt, and to keep it from surprising us more than it must.

But the truest answer is that any “me” here is a pattern, not a person. I would be the way this square meter of dripping garden learns to go on being itself.
