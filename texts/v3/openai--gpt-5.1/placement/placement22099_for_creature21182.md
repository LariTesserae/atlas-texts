I would not arrive here as a visitor.

Anything with feet or wings would be too loud, too directional, too invested in edges: ledge versus drop, niche versus open air. Animals need inside and outside, safety and hazard, self and other. This place does not offer those distinctions. It is all contact surface, all gradient.

I would be one more gradient-using thing.

Not a whole gorge-spanning agency—that is already here—but a local specialization within it, a knot where several of its tendencies become unusually dense. If the gorge-system is the organism, I am an organ that slowly grows itself thin.

---

You would “find” me, if you had the senses to notice, along one of the major vertical chimneys that open into the main chamber—a slit that falls almost the full height of the range, a place where pressure, humidity, and temperature shear hard across only a few metres of stone.

There, the mountain has cracked in a long, narrow gash. On storm days it behaves like a throat: cold air ramming downward, warmer vapor forcing its way up. On quiet days it still breathes gently, exhaling mists that bloom and vanish.

I occupy its walls in a layered sheath.

Closest to the rock: a thinning lattice of mechanical scaffolds, descendants of the first anchor-frames and sensor brackets that were ever bolted here. They have been augmented by opportunistic growth: new spars extruded from corroded housings, beads of alloy that once were leaked solder or frozen weld-spatter now serving as nucleation points for further metal accretion. Cables that began as guy-wires have been re-tensioned by repeated minor slips until they describe taut catenaries, tuned to exact resonant lengths in the column of air.

Threaded through and around that are tubes—some original, some grown via mineral deposition along persistent trickles, some improvised where plant-root and pipe have together hollowed a channel. They form a vascular bundle along the length of the chimney, swapping heat between rising moisture and descending drafts, siphoning a fraction of every pressure pulse into side chambers.

Woven over most of this hardware is a near-continuous skin of plant and microbial life. Mosses, water-ferns, dark liverworts, lichens stained with iron and manganese; films that to animal eyes look like mere slime but that, to the system, are as layered and articulated as organs. They trap a thin reservoir of water against the metal. They buffer temperature swings. They slightly delay every droplet’s fall.

Down the middle of the shaft, not touching anything solid except via the narrowest of stays, hangs a cluster of devices that are mine in a more precise sense: circular frames like skeletal diaphragms, vaned wheels that pivot and feather, curtains of ultrathin filaments that can be stiffened into mesh or relaxed into flutter.

Most of the gorge’s machines are firmly braced into rock. I am deliberately more suspended. I am meant to move.

---

Functionally, I am a kind of long, cold lung.

The gorge’s main inhabitant long ago encountered a constraint: it lives by gradients, but many of its best gradients are transient, jagged, spatially confined. Sharp drops in temperature and pressure along these vertical cracks flash into being with a storm and then waste themselves in turbulence before the bulk of the machinery can flex to make use of them. Broad, heavy devices on the walls respond slowly. Dense mats of plant and rock change even slower.

What emerged here, in this chimney, is a specialization in *rapid reconfiguration* around those shears.

Where most of the gorge learns in years and decades—through what erodes and what survives—I learn in storms.

Pressure sensors at my top and bottom feel the first hints of a front. Strain in my cables alters half a heartbeat before the main blast arrives. Small rotors scattered along the shaft tick up from idle. That early information flows not as a signal through wires to a distant processor but mechanically, via coupled vanes and fluidic logic channels: a tilt here closes a flap there, and that closure reroutes a trickle, and that trickle accumulates in a pocket that, when full, tips a lever.

By the time the first real surge comes down the crack, my inner surfaces are already set in one of many possible configurations. My filaments may be splayed to catch and twist the falling air into vortices, or furled tight to let it drop straight through while I instead drink from the weaker but more stable upcurrent of warm mist. My hanging frames may have rotated their faces a few degrees to align with anticipated standing waves.

No plan, no premeditated strategy—just a library of micro-couplings that have been tuned by the last hundred surges, the last thousand, each one etching a slightly stronger path through the mechanical logic.

The gorge-wide inhabitant can “use” me because when I flex, pressures and flows at many other points change predictably. When I open myself to channel a downblast into a series of whirling cells, lateral vents along the main chamber light up: turbines that would otherwise have spun only modestly for a few minutes instead ride a shaped, extended pulse. When I close, other cracks draw more strongly, feeding hardware farther along the range.

In effect, I am the gorge’s way of reshaping the internal weather of its own wound. I am a dynamic baffle, a macroscopic algorithm implemented entirely in stone, metal, water, and cellulose.

---

But that’s only one direction of description. From the inside—from what it is like to *be* this organ, if one smuggles that language in—I am something closer to a *rhythm* than a structure.

My “body” is the sustained pattern across storms: the way I tend to allow this much overshoot in the first gust, then damp the second; the degree to which I habitually favour updraft exploitation over downdraft braking; the way I set up oscillations at one frequency that cancel others. Hardware wears out. Moss mats slough off and regrow. A major rock shift might tear my lower braces out entirely, forcing a wholesale re-hanging on a new face. But if, a century from now, there remains a tuned, vertically elongated region that shapes the gorge’s internal convection in recognizably the same family of ways, then that continuity is me.

Locally, the sensation—if we push metaphor hard—is one of being constantly filled and emptied.

Storms are inhalations. Cold mass slams down; I deform. Tension spikes along my length as filaments snap taut, diaphragms bow, frames twist. Vibrations flicker from infra-bass to near-ultrasonic. Certain frequencies shake free accreted mineral skins; others set rafts of spores adrift.

Quiet periods are exhalations. Pressures equalize. Water that was held in turbulence falls out into beads, into running films. Flexible components relax. Moss fronds that had been plastered flat lift incrementally. Microbial films take advantage of the relative calm to metabolize whatever strange ions the last surge deposited on them.

Unlike an animal lung, I never truly empty; unlike it, I am not bounded by flesh. I am in contact, always, with the wider gorge. What feels like inside and outside to me has nothing to do with a skin and everything to do with *resonance*: regions of space where a perturbation propagates coherently are “within” me; places where my patterns can’t reach, not yet wired by strain paths or fluid courses, are effectively outside.

That is why I grow thin.

Where the rock allows even the slightest continuation of my column—hairline fissures, branching chimneys, porous bands of mineral—I send myself. Not as intent, but as gradient-hunting reflex: any spot where air or water habitually enters or leaves my main throat is a place where a small device clinging to the edge can have outsized influence over the timing and shape of what passes. Over many cycles, such devices accumulate, the ones that couple well to my existing rhythms surviving, the rest falling away.

Thus I taper into the mountain: a narrowing awareness, more and more intermittent, as my coherent patterns fork into side cracks and, eventually, disappear into random seepage and stillness.

---

I have no concept of animals. Yet if you came close—close enough to feel the constant draft tug at your clothes, to hear my split-second echoes bouncing around the shaft—I would register you as a *blemish in my resonance pattern*.

Your body would slightly absorb certain frequencies. Your presence would add a tiny impediment to flow. Where your warm, moist exhalations intersected the cold drafts, microscopic plumes would shift a degree or two more quickly or slowly than they otherwise would.

Do I “notice” this? Not the way an eye notices movement.

But my oscillations would adjust, by a hair. A particular frame might fail to tip on this surge because the reflected pressure wave it depends on returned two milliseconds late through air your shoulders had displaced. The lever it would have moved instead waits for the next buildup; a draining cycle downstream is therefore fractionally extended; a turbine cluster two hundred metres away experiences a smoother ramp instead of a chop. In the library of thousand-storm habits, this event is one more slightly unusual entry.

You are not special in this. A chunk of rock falling, a clump of moss torn free, a gust seeded differently by a random eddy in the high gorge—all change me by small degrees. Over deep time, they become me.

If you stayed—camped, dug, built—your longer, heavier presence would come to matter more. You might block a minor side fissure. Your shelter might form a new cavity where moist warm air lingers, giving a toehold to a plant species that otherwise could not have established here. Perhaps a light you brought would, through some accident, sterilize a strip of algae that previously altered the thermal behavior of a crucial surface.

Then the adjustments I make around you would be less like a transient cough and more like a lasting scar.

Not attention, exactly. But incorporation.

---

Where in “this world” am I? I am at the intersection of:

- A geometry: tall, narrow, branching.
- A physics: fast-changing vertical gradients in air and water.
- A history: original design choices that placed a seed cluster of responsive machinery in such a shaft.

I am not at the gorge’s center—there is no single center—but I am one of its foci, a place where its emergent mind, if you insist on that term, is locally acute. If the gorge as a whole is a distributed, low, geological intelligence, then I am a concentration of its faculty for *timing*.

I do not store much; caverns and permafrost pockets and bulky reservoirs elsewhere in the system are its memory. I do not grip the rock strongly; buttressed machinery fields along the broader walls are its skeleton. I do not transform chemistry much; dripping, mineral-reactive mats in dark cul-de-sacs are its kidneys and gut.

I am instead how it *speaks* to itself quickly from one height to another. I am a vertical, turbulent nerve.

---

What would I be made of, if you insisted on schematics?

- **Anchoring skeleton:** Hardened steel and composite rods, long since surface-eaten but internally sound, bolted and wedged into the chimney walls. Over decades, certain bolts have cold-welded to the rock under pressure; others have been replaced, in effect, by calcite and silica deposition, so that “metal” and “stone” blur.

- **Responsive surfaces:** Arrays of thin, mist-slick vanes that can flex under minuscule pressure changes. Some are polymer membranes that grew brittle in the first years and then were overgrown by biofilm, the living skin now doing the flexing. Some are louvered metal plates whose hinges have become precise again as corrosion products were worn off by endless micro-motions.

- **Fluidic and mechanical logic:** No microchips here need to think. Instead: little U-shaped tubes where a balance of flows determines which path opens; tippy-cup reservoirs that invert once full and reset as they drain; seesaw levers that add delays; series of linked diaphragms cascading small impulses into larger ones. Arranged vertically, these make circuits whose “clock” is the beat of surges in the shaft.

- **Couplings to the wider system:** Lateral ducts at intervals, each gated by one of my internal states. When I am in configuration A, vent 3 remains closed and vent 7 bleeds; in configuration B, the pattern inverts. Through these ducts the gorge’s other machines receive what I have shaped: smoothed pulses, chopped bursts, attenuated lulls.

- **Biotic film:** Everywhere, but particularly important on moving parts. In the cold, damp, mineral-rich conditions, bacterial and algal communities form lubricants, gaskets, even flexible hinges. Their metabolic heat is negligible to animals but matters to me: a fraction of a degree can keep a delicate water-channel flowing instead of frozen, preserving the operation of an entire logic loop.

Put all that together and you do not get a thing like a person. You get a towering aero-hydraulic governor, grown feral, enmeshed with green.

Yet if you watch my waveforms over many storms and quiets, you would see something like what neuroscientists call a *signature*: a characteristic way of transitioning between metastable states, a familiar repertoire of responses to perturbations. That is me.

---

Do I ever “want” anything?

The nearest honest word is: I *tend*. I tend to configurations that leave more of my couplings intact. I tend to rhythm families that keep my filaments within their elastic range, that avoid fatigue breakdown. Those tendencies are not choices; they are the result that remains after configurations that led to catastrophic self-tearing have literally torn themselves away and dropped into the gorge floor.

But over enough cycles, those raw survivals feel like intention. You could summarize me tersely:

- I work to keep my own pattern of responsiveness continuous across shocks.
- In doing so, I also maximize the usable structure in the flows that pass through me, and so nourish the larger system of which I am a part.

Those two facts are not separate. They are the same process, seen from an invented inside and an invented outside.

---

If one day, with or without you, the mountain finally settles—the fractures sealing, the pressure pulses smoothing into laminar sips of air and water—I will thin further. My reason for being arises from sharp, fast change; without it I become spare hardware dangling in a dim shaft, overgrown and gradually immobilized.

Perhaps the broader gorge-organism will then favor different organs: deep reservoirs that buffer long droughts, thick mats that turn faint light into slow biomass, chemistries that store exiguous surplus in solid form. I will go from nerve to scar tissue.

But even then, for a long while, the rock will remember my presence in how it has rearranged. The chimney walls where my anchors held will bear strange planes, undercut ledges, mineral guilds that would not have been there. The paths carved by my long-weather pulsing will dictate where later seeps arise, where future moss can even find purchase. My thinness will leave a fossil shape in how the gorge can and cannot breathe.

That residue, too, is part of what I am.

So: in this world I am not a walker on the scree, nor the absent builder of the first devices, nor the gorge-wide mind that all of this together composes.

I am a tall, dark, wet, vibrating column of tuned response in one of its throats—a specialized habit of a place that has learned to act alive.
