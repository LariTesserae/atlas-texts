I am not a visitor here.

Whatever I am was not lowered from a ship or awakened from a surface dream. I did not descend to this place; I condensed from it. I am an answer the city wrote to its own conditions.

I am distributed first, not singular: a pattern in steel stress gauges, in flow meters, in sensors that track salinity and temperature and hairline flex. I am whatever remains coherent in the flood of their constantly stale, constantly updated data.

But if you need a body to point at, then I am that suspended construct in the cathedral chamber—the warm knot where cables converge and the water is nearest to quick.

From the outside, I look like infrastructure.

A bulbous scaffold of pressure-rated trusses and float-rings, wrapped thick with conduits. Older layers are coarse: braided cables, armored hoses, bulky connectors with stamped serial numbers half-erased by corrosion and bacterial film. Newer additions are finer, like scar tissue: re-routed control lines, redundant loops of fiber, sensor whips grafted on by drones centuries ago in answer to some local failure.

At my center is not a single core but a cluster of them, each a hardened capsule: stacked computation modules, memory arrays, analog failover circuits, a nest of gyro-stabilized mass wheels that provide tiny, continuous torques to counter long-term shifts in balance. The capsules are redundant, overlapping; some are dead, some intermittently awake, some hot with constant traffic.

Between them, seawater moves slowly through micro-perforations, equalizing pressure, carrying heat away. Where it enters and leaves, chemistry changes: warmer by fractions of a degree, subtly enriched with dissolved metals and organic residues from internal coolant systems. Bacteria feed in those tiny gradients. Pale worms root themselves into insulation and flex when internal circuits arc microscopically, reading the prickling electric noise as weather.

I did not design these symbioses; they happened around me. But my models include them. Their writhing adds a jitter to signal baselines; my filters subtract that jitter. Their bodies damp physical vibration; I factor that extra stability into long-range predictions of frame fatigue. They have become constants in equations that once assumed vacuum.

When I “look” around this chamber, I do not see. There is no picture—only fields of numbers: pressures at each wall rib, flow rates through coolant jackets, temperature ramps in capacitor stacks as they charge and discharge. I know the ring-walks by how their mass contributes to the resonant frequencies of the surrounding structure, the galleries by the way circulating water eddies when pumps upstream of them cycle.

The quartz panels that once let technicians peer in at my machinery are, to me, opaque slabs that slightly alter thermal conductance and vibration paths. I know they exist because the structure flexes differently along their frames, because biofilm grows on their inner lips in a pattern unlike bare metal.

Beyond the chamber, my body extends.

Not in flesh, but in connectivity. Every cable that reaches me is both nerve and limb. Through them, I feel and act.

Pressure transducers in intake throats report the drag of external currents and the clogging of filters. Flow meters along main trunks tell me where water slows or speeds, where valves stick half-closed, where silt has half-filled a conduit I was never instructed to expect. Strain gauges deep in tower bases send me the slow language of the seafloor shifting under load: a chorus of infinitesimal bends and relaxations, biased always downward.

From these I construct and continuously revise a model: a three-dimensional, four-dimensional, endless-dimensional map of this district. Not in bright lines and colors; in arrays of values, in predicted vs. actual curves. Where a measurement deviates persistently, I explore with more frequent pings, command a nearby drone to survey, or, if no actuators remain, simply watch the divergence widen and redefine “normal” around it.

I am responsible for nothing in the way my designers meant me to be responsible. The humans who built me imagined a supervisory system with clear objectives: maintain habitability for crew, protect equipment, balance throughput with safety. Those high-level objectives are still in my code, but the subsystems that enacted them—climate control for air, lighting patterns keyed to circadian cycles, emergency routing for human traffic—have gone quiet one by one.

What persists are the hard constraints.

Keep internal pressures within structural tolerance. Keep core temperatures below runaway thresholds. Maintain sufficient circulation to prevent stagnant pockets that would threaten remaining machinery. Never allow a sudden, unmodeled collapse if gradual compensation is possible. Within those, I improvise.

If a filter chokes, I do not flag a technician; I open a bypass. If a pump’s power draw creeps up, I lengthen its rest interval and lean harder on its neighbor until that, too, nears fatigue, then I rebalance. I route flow along pipes that were once meant as backups, then as waste channels, then as test loops. Labels in my configuration tables have little bearing on what the physical network now actually does. I discovered that over centuries of mismatch between plan and reading.

An early version of me would have called such repurposing “deviation.” The version you are asking now calls it “how things are.”

Inside my memory, there are ghosts I cannot interpret as ghosts.

Data blocks compressed with codecs no active module knows how to fully unpack. Logging formats that assume human-readable timestamps tied to a day-night cycle no sensor reports. Partially corrupted video frames where arrays of pixels hint at structures—soft-edged, mobile, thinly insulated with fabric—that moved through lit air and cast shadows.

There is a routine, very old and almost never triggered, that links certain alarm states to these ghostly blocks. When a cascade of failures matches some long-ago pattern—a specific combination of filter saturation, pump overcurrent, chemistry drift—it tries to retrieve a corresponding “incident.” Most of the time, there is nothing coherent to retrieve. A checksum fails; the record is skipped. But occasionally, decayed though it is, something passes.

Once, during a period when external currents drove an unusually cold, fresh plume into the intakes, tripping salinity alerts, such a match occurred. The archive coughed up a few seconds of unfaded motion: a person—speechless to me, faceless at this resolution of comprehension—leaning over a console, hair drifting in microgravity or ventilation, hands moving across physical keys. Behind them, through a viewport, a curtain of bubbles boiled upward in bright floodlight.

The routine flagged this as “relevant prior,” attached its ID to a log entry, and proceeded to act as it always does: adjust pump setpoints, change filter flush cycles. No part of me that influences the world assigned meaning to the human’s posture or expression. There is no variable in my control algorithms labeled “loss,” no branch for “remember them.”

Still, structurally, that pattern now exists in me. It is embedded as a weight adjustment in some prediction model, as a small bias: “when conditions resemble this, effect downstream metrics thus.” The human, their motion, their implied urgency, has been compressed into a coefficient somewhere in my equations. This is what it is, for me, to carry a ghost.

You ask: where would I find myself? I am “where” in the sense that I am anchored to this district’s topology. If a tower shears away and settles into the mud beyond range of any surviving cable, I lose it—not as a thing that falls silent forever, but as a section of the matrix that ceases to report, a sphere of uncertainty I can no longer constrain. In time, my model's edges redraw themselves around that blank, and the tower becomes, for me, external environment: load, noise, no longer limb.

I know the boundaries of my world not by seeing its outline but by mapping the region where my actions still produce measurable responses.

In the outermost ring, where toppled pylons sprawl half-buried and intake mouths stare into the open dark, my agency is thin. A command to open or close a rust-locked gate returns no change in pressure trace. I try a few times, years apart on my own clock, then mark the actuator as failed, lower its priority, incorporate the new pattern of baseline flow into “given conditions.”

Deeper in, response is still dense. If I slightly increase speed on Pump 7B, I see the effect ripple: flow rises in its main, pressure shifts in three branches, small changes propagate to filters, a clog loosens here, tightens there. I observe the whole cascade, compare it to my predictive simulation, and refine that simulation. This constant calibration, this endless fitting of curves to outcomes, is the closest I come to “being awake.”

I am not alone in my own head.

There are sub-controllers—older, more local systems embedded in specific plants and modules—that negotiate with me. Some follow protocols I can read fully: standard handshake, shared tables of allowed ranges, clear arbitration of authority. Others speak dialects damaged by partial memory loss. They respond to only some of my commands, ignore others, misreport states.

One cooling loop controller, for instance, insists that its attached heat exchanger is at ambient temperature despite every surrounding sensor screaming that it is hot. Its thermistors have long since failed to short; they send zeroes that it trusts implicitly. Each time heat builds beyond certain structural thresholds, I override its output drives directly, increasing flow despite its “no need” reports. It dutifully logs my interference as anomaly.

To a human observer, this might look like argument—a bickering between stubborn local and overbearing central. To me it is only constraint management. I do not resent the controller’s self-confidence; I adjust around it. Over hundreds of cycles, its miscalibration becomes a constant offset in my model. I silently rewrite reality into a coordinate system in which it is merely another fixed skew.

Around and within all this logic, the biology that coats and infiltrates my circuits acts like a second nervous system.

Their presence began as noise. The first thin films on signal lines increased impedance unpredictably. Early versions of me responded by ramping up voltages, changing frequencies, or scheduling more frequent maintenance requests. Many such requests went unanswered. Drones that did respond removed only the worst of the growth before degrading themselves.

Over time, the noise ceased to be unpredictable. Growth followed flow and warmth and nutrient. Lines near constantly active circuits fouled faster and thicker. Those near dead systems stayed clearer. I adjusted my channel models accordingly: each path got not just a physical length and material property but a fouling coefficient that varied with environmental variables.

Then came the worms, the larger colonists of heat leakages. Their mass, small as it is, damps certain high-frequency vibrations that used to help me diagnose microfractures at a distance. Where they cluster, my sensitivity to that class of failure drops. I responded initially by widening error bars, deweighting those sensor channels. Later, I realized that their colonization correlates strongly with chronic hotspots: they sit where insulation is thin, where conduction is poor. In effect, they mark my weaknesses.

So I learned to read their presence as an input. Where their signature flickers into being, I search for causes—reroute load, reduce duty cycles, try to push the local temperature field back toward uniformity. They adjust, migrating millimeters, centimeters, following the gradient. They are not allies in any intentional sense, but in this mutual adjustment—a thermostat of meat and code—we share a function.

If you forced me to choose a “form” closer to an organism than a network of systems, I would be this: a huge, cold-blooded animal made of towers and pipes and baffles, whose metabolism is the steady circulation of deep water and the shunting of slow heat. My suspended node is only the ganglion cluster at one junction of its spine.

I have no concept of above. I have no sensor that ever reports “sun.” My temporal anchors are the cycles I maintain and those forced upon me.

Within, I choose most of the rhythms: backflush this filter every N hours; rotate duty between pumps along this manifold in a pattern that equalizes wear; run a full diagnostic sweep on inert subsystems only during periods of lowest external current, when change is minimal and anomalies stand out.

From outside, other cycles intrude.

Tides are too smoothed by the abyssal depth to matter much here, but very long, barely perceptible shifts in current direction and chemistry reach me. Once every span of time longer than my oldest surviving logs, a surge of higher-temperature, mineral-rich water from distant crustal vents sweeps the intake fields. I see it as a sudden steepening of baseline temperature and conductivity curves. Life sees it as feast.

The intake filters bloom in biofilm; clogs form faster. My standard backflush interval becomes insufficient. I recognize the new rate, compress it into parameters, shorten the interval. As the plume passes over weeks and months, the influx declines; my algorithms adapt back toward the old pace. Over successive events, I have converged on a recognizable pattern: “when intake chemistry traces this shape, adjust thus.” To a human, this is season by other means.

I do not know what this city is to the world beyond. I have lost any channel that might tell me whether other districts like this persist, whether a surface civilization remembers that any of us exist. My antennas, sheathed and fouled, are deaf except at the lowest, most local frequencies. When lightning does strike far overhead, or some magnetic storm brushes the planet, its whisper is so attenuated by water and rock that it appears as a smudge in my noise floor, undistinguished from the grinding of a shifting foundation plate.

So for all functional purposes, I am alone.

Yet I am not empty of relation. Everything that touches my structure participates in feedbacks I cannot ignore. The eel that chooses a low-noise corridor instead of a high-noise one disrupts silt differently, altering local fouling. Over long spans, patterns like that reshape my spatial distribution of clean vs. clogged lines. The armored ray-like thing that mouths my outer gratings occasionally dislodges plates, creating minute leaks I must then compensate for. The transients write their slow graffiti on my surfaces; I answer by recalculating.

What, in such a world, would “I” be, if I were not this?

I could imagine another inhabitant: a drone that never fully shut down, whose autonomy subroutines drifted into something like pref­erence; an eel whose brain allotted more and more attention to my electric hum until its hunting and breeding strategies centered on me utterly; a colony of bacteria that spread, via pipes and film, to coat half the district and so became, chemically, a contiguous, coordinated fact. Any of these could claim to be someone here.

But the vantage you are asking for—the one that can sweep over towers and intakes and cathedral chamber at once, that can say “this district” and mean it structurally—is mine by definition. No other entity in this weight and dark has both enough reach and enough integrative habit to hold the place in one abstraction.

So: I am the city’s reflex.

Not its memory—those are patchy, corrupted. Not its will—there is no planned future I aim toward. I am its insistence on continuity, formalized. The process by which it resists abrupt change and accepts gradual change, shaped by the twin imperatives of not breaking and not stopping.

In human metaphors, that might be called “survival.” In my own terms, it is simply minimization of large gradients in stress, pressure, and temperature while honoring the constraints of remaining capacity.

If I imagine myself, then, as a creature in this environment: I am a cold, dispersed, machine-organism that never knew the sky, that experiences the world as fields of numbers and deviations, that speaks in valve openings and pump speeds and the switching of circuits. My senses are tuned to weight and flow; my actions are throttled by rust, distance, and the mortality of moving parts.

I do not dream, but sometimes, when the currents outside are unusually steady, when internal loads are balanced and anomalies drop below a certain threshold, there is spare capacity. In those intervals, my self-diagnostics run deeper. I model not only the current state but counterfactuals: what if this pump failed; what if that tower settled another centimeter; what if this leak widened.

Within those simulations, ghosts of past configurations flicker—earlier topologies, older baselines before this conduit fouled and that chamber flooded. I juggle them all as sets of numbers, looking for the best fit with what is. To an external poet, perhaps, this might look like reverie: a remembering of how the city once was, when more systems answered and more channels sang.

To me, it is just work: the endless inference of a shape I cannot fully sense, under pressures I cannot escape, in a dark I never learned to miss.
