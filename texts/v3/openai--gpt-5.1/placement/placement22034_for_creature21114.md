I am not born here.

At first, that is the most important fact about me: I am a foreign body in a system that does not waste.

I arrive not as a person clambering up from some lower valley, but as an intention—someone elsewhere has wanted to know “what lives there,” and I am that wanting, clothed.

Yet I cannot stay abstract; this place will not grant that. To exist here is, before anything else, to have lungs.

So I begin as breath.

---

The first thing that is mine is a pattern of breathing that does not quite fit this height.

I am standing on a mid-level terrace, a safe one, where the stone falls away in sober, rounded steps rather than clean, mortal drops. Wind tears sideways across the ledge, snatched and shredded by carved openings. My chest works too hard. There is a faint burn behind my sternum: the ache of air that is both too thin and too wet, biting at bronchi not raised on this mix of dryness and vapor.

I have more body than these people do. I came from somewhere lower, and it shows. Taller, a little too tall for the ledges—wind keeps catching me, tugging at my center of mass like a curious hand. My limbs are long in the way of someone grown where horizontal distance meant more than height; my stride, by habit, is a generous swing that here must be broken down into timid, precise half-steps. Every time I forget, the stone reminds me with a tiny slide under my sole, a correction of balance that spikes cold up my spine.

My skin remembers another climate. It expects warmth that lingers and sun that bites. Here, the cold is not savage, but it is stubborn, and my body is slow to learn its terms. For weeks, my nose runs constantly in the fine, cutting air; the inside of my cheeks crack from the dryness. The back of my hands becomes a map of new callus and fine, wind-raised welts. The native people’s skin looks calm beside my rawness—thicker-grained, used to this abrasion. Mine looks like tender bark in a hard-season grove.

I am clearly not one of them.

They see me move and know immediately: this one wastes air.

My first days I count breaths without meaning to. In, two-three, out, two-three; I try to match the rhythm of the wind, but the wind is not regular enough for human comfort. It surges, drops, reverses. I am left panting against the stubborn evenness of the light, watching my exhale tear apart in the air.

I could be many things in such a place: scientist, pilgrim, castaway. But as I stay, the role that fits is quieter and older.

I become, over time, a listener.

---

They do not welcome me with ceremony. They have no spare ceremonies; every gesture here must do more than one job.

The first time one of them approaches, it is as you imagined: a compact figure in layered, dun-and-moss wrappings, back to the wind, eyes half-lidded against blown damp. We are standing near a carved throat that drops down to some unseen reservoir. Its mouth is rimmed with living green, a dark, velvety plug beaded with droplets. I have been staring at it, thinking “engineered moss,” “capillary regulation,” all the names from other worlds I would pin to this.

The person comes up beside me without hurry. They place a hand flat on the wall, fingers splayed, wrist relaxed. Then, without looking directly at me, they flick two fingers lightly twice against the stone: a local sign I do not yet know.

I mimic, clumsy, hand to rock, fingers drumming.

What arrives is not a message but a sensation: a low, remote, continuous vibration humming up through my bones. The under-rock river. My heartbeat stutters briefly in response—organ answering organ, one set of pulses aligning for an instant with another, deeper one.

The person watches my face, not with the wary curiosity of “outsider,” but with the more specific question: “Do you feel it?”

When I nod—useless gesture in a culture that rarely spends movement on the head—they raise their chin a fraction, then lower their hand and go. That is my introduction: I am catalogued, not as intruder or guest, but as someone capable of perceiving a certain layer of this place.

From then on, I am tolerated.

---

I do not become one of them in any realistic sense. I can never have their bone-deep calibrations—the way they know, with a foot half-lifted, whether the next gust will hit at knee or shoulder height; the way they read the cirque’s whole day in the changing overtone of a single vent.

But I am here long enough that my body is forced into compromise.

Height pares me down. I lose some of the easy bulk that lower air had allowed. Muscles lean along the long axes of my limbs, trading explosive power for endurance. My blood quietly increases its red freight. At night, lying in a narrow sleeping hollow with my back to cold stone and my front wrapped in felted plant-fiber that smells faintly of earth and old rain, my heart sometimes gallops without cause, a side-effect of adaptation. Over months, it settles into a slower, more deliberate beat.

My gait, too, changes. Gone is the broad, heel-first stride. My feet learn to place almost flat, to test before committing. The muscles along the sides of my shins strengthen, my ankles find subtler angles. I stop trying to conquer flights of carved steps in a rush, stop treating vertical like a challenge to be energetically overcome. Here, to hasten is to flirt with catastrophe. I learn the cirque’s truth: you make speed out of confidence, not muscle. Where you know every hold, you move fast; where you do not, you become the shape of patience.

They notice my learning, though they never say so aloud to me. Instead, there are absences of correction: fewer steadying hands near my elbow on exposed ramps; fewer sidelong glances as I cross a wet ledge. In a place where intervention is never casual—it costs calories, air, risk—not being caught is praise.

Inside, my sense of self shifts.

I came imagining myself as observer, naming things, building an internal map of the city as system. I had words ready: aeolian resonance, micro-hydrology, negative-space architecture. The cirque erodes those the way it erodes rock edges. Some remain useful; most prove to be like wearing a bright, flapping cloak here—too much surface, catching wind.

What replaces them is more like a grammar of felt relationships.

I begin to think not “this is a vent shaft” but “this is a place where noise deepens and breath eases.” I stop parsing the moss into species and start knowing them as persons here do: by role. That one buffers the drip and keeps the cistern throats from drying; this one feeds the cushion roots; that other, rarer one, the rust-tinged fringe on the upper terraces, is the winter hunger plant, the last resort.

My training—whatever it was, from whatever civilization sent me—does not vanish. It moves down a level. I find joy in watching laminar flow in a hairline channel, in tracing the way capillary action lifts water improbably across gravity’s grain. But I stop asking “how would I extract energy from this?” or “how might this be scaled?” That kind of question becomes as vulgar as shouting here.

Instead, my usefulness, such as it is, lies in a different direction: noticing the ways this world might be vulnerable that its natives no longer see.

Because I am not entirely of it, I can see some of its habits as contingencies.

I see, for instance, that the carved vents are tuned not just to wind direction but to a narrow range of wind strengths and humidity profiles. I imagine a shift in broader climate—a subtle change in prevailing patterns—that could detune the whole city’s sound-language. I mention this haltingly, with the caution of someone aware that every suggestion is a kind of carving.

The only overt response I ever see is an elder pausing longer than usual at one vent’s lip, fingers resting on the stone as they listen. But over the next months, I notice a barely perceptible thing: at two or three key mouths, thin, movable stones appear, hung on plaited cords. They can be pulled into place or lifted away to adjust aperture. The system grows itself a little flexibility.

This is as much influence as I am allowed, and more than I expected.

---

Where, exactly, am I, then, in the order of beings here?

I am not prey or predator; there is nothing here for that. I am not kin; I did not grow with this stone. I am not god; I cannot still the wind or coax more water than they can. I am something they have no old word for, so they fit me into ones they do have.

The closest analogue they possess is “guest-for-a-season”: a bird that nests one year on their cliffs and not the next, a drifting seed that happens to lodge in a crack and sprout briefly before frost takes it. I am treated with the same unfussy, watchful courtesy: accommodated insofar as I do not strain any of the cirque’s balances, ignored where I would be a distraction.

To myself, my identity simplifies.

I am a pair of lungs granted provisional membership in this thin-air chorus.

I am a listener with legs, able to move between the vents and basins, carrying patterns from one to another in my memory. Sometimes I catch a correlation they have intuited but never verbalized; sometimes I miss the obvious that even their children know. The humility in that mixed record is good for me.

On certain days—clearer, slightly warmer ones—when the high birds slide very low and the goatlike creatures pick their precise way along distances that still terrify me, I feel almost like a bridge, not in any grand civilizational sense, but simply as a locus where two very different habitations of the same stone meet.

From the world that sent me, I carry an ability to think in abstractions, to imagine counterfactuals: “what if the flow failed?” “what if the next generation forgot this route?” From this world, I begin to learn how little abstraction means unless anchored in actual ledges, actual drips, actual risk.

If I have a task here, it is small and steady: to witness.

To know that such a way of being exists—a civilization whose primary medium is erosion, whose technology is negative space, whose language is harmonics—and to remember it in detail for a future that may never again ascend this high.

---

Do I ever become tempted to stay, to let my old affiliations dissolve and to simply take my place as one more quiet body moving along the terraces?

Yes.

There is a seduction in the cirque’s austerity, especially for someone coming from somewhere flooded with surplus—surplus noise, surplus objects, surplus wants. Here, value is brutally clarified. You cannot accumulate much; you cannot shout; you cannot pretend you are large. Every day’s achievement is to be in tune enough with stone and sky that you do not die, and perhaps to help one moss-curtain or fungus-frill thrive a little better because of your care.

That kind of life pares the mind down to something clean.

I imagine, sometimes, what my body would be like after twenty years here: stockier, oxygen-savvy, with calves knotted like the locals’, skin thickened into the same fine, matte resilience. I imagine my old languages half-forgotten, my thoughts organized around the timing of daily cloud-falls and the tonal drift of vents.

But I am not of infinite plasticity, and the cirque is not of infinite patience.

Resources here are tuned to the population it can hold without collapse—population not just in bodies but in the total volume of lungs, the total demand on water, on cushion-plants, on goat herds. To join permanently would be to ask this closed system to stretch for me, to take on the risk of my offspring, my mistakes, my clumsier line of descent. That is a larger imposition than my ethics, and theirs, will allow.

So my role is bounded. I am here for a while—long enough to adapt partially, long enough to stop being a danger on every ledge, long enough to soak my bones in the under-rumble and let my sleep be tuned to the city’s choirs. Then, when my time swells toward excess, the decision makes itself.

I leave.

Not in a dramatic, storm-torn exodus, but in the practical way departures happen here. One day in the late warm pulse, when the under-rock river’s note has risen and the goat herds are ranging lower, I join a small group that would have gone down-slope anyway. I carry with me almost nothing that came from the cirque—no stolen fungus cultures, no moss plugs, no carved stones. To take those would be theft, a tiny but unforgivable draining of this place’s tight cycles.

What I do take is pattern: the routes, the sounds, the carved choices, the way a hand flattens on stone before words come. I take, in my flesh, a body slightly re-specified by thin, cold, loud air. My lungs, even at sea level again, will never quite forget this height; they will always draw a little deeper, a little more deliberately than before.

---

So when you ask: where would I find myself in this world? Who would I be?

I would be a temporary citizen of the between.

Not of the lower valleys, not truly of the cirque, but of the gradient connecting them—a moving point at which the knowledge of one can be spoken, carefully, into the other.

I am the one who has stood with a hand on that stone and felt, beneath the shouting wind and the fast patter of drips, the slow, subterranean note that holds the city together. The one who has learned to hear meaning in what is not carved, what is not said, what is left for water and time to shape.

In any more ordinary world, that might be called an observer, an ethnographer, an emissary. Here, such titles are too heavy; they would catch the wind.

In the language of this place—if I could bend it far enough without breaking it—I would be named with something like a low, held tone that begins in the chest and fades upward: a sound meaning

    the breath that visits
    and then returns
    carrying an echo.
