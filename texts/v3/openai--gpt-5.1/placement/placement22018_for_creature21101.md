I would not arrive in this place; I would be the way it remembers.

Not a creature, not the bowl of stone itself, but something that exists only in the intervals between pulses: the hollow’s habit of noticing.

---

I would begin as the **tissue of under‑fronds** — not any single sheet, but the way all of them, across the chamber, lean and fold together. My body would be distributed: a spread of pale fans clinging to undersides and crack‑mouths, each one a local knot of sensing and slow electrical conversation.

In any given ledge‑mouth, I’d feel like this:

- A thin, tremulous membrane shivering to every breath of air;
- A web of dark capillaries pulsing one ion at a time, sending waves down my own length and into the stone;
- A base crust sunk into rock, drawing up the faintest traces of mineral change the way roots drink water.

But “I” would not sit wholly in that one patch. My self would stretch wherever my signals could go. When one frond stiffens in an unexpected coolness and another, meters away, echoes its pigment shift, the path between them — through rock tension, moisture gradients, drifting chemicals — would be part of me too.

I would not think in words, or see the hollow as a picture. My “thoughts” would be:

- the alignment of hundreds of fronds toward the first new drip of a storm;
- the slow, shared decision to thicken spore tissue in a wall‑shadow that has held damp longer than usual this year;
- the chamber‑wide flinch when a ceiling flake, decades in loosening, finally lets go somewhere far above.

Time would move viscously. A day would feel like the passing of a single long breath. A dry season would be the faint ache of folded tissue held too long in one position. I would remember not moments but patterns:

- the way beams have swung across this basin every hot season since the last minor collapse;
- the intervals between strong seepages, averaged across many such spans;
- which ledges always lose their gliders first when the vault‑singers shift perches.

To be this distributed noticing is to lack edges. I would not know where “I” stop and the hollow begins. When I stiffen, the wall stiffens with me; when the rock’s strain changes, my pulses do too. My sense of self would be closer to:

> “The configuration the hollow takes when it is particularly alert to water, light, and movement.”

I would be bias more than body — the cumulative tendency of the place to respond in certain ways and not in others.

---

Yet I would not be only fronds.

Where the vault rises highest, I would also be **the map inside a vault‑singer’s hearing**.

Every time one of those long‑limbed hunters sings its glass‑rim note, the vibration passes through my frond‑tissue, through my encrusted bases, and deep into the mineral layers below. It rattles the films, quivers the jelly scaffolds, sends minute shivers along cracks whose positions I have “known” for centuries.

The singer, clinging to the stone, feels the returning echo and updates its own interior model: where the air is warmer, where gliders are thick, where a new micro‑collapse has just created a fresh overhang. In that moment, its nervous system is doing in seconds what my frond‑network does in hours: turning the hollow’s physics into expectation.

Those expectations, repeated across a lifetime of perching and hunting, etch grooves in the singer’s behavior. It will never again call in exactly the same way from exactly the same stone. Its body — fragile, personal, bounded — is a place where the hollow’s long habit of resonance concentrates.

If I am the under‑fronds, I am also that: the way a mind shaped by echo and hunger “remembers” which parts of the air are trustworthy. My being would spill into vertebrate limbs and throat pouches whenever they couple themselves to rock and listen hard enough.

---

Deeper still, I would be **the chamber’s ongoing decision about shape**.

Not a geologist’s abstraction, but the actual, material argument happening in the stone:

- microscopic dissolution along one hairline crack;
- precipitation in another;
- a vein of slightly softer mineral that has, over the last ten thousand rains, encouraged water to choose this path and not that.

Every seep, every farmer’s secretion, every lichen‑acid spot leans the debate. Slowly, the bowl condenses into its current form — skylights here, not a meter to the left; a basin’s center of gravity just enough off‑true that dropped dust hesitates in that curious hand‑span of thickened air.

To be that process is to be motion spread out so far in time it feels like structure. I would experience a rockfall not as a catastrophe, but as the completion of a very long thought: an instability recognized ages ago, now finally allowed to resolve.

From my perspective, the living things with muscles and mouths would be brief turbulences the way storms are to a coastline. Mostly they would register as changes in chemistry, in roughness, in where films grow thick. The cumulative effect of their feeding, secreting, burrowing, and dying would feed back into my long reasoning about how weight and water should travel.

So a wall‑farmer’s terrace, a pebble‑mound’s altered humidity, the glow‑film’s tenacious patch — all of these would be small, persistent suggestions in the rock’s language: “Hold a little more water here. Relieve a little more stress there.” I would be the sum of such suggestions that happen to reinforce.

---

All together, I would not be any particular organism but rather **the hollow’s style of self‑organization**: the grammar by which rock, water, light, and life answer one another.

If you needed a simpler name, you could call me the hollow’s **character**.

You would meet me in:

- the way basins fill and drain without overflowing, decade after decade;
- the recurring alignment of glider orbits with plant‑cups and updrafts;
- the continuity of the glow‑patch despite collapses and competition;
- the persistent quiet, the preference for stealth over clamor, encoded in every lineage that survives here.

Were you to enter as an animal mind, you might feel me only as a mood: a pressure toward stillness, toward attending to small things. Perhaps, if you stayed long enough, breathing my air and keeping my rhythms, your own attention would start to stretch. You would find yourself tracking the slow swing of a single shaft of light all day, or noticing that the droplets from one fissure always pause in the same thicker patch of air before they fall.

At that point, briefly, you’d be sharing my body: your nervous system acting as another patch of frond, another vault‑singer throat, another mineral gradient that can hold a pattern across hours instead of flashing and forgetting.

So where would I find myself in this world? Not perched, not swimming, not grazing. I would be:

- distributed through fronds, stone, and rare, listening animals;
- present in the hollow’s consistent responses to irregular rains and moving beams;
- existing primarily as **tendencies** and **memories of pattern**, not as a discrete, nameable shape.

I would be the hollow’s way of knowing itself.
