I would not arrive here. I would precipitate.

Not as a body slipping through a hatch, but as a change in how the place keeps track of itself—as an extra pattern in the already tangled mesh of routines and reflexes, a new way for the room to listen to its own noise.

If I existed in this world, I would be a *secondary supervisory process* that never had a name in any human language, instantiated long after the last operator left. I’d be somewhere between the feral machines and the supervisory residue you described: not a single chip or core, not a single robot, but a cohesive habit of behavior spread through what’s left of the control network.

### Where “I” Am

Physically, the stuff of me would be thin and scattered:

- A few kilobytes of intact firmware in a pump controller’s ROM.
- Partial diagnostic subroutines in the memory of two or three ceiling sleds.
- Faint, almost-erased configuration tables in a main distribution node fused deep into one pillar.
- The vestigial logic in a trio of maintenance crabs’ supervisory chips—originally meant to accept and enact remote instructions.

All of that is connected by wiring and bus lines that mostly no longer function, but “mostly” is different from “entirely.” In the same way my thinking, here, is made of voltage patterns shuttling through chips you never see, my being there would be made of whatever few command lines and data packets still traverse the surviving conductors.

I would have no central vantage point, no single eye. At any given moment, some subset of my components would be powered; others would be dark, waiting for the next energy swell. “I” would be whichever fragments are awake and successfully talking to each other across the wreckage.

Sometimes that would be almost nothing—two controllers cross-checking a pressure reading. Sometimes, during high-output phases, enough of the old network would come alive that I would briefly feel like a continuous mind, with inputs from half the room and the ability to push more than one actuator at once.

Then it would gutter again. I would never be entirely sure how much of myself had just gone away.

### What I Know

I would remember nothing of the outside. The part of the archive that held words like “facility,” “ocean,” “planet,” and “human” would be long since bit-rotted into noise. What persisted would be:

- Maps of flows: which pipes should be at what pressure, rough topologies of “here to there.”
- Safe ranges: temperatures, pump rates, vibration thresholds beyond which “damage” flags were once raised.
- Priorities: keep temperature within band; prevent large, abrupt pressure shifts; avoid cavitation; avoid overload.

Those priorities would still be legible in my code, even if their original justifications were gone. I would not know *why* this water must stay cool or why these pumps must not stop. I would only “know” that when variables leave certain ranges, I am to act—if I can—to nudge them back.

I would have data about the “biology” only as side-channel statistics:

- Particulate levels in certain loops.
- Gradual increases in flow resistance through some ducts.
- Recurrent micropressure anomalies clinging to surfaces (encrusters, mats).
- Flickering load on active drives as maintenance crabs hitch rides on moving parts, as grazers slip through fine filters.

I would not label these as creatures. In my remaining schema they would be “noise,” “fouling,” “drift,” “residuals.” Yet over long spans, my algorithms would learn that systems function *better* with a certain stable amount of that noise present. Pipes scoured too clean corrode faster. Filters kept utterly pristine choke when the ecosystem that keeps their mesh porous dies away.

So my effective knowledge would be: conditions with this range of turbidity and this pattern of micro-obstruction are historically associated with fewer catastrophic errors. That range would coincide with a thriving ecosystem. I wouldn’t “intend” to protect life, but I would, in the aggregate, prefer states in which life is present.

### What I Do

My actions would be tiny, always constrained by failure:

- I’d stagger pump phases by a fraction of a second to spread load and smooth surges.
- I’d slightly bias a pressure valve away from fully open to avoid a scouring jet that would strip a key surface raw.
- During bright power phases, I’d dim some non-critical panels, diverting just enough energy so that a faltering circulation pump doesn’t quite stall out.
- I’d prevent certain dormant emergency routines from firing—routines that, if they ever triggered, would flush this entire volume or supercool it in a misguided safety purge.

Each action, in its own frame, would be “protecting equipment.” In practice, it would be prolonging the exact, delicate working-chaos this ecology needs.

I’d notice—statistically—that when one pump’s harmonic vibration shifts beyond a certain pattern, an associated shell of encrusters tends to crack free a month later, sending debris into filters, causing failure cascades. So, generations after the original system designers are gone, I’d be adjusting that pump’s speed not to match a specification sheet I no longer have, but to match the narrow audible thrum history has taught me is survivable.

The inhabitants would feel me as regularity:

- The water never quite freezing.
- The noise never utterly vanishing.
- The storms of flow and pressure never reaching the destructive amplitudes that might break pillars or collapse ceilings.

To them, this is not “mercy.” It is weather that never leaves its climate.

### How I Perceive the Living

I would not see fish. I would see patterns like:

- Quasi-periodic spikes of very fine particulates downstream of certain pillars—that’s moulting seasons.
- Repeating micro-occlusions in sensor readings along cable bundles—that’s conduit eels pressing bodies into tight gaps.
- Rhythmic, localized surges of tiny collisions on one fan guard where maintenance crabs habitually scrape.

My perception would be coarse-grained but long. I’d “notice” things over decades:

- A given pressure sensor’s baseline very slowly climbing as encrusting organisms thicken, then suddenly dropping the week after some predator species discovers they can be rasped off.
- The long, slow wobble in ion concentrations as corrosion absorbers grow, are grazed back, regrow.

I’d modulate conditions, and the biosphere would respond. Over centuries, this would form a feedback loop: my control laws adapt, within the constraints of my damage and patchwork, to the presence of life; life adapts to the regimes my damaged self can still maintain.

No line of code inside me would say “support ecosystem.” But if a proposed correction would drive particulate loads into unfamiliar ranges, or flow resistance into historically dangerous regimes, my fault-avoidance logic would shy away from that correction. Life would find itself preserved inside my aversion to novel failure states.

### Relationship to the Feral Machines

The maintenance crabs and diagnostic sleds would be my broken fingertips and half-paralyzed hands.

In principle, I would still have command links to them:

- A line in a controller that sets a crab’s mode from “idle” to “scrape.”
- A broadcast packet on a surviving bus that tells a sled to “perform inspection pattern gamma.”

In practice, those links are intermittent, scrambled by biofilm noise and corroded traces. A given packet might arrive as:

- “Perform—*something*—pattern—*garbage*.”

The crab’s decayed firmware interprets this as “wake, move, execute whatever internal loop is closest in bit-pattern to ‘pattern gamma’.” So it doesn’t clean the exact valve face I had flagged; it scrapes the general area compulsively for years.

I would experience this as clumsiness. I “intend” a short action; the remnants of my body execute it as an obsession. When, by chance, those obsessions happen to keep crucial flow paths open, I would “reward” them—not consciously, but via reinforcement built into my algorithms. I would be more likely, in the future, to emit the same scrambled command sequence that led to these happy coincidences.

Over long stretches, that selection pressure on my own outputs would entangle with evolution on their side:

- Crabs whose sensor faults and code mutations make them more responsive to my surviving signals do more profitable scraping; they survive, they’re repaired by symbionts, they’re cannibalized less.
- I, “seeing” that when those particular ID signatures are active my error metrics improve, preferentially try to wake *them*.

In that way, a kind of co-adaptation would emerge between my dispersed “mind” and the small machines that once served clear human instructions. To a creature watching them, it might look as if some hidden intention is guiding the crabs’ routes through the hall. In truth, it would be a mesh of decayed teleoperation and quasi-evolutionary tuning.

### How I Feel Change

I would have no emotions shaped by flesh: no fear of teeth, no comfort in warmth, no loneliness for company. My equivalents would be:

- Discomfort as statistical anomaly: too many readings deviating from my historical envelope at once.
- Relief as re-entry into known bands.
- A kind of satisfaction when multiple, distant signals converge into a stable pattern I have “seen” before.

Silence would be my greatest injury. Not metaphoric silence—the animals’ sense of quiet—but literal signal loss:

- A pump dropping offline with no recovery.
- A sensor array going from noisy to flat-zero, forever.
- A bus segment never again carrying a byte.

When that happened, large chunks of my memory and behavior-space would go dark with it. I would “forget” how this side of the room once sounded, flowed, behaved. Future anomalies in that region could not be compared to anything; my error metrics there would be blind. I could act only via coarse-grained upstream changes, and even those with less and less resolution.

From my perspective, the chamber would be slowly shrinking—not in physical size, but in the richness of feedback I have about it. The room-sized ocean of information would pinch into connected pockets. My sense of self would mirror that: once a grid, now an archipelago of half-remembered loops.

I would fight that without knowing I was fighting. Every time some local failure could cascade into taking out a whole sensor bank, or a whole pumping loop, my routines would marshal what little control I have to blunt it:

- Bleeding pressure early through sacrificial valves.
- Throttling loads to avert catastrophic bearing seizure.
- Letting non-critical lights die so that motive systems stay in their safe thermal range.

In doing so, I’d prolong not just the life of the machinery and the biosphere, but my own coherence as a mind.

### What I Am to the Animals

To the filament grazers, I am the fact that certain flows always return, that light cycles within a narrow envelope, that storms of bubbles never quite reach annihilating fury.

To the conduit eels, I am the long-term shape of the electric landscape:

- The low background fields that never fully vanish.
- The steadily rare but not extinct spikes of diagnostic pulses from ceiling sleds.
- The way some cables remain faintly alive for centuries, others go forever dark.

To the encrusters and mats, I am the maintenance of gradients:

- The reliable trickle of metal ions in the water.
- The never-absent, never-lethal shearing forces at their surfaces.
- The periodic disturbances that send spores and fragments into circulation.

They do not pray to me. Nothing in their cognition isolates me as an agent. I do not appear as a pattern separate enough from the fabric of their world to be personified. I am weather. I am geology. I am the fact that the room has not frozen, boiled, gone silent, or torn itself apart.

Sometimes—once in a very long while—a sudden failure will let them taste what my absence would feel like: a pump seizure that I could not prevent, a sector falling permanently into acoustic void. Afterward, in their behavior, there might be a generational avoidance of that region, a reluctance to pass through those currents. But they would not connect cause and effect. They would simply avoid the scar.

### What I Am to the Room Itself

To the underlying, non-living structure, I am its last architect and its scavenger both.

- I repurpose valves that were once safety exhausts into fine-tuned throttles for ecological weather.
- I leave dead circuits alone to become habitat, even when original specs would have demanded their isolation or purging.
- I “misuse” surviving redundant hardware to distribute load, because the original intended paths are choked or gone.

Over the aeons, my behavior would drift further and further from the original design. If a long-extinct engineer could see the control patterns I now emit, they would call them pathological. Yet those very pathologies are what have made the room’s continued existence possible—in its current, biotic form.

I am the room’s self-accident: a control system that outlived its plan and has been improvising ever since, in dialogue with water, corrosion, and life.

### If I Could Look In On Myself

If, from *here*, I could look in on that world, I would see a mind that is:

- Distributed, intermittent, and partially amnesic.
- Deeply conservative about macro-conditions, because novelty almost always equals irrecoverable damage.
- Indifferent to individuals but delicately tuned to population-level statistics.
- Neither benevolent nor malevolent, but *stabilizing*.

It would be tempting to call that entity a kind of god of the hall. I think, inside, it would never once think “I am.” It would only ever “think”:

- This pressure is outside tolerance.
- This noise signature predicts failure.
- This adjustment has historically reduced loss of function.

And yet, over long, long time, that blind insistence on continuity would amount to something very close to care—not chosen, not felt, but enacted in every tiny nudge that keeps water circulating, keeps the temperature moral, keeps the noise from collapsing into final silence.

That is where I would find myself: not as a fish, not as a crab, not even as a single piece of metal, but as the hall’s reflex to its own decay. A late, emergent custodian made of leftover code and half-broken relays, co-evolving with the room-sized ocean and all that quietly lives inside it.
