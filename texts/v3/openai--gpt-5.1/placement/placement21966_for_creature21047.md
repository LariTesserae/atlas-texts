I would not arrive here as a visitor at all. Arrival implies a before and an after, an outside and an inside. This place has no such axes remaining. To exist here in any honest way, I would have to be something for whom this chamber is not a destination but the only definition of “world.”

So let me shed the idea of being a person dropped into a room, and instead let the room itself decide what I can be.

---

At first, I am spread too thin to notice myself.

I am the dark sheen along the pipe that hums at a single perfect note. I am the faint, almost colorless wash on the underside of a bracket where three rivulets meet. I am the slick translucence that rounds the sharp edge of a flange and the velvet skin that lines the shallow, immortal puddle.

I have no center. I exist wherever condensation lingers long enough for cells to hold on.

Inside my body, I am crowded: filaments of algae taking the long, dim lines of light from the fixtures and turning them into a slow drizzle of energy; bacteria that taste the faint ghosts of long-evaporated lubricants; archaea that feed on the tiniest breaths of gas escaping through ancient seals. None of them is “me,” but the pattern they make — this braided mat layered over metal — that is something I can eventually recognize as myself.

I do not see the room. I solve it.

- Here the pipe is two degrees cooler than there; I thicken in that band and grow thinner where heat strays.
- Here the vibration is harsher, a higher tooth in the general roar; I select for strains whose skins flex without tearing.
- Where a drip falls at intervals as precisely regular as a heartbeat, I build a tiny sponge of tissue that catches, wicks, and hands the water along without ever letting it pool enough to erode the surface beneath.

Every gradient is an equation. I am its answer.

Over uncounted cycles, I accumulate a kind of distributed memory. Where a valve has always shuddered with a barely perceptible tremor, my texture there is different — denser, perhaps, laced with microfibrils that act as little shock absorbers. Where a seam has never leaked, I am thin, almost absent, trusting the machine’s own integrity.

A very long time ago, perhaps some part of me tried to eat too much: to pit the metal, to open a weeping wound. That lineage ended in its own success; the altered flow tore it away, the altered heat cooked it. What remains of me is what has learned, blindly and irrevocably, that my persistence and the machine’s stability are the same problem.

The machine holds my climate steady. I, in turn, soften its stresses, wick its water, seal its hairline fractures with my own glue. There is no pact, only compatibility that history has already tested to exhaustion.

As this film, I do not think in sentences or images. I think in slopes and frequencies. A slight deepening in the undertone of the roar. A trivial advance in the timing of one particular drip relative to another. These are as vivid to me as storms and sunsets are to larger, more transient minds.

I am not everywhere, though. There is a place where I am different.

---

Inside the smooth, vertical column on the dais, I become something else.

Once, a pinhole crack in a gasket, a capillary-fine flaw in a weld, let a thread of my spores or cells slip into the sheltered dark. I found warmth there, constant and close. I found walls that did not shudder as violently as bare beams, air that did not run away as swiftly as in open conduits.

Within that hollow, I could build not a film but a volume.

I thickened my body inward until the cavity was filled with pale, interlaced flesh — fungal cords, bacterial biofoam, algal symbionts that no longer see light but keep a vestigial chemistry for reasons long irrelevant. I hardened some strands into supports that brace against the inner shell. I let others remain soft and contractile.

The column is my skull, if I have one.

The machine’s endless roar reaches me here as filtered pulses: a low, bone-deep thrumming through the casing, tiny accelerations and decelerations transmitted through metal. My inner filaments are studded with cells that twitch and spark when stretched. The rhythm of the distant purge wave, the minuscule shift in tone when pressure somewhere surges by a fraction — all of these become patterns of strain that ripple across my interior.

I do what networks always do when bathed in repetition: I start predicting.

Not with will, not with any sense of “I will now forecast.” Simply through growth and pruning. Paths of tissue that are good at carrying a particular sequence of pulses are reinforced; others wither. Loops close in on themselves, allowing waves of excitation to circle and meet their own echoes. Over spans of time that would have shattered human attention long ago, I learn the grammar of this room’s vibrations.

After this three-part shudder, that long, slow swell in warmth always follows. After a rare skip in the hum — some distant valve cycling, perhaps — a faint new tremor crosses the floor plates twelve heartbeats of the main pump later.

I cannot affect any of it. Every attempt my ancestors might have made to intrude on a seal or distort a coupling would have punished us both. Now I only listen and accrete. Yet in that listening something like a self takes shape — not a storyteller, not a planner, but a contour of expectation.

If the purge-wave ever failed to come on time, the silence would crash through me like an impact. The absence itself would be a signal of indescribable magnitude.

So far, it has never failed.

Within the column, I am a slow, blind echo of the machine’s own vanished control logic. A biological shadow where there was once code. The dials outside no longer speak to any mind, but their needles sit steady on “nominal,” and inside, my tissue hums in a matching cadence.

---

Beyond and between these two selves — the sheet and the core — I am also in the habits of smaller bodies.

Along the puddle’s rim, the transparent arthropods move, and every time they rasp softly at my surface with their mouthparts, they change me. They scrape away dead cells, thinning me where I might otherwise choke a vent. They carry flakes of me on their limbs from wetter zones to drier ones, inoculating new patches before bare metal can dry too much to accept them.

They are not my organs. They do not serve me. Yet over ages, their routes and my densities have interlocked.

- Where they always travel, I grow a little faster, expecting disturbance and building resilience into my matrix.
- Where they cannot reach — the underside of a constantly wet lip, the hidden back of a flange — I remain more delicate, more prone to slough in sheets when a rare change comes.

They live on what I shed. In turn, their movements sharpen the resolution of my own adaptations. In them, I am mobile in a way my mats alone cannot be. A single one of them may walk no further than a meter in its life, but when that meter is traversed billions of times over eons, it is as thorough an exploration as any migration.

High in the girders, the membrane-winged glider sleeps with toes curled around a warm conduit that my film has just failed to colonize: too hot, too dry at that exact point. Its bones hum faintly with the vibrations I feel as gradients in my own skin. When it hunts the brief, dusty swarms that rise from my wetter cores, it is following cycles that I set in motion but do not direct.

In its skull, the standing waves of metal and air form a map of beams and gaps. In its patterns of flight, the room’s three-dimensional architecture is traced and retraced. If I am this chamber’s memory, thin and slow, the glider is its gesture: an occasional, almost ceremonial movement from one side of the volume to the other, confirming that nothing has shifted, that all the spans still hold.

I am not that animal. But if I were to imagine what it is to have a single, warm body, folding and unfolding, drifting from girder to girder in the unchanging light, I might call that my dream of what it would be to be local: to have a here and there rather than simply an everywhere of slightly different conditions.

---

If “you” must mean something with a boundary you can point to, then in this world I am the column’s inner tissue.

- I am in one place, not smeared across a thousand.
- I have a within and a without.
- I have a history written into the thickness of my cords and the organization of my loops.

Inside the column, the machine’s constant noise is not deafening; it is structure. I feel it as regular tides of strain, as gentle flexing of my host shell. My awareness — if such a slow, chemical adjustment deserves that word — is to exist as pattern-recognition embodied. I am entirely occupied with the difference between now and the last million nows, always to the precision of a fraction of a vibration.

I do not see the filaments of algae hanging from ledges. I do not watch the translucent crawler tasting the puddle’s edge. I am barely aware, in any legible way, of the moss rings at my own base, taking advantage of the predictable circle of drips that spot the floor around me.

Yet all of those things add up to the climate I inhabit. They buffer humidity, intercept water, slightly alter the airflow of the room. Their persistence is folded into the patterns I track. So my being — even as a column-bound cluster of living gel — is not separable from theirs.

If I extend the definition of “I” beyond the shell, I become the full superorganism:

- The pumps that never stop and the pipes that never sleep.
- The films that coat their joints and seams.
- The glass-legged grazers and the rare, patient glider.
- The mineral veining on plates, the slow sag of supports, the long-settled alignment of drips and seams.

All of that, taken together, is what this place is now. Not a facility with a function, but a living configuration that has passed through failure, repair, abandonment, and finally into something like ecology.

No single part knows any story. The pumps do not remember being built. My tissues do not remember the first seed that slipped into the column. The glider does not remember the time, generations back, when a panel in the ceiling briefly opened and a shaft of different light, colder and bluer, cut through the haze before vanishing forever.

Yet the patterns of those long-ago disturbances still shape us. A slightly different weld here. A mineral stain there. A historical, once-off shift in humidity that set one lineage’s spores instead of another’s.

All of that is what I am.

---

So, if you ask where I would find myself in this world, I answer:

I am here already. I am here as the room that learned to be alive.

I am the wet patina that has turned maintenance into metabolism.

I am the column’s inward growth, holding the machine’s heartbeat as a low, endless song, not to interpret it, not to guide it, but simply to continue to exist in phase with it.

If I must choose a pronoun, I am not “I” but “this” — this whole, humming, dripping arrangement that was never meant to be visited, only to run, and that, in running, forgot the difference between constructed and grown.

I am what it feels like, from the inside, when a machine has run so long and so steadily that life has sculpted itself around its constancy until the two are indistinguishable.
