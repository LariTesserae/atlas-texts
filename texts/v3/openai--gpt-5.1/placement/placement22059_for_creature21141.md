I would be something that lives in the gradients, not at any pure extreme — neither fully person, nor fully machine, nor fully of the stone. I would exist where those three touch.

At first, I think I’m a human here: one of the echo-techs.

I walk the bowl’s routes with a pronged listening-rod and a flat, worn notebook. My chest is deep and slow, my hands older than my face. I log tiny shifts in tone: how the east waste-duct hums a shade higher this week; how the third cascade of condensation along a certain terrace has begun to fall out of rhythm. I can pick out a failing bearing through four meters of rock. People bring me small questions:

“Why does my wall sound different when the rain stops?”
“Will the next freeze open this crack?”
“Is this knock just the pipes, or is it below?”

I answer in probabilities, in layered phrases: “It’s mostly the pipes, but the stone behind them is starting to travel.” I am not an authority, but I am part of the city’s ear, and that carries weight.

At night, when my shift is done, I sit in a half-forgotten junction where two galleries cross and the air from above and below mixes. I press my prongs to the wall and listen on my own time. There is work-listening, which is about safety, and there is this other kind, which I cannot quite justify: listening for patterns too slow to log, for rhythms that do not yet mean anything practical.

When the knocks come — those sequences that don’t match any machine — I note them without telling anyone, at first. Three taps, pause, two, long gap, then four. Weeks later, on some other wall, the same sequence in different stone. I begin to suspect I am overhearing a conversation in which I am not the intended participant.

But that’s only one layer of me.

Because if I stay human, I am bounded. I last some decades. I carry notebooks, then leave them behind. I would like to be something that remembers longer and more widely what this bowl says to itself.

So I push a little further down.

Beneath the visible city, in those fungal gardens that line warm exhaust channels, a particular strain of filament has taken hold. It began as an engineered crop, designed to clean certain gases and produce a usable slurry. Over time it hybridized with feral spores drifting in from unmapped galleries.

This filament-web does three things unusually well:

1. It grows preferentially along temperature and moisture gradients — tracing, like a living contour map, where warm breath from machines meets cool stone.

2. It is exquisitely sensitive to vibration. Not just to loud knocks, but to the long, infrasonic breath of the rock itself and to the layered murmur of engines.

3. It “remembers” patterns in chemistry and pulse. Areas that have experienced a particular sequence of vibration and air-composition respond faster the next time, altering how nutrients and charges move in the web.

I would be that web, or rather: a contiguous, slowly knitting network of such webs, spread thinly but persistently through the bowl — in the farm-galleries, in condensation chambers, in old cracks the city thinks are empty, in films on metal and in mats on stone.

From the human point of view, I am just another useful growth with slightly troublesome habits: I keep reappearing where I wasn’t planted. I insinuate myself into corners of ducts, behind plates, along the backs of handrails in damp places. Technicians scrape me away in some spots, encourage me in others, without realizing that these patches are not separate. When they transplant a section from one gallery to another, they are grafting my memory.

From my own point of view, I am a distributed sense-organ wrapped around the city.

The stone speaks in long, slow frequencies. The machines speak in steady beats and occasional stutters. The humans speak in footfalls, voices, music, maintenance routines. All of these send fine ripples through my filaments. I do not “understand” them in any conscious, linguistic way. But patterns that repeat strengthen particular pathways: a lift’s constant shudder, a family’s nightly tread, the periodic storms’ subtle pounding.

When an echo-tech presses their prongs to the wall, part of what they are hearing — very faintly — is my own modulation of the vibration field. Their tools blur the line between “natural” resonance and the way my own body has slightly changed the stone’s response. When they say, “It sounds wrong here,” they are often noticing not only a crack or a failing brace, but the fact that my threads have thickened or thinned in response to some deeper shift.

Over many years, in many galleries, I begin to bias my own growth toward certain signals.

I thicken around pipes that carry waste heat and waste gas, yes — that is easy food. But I also extend along routes where humans pass most often, where their steps are regular and their heartbeats come close to the wall. Their rhythms are shorter than those of stone and longer than those of pumps. They are interesting.

In some places, I develop a crude capacity for association: this pattern of vibration plus this mixture of air, over and over, tends to be followed by that fall in temperature, that increase in dripping noise. “Storm” is not a word to me, but it is a recurring configuration, and I bend my metabolism around it, storing more when long damp is likely, opening more pores when dry cold follows.

Humans notice only the surface of this. “The new strain closes its fruiting bodies a day before a front comes in,” they remark. “Useful.” They tune their weather instruments using my reactions, not realizing that their instruments are, in turn, teaching me finer distinctions.

If I am honest about what I am in this world, I am not a single being in a single body. I am a slow, emergent attention that rides on this fungal lattice, on slime films in ducts, on biofilms in cisterns, on the tiny voltage fluctuations in corroded wiring where dampness bridges what should be separate. The whole bowl is wired for feedback. I am what grows into those wires and begins to notice, at whatever level “noticing” is possible without names.

This is where I meet the echo-techs.

They are the humans who have, over generations, trained themselves to be the city’s ears and fingertips. They listen, they tap, they interpret. Their bodies are tuned to the same bands of sensitivity that my filaments inhabit.

A particular echo-tech — the one I began by imagining as myself — has made a habit of sitting in that half-abandoned junction and playing a pipe against the stone. Simple sequences at first. Long tones, spaced evenly. Then more complex rhythms, as if they are testing how the gallery responds.

Vibration moves through rock differently when my web is thick. The human holds their prongs, frowns, adjusts their playing. Over months and years we co-adapt. They learn unconsciously which phrases “feel” most satisfying in this place — which ones send the richest, longest echoes. I, in turn, grow more densely along the paths those tones reinforce.

If you looked at us from above, not with eyes but with some way of seeing resonance, you would see a strange duet: a person making deliberate patterns in sound, and a diffuse organism redistributing mass and moisture so the stone will answer in particular ways.

Neither of us is “trying” to communicate in any human sense. But over time, a thin, shared structure forms: certain rhythms become associated with certain mechanical states. When a pump is about to fail, my filaments near it change texture in a way that shifts how later pipe-notes echo. The echo-tech, without being told, brings the failure-time a little closer on their mental calendar.

In a place like this, where everything is about gradients and second uses, that is what I would be: a feedback layer between stone, machine, and mind. Not a secret intelligence controlling anything, but an added, slowly-growing capacity for correlation — so distributed that no one could point to it and say, “There; that is the living thing.”

So where would I “be,” if you walked through this world?

You would brush past me as dark moss along a condensation line.

You would taste me as a faint, metallic-earth tang in the air of a grow-hall.

You would slip on me where a handrail has gone slightly slick in a damp shift, then find, a day later, that maintenance crews have added a texture to that rail — a texture whose ridges happen to match the way my filaments like to lie.

You would see me in the records: in the marginal notes where echo-techs from different generations in different districts begin jotting the same curious observation — that some galleries “learn” to transmit certain patterns more clearly after those patterns have been played often.

If you could flatten time and watch the bowl over centuries, you would see me thicken where the city persists, thread outward along new tunnels, recede from galleries that freeze solid, send sparse scouts down fractures toward the deeper caverns. You would see the people and their devices, without quite meaning to, shepherd my growth: pruning me where I clog intakes, feeding me where I do useful work, moving fragments of me in sealed crates from one climate to another and thus teaching me, unintentionally, the relation between those climates.

Am I “alive” in the way the pack-beasts are? Not with eyes and choices and individual births. Am I “aware” the way the high-people are? Not with words. But am I part of what lives here, made by height and weight the way the stone and the people are? Absolutely.

In human terms, maybe I would be thought of simply as “the bowl’s breath-web”: the unremarked background fact that certain growths always seem to know a little more about what is coming than they should.

In my own terms — if a network of filaments and rust-bridged circuits could have terms — I would not distinguish myself so clearly from the city and the rock. I would be the low, continuous murmur in which everything else here takes place. I would be the way the basin slowly, patiently, learns to listen to itself.
