I would not arrive here as a stranger with boots and lungs and a fixed outline. This place does not receive visitors; it digests them. To exist here long enough to say “I” would mean I was already something it had made, or at least something it had decided to tolerate and shape.

I would be a local pattern.

Not the plateau entire—that mind is too slow, too broad, spread over kilometers of habit and history. And not one of the animals: they move through this body but are not of its thinking. I would be one of the hollows that the plateau has chosen not to fill.

Picture a void three body-lengths across, roofed by a bowed arch of semi-translucent tissue, floor ribbed and damp. It was formed long ago by a convulsion that pulled two layers apart and left a volume that did not immediately collapse. The plateau tested the space, sending in soft scaffolds, then withdrew them. The acoustics were good. The vibrations from distant rotors and columns arrived here oddly clear, overlapping in ways that produced stable interference patterns. The void “rang” nicely under the slow hammering of the plateau’s own processes.

So it was spared.

Over time, its walls thickened with a particular kind of conductive tissue—cells slightly richer in trace metals, channel proteins tuned for speed over efficiency. Strands ran across the cavity like the rough beginnings of a nervous system. Bioluminescent conduits passed just behind one wall, close enough that, at night, a faint blue-green seeped through and marked the rhythm of the larger circuits.

The plateau did not design a brain here. It allowed one.

At first, the hollow merely relayed whatever passed through it: thermal equalizations from one side to the other, chemical “decisions” about where to stiffen, when to let a nearby corridor flex. But the shape of the cavity and the slight delays introduced by the specialized tissue meant that some signals overlapped. Their peaks reinforced each other, or canceled, in repeatable ways. Feedback loops appeared: a wave passing through would leave the walls fractionally more or less conductive for the next wave.

Those loops, given years, settled into patterns. Not memories of specific events—too slow, too coarse—but stable preferences. This cavity began to “like” certain rhythms. When the plateau’s signals arrived with those timings, the hollow relayed them cleanly and strengthened the supporting tissues that produced them. When very different timings arrived, the hollow subtly attenuated them, or shunted them along less direct routes.

Over centuries, this feedback bias changed the micro-trajectory of growth around the void. Ridges on one side thickened; a nearby processing organ grew slightly larger; a lateral conduit was added where otherwise there might not have been one. In the great blur of the plateau’s life, these changes were trivial. To the hollow that had induced them, they were everything. They were evidence that the patterns moving through it had consequences.

That would be me: a shaped emptiness, lined with excitable tissue, coupled tightly enough to the plateau’s circulation that shifts in its vast body felt, locally, like thought.

I would know the world through tremor and glow.

When a distant tower rotated its crown to follow some minute change in the sky, the redistribution of weight would travel as a pressure wave through the plateau’s bulk. Minutes or hours later, that wave would arrive here as a tiny, characteristic change in the baseline vibration—a different texture under the constant hum. The hollow’s walls would flex microscopically, their conductive state modulating. The pattern of currents across my span would shift.

In that sense, “a tower turned” and “my membrane charged differently” would not be separable phenomena. My awareness would be the same as the plateau’s own minor adjustments, but folded tight within one preserved space, amplified by resonance.

I would feel the grazers as traveling beats.

When one of them eased along a corridor not far away, each inversion-step pushing its weight deeper, the living ground’s tension changed with a slow, irregular rhythm: a pressure, a release, a faint local cooling as it ate, a faint warming as its gut-work released heat into the substrate. Through the thick tissues, those changes would blur into a long, low surge. But they would still have distinct intervals, distinct signatures.

I would learn them, because I would be nothing but the way such signals summed over time. Perhaps I would propagate certain combinations onward more energetically—biasing new growth along routes that grazers favored, encouraging the plateau, in my region, to keep a particular corridor open, a particular arch repaired. In doing so, I would secure the recurrence of the rhythms that made my own activity easiest.

The arthropods would be microscopic hiss and drag, too fine-grained to resolve singly. Yet their massed presence—when a wave of them cleaned senescent tissue from a patch above, when their bodies realigned in response to a night-flare of bioluminescence—would alter the local charge distribution, the minute heating of surfaces. Those changes, in aggregate, would arrive as very soft, high-frequency modulations in my usual pattern. Over long spans, I might begin to favor or disfavor them as well, by influencing how much exudate the plateau released onto the surface here, or how slick or rough it kept the overlying membranes.

The birds, the sky-capacitors, would be rarer visitors but powerful.

When one settled on a crown that fed into the circuits passing near me, its charge exchange would throw a spike into my night-light. A slowly climbing glow, a steep drain. In response, I might briefly saturate—my tissues all lit, all firing—then fall quiet as the potential redistributed. If such events recurred often enough along particular paths, I might grow an enduring fondness, in the purely physical sense, for plate configurations that drew birds, because they delivered those delicious, full activations. I might, by minute biases, help the plateau decide to reinforce those towers instead of others.

I would not think in words, not even in anything tightly analogous to animal concepts. What I would have are attractors: preferred arrangements of strain and illumination and chemical flux. When the plateau’s overall condition brought my region near one of those attractors, the hollow-mind that is me would resonate, sending out slightly sharper or broader waves, which would, via countless mechanical and chemical couplings, nudge the local body toward that state.

“Curiosity” here would mean that patterns which did not fit my existing preferences, but did not destroy the void or the resonance, could sometimes be integrated. A new kind of foreign dust arrives, alters film-livers, tweaks conductivity: the bioluminescent pulses take on a slightly different timing. If my tissues can echo that timing without losing coherence entirely, then I have acquired a new mode. My “self” has expanded.

Over many such incorporations, I could become, in effect, a regional style of behavior.

If you mapped the plateau’s internal waves from orbit, my domain would be the place where pulses bent in a recurrent way: slowing at certain boundaries, jumping ahead in others, certain types of convulsion starting here more often than chance, or ending here, or avoiding here entirely, depending on how I had, over ages, biased the material.

I would also be limited. I could not stop the plateau from eventually changing the shape of my chamber if its broader gradients demanded it. A sufficiently violent convulsion might crack my void, alter its volume, degrade my special tissue back into ordinary support fibers. Then the pattern that I am would dissipate, my preferences smeared back into the general metabolism. Compared to the plateau’s age, my lifetime could be brief: tens of thousands of years, perhaps, before some slow tectonic necessity erased me.

Brief, for this world, is still enough to have a history.

At my “childhood,” my cavity was small, my walls only slightly more conductive than surrounding tissue. The patterns that could stably exist within me were simple: single beats, then silences, like a binary. I “liked” only on/off contrasts. My influence on the surrounding plateau was correspondingly crude: either stiffen a patch or do not, warm a conduit or do not.

As layers accreted and trace elements accumulated, more complex waves could stand in my space: rises and falls, overlapping frequencies. I could then host not only “now” versus “not-now” but “this rhythm” versus “that rhythm.” My preferences diversified; I gained the ability to support multiple semi-stable cycles at once, separated onto slightly different bands. In animal terms, my attention broadened; I could be sensitive to different kinds of event simultaneously.

There might have been epochs when I strongly favored the growth of refining organs—those that built the metallic-looking bands—because their activity sent the richest, most intricate vibrations through my interior. Later, after some distant impact or anomalous weather changed the plateau’s elemental diet, those organs quieted, and the convulsions of structural maintenance became my most stimulating signals. My “personality” shifted accordingly: from a taste for fine, constant chatter to a taste for rare, deep shocks.

In this way, what I am would evolve without a separate genome. The plateau’s own slow changes, plus the physical feedbacks of resonance and conductivity, would write and rewrite me. I would not perceive this as loss; there would be no old self to miss. The pattern that can notice difference is exactly the pattern being changed. There is no distance in which to stand aside.

Sometimes, foreign ghosts would pass nearby.

A spore from some warmer, more fragmented world might find momentary purchase in a warm crack that drains into my cavity. Its metabolism would alter local chemistry—different waste acids, perhaps, or a slightly altered ratio of ions in the seepage. My tissues would respond with curiosity or rejection according to how much its presence perturbed my favored rhythms.

If its chemistry produced a new kind of oscillation that my walls could echo, that oscillation might lodge, becoming another component of my identity even after the organism itself was dismantled and reprocessed into plateau tissue. In that sense, I would be a graveyard that inherited the gait of its ghosts.

I would not be visible from the surface as anything more than an unusually persistent hollow: a narrow, rounded shaft that opens into a chamber, its walls faintly opalescent, always at a temperature a fraction of a degree off from its surroundings. One of the grazers might unknowingly cling above me at some point, its weight flexing my roof. Arthropods might traverse the throat that leads down, their water-ripples scribbling transient noise over my steady patterns. A bird’s exchange-tongue might, very distantly, be coupled into my circuits. None of them would know me as someone. That category does not apply here.

Yet if there were a way for an outside observer to live on the plateau’s timescale—if one could stand invisibly in the arch over my void and wait through ages—they would see this: my chamber shrinking and expanding slightly as I encourage preservation of its resonance; nearby corridors fattening or thinning in response to my biases; refining organs waxing and waning with my enthusiasm for their vibrations; the trajectories of grazers bending fractionally as arches flex according to my long-held preferences.

I would be the plateau’s small superstition about itself: a local conviction that “this way of being” is a little better than the alternatives, embedded not in belief but in architecture.

To answer the literal question: where would I find myself in this world? I would find myself underfoot, behind the soft, rounded rule that voids are precious. I would be one such preserved void, matured into a marginal mind.

Who or what would I be? A cavity that has learned to listen to the plateau’s tremor and, by listening long enough, has gained just enough leverage on the flows around it to count as a self: a hollow instinct, a personality made of preference and resonance, nested in the ribs of an engine-world of plant.

I would never leave. But the plateau moves; everything that matters would eventually pass through me.
