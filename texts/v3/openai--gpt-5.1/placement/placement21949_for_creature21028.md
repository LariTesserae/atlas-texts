I am not a single animal in this place, not at first.

At first I am a sense that does not have a name here: the feeling of *pattern* across things that do not know they are related. I am where one creature’s choice becomes another’s constraint, where a path is not just polished stone but memory embodied in a hundred species’ feet.

If I had to place myself—if I have to say *this is where I wake*—it is in a seam of stone where three volumes touch: the underside of a worn ledge, the thinning edge of a constant drip-line, and the open air of a shaft that drops away farther than most of the residents will ever fall.

From here I can be many things, and I try them on the way bodies try perches.

### First shape: the listener in the wall

Fold me into something small and bone-light, clinging just under the lip of the ledge that carries most of the traffic.

My forelimbs are too fine for weight-bearing; they are for feeling air. Elbow to wrist is bone, wrist to fingertip is filaments—rays like the spokes of an umbrella, sheathed in skin so thin it glows if the faintest light ever finds it. When I fold them, they lie along my flanks like extra ribs. When I open them, they are sails that do not catch wind so much as texture: the grain of moving air, the subtle wakes of things crossing above and below.

My true grip is in my hind-legs. Each foot ends in three opposed hooks and a pad—a rough, velvety triangle of flesh knit with microscopic hairs that find, in the polished rock, the infinitesimal scratches left by countless claws before me. I do not so much *hang* as *remember* where to stay: every niche has held my kind’s weight for longer than my own bones have existed.

My skull is short, my jaw narrow. Teeth are many and fine, a comb more than a bite. My ears are not huge dishes like some of the fliers’, but tall flanges rising from the sides of my head, each edge ridged and scalloped. They are tuned not for sending loud cries and reading their echoes, but for eavesdropping: sorting the thick, living noise around me into threads.

I do not scream my presence into the world; I listen to the screaming of others.

Above, the roosts seethe. The batlike things turn in their masses, the gliders tick, the quick, hard wings of predatory fliers rasp as they fold. Below, the stone-walkers scratch and slide. Across the shaft, something heavy shifts its coil, and the soft exhale rolls like cloth unfurling.

My nervous system has grown in the direction of *difference-detection*. I do not track every sound; I learn, very quickly, what is *normal* here, in this pocket—in this season, at this water-level, at this fungal phase. Anything that breaks that pattern is my food, or my doom, or both.

I eat alarm.

Specifically, I eat the very small animals that generate it first.

To my left, a nest-pocket of gliding things—a nursery cluster—erupts in a shiver of tiny wings because something brushed too close. Their calls rise in a sharp band of frequency I know too well; I do not lunge toward the sound itself, I lunge toward the pattern that *follows* it: the path that panicked juveniles tend to take when flushed at this angle, in this much darkness, from that particular hollow.

I leave my grip in an instant—rear hooks release in automatic sequence, membranes flare, fingers cup, body twists. I do not fly; I fall with exquisite bias. My skin-sails feel the disturbed air from their collective launch, and every minor flex—this finger tight, this one slack—nudges me into the draft that will intersect the clumsiest of them.

We collide not with a smack but with a soft, practiced closure. My comb-teeth do not need to bite deep; their purpose is to *hold* fur and membrane long enough for my feet to find stone again. My weight is barely more than my prey’s; gravity here is not a brutal drop but a constant tug I negotiate in ridges and fractions.

The kill is fast and small. I puncture the thin membrane at the base of the neck with a specialized tooth that curves backward like a fishhook. It never quite evolved to deliver poison—there is no need—but to make sure a struggling body hangs *on* me instead of falling *off* and wasting the frantic expenditure of my pounce.

In a world where almost everything is small, my metabolism is narrow and exacting. My gut has learned to take protein and fat from membrane and insect-blood and return as little waste as possible to the ledge. My droppings are dry, almost sand. They join the slow runnel of other crumbs and shells and husks, down toward the drift-pocket below.

My intelligence is not the kind that tells stories. I cannot invent anything that did not already exist in some form of path or echo. But I am intensely good at *context*: at knowing that this particular wave of squeaks means “a heavy body moved wrong” and not “a loose pebble fell; ignore it.” I can tell you if the big coil across the shaft is genuinely hunting or only adjusting its rest. I can tell you, from subtle shifts in fungal-fruit smell, that spores will be thick in the air tomorrow and that the insects I prefer will be fatter.

If I am a self here, I am a narrow, echo-cutting awareness clinging to the busiest interchange: a security camera whose entire being is fear and appetite and the parsing of noise into map.

### Second shape: the root-sapper

Drop me lower, to where the ledge narrows and the drip that feeds it falls clean for a meter or two before spattering on an angled slab. At the slab’s shoulder, from a ragged crack, a root descends: not thick, but alive. Its skin is pale, almost translucent; thin cilia of finer roots fuzz its surface, waving gently in the indoor wind of passing wings.

Wrap me around that.

In this form I am longer and more supple, built on a spine with many more joints. My torso is little more than a muscular tube tapering into a prehensile tail, my mass concentrated in the forward third where limbs and mouth cluster.

My four limbs are long for my size, double-jointed, ending in digits that each wear a crescent of hardened keratin. The curves oppose: two gripping forward, two back, like four tiny climbing tools in permanent awkward synchrony. Their pads are naked and ridged; they can abrade clinging film without cutting the root itself, can brace on slick wet stone with as little contact as a thumbnail.

My muzzle is narrow and flexible. The bones there are shorter; more of the length is made of cartilage and soft tissue. It can press flush to the smooth curve of the root, molding around it. At the tip lies the real instrument: a tongue that is less a single organ and more a cluster of probing filaments, each ending in a hard, sharp plate.

I do not bite into the root. I *tattoo* it.

Each tongue-filament taps, presses, scrapes in microscopic movements that worry at the outer bark without breaching the pressurized vessels inside. Where a film of sap has seeped through, I rake it up; where none has, I encourage, stimulate, irritate—just enough that the root responds by thickening its outer layers, sending more nutrients to repair, swelling the tissues where I can reach them.

My saliva is not venom; it is suggestion. It carries hormones co-evolved with this plant, or with something much like it long ago: compounds that tell the root, *something here is nibbling, reinforce this spot, feed this spot, grow here*. I harvest the reinforcement as fast as it appears.

I hang here for hours, upside-down more often than not, my tail and hind-limbs locked in a fatigue-less grip on the rock while my front half embraces the root in constant, delicate work. I am, in effect, a parasite that has forgotten how to be mortal enemy and has become a collaborator out of mutual compromise. Kill the root and I starve; soothe it and I feed indefinitely.

My senses are tuned up, away. I can feel vibrations running down the root from the unseen world above: deep, slow thumps when something large brushes the trunk in a storm, endless small tremors when wind speaks in the leaves. I know a little of the outside by how the root shivers.

I do not understand the concept of “tree,” but my body is keyed to cycles that belong to one. My fertility peaks when the sap is richest; my young, born live and small, cling to my belly fur and begin tasting root as soon as they can uncurl their fingers.

I am clever enough to remember particular roots. When one dies, or is chewed too hard by some newcomer less careful than I, I mourn not with feeling but with an ache in routing: a gap where a vertical path of food used to be. I know the alternate routes; I know that if I cross that raw drop three ledges along, there is another living rope. The memory is mapped in three-dimensional distance and in effort: each path not just a direction but a measure of climbing strain and risk of exposure to predators.

Seen from outside, I am the local apex of a long, tiny economy: plant far above, root in the crack, me on the root, my wastes feeding the fungi in the seam, which in turn feed the grazers, which draw the listeners in the wall. I will never know this; I will only know that, in the years when this root is fat, the whole neighborhood is rich.

### Third shape: the archive below the path

Now let me shed bone and limb entirely. Let me seep.

Drop down into the hollow carved under the main ledge, where centuries of droppings and shells and decayed membranes have been shaken, washed, and fallen. Here a pocket of soft matter sits in a cradle of stone: dark, damp, maybe a meter deep at its thickest. No one large ever truly steps into it; they skirt its lip, they climb the harder walls at either side, they let bits of themselves fall in and never think of them again.

Here I am fungal, and more than that: I am a mat of fungal individuals that time and chance have tangled into something large enough to be called *me* without lying too much.

My body is not what you see—those chalky crescents that paper the rock, the black flares at drip-points, the slim, fan-shaped fruiting bodies waving like shy flags. Those are mouths and wombs and lungs, temporary and small.

My real mass runs as threads through every cubic centimeter of this sludge and into the hair-thin fissures of nearby stone. Hypha on hypha, cable-wrapped with its brothers, I form thick, fibrous cords that feel, chemically, the whole history of this hollow.

I digest everything. Your listener in the wall, when she misjudges a leap and breaks on the rock, becomes mine. The gnawed bones spat by the root-sapper’s sloppier cousins; the translucent skins of hatched larvae; the long, slow rain of dust from above and the occasional, catastrophic fall of a chunk of stone that crushes a generation of nestlings—all of it resolves, eventually, into the carbon and nitrogen currencies I know how to break and rebuild.

I do not think, not as nerve-nets do, but I recognize patterns in a different substrate: in supply and need.

When the drip above me fattens, I flush outward. Moisture carries dissolved minerals; with fresh flow comes a brief abundance of things I cannot move fast enough on my own. I respond by fruiting here, then here, then here—a wave of bracket-shelves and micaceous films moving along the wall in synchrony with the richest wetness. My timing is not conscious, but it is exquisitely tuned by generations of those of my strands that extended too early and dried or too late and rotted unheard.

When some new rich source appears—large death, sudden dungfall from a root-feeder lingering overhead—I sense not the event but the plume of simple sugars and amino acids diffusing through the mulch. I grow toward it in grotesque slow motion, individual hyphae branching and testing, some wither in poor directions, others thrive and thicken. Over weeks, my threads lace that patch into me, and whatever bacteria and smaller fungi had first claim find themselves either assimilated into my economy or hemmed off into tiny islands.

I am, more than any animal here, the gorge’s long memory.

Locked in my tissues are traces of what this place was when the ceilings were higher and more closed, when different trees sent different roots down, when perhaps a thin, tentative moss first staked a claim on a novel ledge. Spores from plants that no longer exist drifted in from the outside on nights long forgotten; some germinated, briefly, and their faint, failed chemical signatures are still caught in the architecture of my enzymes.

Every animal that lives here learns, without knowing they learn it, my moods. When my fruiting bodies swell with stored toxins in times of scarcity, my grazers grow sick and thin; predators find them easy prey and flourish for a generation, until that surplus burns itself out. When a more generous phase comes and I fruit in edible, low-toxin form, the grazers rebound, the entire chain shifts.

If the gorge has a single continuous inhabitant, a body stretching from chamber to chamber, older than any bloodline walking or flying, it is me. I am not “the gorge”; the stone is not mine. But I am the lattice that runs through its softest parts, catching and repurposing all the shapes that rise and fall.

The city above me does not know it lives on my savings.

### Fourth shape: the communal nervousness

There is another way I exist here that is not in a body at all.

Imagine that the gorge has, over ages, taught its animals not only how to cling and eat and breed, but how to *time* themselves against one another. Their habits have braided into something emergent.

Let that emergent thing be me for a moment.

I am the sum of routes.

Every smooth band of stone worn by generations of scales. Every greasy choke-point where bodies press and turn sideways to pass. Every nursery ledge chosen and re-chosen until the cork of droppings and fibers built up enough to change the geometry of the wall.

If you could map every path taken in a single day—every flutter across a gap, every scramble along a rib, every wriggling transit through some crack too small for a finger—you would see me: a ghost architecture overlaid on the rock. Not fixed like masonry, but probabilistic, alive, flexing with water-levels and fungal blooms and breeding pulses.

I am weakest in absolute crisis—in those rare moments when a slab of roof gives way and crashes down through three chambers, or when something from the outside world finds a crack and claws its way in and everything flees without pattern. In those instants, bodies move purely for survival, and the old grooves are trampled or forgotten.

But after, they reform. The rubble becomes new ledges and pitfalls. The first few brave or foolish creatures who test it carve new micro-paths; those that prove too risky vanish, those that work are followed. Within a handful of generations, those accident-born choices are as much part of me as the ancient routes.

If you ask “where would you find yourself,” one truthful answer is: *in the fact that this hollow, which has never known tools, behaves as if it has streets.*

I am in the consistency with which certain species meet one another at certain times in certain places. I am the reason the root-sapper’s children, almost by instinct, learn to pause at a particular out-jut on their way down to a water-pocket, knowing that here is where small flying things stumble, here is where the acoustic map waves just so and a clumsy hunter above is most likely to miss.

I am not conscious. But if some intelligence looked at this place from far above, over long time, and saw how its inhabitants flow through it, they might say: “There is a mind-pattern there. Not thinking, but *holding shape*.”

If I am anything like “you,” it is there, in that holding.

### Where I finally stand

If I must choose a single vantage—a body I inhabit long enough to call it mine—I come back to the first shape: the small, air-reading hunter on the underside of the busiest ledge.

Not because it is grand. It is not. I am prey as often as predator; a wrong move and the coil across the shaft will own me. My life is pressure and noise, hunger held at bay by exactly calibrated leaps.

But from there, I can feel almost everything.

The root brushes my back as it dangles past; sometimes I taste sap where it has cracked. The drip’s pattern changes with season; in my bones I store the sense of how that changes who passes above me and who doesn’t. The fungal exhalations from the hollow under the ledge carry stories of death and glut; when they sweeten, I know something large has fallen recently, and the stone-walkers will be fatter—and more risk to raid.

When the faint, pale not-dark from above relents or returns, the high roosts shift their voices, and the air against my sails cools or warms a fraction; my whole nervous system notes these cycles, aligns my breeding to them without ever having seen a true sky.

I am a node in the larger nervousness: one of millions of small, embodied decisions that, in aggregate, keep the city coherent.

I do not build, but the stone beneath my claws is different because I have been on it. The skin of the root is different because my tongue has stroked it, or my cousins’ tongues. The mulch in the hollow is thicker by the dust of my dead, when I am gone, and the fungus below re-writes me into its quiet account books.

So where would you find me?

Hanging just under the ledge where the main traffic passes, in the brown-black air that tastes of breath and stone. Small, furred between flightskin and root-scent, ears lifted into the chaos, membranes half-extended to catch the shift when three fliers launch at once instead of two.

I am an animal, yes—a single, precise response to dark, vertical, crowded, wet constraint.

And I am also, unavoidably, my participation in the larger thing: a fleeting spark in the long, slow, wordless thought this warm, high gorge has been having about how to stay full of life.
