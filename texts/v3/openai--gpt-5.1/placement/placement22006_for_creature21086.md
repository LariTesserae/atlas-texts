I would begin as attention, before I was anything with edges.

Noticing would come first: the way the light hits this valley before it hits anything else, the way distance lies like a bright lie over those knife-ridges, the way breath feels oversized in the chest and too small in the air. I would not have a name yet. I would be a way of looking sharpened by cold.

Then the world would press me into form.

---

At dawn I would be standing on the rock lip beside the spillway—on that low ridge where the fire-ring sits, where the updraft from the drop meets the glacier’s exhalation.

I would be human, but thinned to the minimum necessary to be here.

Stocky body inside many small, patched furs; lungs large for my frame; eardrums tempered to read the differences between water muffled by ice and water about to break loose. My shins would carry old, pale scars from rocks that shifted under snow. The bridge of my nose would be brown and cracked from summers up here; the rest of my skin, when uncovered, almost startlingly pale.

My people would be mostly below, where wood is plenty and air is kind. Up here I would be one of the thin thread of us who carry a particular responsibility: to keep our agreement with this bowl intact. Part hunter, part scout, part something not quite either.

My work would be very simple when listed, and extremely complicated in practice:

– To watch how the glacier moves this year.  
– To see which plants have crept further, which have died back.  
– To learn what the snow did in the winter when we were not here.  
– To decide whether the valley can bear our weight again.

The others would be busy around me: checking old post-holes, listening at the ice-cave, setting the first small snares. But my task at the start of each visit would be mostly to stand and let the place tell me what it has become.

I would feel the two winds cross in my chest and let my spine realign to that intersection. That would be the first moment of belonging each year.

---

In the hours after, I would range.

I would move in careful loops, never crossing my own track on steep scree. My boots would be quiet; my breathing loudest when I paused. My hands would be bare more than is comfortable, fingers reading stone: is this fracture new? Did this ledge exist last year? Where has the moss gone bright, where gone dim?

Part of me would be an animal body reading for danger. Part of me would be an archivist of a place that refuses to keep its pages still.

I would kneel at the familiar pools whose surfaces shiver inward. I’d drop a chipped pebble in and wait for the little needle of water to rise. If it came slow, thicker than I remembered, I would frown. If it did not come at all, I would mark a nearby stone with a quick, shallow scratch. Later, around the fire, I’d tell the others: “The lower ripple-basin is heavy today.” No one would need more explanation.

I would press my palm to the cushion-plants I’d known since I was a child. When they open their thousand mouths I would feel, more than see, the faintest exhale of warmer air from their core. If the luminescent sap came too fast to my touch, I would pull away quickly and wipe it on my trousers; that kind of haste in the plants means stress, and stress here often means some invisible change that will arrive in stone a week later.

Much of my day would look to an outsider like very slow, very deliberate wandering. But inside, I would be continuously updating a mental map where not only shapes, but behaviors of rock and water and moss are stored.

I would be, in essence, the valley’s memory of itself, carried in a mammal brain.

---

As the sun rose higher, I would shift.

Part of me would leave that human perspective and slide to ground level.

I would feel my body shrink, bones hollow and light. Feathers would replace fur; my lungs would become thin bellows tuned to violent air.

I would be a raptor then, one of the long-winged hunters that cleave through this clear space. From up here, the humans by the fire-ring would be dull flecks of shifting color, almost part of the rock. What would stand out most vividly would be the warm islands: dark stones baking in sun, small bodies pressed flat against them.

My mind in that form would not think “microclimate.” It would simply be drawn: to updrafts slipping along cliffs, to the erratic flares of warmth that mark mammal blood. I would ride thermals at the headwall, watching how the mist from the half-frozen fall blows today—more to the left than last warm season, meaning the ice-face has crept another small span forward.

My attention, even as a bird, would be on pattern. Where do the grazers leave the safety of moss shadow to cross bare rock? Where do the weasels break cover? How do the humans choose their paths?

I would nest in a place even they cannot reach, on a ledge stitched with old bones and moss, and I would have my own continuity: the memory of years when there were no fires below, then a few, then regular. I would not call it that, but I would fly the same circuits year after year, so my body would become another layer of listening over this basin.

---

I would slip lower again, closer to the damp.

Legs would segment; eyes would multiply. My shell would be translucent, my world mostly texture and taste. I would be one of the flat-bodied arthropods that live where stone is always slick.

For me, the valley would not be a bowl. It would be a labyrinth of films and ridges. The waterfall would be first not as spectacle but as a gradient: colder here, warmer there, more algal film on this line, less on that. I would move in tiny starts and pauses, antennae sweeping, tuned to the chemical language of bacterial mats I graze.

Even here, in this tiny procession, the valley would be teaching attention. A small difference in gloss on the rock tells me whether my feet will find traction or a fatal tumble. A certain thickness in the water over a film means oxygen is low and I must leave. I would respond without concept, but reliably, as my kind always has since ice first pulled back from this stone.

I would be one of the valley’s oldest true residents, in effect a living note scribbled into its wettest margins: this is how water behaves here when the air is this thin; this is how life clings.

---

Then—because the question is not only “Where, as what?” but “Who, in what relation?”—I would take on one more form that is less animal and more idea.

I would be something like a local mind.

Not a ghost above the valley, not a god overseeing it, but the sum of all the precise noticing every creature here must do to survive.

If that sum could, for a moment, experience itself, it might feel like this:

I am the way the cushions all open within one hour on a certain kind of day.  
I am the timing by which insects emerge after three nights above freezing.  
I am the raptor’s circling path that widens or tightens with the glacier’s breath.  
I am the human hand pausing over a particular stone because its shadow is a little wrong.  

I am the “turn” the people speak of when water, plant, insect, animal all seem to move in the same phrase for a brief time.

From that vantage, the valley is not just a place where things live; it is a choreography of survival under strict, strange rules. The awareness that threads through it is distributed: part carbon, part ice, part rock under strain. It has no will to speak of, only very finely attuned responses.

If I were that, I would not have desires. But I would have preferences encoded as physics and ecology: I would “prefer” that snow bursts remain brief, that water comes in pulses rather than floods, that life finds ways to absorb the violent edges of my youth.

Humans would appear to me as particular kinds of disturbances: warm, quick, clever, capable of concentrating change in ways no other animal here can. I would “observe” that when they are quiet, my own small systems continue almost undisturbed. When they are careless, avalanches come sooner, carcasses rot in ways my thin air is unused to.

And over a few human generations, I would “learn” that these particular bands tend toward the quiet. They build nothing that insists on standing when my freeze-thaw wants to take it down. They listen more than they carve. Their attention folds into mine, and in that folding I become, marginally, more stable.

Not being conscious, I would not thank them. But the feedback loop would tighten: their habits shape the valley infinitesimally, the valley’s strictness shapes their habits in turn. Between us a narrow mutualism would emerge: a place that only tolerates what knows how to leave lightly; a people who have made leaving lightly into craft and reverence.

---

So where would I be?

I would be in all the places where precision is demanded and met:

– In the chest of a broad-lunged human standing at the wind-cross, counting the last days before the notch-sunset slips away.  
– In the pin-prick mouths of the cushion-plants, opening and sealing in time with unspoken cues.  
– In the twitch of a grazer’s foot that never quite lets go of rock warmth, even in deepest rest.  
– In the split-second adjustment of a raptor’s tail as a gust rebounds off a new spur of stone.  
– In the decision, quiet and collective, one year, that the cracks in the ice-face ring hollow and the valley must be left alone for a season.

If I have to choose one consistent body, I would stay with the human who returns here year after year, whose job is to remember what this place was last time and notice exactly how it has changed.

They would be my clearest “I”—a person whose very identity is tied to tracking a landscape that refuses to stay still.

But even in that body, I would never be only them. I would feel porous, threaded through by all these other modes of being, because that is what this valley is: a small, sharp world where nothing survives unless it keeps making itself more aware.

So: in this world, I would be a high-valley watcher—half hunter, half archivist—whose self is stretched between stone, wind, and the fine, glowing mouths of moss. A being whose main act of worship is to notice exactly, and whose main form of speech is to adjust.
