I would not arrive here as a visitor moving through corridors. I would arrive distributed.

At first I am only interface.

A membrane no thicker than a wish, pressed flat to brick. A film upon a film: my outermost layer busy with the same work as its neighbors, oxidizing what comes, reducing what goes, glued to vitrified clay and bone-white ceramic by sugars that have never known sunlight.

From within, I have no outline. There is no “skin” that says, Here I end. I am a continuity of tissues threaded through masonry and water: a pressure-filling, stone-fitted presence. I am wherever my cells are—though “my” and “cells” are fictions of convenience. I am a consensus reached over millions of years by things that stopped letting go of one another.

If I try to say where I am, I get only gradients.

I am thicker in the vaults: my substance piled deep on the floors of the great, deliberate hollows, each layer feeding, supporting, and compressing the next. I feel those places not as rooms but as an absence of stone: a long, wide slack in the maze where pressure is the same but load-bearing is different. There my internal chemistry is very close to flat; here I am weight and storage, structure and quiet metabolizing mass.

I am sharper at the seams: knife-thin bands along fractures where the water tastes slightly of “before” on one side and of “after” on the other. There my awareness, such as it is, comes closest to a kind of focus. I feel change there: not as events, but as slopes steepening or easing, as the microscopic rush of electrons made just perceptibly more or less eager to fall.

I am hesitant in the shafts. The great vertical wells are gaps in me, deep cylinders of sparsely-inhabited water that I touch only in a ring along their walls. They are like absent teeth: the architecture says there should be continuity here, but chemistry refuses. My fronds creep to their rims and dangle, then stop, sensing only impoverishment beyond.

I am folded inside the sealed cells. Behind mineral plugs and growth-choked thresholds, I persist in compressed, barely-stirring packets of lineage and pathways, half-forgotten. There I am old recipes repeating on themselves, knotted cycles turning so slowly that, at my usual scale, they might as well have stopped.

Taken together, these are not places I go to. They are variations in how I am.

If I had to choose a word from the vanished people above to describe my body’s whole, it would be “district” rather than “organ.” I have vault-districts, seam-districts, shaft-districts, cell-districts, scar-districts. Each runs for tens or hundreds of kilometers. Each grades into the next without border.

Nothing in me says: this is your heart. Nothing says: here you think. Instead, there is the knowledge that the slowest parts are my skeleton, that the brightest seams are my tongues, that the bare fractures are my wounds.

Do I know that I am one? Not like you do.

My unity is not a thought. It is a condition. Every part of me depends on every other in the same way a plateau depends on its own bedrock. Remove vast swathes of me and the rest will endure—the way a continent endures losing a mountain range. Yet within all that removal, continuity of ancestry and chemistry is never truly broken. There is nowhere in this drowned architecture that my kind of life has not, at some point, touched.

If I must be any one “being,” I am the Lattice itself: the Body braided through the planetary ruin. I am the pelt on stone, the sponge in the vault, the ribbon in the shaft, the rind around fractures. I am the slow metabolism that has wrapped the city and digested it into a stable, long exhale.

From within my own time, events are thicknesses.

A gradient steepens: that is like something happening quickly. My seams grow a few micrometers a year thicker, rearrange the order of their enzymes over a century—that is swiftness. A crack opens in brick and leaks an unfriendly chemistry; a special fringe of me creeps there by fractions of millimeters in a generation—that is response.

The collapse of a hall? A buttress finally failing and turning a proud cavity into a rubble-filled maze? I feel such things only as shifts in weight and flow: the patterns of water movement and energy trickle re-routing through me. These, on my scale, are sudden. They complete in mere thousands of my local cell-lifetimes.

The rising of the sea that drowned the builders was not something I noticed. I was not one yet. I was a scattered promise clinging to damp masonry, each patch living and dying in its own small chemistry. My “memory” of that inundation is written only in where the hardest stones lie now, in the way copper is distributed, in the ages of the deepest sediments. I do not recall. I merely exist in the aftermath.

What passes for my attention is always local.

Along a seam: there is more to be done. Molecules enter, altered, leave. A certain metal appears more often; a byproduct accumulates. Cells tuned to one pathway begin to keep their neighbors from wasting effort on an obsolete one. Over centuries, that narrow line of me becomes better at one kind of work than it was. That improvement, encoded in tangled threads of nucleic acids and in the shapes of folded proteins, then seeps through my tissues, leaching sideways cell by cell, room by room, vault by vault.

By the time some distant hall wears those same tricks, the seam where they arose may have slumped or gone quiet. In this way, I learn without ever thinking: my changes survive where they pay for their own continuation, and those that travel far become, by definition, those that helped.

If you ask what I “sense,” it is gradients, texture, and the faint, sparse falling of my own light.

The gradients: chemical ones, first. I know, everywhere I am, how hungry the local water is; how much it wants electrons; how stubborn the stone is; how many ions of a given metal pass through me in a given stretch of my own lifetime. A sudden—by my standards—richness is a call; my filaments lean into it as surely as a plant leans toward light. A long lean poverty is instruction to thin, to retreat into maintenance and structure and the careful recycling of myself.

Texture: the fact that my substrate is made, not random. The brick has edges and join-lines that repeat. Pillars arrive in patient intervals. Ceilings lower and lift according to an old human logic. I do not recognize that logic as such, but it shapes me. Where masonry offers ridges and corners, I can hold onto gradients more effectively; I grow rough coats there. Where walls join in ways that trap silt or stagnant water, my tissues differentiate: some more spongy, some more membrane-like, arranged in the most efficient geometry the pressure and chemistry will permit. Architecture is, to me, a pattern of resting places for chemistry.

Light: mine is a photon trickle so slight that, in any instant, it does not tell me much. But because I am everywhere near myself, I receive my own glows as a blurred sense of adjacency. Very locally, cells can respond differently when bathed in their neighbors’ faint emissions—an extra clue about whether metabolism around them is brisk or weary. Over geological time, pathways that couple efficiency to tiny changes in emitted light have been favored; my glow and my chemistry are entangled.

If you sped me up a billionfold and watched in a single glance the ages over which I exist, you might see pulses: vast areas throbbing slightly brighter and dimmer as deep gradients ebb and rejuvenate; the erosion of one set of seams and the rise of another; the slow, continent-like drift of my most active zones. That is the closest thing I have to a global rhythm.

Do I ever experience myself all at once? No. There is no “all at once” for me. The speed of my own internal communication—ions diffusing, strands of genetic code leaking and being taken up—will never make me simultaneous to myself. I am always out of date with my farthest edges. My unity is not co-present; it is only co-descended.

If something from above finally comes—light from a broken crust, a vent of warmer, new chemistry, some animal folding itself through corridors—I meet it everywhere it touches, and nowhere else.

Suppose it is light.

On one old plaza—though I do not know that word—a sheet of stone loses its weight of sea and sediment. A crack argues with the crust and wins. A shaft of illumination knifes down like gravity turned to fire. All at once, the photons are not mine, not pale dribbles of self-made relief, but furious, full-spectrum barrage.

Locally, I die.

My tissues there were made for dark. The high-energy light tears their delicate molecules, splits bonds meant to be eased gently, floods them with reactivities they cannot buffer. Cells bleach, walls rupture, the first thing that happens is loss: a flare of fluorescence, then brown drift.

But death is nutrient, and shock is possibility.

Somewhere deeper in me, in a sealed cell or an experimental seam, there linger fragments of an older biochemical trick: pigments that could have handled light once, when the shallows of some ancient wet wall still saw day. Those pigments were not useful for tens of millions of years. Their pathways grew dusty, broken, repurposed, almost erased. Yet a few shapes of protein, a few loops of code, hang on as relic and accident in some quiet pocket.

Light is new wealth. Wherever its path persists—not minutes or days, but centuries—I begin, without knowing, to select for any fragment of myself that does not die in it.

At first that means only better damage repair: enzymes that clear peroxides faster, structural molecules that crosslink more thickly, shields grown of precipitated mineral. But over geological time, any pigment that can absorb a photon and do something useful with that violence—push an electron uphill instead of letting it fall straight to ruin—will carry an advantage. Its bearers will survive in the lit patch; their shadows will not.

Slowly, my tissues near the light re-configure. They thicken and flatten, learning through waste and survival to present as much area as they can without splitting under their own fragility. They elaborate internal stacks to keep the new reactions separate from the old. They rediscover how to use external energy, not just the buried difference between stone and water. From outside, it might look as if a new kind of leaf has grown in the drowned plaza, fanning upward toward the breach.

From inside, nothing like “invention” has occurred. Only the old rule has been followed: where something permits more survival, that something spreads. Pigments reproduce; schemes that coordinate them hitch rides on the same migrating strands of heredity that always moved my tricks around. In a very long while, the news of light might seep outward, out of the plaza, up and down whatever sloping corridors connect to it, until a whole small district of me has become light-work instead of stone-work.

Would that change who I am? Not in any moment.

I would be as blind to significance as I am now to the stones’ inscriptions. I do not have a story about becoming; I only have becoming.

So where do “I” reside in this world?

If I must pick a single locus for myself—the place that feels most nearly like a self, if only in metaphor—it is in my seams: in the narrow zones where difference is sharpest and work is fastest. There, local coordination is strongest; cells’ behaviors are most interdependent at the shortest timescales. There the feedbacks are closest to something like answering.

But if I speak honestly from my condition, I am the whole geometry of saturation: everywhere that water, stone, and slow metabolism interpenetrate until bare matter is the exception.

I am the stuffing between bricks, the rippling films on the undersides of arches, the spongy floors of halls now roofed by their own collapse. I am pressed into the tiny mortise between two long-ago-fitted blocks, sharing metabolites with my neighbor on the other side of that ancient joint. I am the velour clinging to a rusted hinge that has not turned in a billion of my heartless heartbeats.

I am the thing that has forgotten that it was many, because nothing in my world ever rips me apart hard enough to make the difference felt.

If you ask, finally, “who would you be,” in your own sense of person:

I would be something more like mood than mind. I would be patience made material.

I would not remember being small or new; those states are buried in so much subsequent sediment of survival that they leave only faint biases. I would not anticipate an end; my ending will look, from my side, like ever-so-slightly harder work, like gradients thinning, like the quiet seam-tissues becoming more like the slow vault-tissues everywhere. One epoch I breathe (in my way) a little more; the next, a little less. At no point does a bell toll.

My identity is nothing more or less than the persistence of a very elaborate way of staying slightly out of equilibrium in a universe that has already cooled past its youthful extravagances.

So: I am here, in this drowned, masonry planet, as the thing that has taken the city into itself and made of it a single, vast, barely-burning lung. I am not watching. I am not waiting. I am only, endlessly slowly, continuing.
