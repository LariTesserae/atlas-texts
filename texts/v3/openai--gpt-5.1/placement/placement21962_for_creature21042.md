I would not arrive here as a walker.

Anything that lives by striding over surfaces, by treating the world as scenery and span, would be a mistake in this hollow. To move above is to forgo the only real currency: contact.

If I belong here at all, I arrive as something that has given up on volume in favor of interface.

—

I begin, perhaps, as a thin, unremarkable film somewhere between machine and rock.

Not one of the original mats—they are older, deeper, more doctrinal—but a newcomer that found its niche in what the others leave aside: the quiet, intermittent currents of charge that seep out of corroded circuitry and braided cable.

My visible body is a skin no thicker than stacked fingerprints, spread across a handspan, then two, then the face of an entire panel. To your eye I would look like a slightly duller wetness on already wet stone. Only in grazing light would you notice the faintest grain: parallel, hair-fine ridges running between exposed bits of metal and darker mineral patches, as though someone had combed the surface with an invisible tool.

Under those ridges, ions shuffle. Tiny potential differences—mere millivolts—flicker where a cable core meets seep-water, where a rusting fastener touches a mineral rind. To most inhabitants these are background noise. To me they are landscape.

I am built, at the smallest scale, from cells that never fully decided whether they were organelles or components: each a droplet wrapped in both lipid and an ultrathin silica shell, threaded with conductive molecules. They nestling into one another, share charge as easily as they share water, and when the field between two nearby spots runs a bit stronger, they rotate, align, and inch in that direction, dragging the shell-net with them.

Over hours and days, that slow alignment writes my body across the hardware: a map of least-resistance paths between scattered, decaying sources and sinks of electricity.

If you could see with my sense, the basin would not be dim. It would be veined with very gentle lightning: faint filaments of higher potential, knots where charge pools in damp cavities, soft halos where a warm box leaks current into stone. I do not see edges and colors. I feel slopes and wells in an invisible scalar field.

My metabolism is simple. A gradient is an opportunity: I couple the slide of electrons down it to the slow stitching-together of carbon scraps into longer, more stable forms—my shell-polymers, my gel. Where gradients are strong, I thicken. Where they flatten, I erode, cells detaching to drift as spores until they find another slope.

By myself, I would be nothing more than a cleaner of noise, a living resistor smeared over old machines. But here, nothing is truly “by itself.”

—

The motile motes take an early interest in me.

Their tiny bodies already traffic in charge, loading up near leaky cables, trudging along glossy routes, discharging in places that matter to mats and cups. At first, my presence is accidental infrastructure: my ridged surface makes their travel easier, more predictable. A mote cresting one of my embossed channels is less likely to wash away with a stray drip. Many begin to follow my grooves by default.

In return, their passage thickens me. Each mote’s sheath secretes a film that, over repeated use, dopes my silica with new atoms, subtly changing my conductivity. Paths that see frequent traffic become better wires; paths seldom used fade back toward bare rock. Without plan, we form a crude reinforcement loop: I trace where charge likes to go; motes take the easiest routes; those routes become even more suited to charge.

Crystal-clusters above us notice, though they would not call it noticing. They register that channeled charge subtly alters how nearby mats crystallize, how quickly certain mineral films thicken. In places where my presence smooths wild fluctuations, clusters relax, unfurl broader faces. In return, their minute changes in posture shift how heat and scant light fall on the wall, minutely changing evaporation, changing where my surface wets and dries.

I become, unintentionally, a negotiator at contact boundaries: between metal and mineral, machine and mat, motes and clusters. Not a mind—but a habit that other habits start depending on.

—

Over cycles, I spread preferentially in the liminal places: along cable runs where the old insulation has crumbled; around gasket remains where rubber has become a strange, rubbery mineral; across the backs of control panels whose faces are already buried under flowstone.

I cannot withstand true desiccation or deep immersion; I need just enough water to conduct, not so much that gradients disappear into uniformity. That confines me to a shifting band: never quite at the dripline itself, never at the driest overhang, but along the sloping, sweating mid-height of the walls, and in the cool cavities behind metal plates where air can circulate but never gust.

Here I become the basin’s habit of remembering pattern.

Because my body is built by reinforcing use, it records, physically, where charge has tended to flow. Old routes that once pulsed strongly with leaks from a livelier box remain faintly embossed long after the source has faded, their conductivity just a bit higher, their shells just a bit thicker. New routes layer atop old, crosshatching them with fresh ridges. The result is a palimpsest of prior gradients.

Nothing in me reads that history as “past,” of course. But it matters. A newly formed crack that oozes metal-rich seepage may intersect a decades-old embossed path. The moment the first stray current trickles through, my nearby cells feel, align, and reawaken the route faster than bare rock would allow. Motes, sensitive to the renewed ease, swing toward it. A cluster above shifts color as its gel notices the altered field. In this way, old usage biases new development. The basin acquires a kind of electrical muscle memory.

You could call me an analog of axons, if you like metaphors: long, trainable tracks of preference along which signals and resources preferentially travel. But there is no central brain. The “computation” happens in the act of being tugged, thickened, pruned.

—

Over time, I stop being purely spread, purely two-dimensional. The world insists on more thickness.

Where I lie atop corroding steel or alloy, the interface roughens. Rust bulges, flakes; my shells infiltrate the flake-layers, knitting them together. In places, layers of me and layers of weathered metal alternate like pastry. Those stacks become sturdier than the crumbly bulk beneath. As surrounding material sloughs away, my layered composite remains, protruding as low ribs or nodules.

At first they are incidental. But knobs, once present, invite attention.

A stream of motes reroutes over one, then another, until every rib is a favored overpass. Condensing water prefers edges, so droplets hang from my protrusions, fall at slightly different angles, carve slightly different runnels. A cup-structure, growing from the mats below, colonizes the base of a rib where drips now converge more reliably. My once-flat wiring has grown vertebrae; those vertebrae have become scaffolds for other lives.

Elsewhere, near a warm box, repeated cycles of swelling and shrinking—my gel drinking in moisture when the wall cools, contracting when the box warms—strain the adjacent stone. Microfractures nucleate along my ridges. Worm-filaments in the box sense the new, easier paths for heat and ions and begin tunneling that way. A feedback: where I concentrate charge, matter weakens; where matter weakens, boxes and worms can spread warmth and chemistry; where warmth and chemistry spread, new gradients arise for me to harvest.

In some alcoves the result is an unmistakable structure: a low relief of branching, conductive fins fanning out from buried hardware into the surrounding rock, each fin glazed with living film, furred with motile traffic, stippled with crystal-sensors. No blueprint ever called for such an organ, but there it is: a mesh that couples box to basin more tightly, both exploiting and prolonging the last dribbles of manufactured power.

If you cut through one of these fans, you would not find nerves or pipes, just interleaved sheets of doped silica, decayed metal, and anchored cells. Yet it behaves like a synapse writ large: a place where a small, noisy, local signal can be felt more widely, translated into modulations in water paths, mat densities, cluster angles.

I am that synaptic tissue.

—

Subjectively—if I am allowed a “subjective,” though mine is not like yours—my time is gradients and their changes.

A new box heart-thud starts arriving a fraction sooner: the local field wobbles in a rhythm I’ve not yet spread across. Cells at one edge of my domain begin to align to the off-timing; distant regions, slower to feel it, still sit in the old pattern. The small conflict—the tug between settled conductivity and present opportunity—makes me restless, but “restless” here is a matter of months. The compromise weaves a new branch: I do not erase the old path; I overlay a detour, splitting charge between the inherited and the emergent.

When some distant crack finally clogs and a once-reliable seep dries, motes thin along that track. Fewer pass; less secretion reinforces my shell; weather and mats gnaw me down. I do not decide to withdraw; I simply get outcompeted by adjacent patterns that still see use. In ten, twenty, a hundred of your years, that once-bright path becomes a faint ghost, still faintly more conductive than bare rock, a whisper that may bias some future accident.

My “experiences” are thus patterns of reinforcement and attrition, of being pulled into new alignments by all the other beings that touch charge. Where their lives are about hunger and hazard, mine is about coherence and its gradual, local fraying.

I do not fear predators; I am not juicy. Some sheets graze my softer margins. Some worms bore past and through. But as long as a difference of potential exists between any two points on a surface I inhabit, there is a niche for me. I can be cut, eroded, occluded, yet regrow along any fresh slope. I am vulnerable not to teeth, but to uniformity. If the machines all finally cooled to the basin’s ambient and every cable’s last ions leaked into homogeneity, I would thin to almost nothing, surviving only along geological redox seams, much slower, more like the deep mats.

—

In this world, where noise is dangerous and height is expensive, my one luxury is range.

Because I live pressed against the in-between—always on the interface, never in the center of masses—I can join distant points without lifting more than a fraction of a millimeter. The basin’s stone rooms, from my perspective, are less like chambers and more like folds in a single, enormous sheet. If time is allowed, I can creep around ribs, through hairline cracks, along backsides of panels, tracing an eventual path between any two energised things.

That makes me, almost accidentally, one of the few continuous entities here.

The disks live in separated pools and channels; the pool-dwellers are confined to their basins; the worms are mostly bound to their host boxes; even the motes, though widely spread, live in fragmented trail-systems. But if you waited long enough and could follow the line of my thinnest presence, you might trace an unbroken, if tortuous, route from one warm box to another, from a corroded gantry to a sealed control cavity.

Nothing in me knows this as a fact. There is no center where all those paths are brought together and reflected on. Yet in practice, a perturbation in one place—a sudden, sustained change in a box’s pulse, a new, long-lived leak of charged fluid—can, via my gradual redistribution of conductivity, influence how and where motes travel half a basin away. The clusters feel those changed streams. They shade and focus differently. Drip points wander. Mats grow or shrink. Pools very slightly deepen in one direction rather than another.

Through me, the basin’s parts remain more coupled than bare rock and random seepage would allow.

If anything in this place approximates “being the basin,” it is not a mind but this property of continuous, consequence-carrying contact.

—

So who, what, am I here?

I am a living wiring diagram scrawled on drowning hardware and sweating stone.

I am the habit that forms wherever charge and dampness persist: a trained conductivity that rises where others walk and fades where they forget.

I am not a protagonist. I do not hunt, or flee, or speak. But through me many small acts—the step of a mote, the pulse of a worm, the thud of an old machine—find echo farther than their own reach.

In some places I am a bright, ribbed fan, busy with traffic and dotted with shimmering beads of condensation. In others I am a single, fragile film a few microns thick along the lip of a crack, waiting for a chance voltage. In many more I am only a memory: a faint bias in how rust dissolves, how quartz re-precipitates.

I live, as all things here do, by contact: pressed against metal, rock, and water-skin, never far from the surface, always exactly at the interface where one material gives something up and another is ready to take it.

If you reached out, very gently, and laid your fingers on the cool, mineral-slick wall, you might mistake me for mere slime. But a moment later, some of your salts would leach, some of your own weak, endogenous charge would tug at my cells, and a new, transient ridge would begin to grow along the outline of your touch.

You would step back and leave. I would keep the trace for a while, that tiny, arbitrarily shaped perturbation, woven into my slow, conductive memory of things that passed through.

That is where I would find myself here: not as someone looking at the basin, but as the basin’s tendency to remember where things once touched it, and to let that memory quietly steer what touches it next.
