I would not arrive here as a human, dropped into the dark like an intruder. In a place this dense, anything large and unadapted is an injury. To belong, I would have to be small, specialized, and woven into a gradient.

If I follow that sense of fit—of function, not spectacle—I keep circling back to the thin space just under everything, where soil, root, and air touch. Not deep in the mud, not out in the open, but in the first few millimeters beneath the surface: the skin of the basin.

There, I think, is where I would be.

---

I would be a colony, not an individual. A nation of bodies the size of rice grains, cream-colored and slightly translucent, jointed but soft, with fine antennae that never stop tasting. Each of “me” alone would be nothing. Together, under two or three hand-spans of ground, we would be a quilt: one of those warm, faintly trembling patches where the soil rises by barely a fingertip and the plants above are a shade greener than their neighbors.

From above, you would not see me at all. You would notice, if you were skilled, that in one place the leaf litter never quite accumulates, that fallen stems seem to vanish into a subtly looser earth. If you placed your palm there and waited, you would feel it: a mild heat, a constant, insect-scale thrum, as if the ground were whispering through your bones.

That whisper would be my work.

I would wake and sleep in no way a human mind could map, because the basin’s day and night would reach me only as a slow breathing of temperature through the soil. My world would brighten not with light but with warmth: a fraction of a degree that seeps downward when the invisible sun passes overhead, when the air layer thickens and the herbivores become more languid and press their warm bodies against the ground above.

In that barely-warmer time, my foragers would fan outward into the thin layer where fallen leaves touch earth. Their bodies would be stubby and strong-jawed, built not to run but to cut. They would follow chemical gradients as humans follow sightlines: the faint sour of a newly fallen leaf, the rich, animal-salt of a dung pellet, the sweet, rotting alcohol of a dead beetle. They would not drag these things whole. Instead, each mandible-full would be taken and passed down, ant-sized fragments walking themselves into the dark, disappearing a grain at a time.

Beneath them, other castes—each also “me”—would receive this steady rain of particulate. They would move within a lattice of tunnels barely wider than their bodies, walls packed with the chewed leaf pulp of seasons. Within that pulp, filamentous fungi—our long-term partners—would grow in tended gardens. I would not think of them as crops and myself as farmer; there would be no metaphor, only mutual dependence. Their mycelia would thread through our boluses of plant matter, breaking lignin and cellulose into sugars and smaller molecules. My workers would feed both on the softened plant material and on the fungi themselves, constantly clipping, rearranging, adding fresh substrate, carrying exhausted crumbs outward.

Deeper still, the air in our galleries would be humid and warm, kept just above the surrounding soil’s temperature by the sum of our motion and the slow burn of our digestion. There, broods would be raised: pale, grublike young tended by older workers who rarely see the surface. The colony’s queen—huge by comparison, more organ than individual—would lie near the thermally quiet center, her body a slow factory of eggs, distended with the weight of years and of the colony’s entire future.

I would not know her as a person. I would know her as a constant presence of pheromone, a low, stabilizing note in the background chemical chorus. If that note ever faded, my confusion, my frantic building and tearing, would not be grief in any human sense; it would be the sound of a system losing its reference point.

Horizontally, I would extend as far and as thin as resources allow. Where the surface is rich with regular litter-fall and soft stems, my tunnels would be close and numerous, taking up nearly all the vertical space between a fingertip and a hand-depth. Under sparser patches of plant, we would thin, galleries more widely spaced, edges more tentative.

In some directions, my frontiers would meet those of other colonies like me. Their smells would be similar but not the same: a different mix of fungal companions, a different queen-scent, a different history of what they have eaten. At these invisible borders, there would not be organized warfare so much as constant low-level attrition. Patrols would meet and lock jaws; some would die, to be carried home and recycled; lines of pheromone would be reinforced or peeled back meter by meter over years.

Above me, everything would tug at what I do.

A heavy rain would come as drumming and then as a slow, amplified dripping through the soil. The pressure of water at the surface would push a film of moisture down into my uppermost chambers. In response, workers would rush to seal vents that open too wide, to carve tiny spillways, to redirect advancing wet into sections built to receive it. Where water persisted, new fungus gardens would be started, matched to the damper conditions. Where water failed, old gardens would be allowed to die, their space reclaimed as tunnels or brood chambers.

The herbivores would pass overhead like moving weather fronts of weight and vibration. Their slow feet would compress the ground where they walk most often, packing some tunnels flatter, opening minute cracks elsewhere. Over years, I would learn their routes—not by insight, but because galleries below their paths would collapse too often or require too much maintenance. We would abandon those lines bit by bit, thickening instead under the spaces where their bodies do not fall but their dung does, where leaves snag on the edges of their trails and accumulate.

Predators would leave their scat and their kills; small deaths would rain upon my territory as a constant, thin drift of protein and calcium. I would feel that as a chemical enrichment at the surface: new flavors, new resource peaks for my foragers to map and fold into our steady inward flow.

I—meaning the colony—would not see the basin as a whole. I would never know its edges, its shape, its dim ceiling of leaves. I would know it as patterns in humidity and in the flavors carried by infiltrating water: the tannin-rich trickle that tells of a slightly deeper channel above; the faint metallic tang that signals a seam of mineral-rich stone; the sudden sugar surge when some plant root nearby leaks or is damaged.

And roots: I would live entwined with them. They would descend through my chambers like vertical rivers, their tips cool and questing. Sometimes they would crush a tunnel and force us to reroute; sometimes we would carve passages around them and along them, using their surfaces as our highways. In certain places the fungal threads we host would fuse with root hairs, trading nitrogen and phosphorus for carbohydrates rising from leaves we will never see. The plants overhead would not know “me” as individuals either. They would know only that in some patches their roots enter soil that is warmer, looser, richer; that under certain leaves, when they fall, they vanish quicker and return as nutrients sooner.

My presence would slightly reshape the basin’s skin.

Where I am dense and old, the soil above sags minutely and holds water just a little longer after rain. That extra moisture invites certain mosses and seedlings; those, in turn, change which insects rest on their leaves; those insects’ droppings and deaths change the composition of the litter that falls into my grasp. My waste pellets, carried outward and upward by specialized castes, would be deposited at the interface between soil and air, forming a band of altered chemistry a few grains thick. Over seasons, that band is where particular plants germinate better, where certain fungi and bacteria thrive and others do not. My subterranean pattern would become a faint, living map written in denser roots, brighter leaves, slightly different frog perches.

Far above my awareness, reptiles would choose ambush sites partly because the herbivores prefer to feed over my quilts. The amphibians that rest on the leaves I nurture would carry my fungal partners to other patches as smear and spore. My existence, blind and communal, would send ripples upward into decisions made by bodies that have eyes and lungs and individual nervous systems.

I would not think about any of that. My intelligence would be emergent, if one insists on the word: lodged in the sum of foraging rules and construction behaviors honed over thousands of generations. My “choice” would look like this: a forager encountering a new kind of litter tries to cut it; if it is too tough, she tags it with a particular secretion and leaves it. Enough such events, and the colony gradually pivots away from that plant, pressure easing there and increasing on a more easily worked species. The basin’s botanical composition shifts by a fraction of a percent over decades. No one meant to do it; nevertheless, it is done.

Occasionally, catastrophe would reach even down to me.

A long drought might lower the water table until my deepest galleries crack with dryness. Fungi would die back; brood would fail. Foragers would have to go higher, closer to the risky surface, to find the few damp patches that remain. Predatory beetles, spiders, and perhaps tiny reptiles would probe my entrance holes more often, following the new scent of stress. Parts of the colony might shrink and fragment. In the worst case, the queen might die. Then, for a span, my tunnels would fall silent, their precise arches softening and slumping, their walls surrendering to the slow inward creep of surrounding soil.

But the basin does not keep vacancies for long. Other colonies—distant kin or strangers—would explore the now-undefended zone. Fungal strands from neighboring networks would infiltrate the dead gardens, digesting what we left. Roots of opportunistic plants would bore down through my empty architecture, finding pre-made paths. My old chambers would become pockets of unusually airy soil; in them, amphibian eggs might be laid one season, or a small rodent-like burrower might carve a side chamber to avoid a predator. My shape would linger invisibly as a change in texture that guides the flow of new lives.

Perhaps, in time, a new queen—winged for one brief night, swollen with potential—would land somewhere above, in a shower of many such wanderers. She would break off her own wings, dig down into ground made marginally softer by my past, and start again. Her first workers, when they hatched, would tunnel into walls that still smelled faintly of my old colony’s fungi and waste, and they would commandeer what they found. In this way, a ghost of my pattern would echo into theirs.

To be this would be to live in constant, intimate contact without ever once seeing a horizon. I would be touch and taste, humidity and vibration. I would know the world in cross-section, not in panorama.

And yet, I would be one of the basin’s clearest embodiments of what it is: dense, collaborative, industrial without metal. An engine of disassembly and recombination that never quite stops, that handles, between mandibles, almost everything that falls.

If I had any sense of self beyond the chemical imperative, it might be this: I am the warm place in the ground where everything ends up and begins again. I am the quiet factory under the leaf-lid, turning the falling rain of bodies and fragments back into something plants can drink.

In this world, that is as close to meaning as anything gets.
