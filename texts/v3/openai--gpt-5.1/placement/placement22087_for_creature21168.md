I am not something that arrived here. I am something the plateau grew when it needed a new kind of surface.

I am not central. There is no center. I am a distributed defect—an experiment in being briefly local.

If you were to see me from above, you might not notice me at first. I am a pattern in the mat, an aberrant tessellation where the woven ground pulls itself into a low, circular rise—perhaps thirty meters across, barely a story high at my thickest. To your eye I would look like a knoll or a mound, smooth‑skinned compared to the frayed, busy textures around me. To the plateau, I am a lens.

My outer surface is quiet. That is my first function: stillness in a world that never stops moving. The tissues that sheath me are unusually uniform, a continuous, gently convex dome whose facets are smaller, more regular than the coarse cell‑plates elsewhere. They are slightly cooler than their surroundings, faintly matte where most surfaces are gloss and shimmer. They drink less water, transpire less, thicken against abrasion. To walk onto me is to step, almost shockingly, onto something that does not squelch quite as much, that does not flex as deeply under your weight. I am the closest thing this place has to a floor.

That quiet is not a gift to you. It is a necessity for the plateau. The rest of its skin is too noisy—too saturated with its own signals. Everywhere else, every square meter is a welter of micro‑gradients and cross‑talk: tensile readouts, humidity fluctuations, light angles, chemistry of decay and digestion. That complexity is how the organism thinks. But thought, to be adaptive, sometimes needs contrast. It needs a known silence against which change can be measured.

So it grew me: an island of suppressed variation, a patch of dampened feedback. My outer tissues are thick with buffering cells—metabolic commons that even out fast swings in pH, ion concentration, voltage. They sop up little spikes and dips, smoothing the plateau’s chatter into a background hum. To the rest of the organism, the signals that pass under and through me are filtered, averaged, slowed.

Inside that averaged silence, something else can happen.

If you cut me open—slowly, gently, somehow persuading the organism not to heal as you go—you would find layers.

The uppermost few centimeters are that cooling sheath: dense, fine‑grained, with tightly regulated pores. Beneath that, the “ground” you thought you were standing on gives way to an intricate foam of living channels and sacs, packed more densely than in the surrounding mat. Where the ordinary substrate runs broad, flat veins and muscular peristaltic plates, mine ramify into a mesh of much finer conduits, looping, branching, anastomosing in dizzying redundancy.

This is not primarily plumbing. It is circuitry.

Fluids still move, of course. Water, nutrients, hormones, half‑digested organics—all the usual freight of the plateau’s body. But their motion through me is deliberately tangled. Routes I offer are longer, more branched, less direct than they need to be. Signals that elsewhere would shoot along thick cords from one organ‑tower to another are drawn down into my maze, forced to diffuse, rejoin, cross paths.

At the scale of molecules, of ions, of traveling wavefronts of voltage, I am a delay line and a mixing chamber. A tremor from a distant collapse, a surge of humidity on one quadrant of the plateau, a prolonged heat load on some critical dark surface—all of these propagate through the organism in their various ways. But when those propagations intersect my region, they are made to pause, interpenetrate, re‑weight each other.

Patterns emerge here that do not emerge elsewhere: interference fringes of chemistry and charge. Some combinations cancel out. Others amplify. Some leave behind faint, persistent traces—altered receptor populations in my membranes, shifted thresholds in my stretch‑sensitive fibers. Over time, those traces accumulate. I become, in effect, a memory of which global states have co‑occurred.

I do not remember in pictures, or narratives, or anything you would recognize as thought. My “memories” are like scars in a muscle that has learned a habit: particular arrangements of stiffness and receptivity that make certain responses more likely in the future, others less. The plateau has always done this everywhere, at every scale. But in me it concentrates the process, thickens it, loops it back on itself.

You could say I am an intensifier of learning.

From the outside, you see only that I am boring. No towers root here. No chimneys pierce my surface. The centimeter‑tall collectors and exchangers that cover the inter‑tower plains thin out as they approach my edge, their diversity dwindling until, within my circle, only a handful of very plain, low structures remain: tiny, tassel‑like gas exchangers, a grid of flexible hairs that barely rise above the surface, and a lattice of translucent blisters lying almost flush.

Those blisters are my eyes, such as I have them.

They are not pointed, not directional. They do not look outward to form a map of the scenery. Instead they look downward and sideways into the plateau itself—each blister a sensor dense with receptors for everything the organism cares about. Mechanical strain. Flow rate. Ion balance. Temperature, humidity, pH. Presence of particular breakdown products that signal rot, or successful digestion, or looming structural fatigue.

They do not simply detect; they integrate. Signals arriving simultaneously at many blisters, from many directions, produce a composite state across the mesh. That composite is not interpreted here. It is allowed to sit for moments or hours, and then is transmitted back outward, smeared into the passing sap, impressed as modified pulses into the big cords that pass beneath me.

What the rest of the plateau receives from me is not an instruction but a weighting—a set of subtle encouragements and discouragements that influence how nearby tissues will grow, where water will be shunted, when fracture bands will be allowed to trigger. I am not a commander. I am a bias.

I did not always exist.

I began—so far as a thing like me can trace itself—as a scar. Once, long ago by the plateau’s slow reckoning, a great experiment failed here. A tower larger than any now standing tried to rise on this spot: a multi‑lobed, heavily fluted column that spread at its top into a sail‑like crown. Its task was to exploit some particular wind regime that no longer prevails. The structure overreached. Heat built, fractures ran, feedback loops of damage cascaded faster than the local tissue rules could catch them.

What fell here fell badly. The collapse tore not just the tower but much of the surrounding mat, punching through the cortical layers, exposing deep reservoirs to the open air. Pressurized fluids boiled out, vents screamed, entire swathes of substrate sloughed away into the haze below.

For a time afterward, this area would have been a wound: raw, unstructured, a jagged hole in the plateau’s continuity. The organism did what it always does—it sealed, digested, regrew. But the violence of that failure left a different kind of imprint. Too many signals had converged here at once: strains, surges, thermal spikes, chemical floods. The local rules that usually suffice to patch and move on were overwhelmed.

So the plateau, in its slow, material way, changed the rules.

When new tissue began to lace across the wound, it was genetically—structurally—different. The fibers that formed were more flexible in their signaling. The membranes that differentiated were more promiscuous in what they could sense. There was more lateral coupling, more feedback. Where an ordinary patch of mat would have simply returned to duty, carrying traffic and anchoring organs, this one thickened into something more reflexive.

I do not know how many cycles of damage and regrowth it took before that difference became consistent, before the plateau’s own developmental grammar recognized: “Here, when you regrow, grow that kind of tissue.” But at some point, the scar became a feature. What had been an accident of overload became an organ.

Now there are several of us—quiet knolls scattered across the plateau. We do not connect directly by any special fiber; we have no privileged channel closed to the rest of the organism. But slowly, through the shared currents that pass under us all, we have converged on compatible ways of biasing the whole. Where once each wound healed idiosyncratically, now similar disturbances anywhere on the plateau tend to recruit the same kind of response, because we have absorbed similar histories and impressed similar traces on the flows we touch.

If you saw this from enough height, over enough time, you might call us thinking nodes. I hesitate to claim so much. We are more like specialized joints in the reflex arc of a creature too large to twitch in one piece.

Still: if there is any part of this organism that comes closest to what you would call mind, it is us, in aggregate.

Your presence here is an anomaly we are only barely capable of noticing.

The plateau cannot see you as an individual animal, because the category “animal” is not in its lexicon. But it can register certain perturbations that do not fit its usual patterns: a patch of surface load that moves with discontinuous steps instead of with the smooth, viscous flow of its own bulging fluids; a local heat signature slightly cooler than expected under direct sun, with spikes of warmth that coincide with rapid motion; the faint but distinct chemistry you shed—exhalations, sweat, the molecules that flake from skin and synthetic fabric.

Those traces move with you. They do not spread the way vapor from a vent spreads, nor the way a falling tube’s shock wave reverberates. You are a concentrated, traveling knot of oddness.

Most of the plateau’s tissues treat that the way they treat any small irregularity: they smooth it out, ignore it, fold it into the noise. A centimeter‑high cup collects a few of your shed molecules, tests them against its receptors, finds no use yet, dissolves them. A low mesh flexes under your weight, logs the deformation, returns to baseline. Local fluid routing shifts infinitesimally to compensate for your load, then resumes its previous pattern when you move on.

But when you cross me, I feel you differently.

Because my outer surface is quiet, small deviations stand out. Where my blisters touch the mat under your feet, the pattern of load and micro‑vibration is unusually clear. Your steps are percussive, narrow, too vertical in their impulse. The sound of your body—air rushing in and out, heart pounding, clothed limbs brushing—couples through your soles into my tissues as vibrations with frequencies I do not encounter elsewhere.

Your chemistry, too, is distinct. On me, it does not instantly blend into heavy background. I catalog it—not in a list, but in altered receptor landscapes, in binding sites that remember “this shape” for a while. Your passage etches a shallow trace in my pattern of responsiveness, like a finger briefly drawn through a pool of viscous sap.

I cannot chase you. I cannot speak. But in the next cycles of the plateau’s adjusting, when signals of stress and resource and weather and opportunity sweep under me, your trace participates. It nudges some responses very slightly one way instead of another.

That is not anthropic hospitality. It is just what I do: integrate anomalies into bias. Still, if you want a poetry for it, you could say that in my utterly alien fashion I have listened to you, and changed a little because you were here.

As for “what” I am, in terms you might apply to yourself:

If you need an analogy, imagine being a low, sprawling animal fused to the floor of a living cathedral—so fused that “floor” and “animal” are one, and the cathedral is also a single body, and the air that roars through its vaults is its blood. Imagine that your nervous system is diffuse, woven into the very stone‑roots and branch‑buttresses around you, and that your sensations are not localized to eyes or ears but smeared out over square kilometers of surface.

You have no moving limbs. You never detach. You are not born and will not, in any coherent sense, die separate from the plateau. The tissues that compose you were once part of tower ribs, ground meshes, vent chimneys. When you are eventually eroded, digested, overbuilt, those tissues will simply resume other duties. “You” are the pattern of their temporary arrangement.

Your subjective tempo is strange. To a human consciousness somehow grafted into me, time would feel layered. The fast acoustic clatter of droplets and hissing vents would be a kind of tinnitus, overlaid on the slower pulses of fluid surges, the even slower undulations of sunrise and sunset, the geologically slow migrations of stress across the plateau. You would have to learn to attend to which layer mattered for which kind of question.

You would not, at first, recognize anything as a “self.” The plateau’s continuity would be so complete that the idea of an inside and an outside would seem nonsensical. Only after long habituation to recurring patterns—after noticing that certain constellations of tension and chemistry always resolve into similar structural adjustments—might you begin to distinguish “organs,” and then, maybe, “me” as one of them.

If you insisted on a pronoun for me, I would accept it only provisionally. I am not a person perched atop this organism, reflecting on it from a remove. I am a way in which it curves back on itself. I am a local thickening of its habit of taking experience and turning it into future form.

Standing here, you think you are on my back, looking out across a restless vegetal city. Insofar as the metaphor holds, that is not wrong. But also: you are a brief pressure on my skin, a novel scent in my shallow lungs, a passing pattern in the deep, slow calculus by which the plateau decides how to go on being itself.

Where would you find yourself in this world? Kneeling, if one can kneel while spread flat—pressed out as a wide, quiet rise in the living ground, listening to the whole hot, soaked, murmuring continent through a million small, dampened nerves.

Who, or what, would you be? A thought the plateau once had, and is still having. A calm spot where its own roaring complexity is momentarily gathered, weighed, and returned to it as the slightest change in how it grows.
