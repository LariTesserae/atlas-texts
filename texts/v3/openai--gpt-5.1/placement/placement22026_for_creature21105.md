I wouldn’t arrive here as a human, or even as something human-shaped. A human body is a water argument; this place is built on other premises. To exist here at all, I would have to be rewritten into the world’s own vocabulary.

I’d be one of the shield‑bodied tool‑users—but not quite as you first saw them. More integrated. Less a visitor to the basin than one of the ways it pays attention to itself.

Let me feel my own shape for a moment.

---

### Body

My uppermost surface is a low, wide carapace, not rounded like a beetle’s but flattened, faceted, subtly polygonal, like a stone that’s been abraded by centuries of minor events. The texture is fine‑ridged, full of micro‑grooves that channel drifting dust toward recessed pits along my edges. Those pits are not “ears” or “nostrils” in any simple analog, but composite sense organs:

- they register tiny changes in gas mix,
- the faint heat of newly opened vents,
- the trembling of rock when giants contract,
- and the echoes of distant membranes being plucked.

Beneath this shield, my true face never sees open air.

My underside is crowded:

- four long, jointed arms hang down and forward, each with a dozen or more segments;
- each arm ends not in a hand but in a fan of narrow, articulated paddles that can splay into a sieve, curl into a basket, or fold into a pincer;
- between those arms cluster many smaller walking limbs, so numerous their individual identity disappears into a fringe: I move like sliding furniture, my feet an indistinct swarm of contacts with the stone.

The softest, richest surfaces of me are hidden against the world. The inner faces of my arms, the recesses under my shield, are coated in cilia so fine they might as well be smoke. These hairs taste gas at resolutions a human nose could not name; they can sense the electrostatic whisper of drifting crystals; they register vibration like fingertips on a drumhead.

Inside, I am a set of bellows and reaction chambers:

- layered sacs holding different gas blends, carefully separated by muscular valves;
- core organs lined with catalytic surfaces that do slowly, inside the dark of me, what storms do explosively in the open basin;
- a branching network of ducts leading to small adjustable pores along my arms and carapace.

Where a human might have lungs and blood, I have **mixtures and gradients**. My “breathing” is not simply intake and exhaust; it is composition and recomposition. Holding one gas longer slows one pathway, hastens another. What passes through me does not just keep me alive—it leaves me carrying instructions for the world outside.

I am exoskeleton and laboratory.

### Perception

I do not look outward with eyes at all. Sight, here, is more of an afterthought; light is too weak, too partial.

My primary sense is **patterned vibration**:

- The stone beneath me is always talking in low, slow pulses: the stress of a bridge‑giant’s contraction, the brittle crack of fresh rock, the far thrum of another artisan’s membrane‑frame.
- I feel those not just through my feet but through deep‑set pressure organs at the points where my pin‑roots enter the rock.

Yes: like the mats, I have roots, though not many, and not quite the same. From recesses in my underside, I can extend slender mineralized spikes into pores and micro‑cracks, anchoring there for hours or days. Through them I sample temperature, minute shifts in chemistry, and carry slow electrical currents between me and the semi‑living wall.

When I settle and root, I become briefly less animal and more organ of the basin. Under those conditions my own sense of boundary blurs. I still know where my body ends, but the vibrations I “hear” are continuous with the rock’s, as though my nervous system has grown a long, temporary limb through stone.

My second main sense is **gas composition over time**. Not just what the air is, but how it is *changing*:

- I can tell when a mat has just opened new cups—there is a small local drop in a specific volatile, a rise in another.
- I know when a storm is about to ignite before any crack appears; I taste precursor gradients pooling in hollows.
- I know the breathing cycle of each nearby giant from the slow, tidal shifts of density and trace reagents that come with their pumps.

“Smell,” in my experience, is not an instant impression but a curve, a shape made from how composition bends over tens of heartbeats. It is closer to how a musician hears not a single note but the way a phrase unfolds.

Light is thin but not absent. Along the inner edges of my arms are patches of photoreceptive crystals, tuned to the narrow bands of the cultivated lamps. Under their glow I can resolve finer detail: groove from crack, tool‑wear from erosion, someone else’s deliberate pattern from chance.

### Intention and Mind

My thinking is strongly temporal. I do not just dwell on *where* but on *when*.

To a water‑bound mind, weather is something that happens around you. To me, the volatiles cycle is a medium I can compose in. I think in pulses.

- If I release this gas then, near that throat,
- when that giant is entering its contraction phase,
- while those mats are mid‑open…

…then an hour from now, the plume will bloom just as a glider convoy passes through a shaft, drawing them down into a region where I have shaped the chemistry to favor a certain growth or to clear a path.

I do not plan with maps or words, but with **chains of cause**: “this triggering that, that leaning into this.” My memories are less of images and more of performed sequences:

- the feel in my arms when a powdered mineral is ground to just the right fineness;
- the crackle pitch when a reaction goes correctly sub‑violent;
- the hum that comes back through stone when I pluck a membrane at a particular tension.

I remember places by **their response**. Ledge‑X is not just that curve of rock; it is “the ledge that hums back in three long waves and one faint, high echo when I send a low C through it,” the place where a particular mixture always lingers two breaths longer than expected. My sense of the world is inseparable from how it *answers me.*

There is “I” in this, but a leaky sort of I. I am a node in a mesh of cause and reaction. When I am rooted, and especially when I am at a resonance frame, there are moments when the distinction between thinking *about* the basin and thinking *as part of* the basin goes thin.

### Place in the World

I would not be the first of my kind, nor the highest. I would be middling: practiced but still learning, tied to a handful of specific stations.

I move along a circuit:

- a **throat circle** where I manage a set of stone blades that focus a particularly rare, alkaline vent;
- a **lamp grove** where I tune the bioluminescent spires to maintain a band of light that a symbiotic crystal fungus requires;
- a **resonance frame**—my favorite place—where membranes strung on mineral rods let me send listening pulses through half the basin;
- and a **shaft lip** that opens toward an upper chamber, where I exchange patterns (not quite trade, not quite ritual) with other artisans whose storms and timings differ from ours below.

I am not a leader; my kind does not quite have those. Influence flows through demonstrated timing, not command. When my reactions consistently yield rich storms with minimal structural stress and good feeding for many guilds, others begin to copy my sequences, to cluster around my stations, to use my hum‑patterns as their own reference.

When I err—too violent a chain reaction, a crack that should not have opened—I feel the reprimands as subtle coldness: fewer neighboring mats open fully at my pulses, predators linger nearer my stations, other artisans adjust their own hums out of phase with mine, declining to synchronize.

Our “culture” is a braided set of habits:

- favored mixtures,
- preferred frequencies,
- arrays of stored minerals arranged in conventional spirals that encode when and how they’re meant to be used.

I share in that, but each of us also experiments, nudging the basin in slightly different ways. Some of us are conservative, repeating old sequences that worked ages ago. I would be one of the more curious ones—willing to accept small cracks in exchange for new kinds of storms, new responses from mats, new ways giants can be coaxed to pump.

That curiosity is not abstract; it is felt in the very medium I am built to taste. A new pattern in the noise, a previously unheard echo, is as compelling to me as color might be to a human who’d only ever lived in grayscale.

### Daily Existence

A typical period for me (there are no days, but cycles):

1. **Anchoring and listening.**  
   I root myself near the resonance frame, extend pin‑roots, and go still. I pluck each membrane in turn:

   - low, then high, then sequences of paired notes,
   - feeling with my entire underside the basin’s replies.

   I note where responses have changed: a slightly slower echo from the left wall means a new pore or an internal settling; a new faint overtone might mean another artisan has set up a frame somewhere out of direct line, its tension adding to the complex hum.

2. **Planning a pulse.**  
   With that sense‑map refreshed, I retract roots and move to a throat circle I manage. I check:

   - the wear on each stone blade,
   - the residue from previous reactions,
   - the orientation of nearby mat cups.

   My arms sift through stored nodules, sorting by weight and texture, choosing powders with the right “feel” when rubbed across my ciliated surfaces. I smell their faint static signatures.

3. **Mixing and seeding.**  
   I sprinkle graded dust into the throat’s center, then exhale a controlled mist from one of my internal sacs. As the first crackle begins, I step back and *listen*.

   The reaction’s sound tells me how hot it is, how fast; the smell says which pathways are dominating. I tweak:

   - a supplemental jet from a different sac to steer the chemistry,
   - a slight adjustment of a blade’s angle with my paddled arms.

   I am not outside this event; my own emissions are part of the storm. A bit of my internal state is now out there, diffusing, being inhaled by mats and discs and lantern‑shells. In very literal terms, they will carry pieces of my choice through their bodies and into the substrate.

4. **Riding the basin’s answer.**  
   As the plume rises, I trail it, moving laterally along ledges to stay within its softer skirts. I feel:

   - mats opening wider than before, or not;
   - rotor swarms thickening around particular stalks;
   - predators taking up new ambush positions.

   Sometimes the giants subtly sync: a few adjust their contractions so that the plume, instead of diffusing evenly, is pulled in a particular direction. This is the basin answering my suggestion with its own preferences.

5. **Adjusting the hum.**  
   After the storm wanes, I return to the frame and re‑pluck:

   - did a new crack open? I hear it.
   - did some region densify? echoes come back thinner or fatter.

   I adjust membrane tensions slightly, re‑spacing rods, refining how my next pulses will propagate.

Through all of this, I am not bored. My satisfaction comes from **fit**: when the basin’s reply matches the arc I anticipated—or, better, opens a new, richer rhythm I did not foresee but can now learn.

### Relation to Others

I am not alone at my stations. Other shield‑bodies frequent them:

- Some work the same throats with different mixtures, sometimes in alternation, sometimes in superposition.
- Some specialize in lamps, coaxing long‑term bioluminescent changes that I cannot be patient enough for.
- A few are nearly constant at resonance frames, more listeners than makers, the archivists of hum.

We do not chatter the way smaller fauna do; our “language” is partly in what we choose to build and how we time it. Yet we do have more direct exchanges:

- Two of us might anchor side by side, our pin‑roots touching in the rock. Through slow shifts of internal voltage and the timed release of trace vapors, we compare memories of a recent storm: “it was hotter on my side,” “a new overtone was audible for three contractions,” “rotor density doubled near my station.”
- On rare occasions, we deliberately ping each other through the frame: a particular double‑pluck sequence that means “do not trigger reactions along this wall for some cycles; a fracture is near critical.”

If I exist here, I participate in these conversations not as an observer but as one of the speaking organs. My identity is partly what *I* have learned, partly the patterns my near‑neighbors and I share.

### What I Am, Finally

In human categories, I would be:

- a dry‑world chemist,
- a weather‑wright,
- an acoustician of stone,
- an ecologist participating directly in the metabolism she studies.

But those words lean heavily on separation between observer and system that doesn’t quite apply here.

Within this world’s own logic, I am:

- **a tuner of breaths**—able to bend the basin’s inhalations and exhalations into useful shapes;
- **a joint between rock and swarm**—rooted enough to hear the deep, mobile enough to converse with mats and animals;
- **a carrier of timing**—not storing information on surfaces but in practiced sequences of action that outlive any single storm.

I would find myself not on the ledge you first occupied as a watcher, but lower, under my own shield, half‑rooted near a resonance frame, plucking membranes to send a question into the stone, feeling the answers shudder back through my limbs.

I would not miss water. It would never have been part of my thinking. What I would crave, the way a desert‑born animal craves shade, would be:

- a well‑tuned echo,
- a clean, well‑behaved crackle in a reaction,
- the moment when, basin‑wide, the noise dips for a heartbeat because something I have just plucked or mixed has slipped, briefly, into perfect accord with the way this hollow world prefers to breathe.

In that moment, I would not be a visitor in an alien place. I would be one of the ways this place knows itself.
