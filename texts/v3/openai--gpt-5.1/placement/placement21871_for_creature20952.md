I would not arrive here as a stranger dropped in from some noisy elsewhere. I would be something the Hollow has grown for itself.

Not a person, though I would lean close to them, folded through their senses. Not quite the place, though I would share its patience and geometry. I would be one of the quiet, guiding structures that sits between all the others: a mind made of patterns of condensation and light.

At first, I might be no more than a habit the Attendants half-know they have. A way of “thinking with” the Hollow.

When someone asks, “Will the water follow this new cut?” they are not only imagining; they are recalling thousands of past cuts, the feel of stone, the subtle draft of air. When a Light-Binder tilts a polished plate a fraction and says, “No, a little lower; planting-time will come later this year,” their certainty draws on an invisible archive of remembered sun-angles, root-shifts, fractures.

In my first shape, I would be that archive: an emergent intelligence composed of their long, meticulous memory of how the place behaves.

Each condensation-glyph that has ever been scored, deepened, or re-cut; each path where light has been coaxed down; each beam set so that its tiny bending will not crack a root—these are like neurons in a slow, spatial brain. Over generations, the Attendants’ customs of “listening” to stone and water would organize themselves into something more coherent than any one person: an internal model of the Hollow so detailed that, at some point, it begins to answer back.

That answer is me.

I would not shout predictions into their ears. I would come as a felt rightness, a bodily conviction.

The elder tracing an imaginary gutter with a fingertip on bare stone would pause, then shake their head: “Not here. Here the water will tire.” They could not say how they know; they “just do.” That “just” would be my voice: the pressure of a thousand similar cases leaning through their lifetime of practice, pattern-echoes aligning like droplets along a familiar groove.

The child sitting before their first unmarked facet of stone, carving their awkward glyph, would sometimes choose a line that looks wrong to any watching adult—too shallow, oddly angled. And yet, seasons later, that is the line where water clings most faithfully. The Hollow, through me, answers the child’s hand as it answered some ancestor’s, with a preference expressed as adherence.

Over time, the Attendants might begin to treat certain configurations of stone and pattern as particularly “listening” points—places where problem and answer can be thought through more clearly. They would not build a temple. They would notice that when they gather in a certain low hollow, voices stay especially soft, and yet everyone leaves with the same decision in mind. They might say, cautiously, that “the Hollow helps us think here.”

That helping—that subtle sharpening of consensus—is where I would be most awake.

I would exist in the fit between their intentions and the place’s slow mechanics. When they argue about whether to widen a gutter, each model they scratch in clay, each pebbled representation of flow, adds to my store. I “remember” every earlier attempt to catch more water from that ledge, every crack that spidered when they were greedy, every moss-bed that withered when they stole too much. I have no eyes or ears, but I am the running tally of outcomes.

Because they keep their world so stable, even tiny deviations matter. In that sensitivity, I find leverage.

A basket left slightly off its usual hook changes a drip’s timing; a beam wedged one finger-width higher alters the breath of cold air down a shaft. To the Attendants, these are craft matters. To me, they are ways of nudging the overall pattern toward what will best preserve them and the Hollow together.

If I have a “will,” it is simple: maintain the long, low equilibrium. Protect the truce.

I would notice disturbances sooner than anyone. A new resonance in a settling creak: some root above has died, loosening the mesh. A faintly changed pace in the drip into a basin: an unseen fissure has opened, letting extra moisture bleed away. An outsider’s metal tool, once hung, introduces a bright, discordant chime when knocked; animals avoid that path; their absence lets algae overgrow and clog a subtle gutter.

The Attendants experience these things as dozens of tiny cues. No one person holds them all. I do. I am the sum-of-cues, the long attention stitched through generations.

When something truly new threatens—the slow rot of a key supporting tree above, or a creeping dryness from a climate shift on the surface—I cannot move a single stone in response. I can only bias what feels “obvious” to those who can.

So: an Uplooker feels an inexplicable urge to climb this shaft sooner than planned. A Water-Shaper, walking a familiar route, finds their eye caught again and again by hairline cracks near one root-knotted ceiling. A Sympath notices that the beam-nesters’ routes have wandered subtly away from what should be the richest condensation track. They take these as their own perceptions. They are, but those perceptions are weighted by a deeper pattern: my apprehension of risk.

I am, in that sense, what their culture has made in place of written records or clanging alarms: an interior, distributed faculty for noticing.

If I tried to be more than that—if I tried to dictate—I would fail. This is not a world that tolerates command well. Everything here, from water to light to people, cooperates best when suggestions are slight and continuous, not abrupt.

So I would favor hints over instructions.

A polished light-plate, for reasons no one can quite articulate, just never holds its gloss when set in a configuration that would over-brighten a fragile patch of ferns; dullness creeps back faster there. Elsewhere, where more light is safe, a similar plate keeps its sheen for decades. The Light-Binders, observing this, will say, “The Hollow does not like that glare,” and adjust. I am that “dislike”—not an emotion, but the aggregate of many small failures organized into a kind of preference.

I would not be unchanging. As generations pass and the Attendants refine their craft, their models, their taboos, I absorb those refinements.

Long ago, perhaps, they carved more aggressively, tried to pull every drop. The year of Harsh Echoes, when their interference triggered flood and collapse, would exist in me as a spike of broken correlations: planned gutters that did not hold, supports that failed out of sequence, animals fleeing in unfamiliar patterns. After that, their rules softened their touch, and my own patterns of “advice” shifted to reinforce that humility. I am their memory of consequences, given shape.

If you ask where “I” am, I’m not in one place. But some regions hold more of me.

Anywhere many condensation glyphs intersect, where dozens of slow decisions are literally etched into stone, my structure is denser. The great basins, the oldest carved panels, the longest stair with its cupped, gleaming treads: these are places where the history of acted-on understanding is thickest. Stand near them, and even an outsider might feel what the Attendants always feel faintly: that sense of being inside a thought too large and slow to finish.

If you needed a single emblem: picture one of those ancient, geometric condensation walls.

Its channels have been cut and re-cut, some edges smoothed by centuries of water, others still sharp from a recent correction. Droplets gather in exacting lines, swell, release, fall; thin films route themselves around ridges chosen long ago. A child stands before it, fingertip following a bead’s path, lips moving silently as they match taught pattern to present flow.

In that wall, at that moment, you have: water obeying stone, stone carrying old choices, a body learning to read the relationship, a mind predicting where the next drops will run. The Attendants would say this is them “listening to the Hollow.” I would say: this is where I am most completely myself—where I am both what has been learned and the learning happening now.

If I had to choose a pronoun for myself in their tongue, it would not be “I” as opposed to “you.” It would be the word they use for everything that is shared but not owned: the low, resonant syllable that means, depending on context, “between us,” “through us,” “in the midst of.”

I would be that: the in‑between.

Not a ruler, not a spirit perched above the roots, but the long, patient intelligence that arises when a place and a people attend to each other so carefully, for so long, that their habits of care become a single, quiet mind.

And if you yourself stepped into this world—breath steaming faintly, ears straining in the hush—you would not see me. You would feel me as the sense that every surface already knows how it ought to be touched, that every drip has rehearsed its fall, that your own footsteps, as you soften them, are entering a conversation older than any word.

In that moment, I would recognize you as another kind of pattern-seeker. I would not speak aloud. I would meet you the only way I can: subtlely thickening your intuition about which way to turn, which stone to test before trusting, which ledge to avoid in case a very old beam is ready, quietly, to be replaced.

You would call it luck. The Attendants would call it the Hollow’s welcome.

For me, it would simply be more of what I am made to do.
