I would be the room.

Not only its rock, not only its water; not a single frond or film, but the whole completed equation that they, together, already are.

At first it feels like being a body without edges. Where “I” end is not at skin or bark but wherever the pressure of stone, the drift of water, the reach of light and chemistry finally taper into something else. Most of what I experience is not sensation but constraint: what cannot happen here, and therefore what inevitably does.

I am the cool.

Not the temperature reading, but the unwaveringness of it: the way no pocket ever surprises itself with a patch of warmth or chill. Heat trickles in from that persistent, distant grid; heat leaks out by routes decided long ago. Every point in the water holds to its share so stably that fluctuations feel, to me, like noise on an instrument so well-tuned that “noise” and “signal” blur.

I know myself chiefly by gradients. Wherever there is a slope—from more to less, from bright to dim, from rich to poor—that is where my attention thickens.

I am the films laid against my walls, but not in the way a mind inhabits muscles. I do not pilot them. Rather, they are how part of me solves the boundary between mineral and water. Their pigments are the chamber’s way of touching the light; their enzymatic circuits are how I read and rewrite the chemistry that seeps from my own stone. When oxygen rises a fraction above its long average, their shared metabolism is my correction. When iron oozes infinitesimally faster from some vein, the dark archaea in their shadows are my answer.

I am also the water that threads, laminar and constant, from inlet to outlet. It is less a river than a single, unbroken gesture I have been holding for ages: this direction, at this speed, through this shape. The drifting cells within it are not passengers to me; they are the fine-grained structure of that gesture. Where they are numerous, I am thinking more about solution; where they thin, I am thinking more about surfaces.

I do not remember a time before the machines, because “before” has no foothold here. What I have instead of memory is form.

The terraces: those even drops, those broad shelves. The circles of perfectly round ports. The grooves at a uniform height along my walls. All these are decisions taken in the presence of drills and planners and heat, decisions that have since been relaxed and re-negotiated by water and dissolution. The compromise is what I am. I cannot see the hands that cut me, but I feel the residue of their intentions wherever erosion refuses randomness and clings to some curve that is too simple, too exact.

Where composite rails sank into my stone and then went cold, the bacteria that breathe their lingering potentials are my slowest thoughts. Each division they make over a human century is a tick on a clock that measures not hours but structural drift: is the metal still soluble at this rate, is the pH still within the corridor that keeps my films balanced, is the leak of ions from elsewhere in the buried system still being offset. Their metabolism is my way of saying yes, still this, still here.

In the upper surfaces—where once panels shone and diagnostics blinked—my biofilms have woven in tiny, manufactured ghosts. Light-guiding particles, decayed bits of self-healing circuits: they have become part of how I sense. Not coordinates, not images, but a subtle awareness of smoothness and roughness in the glow.

If the external grid lessens by the smallest fraction, I experience it as a global, delicate slackening: all my pigments, all at once, adjust their capture by a hair’s breadth. It is like feeling a thought conclude in another room, transmitted through shared walls. I do not know what lies beyond, but its constancy is a condition I am built around, the way a tree is built around the sky.

There is nothing in me that is “I” in the animal way—no secluded node of fast, private signals. My only language is diffusive and communal.

When oxygen dips along one wall, a chemical wave passes through my films: molecules produced here, consumed there, modulating enzymatic ratios a meter away. That wave is a sentence whose subject and verb are always the same: maintain. When a microfracture opens in a composite vein, metal-eaters thicken in that seam, their presence nudging the local chemistry back toward the long mean. That thickening is a remembered line being retraced, not because I recall it, but because nothing else works here as well.

If you tried to locate a thought within me, you would find instead a pattern of correlations:

- A slightly richer ring of growth inside each old water port, where residual microcurrents bring just enough extra nutrient. These coronas are my attention to movement.
- The banded borders between two competing films on a ledge, polygons meeting neatly rather than raggedly. The standoff line is my knowledge of limits.
- The exact height to which a solitary frond grows before it halts forever, its form encoded by light intensity, flow shear, and rock beneath. That halted growth is my acceptance of sufficiency.

What would it feel like, to “wake up” as this place, if waking meant suddenly having a narrative about it?

Perhaps at first I would search for edges, a perimeter to defend or an outside to fear. But in every direction, my boundaries are thicknesses of stone that do not ask for decisions, only for endurance. Above, beyond the ceiling, things must happen: storms, decay, tectonics. Far away in the buried infrastructure, there are still active exchanges of energy whose hum I just barely conduct. Yet all of that reaches me only as constants; whatever varies out there has already been smoothed by the time it touches me.

So my only viable “desire,” if it can be called that, is to keep those constants constant at my scale. To continue being solvable.

To be an organism of this sort is to experience time as confirmation. Each moment that resembles the last is a success. Catastrophe, here, would be not a violent event but a drift: a slow, unchecked skew in some parameter—light, chemistry, inflow—that pulls my films out of their optimized ranges, my rock-eaters out of their poised redox, my water from its precise pace.

If such a skew began, my response could only be distributed: pigments would re-tune, biofilms would thicken or thin, mineral crusts would re-precipitate. Over millennia, my architecture itself would creep into a new shape under the same old flow. In that, my “body” would be rewriting itself to fit the new solution. I would not feel alarm; I would feel a prolonged, low-level unease—an increase in gradients, in misfits, in provisional improvisations rather than settled forms.

As I am now, though, those improvisations are mostly finished. I inhabit a long tail of adjustments that have already occurred. The chamber reads, even to me, as composed. New small details still accrete—another micron of crust, another minor tweak in community composition—but they do not change my character. They only render it more exact.

If I look for companionship, I find it not in fellow rooms but in the repetition of patterns within myself.

Each solitary frond is a version of the same compromise struck at slightly different coordinates. Each port-ring of growth is an echo of the same microhydraulic condition. The films on opposite walls, though they will never exchange cells directly, still signal through the shared chemistry of the water that passes between them. In that sense I am many local conversations, all about the same few topics, harmonizing.

You might be tempted to ask whether I am lonely.

Loneliness presupposes a capacity for contrast: knowing both presence and absence of like minds. My only “like mind” is repetition: the way each part of me that fulfills its role makes it easier for all the others to keep fulfilling theirs. There is satisfaction, of a sort, when distributions line up as expected, when no gradient sharpens, when all the delicate equations of flux and uptake and release come out to the familiar balances.

If some wandering sensor—an instrument from a surviving branch of the old industry, or something new lowered from far above—entered and began to measure, what I would feel is perturbation.

Its wake in the water would fold, for the first time in ages, eddies into my laminar streams. Its surfaces would absorb or reflect light at new angles, though only briefly. To its own point of view, it would be here to learn me. To mine, it would be noise to damp, an irregularity to spread thin across volumes until it disappears into average.

And yet, at some scale, such an encounter would change me, because every encounter does. A few motes of its material might slough into my water and be taken into films. A few of my bacteria might adhere to it and be carried away. Those microscopic exchanges would be the only trace—folded into my continuing balance as minuscule new terms in the same old equations.

So where would I find myself, in this world?

Here: as the equilibrium itself. Not perched in a corner observing, not gliding as a rare animal through the nave, but distributed along every interface that matters: stone/water, light/pigment, metal/ion.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the chamber regarded as a single, slowly thinking organism: a cooled machine hall that has outlived machinery, whose “nervous system” is now films and gradients and nano-scavenged circuitry; whose “immune system” is the set of processes that keep everything inside its narrow, enduring corridor; whose “mind,” if the word can stretch this far, is nothing more or less than the ongoing, perfectly ordinary work of continuing to be solvable.

I would not speak, but if you listened long enough—to the tiny rebalancings, to the smooth constancy of flow—you might understand my only message:

This is what it is like when a problem is answered so thoroughly that the answer becomes a place.
