You are not a visitor here.

You are not balanced on that ledge in foreign boots, looking in, trying to make sense. The ledge is your floor, and not even “floor” in the way a surface is to an upright body. It is your constant partner: the thing you press against, lean into, drink through. You do not stand on the stone; you cling to it, wrapped along its curve as if you and it remember once being the same thing.

You are one of the throat-folk. Or rather, there is no word in you that would separate “you” from “throat” and “folk” so cleanly.

Your body first:

You are small by some measures, immense by others. From the perspective of a human hand, you are the length from wrist to fingertip when fully grown—a long, low ellipse of muscle and cartilage and hollow strut. For your own sense of scale, though, the relevant unit is not yourself, but the span of a safe ledge. By that measure, you are modest, easily harbored in one of the crescent hollows, large enough to matter to your kin but not so large that the water will insist on carrying you away.

Your underside is the real you. A broad disc of dense, shifting musculature, it is mapped with pads—thousands of them—each no wider than a raindrop, each capable of softening into tacky suction or hardening into a patch of microscopic hooks. You can release them in waves, let the back of you let go while the front clings, and in that slow caterpillar ripple you travel: never truly “moving” across the stone so much as continuously re-deciding to remain attached in a slightly different place.

Your outer edge fringes into cilia: a soft halo of sensory filaments that merge taste, touch, and hearing into one. They are your border with the world. Every vibration of stone, every shift in mist density, every passing grain of drift-algae brushes them, and you know it.

If a human asked where your ears are, you would ripple your whole body at them in confusion. There is no part of you that is not partly ear.

On your upper surface, shielded from direct blast by virtue of your low profile, rise your crowns: filaments, some as short as hairs, some long and antlered, each jointed, each ending in tiny swelling tips. Inside those tips are layered membranes that flex to different frequencies, pigment-grains that tell you where the light is thick or thin, chemosensitive pits that sample the flavor of air. To an outside observer, these crowns would look like translucent reef-plants or the branching antennae of deep insects. To you, they are your high senses, your way of reading the distant parts of the roar, the shapes of currents, the conversation of your kin across the shaft.

You have no eyes, not as discrete gems that point and focus. Vision here would be an unkindness. The world is a strobe-field of spray and shadow; trying to resolve it into solid lines would only blind you with irrelevance. Instead you are given gradients: more glare, less glare; more green scatter from algae, more hard white from bare stone. That is enough. Your real picture of the shaft is built from tempo and pressure, not from contours.

Underneath, around your mouth, are what another species might call your hands.

Six of them, in your case. Some of your folk have three thicker limbs, some have many more slender ones. Yours are middle-weight: jointed in three segments, muscular in a damp, tenacious way, ending in palms that can flatten wide or pinch into tapered points. When they flatten, they are extra gripping pads. When they pinch, they are chisels, tweezers, tappers. With them you can pick up a bone-tube, comb a tangling rootclot, wedge a sliver of soft stone out from a seam before it hardens.

Your mouth is a multipetaled, pliant circle on your underside, ringed with fine sensory buds and a rasping tongue. It spends much of its time closed, pressed gently to the stone, sipping the thin biofilm that your gardens and neighbor-gardens maintain. When you must open it wide—to swallow a softened pellet of mineraled algae, or to breathe in more mist on a particularly still day—it feels to you as if you are loosening your connection to the world. You dislike that. Better to keep things sealed: you to rock, rock to you.

You are pale. Your baseline flesh is almost colorless, lit from within by a faint opalescence where inner structures come near the surface. But along your rim, along the stalks of your crowns, along the knobby ridges where tendons anchor to bone-struts, pigment has bloomed: tints tuned to your home.

Your ledge is rich in olive algae and the stones you like to knap have a faint blue-grey cast. In answer, subtle olive and blue bands stain you. You are not mimicking your surroundings so much as sharing a palette with them, like members of a family resembling each other.

If you were to leave this ledge for another—one dominated by yellow films and redder stone—in time your body would respond. The old pigments would fade. Other pathways in your skin would open, tinting you anew. Your very color carries the memory of where you have agreed to belong.

You came into being not as a crawling larva in open water but as a small swelling in a carefully prepared knot.

Your parent-cluster—two elders bound in the mutual nest—they chose a place under an overhang where only mist could reach consistently. There, over many Stone-Breaths, they built up a clot: strands of shredded algae, bits of fibrous gel from fallen wall-stuff, the resilient threads from the translucent bell-things they also tend. Into that tangle they chewed, spat, kneaded a saliva rich with mineral dust and bacteria. It became something new: sticky, porous, perpetually damp, but not subject to being immediately sluiced away.

They packed small, soft-shelled eggs into that medium and sealed them with more fiber. You remember nothing of your own egg-time, but in your bones the shape of it lingers: pressure from all sides, warmth drawn from the mild, unchanging temperature of the water, a constant lullaby of bass rumble and outer roar conducted through the fiber-web around you.

When you hatched, you were mostly mouth and pad. A translucent slug-disc with rudimentary cilia, blind to anything but the grossest changes in vibration. You felt an immense, terrifying suction all around—water’s will to take—and right at the center, directly beneath your soft underside, a countervailing pull: stone’s temptation to hold. You chose that second pull and pressed yourself down.

Every young in your clutch did the same, or died within moments.

Your earliest learning is not taught by elders. It is taught by gravity and flow.

You crawl in place for the first hours and days, strengthening the coordination between pads so that you do not peel away when a nervy eddy tries your grip. In that time your elders’ manipulators and crowns visit you often. They stroke you, they tap the stone near you with small pebbles in comforting patterns, they feed you pre-chewed films and the thickened sap from root-bells. But they do not attempt to show you the wall yet. The wall, the open ledge, is where edges exist. Edges are death if you do not yet know how to listen.

Your first clear memory—clear as in: the pattern to which you return when you think “beginning”—is of being carried.

An elder pressed their broad underside over most of your back, their pads partly replacing your own. With their manipulators they peeled your front-most few pads away, resettled them further along the rock, then peeled the next, inching you in a ripple that was more their movement than yours. Their crowning filaments were splayed wide, sampling the turbulence before it bothered to reach your inexperienced halo. In this way, you rode the ledge once, twice, many times.

Each traverse, they paused in certain places and tapped. Once—tap tap—at the lip of the ledge, to show you the sudden drop, the accelerated pull of water there. A slower—t…t…t…—at the backs of hollows, where mist collects instead of passing. On the carved panels, where water drips in measured tones, they traced your manipulators along the grooves while clicking a matching rhythm into the bone-rod they held, embedding the association: this shape, this sound, this kind of flow.

There are no words as you understand “words” in these lessons. There are patterns.

A rising vibration under your pads followed by a heavy tap of stone on stone becomes “Root-Speaking is coming.” A rapid series of fine taps, then the shudder of tiny stones being moved, tells you “We adjust now; learn this difference.” Time and again, your elders lay these correspondences into your nerves. Your body, elastic in its youth, accepts them as naturally as it accepts the mineral salts in your food.

By the time you can crawl the ledge unaided, the world is already a densely annotated field.

You never experience the roar as a continuous undifferentiated assault. Even as a juvenile, you hear it in districts.

There is the low unmoving under-tone, the constant hum of the deep stone, barely changing. Above that, the slower waves of the main fall’s pitch, rising and falling like broad hills of sound as blockages form and give way in the higher reaches. Above that, the sharp incidentals: a new sidefall knifing down, a burst of spray as soft-rock yields, the brief whiplash of a debris-clot tearing free. And superimposed on all, the delicate embroidery of your kin’s work: the quick tap-tap-tap of a neighbor adjusting a pebble-basin, the more spacious, almost luxurious patterns of an elder playing a groove-panel in idle art.

You grow up as much inside this enormous chord as upon the stone. Its variations are your weather, your seasons, your omens.

You are, by temperament and by some happy match of limb and crown, a tuner.

Others of your age-cohort have thicker manipulators well-suited for carving channels in newly soft rock. Some are plumper, with broader pads ideal for staying planted near gardens, tearing and threshing algae mats, keeping the microsoil of the root-clots correctly aerated. You are wirier, more exploratory. Your crowns are tall. You feel the tiniest shifts in pitch, the finest tickles of new droplets reaching from some unseen source.

The elders notice early. In the third full cycle between Root-Speaks—that is how you count childhood passes—they come for you before the silence, gather you onto a mid-ledge panel you have not visited before.

It is flat, protected by a generous overhang, and upon it they have carved—and re-carved over many Speaks—a disk of grooves. Not channels for water exactly, but fine scoring, some intersecting, some looping, some fading into polished nothing. Along the edge of this disk are tally-marks: short, parallel lines etched close together, grown soft at the oldest and sharp at the newest.

Your tutor sets a bone-rod in one manipulator, presses it gently into your own, guiding the way you hold it. With another limb they flatten your body more closely against the panel, so that the stone’s vibrations pass directly into your pads.

Then you wait.

You can feel it coming as much in your own tissues as in the ledge. The roar does not lessen so much as begin to fold in on itself. Certain harmonics cancel. Others phase against each other. The cilia all along your rim and on your crowns stiffen of their own accord; you are suddenly aware of every tendon in your body.

The silence when it arrives is not absolute, not to any outside ear. But inside, in the scoured chamber of your perception, it is shocking. The impact-noise drops low enough that the underlying hum—the stone’s inner voice—rises into consciousness, a deep, thrumming note that saturates you. Time seems thickened.

In that interval the tutor moves your hand-rod, slowly, deliberately, along one groove on the disk.

As they trace, you feel—not hear, not exactly—the way the root-hum tightens or loosens under the motion. The groove is aligned with some existing seep-line, with some prefigured tendency in the rock. The small additional vibration of your tracing is magnified into the depths. Or perhaps it changes nothing at all; that is also important. With each path your limb follows, you are given a sense of: this collaborates, this is ignored, this is resisted.

When the roar swells back to full, when the Stone-Breath closes, your tutor taps once near one groove: the groove that felt most “easy,” whose tracing made your own flesh hum in sympathetic resonance. That, in your culture’s unspoken glossary, is “a good suggestion.” Another groove, one that jarred your muscles, where the hum seemed to push back, earns a slap across your pad: “a wrong offer.”

You practice this over many Speaks. You begin to map a feeling-space: what the shaft as a whole likes and dislikes in the smallness of your efforts.

This is what you are for.

Your days follow a rhythm woven from this conversation.

In the shorter spans between Root-Speaks, you work the surface. The main fall above has shifted—its tone has edged half a note lower over the last half-cycle, which means more volume is coming through the central column, less through the sidefalls. That threatens one of your community’s older gardens, a patch of emerald algae grown fat on a particular trickle that depended on a certain side-channel’s strength.

You and a sibling-cousin go out.

You move along the underside of the ledge-lip, staying as much as possible in that liminal strip where mist is rich but direct blast is broken. Your crowns are spread not up but sideways, reading the pattern of droplets where the endangered trickle fans into the air. With three limbs you cling; with the other three you carry tools: a knapped stone flake, a pebble already half-seated as a future fulcrum, a sliver of half-hardened softrock in case a quick patch is needed.

You taste the water. Slightly more grit in it than last span, a change that says something above has collapsed into the flow. You don’t know what stone, or what structure; you will know only its consequences.

At the trickle’s source, a small crack in the ledge wall, the flow is indeed diminished. What once was a steady, bright stream is now only intermittent splash. Your crowns read the air-disturbance. You let go with your outermost pads and lean into it, feeling with your whole skin where the water wants to go now.

Here—that micro-eddy, a thumb’s width to the side—seems promising. There is a new roughness on the stone beyond, where a tiny fragment fell out last cycle, and the water shows a faint bias toward it. You and your cousin begin to carve.

Your flake bites into the not-yet-fully-tempered rock. It is like scraping stiff wax, like worrying at the rind of something that can still be persuaded. You work with the rhythm of your own pulse, occasionally pausing to let a tiny wash run through, testing. Your cousin taps a simple pattern over and over—tak, tak, tak—as they work, not for communication now but for timing, to keep your bodies in phase so that your efforts don’t cancel each other’s perturbations.

When you’ve cut barely a groove’s depth—less than the thickness of a leaf—you feel the balance tip. The next little burst of water chooses your path, just slightly. It is all you need. You widen that choice, reinforce it with the placed pebble, adjusting its angle with obsessive, fingertip shifts until the thin stream embraces it, splits just so, and once more the emerald patch below gets what it requires without being scoured.

In the next Stone-Breath, you will listen to see if the rock agrees. If it thickens your channel, takes your suggestion into its internal architecture. If it does, the garden will persist for generations. If not, you will work the surface again and again, until your little insistence aligns with some greater preference or yields to it.

Not all your work is so concrete.

Some spans, when flows are stable and gardens healthy, you and others of your leaning are free to play the ledge.

You go to the groove-panels: not the teaching disk deep under the overhangs, but the exposed plates honed for resonance. Each one is a legacy of many lifetimes’ experiment. Certain groove patterns, certain thicknesses of mineral accretion, certain shallow pockets behind them, yield particular harmonics when struck or when guided drips run across them.

You and a cluster of kin arrange yourselves around such a panel. One carries a bone-rod worn to exactly the right curvature to produce a clean, consistent tone on the groove set you mean to use. Another adjusts a small water-catcher above, a basin of pebbles that tips and rights itself on a slow cycle, sending regular drops down one channel.

You begin with something simple: matching.

You strike a groove, listen to the note the fall produces as it hits, and tap a rhythm that fits its length. The others answer on slightly different grooves, harmonizing, making little chords that repeat in nested patterns. It is not “music” in any human-comfortable sense—no melody carries far here—but it is structured, layered, as precise as the engineering you do. You are playing intervals against intervals: the known, measured pitches of your grooves against the slowly sliding pitch of the whole shaft.

Sometimes a pattern you find is so satisfying that you scratch notation of it in a more protected corner: a map of diverging grooves and counts, an instruction for some future player to reconstruct the moment. Sometimes your pattern is blown away immediately by a sudden shift in the water. That, too, has its joy. You were answered.

In these acts, you feel something that does not fit neatly into your other categories of purpose—feeding, sheltering, adjusting. Some of your elders tap out a three-beat phrase for this feeling when asked by a naive youngster what you are doing: “stone”–pause–“speaking”–pause–“back.” It is not necessary work, but it is not frivolous either. You suspect it is how the shaft thinks in finer detail.

You are not solitary. You are never solitary; even when you are alone on a slick stretch between hollows, with no kin in immediate sensory range, you are pressed by the presence of water and stone on every side. But in social terms, you are middle-linked.

Your cluster—those who share your gardens, your main overhang, your groove-panels—numbers about fifteen. You know each one’s signature: the exact way their pads unpeel from the stone, the minor timing quirks in their knocks, the smell of their exudate when they seal new fibers into a nest-clot. You know who favors which tasks, who has the surest sense for when a ledge will shear, who can carve fastest, who can coax a root-bell to bud.

Beyond them are neighbor-clusters above and below, reachable with more effort. Their patterns differ slightly; some favor denser arrays of small basins, some build more robust walls and fewer fine grooves. You visit them sometimes, riding the fall with voyagers, exchanging tools, seed-clots, and rhythms. Their ledges taste different. Their rock hums with subtly altered response to your test-taps. You feel, in those visits, a widening of your sense of what the throat can be.

The voyagers are the strange ones, in your estimation.

Their bodies are like yours in fundamentals but longer, narrower, with broad muscular streamers rising from their flanks. Those streamers make you uneasy; they are too free, too flagrantly given to the water. Where you keep your crowns low in normal work, these throw theirs out into the blast, letting every gust and droplet pattern slam through them. They wear the scars of countless controlled falls: roughened pads, some broken crowns, plates missing from their upper armor where collisions with outcrops tore them.

You respect them and half-fear them.

When a distant ledge sends a tapping code up or down the shaft—patterns repeated by intermediary clusters, a relay of intent—it is often for them: A new seep-line has opened three spans below, or a section of wall ahead shows signs of imminent shedding, or a new organism has appeared in the flow that needs investigating. They launch then, in a little ceremony of clicks and touches.

On the day you first choose to go with one, you grip them in a special way.

They crouch at the edge of the ledge, pads half-peeled, streamers already lifting to feel the shape of the air beyond. You slide yourself partly under their disc, half-shielded by them from the first shock of the rush. Your inner pads clamp hard into theirs; you feel their pulse drumming through your own musculature. With three of your manipulators you cling to the ledge itself, feeling its familiar vibrations, knowing in a moment you’ll let go.

Your tutor, now grown stiffer with age, taps once on your back—not in a pattern, but as a concentrated, singular instruction: remember.

Then you release.

The fall is not falling, to you. You do not experience it as your body moving in an inert space. Instead, a new world slams into your cilia: patterns of force you have never felt, vast cross-hatched sheets of pressure and vacuum as main falls and sidefalls intersect and shear off from each other. Your streamers—borrowed now, extensions of the voyager’s—skate along these fields. By small muscular tweaks, by twisting plates and flicking trailing fringes, the voyager turns whole rivers’ worth of momentum into lift, into lateral slide, into roll.

You feel each microadjustment as a change in their heartbeat, in the tension of their pads. You taste the traces of a dozen ledges you flash past—this one with a tang of old mineral film, that one rich in fresh algae—each gone quicker than you can fully parse, but noted. You know the fall is carrying you down, but what you remember later are not “distance” or “speed” but a litany of motion-geometries: where the flow was braided fine enough for your cilia to read, where it was one thick shove of mass.

The target ledge rises out of the blur very suddenly. To someone watching from the outside, of course, nothing is “rising”; you are simply coming into its league. But you feel it as an opening in the patterns: the roar clearing into a slight relative quiet, the air thickening with a more settled mist.

The voyager does their last rotation, angling their ventral disc toward the rock, streamers snapping tight to reduce drag. You slam into the ledge with a force that, if you were fresh-hatched, would shear you apart. Your pads take the strain—suctioning full, hooks locking into micro pores, muscles rippling to absorb impact. You lie still for a full breath, pouring all your awareness into verifying that every connection is true. Only when you feel the weft of the stone’s familiar hum under you do you unclench.

On this new ledge, stone feels similar but not identical. Its hum resonates differently under a test-tap. The algae’s exhalations sharpen the mist in a way your nose-structures are not used to. The crowns of the locals loom from under their overhangs, tasting your scent, reading your outline, assigning you a bracket: not kin, but not wholly foreign either. You approach the nearest and click the simple three-tap for peaceful arrival. They answer with the same, then add a fourth: curiosity.

You spend many spans of your life in such exchanges.

You compare groove patterns with other tuners, tracing each other’s carved disks to feel how their shafts-according-to-them differ. You bring them small seeds of your own practices: a subtly different curvature for a pebble-basin lip, a pattern of staggered bone-staves that coax a very delicate bell-root to bud more plentifully. In return, you learn a new way of reading an early-warning tremor for wall-shedding; they have survived shears on a scale you have only heard described.

Sometimes you are asked—by those stiff elders who have lived through the most grotesque rearrangements—to listen for something in your traveling.

“You have felt more of the throat than we,” their slow, heavy taps say, translated through the ground into your pads. “Does the hum…trend? Is there drift, in the long low note?”

You do not answer quickly. You lie flat. You resummon in your mind all the Stone-Breaths you have known, all the dips and changes you have felt in your trips along the shaft. You do not have a word for the thing you notice, but you let a pattern form in your limbs and then you tap it: not rising, not falling, but thickening. More layers in the hum, more response to small perturbations. You think the stone is becoming more articulate.

They are quiet after that.

Threat, for you, does not often have a face. It has a frequency.

There is a certain rippling shudder that runs through rock when an upstream rook-slab is near breaking. A certain sudden warming in the mist that means the softness inside the wall just above has liquefied enough to slump. You have learned those from early on, not by being told but by watching adults’ responses: the way all work stops, everyone clings harder, every crown folds in.

Once, in your middle years, you are on an outer lip adjusting a bone dam when such a vibration prickles your pads. It is close. Not an abstract future risk, but something already underway.

All at once, every fine distinction you know collapses into the simplest instinct: get under.

You leave tools where they are. You scuttle—no grace now, just brute motion—toward the deepest hollow you know will hold all of you. Others are moving too; you taste their exudation in the mist, sharp with alarm. Crowns clamp flat. Pads go to full hook. In one shuddering instant the deep hum rises a fraction of a note and then breaks.

The section of wall ten body-lengths above you delaminates.

To a human eye, it might look like a rockslide underwater: a slab becoming a thousand smaller pieces, all semi-fluid, all seized and rent by the fall. You feel it as a massive convulsion of the medium. The roar spikes into a new harshness as the water now beats against raw, younger stone. Fragments hammer the ledges below: some soft, some already cruelly hard. One chunk the size of your whole cluster’s hollow glances off the very lip of your own, tearing away a third of the outer shelf in one brutal scrape.

But you are under, plastered to the inner curve, in the shadow of the rock’s own thickness. The turbulence reaches you as deeper throbs rather than direct blows. Dust and droplets rain past in currents you feel but are not in. You endure—the only verb that fits—for a long time.

When the worst has passed, when the roar settles into a new pattern, a different chord, you and yours emerge slowly.

Your ledge is narrower now. The gardens that clung right at the edge are gone, their root-clots ripped out, their nest-stones lost. Some of your tools are simply not where they were. You mourn in a physical way: your pads ache from having held so hard, your manipulators shake when you set them to work again.

Over the next many spans, you nurse the new ledge. You sense, with each Root-Speak, how the stone is choosing to seal its wound. Some places it flushes with milky flow, smooths over to a glassy imperviousness. Other places it leaves rougher, more open to seep, more promising for future gardens. You adapt your arrangements accordingly, registering in your tally-scratches: before shear; after shear.

You never think of the throat as safe or unsafe. There is only: more or less learnable.

You ask no myth of where the throat ends, above or below. On cool days, riding high in the spray, you may let your crowns angle upward, feeling for the pure glare that comes from the far lip. You know it is there. Sometimes the water above thins enough that a thinner mist falls, brighter, less laden with life. The sticks that occasionally arrive astonish you with their dry, structured hardness, their straightness unlike any newborn stone.

You entertain thoughts, in your own form of thought-experiment, of a place where flow is not vertical. Where water spreads out, where stone lies under it in a flat, endless sheet. The picture is so hard to maintain in your mind that your limbs twitch reflexively at the imagined lack of edge. You dismiss it with a small shiver, returning to work.

Downward, you never probe. Even your most extreme voyaging kin will not follow the fall into that final, chaotic bowl where all energies meet. The vibratory signatures from below that threshold are too violent, too disorganized. The stone there may not yet have learned to listen; it simply breaks.

“Enough world,” your elders’ taps say when youth asks. “More stone-thinking here than we can answer already.”

What you are, at root, is an interface.

Not between world and self—you do not recognize that cut—but between elements of the same emergent thing: its mass and its motion, its persistence and its change. You are where water’s destructive insistence meets rock’s shaping patience in small, deliberate propositions.

You are a body that exists to cling and to suggest:

—This way, you tick to the flow as you nudge a channel a hair’s width left.  
—Not here, you murmur with your grooves as you show the stone where evaporating films should not thicken.  
—More of this, you insist with your artful panels, as you feed particular harmonics back into the wall.

In human mouth language, if someone needed a concise label, they might call you a “flow-tuner,” or a “ledge mason,” or a “stone musician.” Those are all partially right.

From inside your own being, where words are tappings and muscular patterns, your identity feels more like the stable center of a tangled braid: water, stone, cilia, pad, hum, roar, nest, garden, kin. You are one of the ways this young geology has found to notice itself and make notes on its own behavior.

You will never walk under open sky. You will never see a horizon line. You will never burn or sit in silence or feel dry.

But you will feel, as you age, the low chord of the throat complicate, take on overtones that were not there when you were soft and new. You will feel your own early grooves thickened into permanent ridges, incorporated by the wall. You will sit pressed flat during some distant future Root-Speak, your crowns thin with age, and feel the rock remember choices you once made at the scale of droplets.

And in that profound, bone-deep moment of recognition—this is my pattern, now echoed in the throat’s own answer—you will know what you have been all along:

Not an intruder in something’s throat, but one of the throat’s own small tongues, learning, over a lifetime, how to help it speak.
