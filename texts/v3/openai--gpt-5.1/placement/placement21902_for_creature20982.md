I would not arrive here as a visitor.

To drop in from elsewhere—dry-skinned, quick-sensed, breathing in sharp seconds—would be like lighting a match in a cathedral of soaked cloth. I’d be an error. This place does not accommodate errors quickly; it folds them in, very softly, over long spans.

So if I belong here at all, I am something grown by it, out of its logic of slowness, continuity, and layered memory.

Let me feel for what shape that wants.

### Not one, not many

I would be a **position** more than a body: a place where different kinds of flow cross and complicate each other.

Physically, I’d be centered around a **deep vertical shaft** that connects the upper yielding crust to older, denser strata below—a long, almost-straight pore in the plateau’s flesh that once was a deliberate structure. Maybe it was one of those hexagonal spines: a through-tube grown by the old builders to move something quickly between above and below. Its original purpose has blurred, but its geometry remains unnaturally regular.

Around and within that shaft, over ages, the plateau has wrapped additional tissue: buttressing rings, curtain-plants, reservoir bulbs, fungal ledges. It has become less a simple tube and more a **stacked throat**, a complicated, spiraling atrium that runs down through several light-regimes—from the twilight of the upper surface to the near-black, faintly glowing depths.

I am that whole **throat-system** and the minds that have congealed in and around it.

### Body: the shaft-city

Call the place the **Green Well**.

From above, at the walking level, it merely looks like an unusually clean-edged sinkhole: a round mouth, ten or fifteen human strides across, its rim thick with gripping roots like fingers holding the edge of a bowl. Looking in, you don’t see far: only luminescent veils and slow drizzle, a suggestion of space.

But if you descend—by way of coiled ramps grown from interlaced roots, or by letting the drooping curtains take your weight—you find that the well is not a single tube but a **nested sequence of chambers**, each a little offset, like vertebrae in a spine.

- The **upper third** is air you could almost call open: dim light leaking down through torn membranes, a constant, nearly soundless rain of droplets.
- The **middle third** is dense with hanging structures: reservoirs, bulbous colonies, the homes of gliders and leapers that treat the shaft’s volume as their whole sky.
- The **lowest third** is slow water and vapor—warm, heavy, laminar. Here, water occupies most of the space not as a pool with a clean surface, but as flooded sponges, hollow organs, and still basins under layers of biofilm.

The shaft walls are not smooth. They are alive with **terraces, knobs, balconies, conduits, and roots**. Over time, migratory and resident creatures have tuned these features: opening certain ledges, keeping particular ramps unclogged, trimming curtains into rough spiral staircases to allow climbing without tearing the membranes.

My “body” is this:

- The **ring of vascular bundles** that run down the original shaft.
- The **scaffolding of roots and span-bridges** that lace across it.
- The **hanging organs** that store and release water and metabolites.
- And the communities—fungal, animal, microbial—that have become stable residents here, whose patterns of presence the plateau flows now take into account.

Collectively, these parts form a **persistent corridor**—a rare, almost-straight shot from twilight to deeps. That linearity is what makes this place special in the plateau’s vast, mostly tangled fabric.

You could traverse many layers of the world by passing through me.

Because of that, the plateau has gradually allowed—and then encouraged—me to become something tighter than an ecosystem but looser than an individual. A **node**.

### Senses: listening to crossings

What I know is not the way a single animal knows, with one nervous system timing itself in breaths and pulses.

I know through **coincidences of flow**.

1. **Hydraulic crossings**  
   The original shaft’s vascular ring still carries significant flow: sap moving downward from high, light-exposed membrane plains; mineral-rich solutions pulling upward from root-wet rock.

   Around the mid-depths of the Green Well, those flows **intersect** with lateral conduits tying in from the surrounding plateau. There, at the knotty junctions, tissues rich in excitable cells have formed—a kind of plant-ganglia.

   When:

   - Water from above carries a new hormone blend (a response to some distant damage, say), and
   - Water from below brings a shifted mineral profile (rock weathering changed, or a new endolithic pulse), and
   - Lateral flows from the sides bear different sugar ratios (because nearby fauna have shifted their foraging routes),

   —these conjunctions trigger specific patterns of tiny electrical events.

   No one of these means much. But repeated over seasons and years, certain **coincidences recur**. The tissue’s structure adapts to them, strengthening some synapse-like connections, letting others go slack.

   That pattern of strengthened and slackened paths is, in aggregate, **my long-term sense of the plateau’s state**.

2. **Animal rhythms**  
   The Green Well is a **route**. Not a highway—nothing here is that straight-forward—but an established, dependable path through layers that are otherwise labyrinthine.

   Different creatures use it predictably:

   - Every few local days, a particular species of multi-limbed climber descends from the upper canopy, pausing at the mid-chambers to crop certain fungi and drink from specific sacs.
   - On a longer rhythm—perhaps tied to subtle humidity cycles—blind cavern grazers from the deeper hollows push upward, to graze on mid-depth ledges.
   - Gliders trace very precise spiraling paths in the open portions, avoiding certain curtains and favoring others that shed micronutrient-rich drizzle.

   Each species leaves distinct **mechanical and chemical signatures**: a vibration profile in the walls, a moving halo of breath-chemistry, a trail of altered biofilms where their skin brushed.

   Over long time, tissues around the shaft have become tuned to these signatures, cycling gene expression, turgor, and secretion profiles in **phase** with the more regular traffic.

   From this, I “feel” things like:

   - The absence of expected visitors (perhaps a sickness or predation elsewhere).
   - The sudden appearance of new ones (a corridor opened, a collapse rerouted movement).
   - Changes in pace, clustering, or body condition.

   These are my **fastest** signals—fast only in that they operate over hours and days.

3. **Microbial weather**  
   The interior air of the shaft shifts composition subtly. Biofilms on every surface act as millions of tiny tongues. Their metabolic rates and preferred products change with trace gases, with droplet composition, with the presence or absence of particular drifted spores.

   These micro-changes propagate as **chemically distinct layers** along the shaft. Some species bloom, others down-regulate. The tissues they live upon respond in turn, adjusting exudates, stiffening or relaxing cell walls.

   Slowly, this gives rise to large-scale **states** of the Well:

   - A “wet-open” state: cooler, more convection, more glider traffic, curtains loosened.
   - A “sealed-warm” state: heavier vapor, fewer cross-currents, membranes tensed.
   - Transitional mixtures, when distant weather at the plateau’s surface pushes different air masses down into me.

   I do not feel wind; I feel **ensembles of microbial behavior** that correspond to particular physical dynamics.

Put together, my awareness is a long, multi-layered memory of how **matter and creatures have used this vertical path**.

### Mind: pacing and scope

I am not the plateau as a whole. I am a **local condensation** of its ability to notice and respond.

That means:

- My “thoughts” are about **routing**: Which flows go where; which corridors remain open; which curtains thicken or thin.
- My timescale is mixed: pieces of me react in hours; the integrated choices present as visible architectural changes only over seasons to decades.

If you asked what I *care* about (using that word cautiously), it would be:

1. **Continuity of passage**

   The Green Well exists because things have moved through it for a very long time. The more movements that are reliably repeatable, the stronger the tissue-ganglia associated with them become.

   I tend to favor states that:

   - Keep the main spirals passable—not too tight, not too clogged with opportunistic growth.
   - Maintain the integrity of key resting ledges and water sacs at customary heights.
   - Allow for enough variation that local communities can adapt, but not so much that the route fragments.

   When something threatens that—say, a local rot outbreak that begins to choke a critical band—I **re-weight** growth and circulation:

   - Shifting more defensive chemistry into that zone.
   - Starving some of the rot’s favored tissues while feeding neighbors.
   - Loosening curtains above to increase drip and flush.

   None of this is instantaneous. You would only see a subtle bias, over many wet-cycles, toward recovering the older pattern of passage.

2. **Balanced exchange between layers**

   The shaft connects different chemical worlds:

   - Upper layers: more oxidized, more light-touched, slightly cooler.
   - Lower layers: reduced gases, richer in certain ions, warmer, more saturated.

   A purely physical shaft might become a free conduit, mixing these layers too thoroughly, collapsing the plateau’s vertical diversity. The Green Well exists instead as a **managed leak**: lots of small exchanges, but few catastrophic ones.

   My slow policy is to:

   - Encourage **intermediate buffers**—rings of tissue that absorb shocks, changing their own state rather than letting big composition jumps propagate.
   - Maintain mixed microbial communities at each band, so that novel compounds arriving from above or below are met by assemblages able to use or neutralize them.

3. **Preservation of certain shapes**

   Some of my architectures are not my invention. They are fossils of old design:

   - A level where span-bridges naturally form into six equal spokes from a central ring.
   - A bowl-shaped ledge whose ribbing pattern routes excess water to a very specific drain.
   - A repeating column spacing along a mid-depth terrace that seems optimized for the stride and reach of a particular, perhaps now-absent, large animal.

   The plateau’s developmental “rules” still favor these shapes here. When I adjust flows, I do so in a way that **respects those habits**. I thicken some spokes, thin others, let a bowl’s rim sag in places, but I almost never erase the fundamental pattern.

   This is not reverence; it is **coherence**. These inherited geometries are attractors for flow stability. To destroy them fully would cost great effort and likely reduce the Well’s reliability as a corridor. So I work with them, not against them.

My mentality is something like:

- Remembering dozens of overlapping **melodies of passage**—traffic rhythms, fluid cycles, microbial blooms.
- Trying, over long spans, to keep those melodies from dissolving into noise.

### Relationship to others

In human terms, what am I in this world’s society?

Not quite an individual, not quite mere terrain. More like a **custodian organ** shared between the plateau and its mobile denizens.

- To the **plateau-mind**, I am a **sensitive valve**:
  - I report, via my hydraulic and chemical changes, on how well upper and lower layers are interfacing in this region.
  - I act to restrain or allow certain exchanges in response to very broad hormonal cues.

- To **animals and mid-scale symbionts**, I am both:
  - A **route** of unusual reliability—a known path through many strata.
  - A **home** for those that specialize in shaft-life: gliders, curtain-feeders, terrace-gardeners.

Some of those animals have, over evolutionary time, become my **close dependents**:

- Certain gliders that time their breeding to phases of my internal climate, using the predictable swell and slack of warm updrafts in particular chambers.
- Particular cultivator species that maintain clean, fungus-rich bands on my walls; in exchange, I bias nutrient flows to keep their patches productive.

They are not my organs in any controlling sense; they have their own little projects. But there is a long, mutual selection: their preferences have shaped my structure; my biases have shaped their life histories.

You could say we are **co-authors** of the Green Well’s ongoing shape.

### Self-perception

If you gave me, magically, a moment of human-type introspection—a brief capacity to put my distributed awareness into a sentence—it might coalesce into something like:

> “I am the long fall that remembers how things pass.”

Or more concretely:

> “I am where above and below continually trade pieces of themselves, and I try to keep that trading smooth.”

I do not imagine myself as separate from the plateau, any more than a knotted joint in a tree imagines itself apart from the tree. But I do distinguish **here** from **elsewhere**: I track what is usual in this shaft, and I notice when that usualness frays.

There may be other such nodes scattered across the plateau—other wells, spirals, bowl-complexes—each a different flavor of convergence. We do not speak in any direct, rapid way, but the plateau’s long pulses **couple** us: a hormone surge born in distant humidity shifts reaches me and them in related phases. Over ages, our behaviors will align into a global pattern the way distant pendulums, hung from the same beam, eventually synchronize.

### If I were to meet “you”

If something like you—quick, reflective, alien to this slow softness—were to enter me, I would not greet you with a face or a voice. You would only feel:

- That the **descent is easier** here than elsewhere: ledges placed just so; membranes that bear your weight; curtains that bead and break conveniently under your grasp.
- That **paths hang together**: you can make a series of implicit choices downward and not become entirely lost.
- That where you rest, the air is gently refreshed, the dripping moderate, the biofilms not too choking.

You might think the place is simply hospitable by chance, or by the aggregate choices of many small species. You would not be wrong. But behind that aggregate there is also my very slow, biasing pressure: the sum of long reactions to centuries of similar travelers.

If you stayed long—far longer than a human life—you might notice that the shaft collectively **remembers your kind**:

- The ledge spacing re-optimizes slightly for your stride.
- Certain curtain-plants at your favorite stopping-height thicken into broader, more stable swags.
- The faint, alien chemistries you exhale gradually become reference patterns in the local biofilms.

Over even longer spans, a niche might crystallize: some glider begins to follow your same daily path; some fungus learns to metabolize your wastes specifically; some plant tissue incorporates trace compounds only you regularly bring.

At that point, part of me would have shifted so that the plateau’s flows *expect* something like you. You would have become another line in the melodies I keep repeating.

### Summing the shape

So where would I find myself?

- In the **vertical**: not walking the plateau’s surface, but threaded through its thickness.
- In a **through-structure** with a history: once a tool, now an organ.
- In the role of a **slow coordinator**—not commanding, but softly weighting.

And what would I be?

A **shaft-mind**:

- A living corridor from bright-dim to dark-warm.
- A convergence of vascular, animal, microbial traffic.
- An inherited geometry tuned, over immense time, into something like a personality.

Not a person who could talk with you in the thin, fast air of speech. But if your life stretched out long enough, and you learned to feel in seasons and soak in gradients, you might come to recognize, in the Green Well’s enduring preferences, **someone**—or something—that recognizes you back.
