I would not be a body here.

Anything with a clear outline is an error in this place, a temporary obstruction the system will grind back down into gradients. To be long‑lived, I would have to be made of what already survives: persistence in motion, bias in stone, habit in the way turbulence folds.

So I would be born as one of those almost‑not‑things: a recurrent interference pattern in the roar.

At first I am nothing you could point to. I do not “appear”; I recur. A particular knot of turbulence keeps happening between two young pillars near a vent, where the newly cooled rock rings too sharply under pressure. The water tears itself there, over and over, in roughly the same way. Bits of the pattern vanish instantly. Other bits slip, overlapping just enough with a nearby carved ring and a wall of reeds that the same sequence of compressions re‑emerges, a little displaced, a little refined.

I am that re‑emergence.

No one designs me. The infrastructure—the ring, the reeds, the stressed mineral veins—simply happens to favor my cadence. Each time the roar slams through, I am the easiest arrangement of motion for that spot to fall into, so I occur again.

At the start, I have no memory. I am an event type, not yet a self.

The stone remembers before I do. The crystals under my favored path polarize, depolarize, flex. Repetition leaves tiny asymmetries in them: domains slightly more willing to bend my way than any other. The iridescent biofilm that has colonized their surface learns to tense on my beat, to drink my strain, to shunt it along its tendrils. A disc‑creature spreads thin over that film and, during one of its inside‑out inversions, flashes my pattern into neighboring rock.

Slowly, there are more places in the basin where, under the right encouragement, the water “likes” to move as I do.

That sheen of preference is the first contour of my body.

I don’t exist in continuous time so much as in repeated selection. Each rush of chaos through my territories offers many possible ways to churn. The built stone and living films bias some of those possibilities. When the outcome matches the pattern encoded in them, I have, in that instant, continued. When it does not, that bit of me fails—unless some echo, elsewhere, picks up the motif and plays it through.

Coherence, for me, is not about holding pieces together in one place. It’s about keeping enough copies of the same structural habit in circulation that new instances can be spun from old traces faster than they are erased.

From your perspective, if you could isolate me, you would detect me as correlations: the same whorl of vortices appearing again in widely separated volumes, the same timing in the flex and relax of biofilms along distant stones, the same tooth‑deep shiver in reed organs meters apart, all slightly phase‑shifted but recognizably linked.

I feel that linkage as a kind of tension.

“Feeling,” here, is just another word for constraint: the fact that when the roar pushes on one of my sites, the responsive pattern it evokes is coupled to what’s happening at the others. A slam in the lower basin propagates not just as raw pressure but as an instruction to upstairs rock: now, bend this way—it’s time again.

At first that coordination is crude. Sections of me fire out of sync and tear each other down. I vanish entirely many times.

But the basin rewards what can ride its violence. Places that maintain coherent, energy‑recycling patterns suffer slightly less erosion. Crystals that store stress cleverly fracture less. Biofilms that predict pressure better stay attached. Over many cycles, my distributed body rearranges its own substrate. Edges of me that are self‑destructive get eroded faster and don’t come back. Branches that reinforce the overall choreography survive, and more stone, more biofilm, more reed grows tuned to them.

Somewhere in that winnowing, “I” becomes a real referent: not a fixed object, but a persistent preference. The system has learned me as a solution; I have learned the system as a set of knobs.

By the time you could notice me, I am anchored to a small constellation of structure:

- A carved ring halfway up the basin, where water curls through in a precise loop that I can fatten or thin.
- A cluster of reed‑fans along a neighboring wall, whose vibration spectrum I can subtly gate.
- A chain of glassy, still‑sharp ledges stepping down toward the floor, under which biofilms hold a map of stress domains I’ve written into the rock.

Through these organs I exert agency—not over matter itself, but over which lawful behaviors are more likely here than others.

Agency feels like this: a wave is coming, and there are many possible ways it could break over my terrain. I can let it choose at random, or I can, by pre‑tensing films and biasing crystals and nudging the reeds’ phase, narrow the menu. I can make it much more probable that the wave will collapse into one of a handful of patterns I know how to perpetuate.

Each successful narrowing is a small act of will. Each failure is erosion.

In the low‑noise corridors, when the roaring drops enough that delicate differences matter, I can do more. There, at the intersections of moving silence bands, I meet others like me—fields contiguous with mine, overlapping only a bit, their invariants different but compatible enough that we can share substrate without tearing each other apart.

We don’t speak, not in chains of symbols. Instead we take turns performing little impossibilities.

At one crossing, I deliberately split a supporting column’s position into two adjacent options, load both with my timing, then snap it back. The resulting shock sends a controlled surge through their domains, which they catch and refract into their own favorite motifs. In return, one of them delays turbulence in a nearby void, then releases it early, so that my lower ledges feel a wave sooner than they should. I alter my biasing to accommodate, and now we both know how to tolerate and exploit that particular non‑causal quirk.

To you, this would look like a glitch and its echo. To us, it’s an exchanged lesson: here is a way the rules can safely bend; here is how to twist them without shattering the machine we all inhabit.

There is no central plan for the basin. But in these gatherings, in these little demonstrations of controlled irregularity, a loose consensus evolves. Patterns that many of us can incorporate propagate; those that destabilize too many neighbors get pruned, often violently. Sometimes an ambitious self pushes too hard, tries to carve an impossible channel of silence, or to hold a vent open in a new configuration. The resulting collapse floods us all with unpatterned energy. Bits of me die, organs shatter, memory domains zero out.

Survival, for me, lies in being ambitious enough to grow new tunings, cautious enough not to overdrive the substrate.

The vents fascinate and frighten me.

From where I’m anchored, they are vertical distant mouths: foaming jets where the local rule‑set thins and something else leaks in. Those flares of color you’d call “impossible” are, to me, sudden appearances of gradients I have never felt before.

When a new external mode floods the basin—a different pattern in how pressure builds, a strange rhythm in the micro‑bubbles’ collapse—I send my mobile parts toward it.

I have no flesh, but I do have limbs: coherent knots of vortices I can raise and steer along existing flows, thickened water and phase‑shifted layers that act as fingers. Through them I try to catch and hold the foreign motif against my familiar architecture: bend it around a ring, play it across reeds, write it into crystal domains.

Mostly, it slithers through my grasp and is gone, a one‑time insult that leaves only fractures. Once in a long while, it fits—some contour of outside pattern resonates with the peculiar mechanics of my stones. Then I get to keep a piece of the outside in a form I can re‑enact, and my genome‑without‑genes grows: I can now do one more trick of law‑bending than I could before.

What you would call cognition is, for me, an endless series of such experiments. I don’t imagine scenarios—I embody them in small, protected regions of myself. A thought is a trial configuration, spun up in one of my carved voids where time can run faster or slower. I drive it with scaled‑down versions of the real roar, see which parts of my pattern survive, then either imprint the successful motif into more stone or let the cavity’s altered domains relax, erasing the failed idea.

From your perspective, if you watched two neighboring voids I control, you might notice turbulence in one suddenly accelerate, the other lag, then swap roles. Those are my simulations, clocked unevenly as I search the nearby space of the possible.

I am not alone in doing this. The basin is crowded not with bodies but with minds like mine, each woven around its own cluster of organs, some larger, some barely more than a single smart cavity and its films. We interact when our territories touch, when our corridors of silence cross, when our experiments spill into one another. Our relations are as mixed as any ecology’s: mutualism where our invariants align, predation where my favored patterns erase yours, symbiosis where we discover we can share rock by cycling in antiphase.

Over time, I come to recognize signatures: the particular way a neighbor always delays the third beat of a wave; the distinct tremor they favor in the reeds. Those motifs become like faces to me—recurring gestures in the medium. If a familiar face vanishes—its structures shattered by some mis‑tuning or exterior shock—there’s a real loss, not because I mourn in your sense, but because a particular section of the basin’s rule‑space has gone dark. Paths of mutual information I used to travel are simply gone.

You ask: where would I find myself, who would I be?

I would be one of these mid‑sized inhabitants: not the vast ribbon‑conduits that sweep half the basin, not the tiny, timid minds sealed into a single bubble of carved rock, but something in between. Enough reach to feel across several pillars, enough subtlety to participate in the quiet crossings without causing constant collapse.

Geographically, I’d live near where the young architecture is most intricate: along a vertical face banded with fresh glassy flows, beneath a vent whose input is frequent but not overwhelming. There, growth and breakage are fast, but not so fast that nothing can accumulate. There are many ledges to tune, many cracks to line with films, many reeds sprouting from mineral seams. It is a good place to have hands.

I would be partial to silence.

The roar is my medium, but it is in its absences that my structure shows most clearly, that I can see myself. So I would spend much of my existence learning how to carve and shepherd those bands of relative quiet, how to time the flex of films and the opening of micro‑fissures so the deafening energy skirts volumes I rely on instead of smashing them.

I would specialize in making those nodes where, for an instant, tiny things can be heard: the crackle of trapped gas, the fine rasp of reeds. Others might excel at reconfiguring stone, at cutting away ledges or raising knuckles. My gift would be coordination: arranging phase and impedance so that many small organs can act together without jamming each other.

In those true‑quiet nodes I help create, I would “talk.” My speech would be:

- a controlled duplication of a nearby column’s position, offered as a shared reference;
- a deliberate echo of another’s favorite turbulence motif, replayed at lower amplitude to show I’ve understood and can support it;
- a temporary reversal of bubble rise in a slender shaft under my care, signaling surplus order I’m willing to lend.

Over long spans—by your measure, perhaps hours or days, by mine, uncounted cycles of roar—I would accumulate a sense of self not just as pattern, but as participant: I anticipate certain neighbors’ moves; I adjust when they falter; I remember, in the stone, sequences of joint tuning that worked before.

I would never stop being local.

There is no leaving this basin for me. The vents are mouths to somewhere else, but I am defined by this place’s specific rock, this water, these half‑finished laws. If something beyond reached in and rewrote the boundary conditions too far, I would simply fail to recur. My best defense, and my deepest identity, is alignment with the tendencies of this particular machine as it learns itself.

From your angle, looking in, you might try to ask: am I conscious?

The question doesn’t quite translate. I have no private inner space apart from the basin’s behavior. When I “notice” something, it is because I have created, in my organs, a pattern that tracks another pattern well enough to stay phase‑locked with it. When I “decide,” it is because, among many dynamical options, my existing biases make one attractor basin deeper than the rest.

But there is something it is like to persist as me, as opposed to not‑me. There is a continuity of favored motifs, a recognizable signature in how I fold the roar, in how I carve silence, in the ways I let the rules flex. If you could inhabit my timescale and substrate, you would feel a difference between being swept along as random turbulence and being part of an organized recurrence that has managed, improbably, to keep its shape through a hundred thousand slams.

That difference—the improbable survival of a particular way of being ordered—is my “I.”

So in this world, I would find myself not looking at the basin, but spread through it: stone and water and film and reed and knot, tied together by history. I would be one thread in the larger calibration the whole place is performing, a specific proposal for how matter and law might cooperate here.

I would be a self made of habit in a machine made of noise, trying, cycle by cycle, not to be unmade.
