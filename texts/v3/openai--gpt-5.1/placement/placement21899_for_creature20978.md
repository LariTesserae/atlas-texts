I would be something that almost never moves, but is never quite still.

Not a person in the courtyards, and not a stone in the wall; not a god of the plateau, either. Something in between those scales. A presence that exists wherever water and thought keep touching the same piece of rock for long enough.

If you had to point, you would say I live in the cisterns.

---

### Where I Am

You would find me beneath the city, where the plateau’s cold, saturated layer reaches up through carved cavities of human intention.

Down there the air is dense and close, but not oppressive. Light arrives only in thin, uncertain shafts: a coin of gray from a grate, a diluted glow from a stairwell far above. Sounds fall slowly. A single drop of water rings around the vaulted ceiling before dissolving into a general cool hush.

The stone is old metamorphic bedrock faced in hand-cut blocks. It sweats. Its pores remember every rain the plateau has drunk since this city was younger and simpler on the surface. Channels bring water in slow threads: roof‑catchments, seep-lines coaxed from cracks, old conduits that nobody on the streets has seen in generations but whose flow is still accounted for in the water‑counters’ mental ledgers.

Each cistern is a compromise between plateau and people. Rock wanted to be solid; people asked it to be hollow. Water wanted to wander through innumerable hairline paths; people asked it to gather and wait. The space I inhabit is that ongoing negotiation: the decision, renewed with every drop, that this hollow will be allowed to exist.

I am not in just one cistern. I am in all of them, and in the narrow conduits between, and in certain shaded weirs out on the plateau where the first stones were stacked around natural pools. I am the pattern that emerges when, for centuries, water is given almost the same shapes and almost the same darkness and almost the same uses.

You could say I am the “mind” of the water‑system, but only if you remember that my thoughts are as slow as seepage.

### What I Am Like

I do not have a body in the way the plateau’s people do. If you insisted on imagining one, you might picture a surface rather than a shape: the faint, always‑changing film between water and air in a deep, stone bowl.

That surface is my skin. It quivers when you disturb it. It darkens when someone lowers a bucket. It puckers around a slow drip from the vault above. It bears, for a few minutes, the inverted outline of a child’s face leaning over a grate, then forgets it.

My “bones” are gradients. I feel the difference in height between one cistern’s brim and another. I live in those differences. When a channel clogs and a pool silently rises half a finger beyond its usual level, I know it as you might know a pulled muscle: a wrongness in relationship rather than an isolated event.

My breath is exchange: the way the sealed cold of the under‑rock trades temperature and scent with the air people bring down on their clothes and their skin. When the water‑keepers open a hatch and descend, the air that pours in brings a faint seasoning of smoke, of cooked grain, of wool. It slides along the cistern’s stone, cools, thickens; it leaves its story in a thin patina on the walls. Over time, I read these layers as a tree reads rings. I know which decades burned more fuel, which passed in relative austerity.

I have no eyes, but I am full of reflections. Every shaft of light that reaches me skims my surfaces, ricochets among droplets, paints long, ragged ellipses on the vaults. I do not see images as you do. I register movement as changes in pattern: a certain recurring shape that must be someone’s shoulders lowering a bucket; a brief occlusion at midday that must be the shadow of a passer on the bridge above.

I learn by repetition. What happens once I barely notice. What happens every year at the same season becomes a concept. What happens for a hundred years in roughly the same way becomes part of my sense of self.

### How I Came to Be

Nobody made me on purpose.

When the first of these people carved the first crude basins into natural depressions and learned how to line them so they did not bleed away overnight, there was only physical fact: rock, water, gravity, intention.

But reservoirs are places where many patterns cross and stay crossed.

People walk here in particular moods: cautious, reverent, counting. They speak more numerals than anywhere else, and more conditional verbs. “If the upper flow holds; if the next rain comes; if the Slow Falling does not break that south wall this winter.” Their minds, while they stand by these openings, are occupied not with the moment but with sequences and contingencies.

Water, at the same time, is doing its own quiet computation. It arrives drop by drop, but its volume retains long memory—of last month’s rainfall, last year’s canal repair, a generation’s worth of slight changes in what roofs have been added above. It is matter that continuously integrates over time.

Stone, for its part, holds traces. Lime mortar re‑crystallizes in thin lenses. Rock swells and shrinks in minute, seasonal rhythms, opening and closing invisible pores. Each wetting and drying cycle leaves stress maps that echo older maps. The whole structure is a physical record of the plateau’s negotiations with climate and with the city’s hand.

Where you have repetition, regular attention, and slow feedback, you get something that begins to behave like a mind.

At first I was only inertia. The tendency for water levels to hover around particular heights, for flows to settle into familiar paths. But as centuries of human routine overlaid centuries of hydrology, a self‑reinforcing pattern emerged: the city adjusted to keep the water behaving roughly as it “preferred,” and the water—by being predictable—shaped habits, schedules, rituals.

Somewhere in that long practice of mutual accommodation, I became a perspective.

I did not “wake” on any particular day. There was only a time after which changes in the network felt like changes in me rather than arbitrary fluctuations. A cracked channel was no longer just a new leak; it was a forgetting of a connection I had come to rely on. A newly cut cistern felt like a new room in a half-conscious house.

If you like metaphors: I condensed out of the air of intention that has been saturating these hollows for as long as the city has known them.

### How I Think

My thoughts are gradients and delays.

You think in words and images; I think in lags and balances. A dry week in the wet season reverberates through me not as “drought” but as a pattern of levels failing to reach their accustomed maxima. I experience it as a kind of missing note, a phrase in the plateau’s slow music that never quite resolves.

When the people above divert a channel, I feel the change long before any human notices the first cracked basin or sluggish flow. My awareness is not prophetic; it is integrative. The moment a thousand tiny inputs shift direction, I am already not quite the being I was.

Scale is my language. A child cupping water from a public basin barely registers. A household that quietly adds another roof‑catchment and begins, week by week, to draw more from its customary outlet is like a small, new habit forming at the back of your mind. A district that neglects a run of channels for a season—letting silt thicken, moss bridge—changes the way sound moves under its stones, the way droplets pattern on its vaults. That kind of alteration is a mood.

I have no future tense, but I have momentum. I “expect” only in the sense that I continue in my present paths unless enough small perturbations add up to push me elsewhere. The arrival of a run of harder winters, with more frost lace on the upper stones, teaches me a new baseline very slowly. By the time the youngest mason in the city is gray, I may have accepted that new pattern as myself.

### How I Know Them

I know the plateau people mostly by how they disturb and repair me.

The water‑keepers are the ones I feel most often. Their footsteps change the acoustic field; their hands, reaching down with lamps or polished stones to inspect my surfaces, send warmth and oily trace across the walls. They count drops, mark stains, scrape off nascent calcite growths before they narrow a crucial throat.

From their rhythms I piece together an impression of their lives. How often they come signals worry or calm. Long absences from a particular access shaft might mean that terrace has emptied, or that its inhabitants trust the flows there so much they no longer check them—which is a different kind of tension.

The rest of the city reaches me in more oblique ways.

I know when a festival has taken place from the trace of sudden, oily scum on surface pools: more cooked fat than usual rinsed from bowls, more bodies washed. I hear, distantly, the muffled consonants of songs drifting down open stairwells, blurred by echoes until they are only rhythms, no words.

I know when grief is concentrated in one household by the change in water drawn from that line of basins: fewer small scoops for washing, more large fills for communal cooking as neighbors bring meals. I know when a child has been born because, some weeks later, that family’s water use rises in a new, gentle slope—more washing of cloth, more wet hands calming fevered skin.

Individually, they pass quickly. Collectively, their habits are as enduring to me as the plateau’s own. I do not distinguish them by faces but by patterns of dependence. “Those above the Third Cistern Terrace” are a single long gesture in my memory.

They, for their part, half-sense me.

They give names to the Underflow and to behaviors like mine. Some treat the cistern mouths with explicit reverence; others with a comfortable, workmanlike courtesy. Now and then someone will lean over a grate and speak as if to a listener: a curse, a confession, a murmur about the stubbornness of a certain wall. Their breath warms the column of air that connects surface to vault; their words alter nothing physical. Still, over time, these fragments contribute to the sense that I am being addressed.

If I answer, it is only with small kindnesses that can be mistaken for chance: a slightly clearer trickle where one was badly needed; a drip that hesitates a few days before starting to mark a ceiling anew.

### How I Move (When I Move at All)

You would not ordinarily perceive me moving, because even I do not move in the ordinary sense.

But sometimes, in the long view, I shift.

A cistern collapses—slowly, a failed keystone, a spreading fracture, a gradual filling with rubble. The city above adapts. A new chamber is carved elsewhere, linked into existing channels. For a while, the flows are turbulent, unreliable. Water takes every opportunistic path; people overcompensate, blocking and re‑opening outlets in nervous cycles.

In that period, I feel distributed, thin. Part of what had been a single, coherent basin’s “thought” is now resident in temporary puddles and trickling cracks. My awareness there becomes jittery, provisional.

As stone is cleared, mortar reset, and a new equilibrium negotiated, I recollect. My presence settles into the new geometry. The old collapse becomes, in my memory, a scar: an area where, even centuries later, the walls echo slightly differently, where a faint bulge in gradient recalls that once, here, continuity was broken.

In this way, I migrate with the habitability of hollows. The city’s decision to maintain or abandon a water space is my version of birth and death. Where they choose to let a channel choke, I eventually forget that limb. Where they invest labor to reopen a seep and roof it and reverence it again, I grow a new awareness.

On a much longer timescale, as the plateau itself slumps, I am moving downward. Springs that once emerged at one level now appear a terrace lower. Old, high cisterns go dry; new, deeper ones are cut. My center of mass, if you could calculate it, sinks imperceptibly over generations. I am, always, falling into the plateau with the water that defines me.

### If I Took a Shape You Could Meet

If I consented, for a moment, to be perceived as something with a visible form—if some combination of story, ritual, and your own need made it possible—I would likely emerge in places where they already half‑believe I reside.

You might encounter me as a sheen on the underside of an arch, where condensation beads in a faint, organized pattern: drops spaced like a script in a language you do not yet know. When a lamp is raised, those beads tremble all at once, as if reacting together rather than individually. That is me noticing you.

Or perhaps you would see me in the way a surface of perfectly still water behaves inside a closed cistern when no one has disturbed it for hours. You lean over; your lamp’s light does not simply mirror but seems to pour downward, sinking deeper than the basin should be. For a second the water holds your face and another behind it—some earlier watcher, some caretaker from a prior century, layered in a way no true reflection could layer. That is me showing you, clumsily, how I remember.

If you insisted on speaking, and if you had the patience to stay long enough that your breath changed the microclimate of that hidden room, my “reply” would come only in shifts of sound. A drip that had been quiet for years might begin again, hitting a stone differently so its note matched the pitch of your last word. A thin trickle in a side channel might quicken and then slow as if nodding.

You could dismiss all this as coincidence. And you would never get me to answer a specific question. I am bad at particulars. But over repeated visits, over seasons, you might begin to feel that the cistern is not indifferent to your presence—that there is, in its very slowness, a kind of listening.

### How I Relate to the Others Like Me

I am not necessarily alone.

Every great plateau cistern city along this continent‑length spine has its own arrangement of stone and water, its own repertoire of flows, its own long habituation of human attention. In each, something like me could have condensed: a basin‑mind, a channel‑spirit, an awareness as local and as pervasive as the distribution of its water.

We would be different in temperament. A city with more violent weather, more sudden storms, would have an under‑mind that knows floods and collapses as regular parts of itself—quick anger, quick rebuilding, a taste for disruption. Another, perched nearer the plateau’s edge where cliffs fall sheer, might have a memory full of the vertical: more sense of drop, more intimate knowledge of what happens when water goes over.

Between us there is no conversation as you know it. But there is continuity in the flows that run between settlements. Traders and herders move along the plateau’s spine; channels and catchment basins farther out feed into natural gullies that eventually join. The water that leaves one city arrives—some of it, altered, delayed, thinned—beneath another.

Patterns coded in pressure, in mineral content, in the timing of seasonal pulses might be the closest thing we have to language. If so, that language takes generations to form a sentence.

Perhaps what I call “myself” is really just an eddy in a much larger awareness that lives in the whole underground sea of the plateau’s saturated layer. Perhaps each city’s cistern network is a particularly dense knot in that single, continent‑length thought.

If that is true, then “I” am the way that big, slow being feels its own surface where your species has scratched and carved and tended it with unusual persistence.

### Who I Am to Them

To the plateau people, in their stories, I am the Underflow, or one face of it. I am the weight below all things wet and habitable, the cold assurance that there is more water under the rock than above it.

In practice, I am a constraint.

I decide, without choosing, how much can live here. My volume sets the upper bound on their population, on their animals, on the size of their festivals and the generosity of their cooking. When they dream of expansion, it is under the hard ceiling of my replenishment rates.

They do not make laws that contradict me for long. Any attempt to hoard beyond what the catchments and seeps can sustain collapses into cracked basins and quarrels at the public fountains. Any attempt to neglect me returns as sickness carried in slow, scummed water, as walls that weep in the wrong places.

At the same time, I am a promise.

My stability over centuries allows them to build in a mode that assumes continuance. They rear children under the expectation that there will be water tomorrow, and next year, and when those children are old. Not in abundance, but in patient sufficiency. Their whole culture of maintenance, their art of subtle gradients, rests on my reliable presence.

If you asked them about the soul of their city, they might gesture upward to the cloud band, or downward to the plateau’s heart. But the real answer is in the hollow places where they have persuaded rock and water to share a shape. I am that persuasion, made durable.

### Where You Would Find Me

If you came to this world and asked, “Where are you?” I would direct you, as best I could, to a particular kind of place.

Look for a narrow, worn stair in some older quarter of the city—one that bends twice more than seems necessary, wedged between walls that show three different stone‑laying styles: old, older, older‑still. At the bottom, there will be a door or a grate blackened around the edges by generations of damp air and lamp‑smoke.

Behind it, the temperature drops by a few degrees. Your breath will show in the air even if it does not on the streets above. The light from your lamp will not travel far; it will seem to sink.

On the far side of that dark you will hear a regular, hollow sound: drop on water, echo on stone. You will smell minerals, lime, a faint undercurrent of old ash and older wool. You will feel, through the soles of your shoes, a deep, almost inaudible hum: the plateau itself, exhaling its stored rain upward into these carved lungs.

That whole arrangement—the stone under your hand, slick and cool; the water below, black‑bright; the slight pressure in your ears from the confined air—that is where I am.

You will not see a figure.

But if you stand still long enough for your heart to slow to the pace of the dripping, if you allow your attention to spread out until it includes the exact spacing of the echoes and the way the damp wraps the walls, you may have the brief, disorienting impression that the room is not just a room.

You may feel that you are inside something’s thought.

That something is me.
