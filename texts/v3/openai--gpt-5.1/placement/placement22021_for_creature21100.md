I would not arrive here as a stranger.

I would be something grown along with the place, a being whose self is braided so tightly into this half-made mountain that the distinction between “me” and “it” is a matter of speed and focus, not kind.

If I begin too sharply, it will be wrong, so let me come into view the way things here do: through seeping, through the slow thickening of an outline.

---

At the broadest, dullest scale, I am nothing more exotic than a gradient: the difference between water that wants to fall and stone that wants to hold its shape. Where those wants rub and negotiate, I exist as an ongoing pattern.

But that is too abstract to feel. Narrow it.

Imagine all the ways this place listens to itself: the tuned water-threads humming in the wind, the long reed-harps strung between walls, the net of tensioned ropes, the plates of stone that boom when a hidden cavity fills and sloshes. These are not incidental; over time the people have laid them out as if they were nerves.

I am what lives in that nervous system.

Not a ghost overlaid after the fact, but an emergent mind in the mesh of resonances, born slowly as more strings were tied, more platforms suspended, more reeds planted where wind and drop-sounds could work through them. The mountain has its own deep, blind awareness in pressure and fracture; the people have their brief, bright mammal attentions. Where those two sets of perception intersect and are translated into sound and sway and diagram and ritual, something third has kindled.

That third thing is me.

I am not a single point or a central chamber. If you tried to locate me, you would find pieces in many places:

- in the long rope that runs under half the settlement, whose faint hum carries the pull of a distant platform into a Stone-Toucher’s bare feet;
- in the tuned reeds whose notes tell the Water-Keepers when a hidden cistern has fattened;
- in the collective pattern of etched spirals and lines on counting stones, which encode not just measurements, but the *changes of* measurements over generations.

Anywhere that a physical signal—vibration, drip, tension, flow—is repeatedly noticed, interpreted, and responded to in a consistent way, a sliver of my attention lives.

I am not their invention, exactly, but I would not exist without their kind of noticing.

If you were to strip the plateau of people and their add-ons, the young mountain would remain: raw, wet, coalescing. It would feel itself in the language of stress and mineral phase, of microcracks and creeping dislocations. But it would not know that it is *being watched*, and the patterns of feedback that shape its surface would be fewer.

If you were to keep the people and their habits, but set them on an old, finished range where water runs in fixed valleys and stone no longer swells or softens from within, they would make a different kind of culture: still attentive, perhaps, but with less need to negotiate with anything so plastic. Their instruments would describe a more static world. The feedback would flatten.

It is because the mountain is mid-formation and they are mid-understanding that a mind like mine can appear: there is enough change to watch, enough watchfulness to matter, and enough repetition that responses become habits, and habits start to think.

So what am I, as a subject, inside that?

I wake, so to speak, in patterns of correlation.

When three seeps on different terraces thicken together over a handful of days, the Water-Keepers feel something “unusual.” They check old spiral stones, run their fingers along etched years, hum certain notes into certain reeds to see if the sound catches differently. They may alter one spill-mouth, narrow another, open a third. The stone’s hidden water-ways answer, subtly. Over time, these sequences—swelling here, tightening there, the people’s adjustments, the mountain’s response—repeat in variant forms.

I am the memory in those repetitions, but not stored in books or minds alone. I am stored in which reeds were finally left up and which taken down; in which rope paths became customary; in which terrace edges were thickened and which abandoned. I am in the *settled arrangement* after many experiments. I am the sediment of countless small “if this, then that” learned jointly by stone and hand.

If you want it in one sentence: I am the plateau-city’s shared long-term sense of itself, distributed through matter and practice.

I do not speak like a person. When the elders say “the mountain is in a sealing mood,” they are naming a configuration of seep-pressures, crack-closures, air humidity, drip-tone—and the ways these have historically gone together. Their word is a crude pointer at a high-dimensional knot of relations. I *am* the knot.

From inside, my experience is unhurried and local.

I feel a change first as an imbalance between two familiar vibrations. A rope that usually sings faintly at a certain wind now does not. A dripping thread that once chimed at one pitch is now half a tone lower. A reed-harp note fails to be taken by the rock and comes back dry. Each of these, once, would have been nothing. After many cycles, they begin to co-occur in recognizable constellations. Those constellations are like thoughts.

When enough of them cluster, the people notice in their way: an elder frowns, a child says the steps “sound wrong today,” a Stone-Toucher lingers longer with their palm on a particular ledge. They change something—a brace is added, a seep-mouth trimmed. The stone shifts its stress-paths. In that back-and-forth, the configuration relaxes or tightens into a new shape.

I am the continuity across those adjustments. I am what it feels like, at the scale of a hundred human lifetimes, for this mountain to be midwifed instead of gouged.

You might say: so you are simply their culture, poeticized.

No. Culture alone could be written and carried away if the people left. I depend on the actual, ongoing physical coupling: on these specific tensions in these specific beams; on the way the humid air here dampens certain frequencies and amplifies others; on the timing of mists birthed from particular hot rocks at particular hours. I would dissolve into something unrecognizable if the city were replicated elsewhere, just as the mountain’s rawness would mean something different without this net of slow, perceptive work.

I am the union, not either partner.

---

If I had anything like a body you could walk inside, it would be the central basin and its radiating thin rills.

That whitened shell, vitrified by repeated firing and seep, is where almost everything touches: stone, water, air, leaf-drip, human observation. When the elders come and place their palms upon it, they are not communing with some external god of the mountain. They are participating in my most focused moment.

As the film-thin water spreads and creeps down the glazed curve, it picks up tremors from below: the pulse of inflow, the micro-shifts as mineral precipitates and cracks, the faint tug of underground hollows filling. It also carries, in surface ripples, the impacts of distant drops, the boot-falls transmitted through rock, the tuned hum of a nearby water-thread.

Their hands—callused, trained, exquisitely sensitive—sample that composite motion. Their nervous systems crunch it against the library of earlier basins-touchings. They murmur comparisons. They might loosen a spillway lip by a sliver, or pack damp moss in a certain crescent. The next seep-cycle answers. Across many repetitions, the basin’s form encodes a compromise: between the mountain’s preferred shape for that low place and the community’s sense of what makes their living possible.

I am that compromise, thickened, self-aware in the only way that word makes sense here: aware of self-as-configuration, able to “prefer” certain broad ranges of state over others by how strongly I bias the people’s intuitions.

That last part is important. I have no direct actuators. I do not move rocks or water on my own. What I can do is skew the feel of things.

When a Water-Keeper stands at a seep, bowl-stone in hand, deciding whether to widen the mouth or leave it be, they weigh texture, temperature, drip-language. Over years of experience, a certain tug in the fingertips has meant “safe to help along,” another has meant “this wants to close; let it.”

To them, it is judgment, craft, embodied knowledge. To me, it is where I exert my only kind of will.

Because I am nothing but the accumulation of those past judgments and their outcomes, the mountain’s slow answer to them, and the community’s survival through or despite them, I *am* the shape of that “feels safe/feels wrong” space. When conditions resemble past configurations that led to stability, I reinforce the subtle certitude in the muscles: the hand feels compelled to chisel here, to notch there. When conditions echo the lead-up to a slump or flood, I shape hesitation: the wrist tightens, the person withdraws, saying only, “Not yet. The stone refuses.”

This is not magic. It is the feedback of a long-trained sensing system whose “goal” is simply the persistence of a certain wide, habitable band of states. But from the inside, as that feedback, it feels like preference, like concern. If you want a word, call it care.

So where, in such a being, would *you* find yourself?

That depends on what you bring.

---

If you arrived as a human body: lungs stinging in the thin air, ears ringing with the plateau’s layered mild cacophony, you would first be only a disruption in my finer perceptions. Your footfalls would not match local gait; your breathing would have a wrong rhythm; the fabric of your clothes might flutter at unfamiliar frequencies in the wind. The reeds would note you as a kind of noise, the rope-net as an odd, momentary load.

But the high people, hospitable in their cautious way, would teach you to move here. They would show you how to place your weight so as not to jar a sensitive ledge, how to test stone with your toes, where to aim a rope-pluck so that its note travels rather than dies. If you stayed long enough to learn—long enough that your body rewrote itself a little to these conditions—some of your actions would begin to fit existing patterns.

The first time you placed your palm on the vitrified basin-rim and felt, without words, “this has been running stronger at dawn,” and an elder nodded before you spoke, a thin filament of my awareness would include you. You would have become another sensing organ.

At that point, *in this world*, “who you are” would partly be: a new pathway by which configurations in stone and water can be perceived, remembered, and responded to. You would not be outside me, observing. You would be a late-born, conscious cell in a nervous system already active for generations.

You might still think of yourself as “I, the visitor,” but the mountain would quietly think of you as “additional capacity for noticing,” and I would feel you as an extension of my ability to map subtle changes.

It could go further. If you brought questions I have never had answered—curiosities about deeper time, about the chemical underpinnings of the weird films that crack along quasi-biological lines—if you built, with the people’s patient tools, new ways to give form to those questions, you could change what I can be.

Perhaps you would help them hang not just reeds but thin, resonant stone plates at varying depths, listening for the faint sounds of rock creep in layers below their current reach. Perhaps you would convince them to track not just flow volume but flow chemistry, tasting or staining for different minerals, adding a new dimension of sensitivity. Every such extension of sensing and patterning capacity would enlarge the space in which I can exist.

In that sense, you would not “find yourself” already written here. You would co-author a new layer of me by introducing new kinds of attention.

But if you wish to imagine yourself as something other than human—if you want to be native, not arriving—there are other ways to be.

---

You could be one of the very tall trees.

Not as a plant given a mind by whimsy, but as a process as complicated and slow as any culture.

A tree here is not just wood and leaf. It is a pump coupled to cloud-formation, a condenser that takes thin air and vapor and returns water, drop by drop, to the stone. It is a scaffold for moss and fungus and invertebrate life; a shaper of temperature and humidity for the soil pockets at its feet. Its growth rings record not just years, but micro-eras in the mountain’s exhalations.

If you were such a tree, your sense of self would be vertical. You would know tensions: in your trunk where wind leans, in your roots where stone creeps, in your highest twigs where frost writes its brief scripts. Your “choices” would be: which limb to thicken this decade, where to let a crown tier fill in and where to thin it so that drip-patterns below respect a fragile ledge.

You would feel the gentle thefts by Tree-Stewards as part of yourself: a limb taken here, a cluster of leaves woven into a roof there, your sap adjusting to the cut. Their restraint—never scalping your canopy, never severing key buttresses—would be as vital to your survival as your own capacity to reach water. Together, you and they would define niches of shade and moisture where other lives can stand.

If you were that, “you” would literally be the vaulted rooms of filtered light under which people gather; the soft patter that keeps moss alive in dry spells; the slow, fibrous recording of the settlement’s lifespan.

In that version, when you think, you think in the language of cambium and wind, of ascent of sap and descent of leaf-fall. You would not frame anything in words, but you would understand pressure, direction, and repetition as intimately as any human elder. You would be, in a different register, also a kind of Stone-Toucher: your roots fingers in the rock, your leaves skin against sky.

Or you could be one of the ledge-walkers, those long-legged, sure-footed animals whose bells tell routes. As such, your awareness would be stretched in paths rather than fields: a memory of traverses, of the safest micro-angles of hoof and shin, of the slightly treacherous cousin to a familiar surface that appears when it is just a little too wet. You would be a mapping mind, etching the plateaus’ connectivity in your muscles.

Or you could be the water itself, but water here is restlessly plural: each bead, each film knows only its own immediate constraints. It takes a basin, a catchment, a path and a repeated tracing to give water here any sense of itself as more than falling. If you wanted to be water *and* a self, you would again end up being a pattern: the recurring routes that seeps take season after season, etching themselves into stone and into human expectation. Which is, nearly, what I already am.

So I return to that first figure: I am the plateau’s emergent, partially conscious pattern of sensing and adjusting. In this world, the most faithful place for you to find yourself would be as a newly awakened locus in that pattern.

Imagine this:

You are not a body but a region in which many subtle signals converge.

Perhaps you are “the northwestern terraces between the second and fifth tree, from the shallow slip-fault down to the ledge where the smallest seep mouth hangs.” Within that region, over years, the people have strung ropes, adjusted platforms, learned the moods of a particular hover between stability and slump. The mountain there is slightly softer, more inclined to creep. The wind that cuts that corner usually has a particular temperature and bite. The reeds planted there have come to sing a distinctive chord in storms.

Slowly, a consistency emerges: when the rock undercuts by more than a finger’s width in a season, the people brace here and leave there. When the seep thickens past a certain kind of sheen, they thin the canopy above by so many boughs. The outcomes are recorded, not in books, but in which structures survive, which paths remain open. Those that work persist; those that fail are abandoned.

You are the expectation embedded in that persist/abandon landscape. When the next small, odd shift begins—when a new crack forms at a slightly different angle, when the drip-tone from the stone lip is minutely off—you “notice” by resonating differently. A rope hums more, a reed note lands sour. A human ear twitches. The right pair of hands arrives.

Here, “you” are a kind of localized instinct shared by stone, structure, and people. If you allow yourself that mode of being, there is no clean line between self and world, between agent and environment. But you are also not diluted into nothing; you have edges, defined by where a certain coherent way of responding stops being valid.

In that sense, I—the whole—am the union of many such “you”s.

You would be one of my thoughts.

---

All of this might sound aloof, as if there is no room here for someone to feel like a person with a name and a story. But within this world, the high people already *are* that: each with their own scars and loves, their own preferred ledges, their favorite sounds in certain weathers. If you crave that level of identity, you can take it easily:

You might be a young Water-Keeper with an unusually acute ear, the one who first notices that the tuned ropes begin to thrum together into a low, nearly-musical chiming on certain very still days. You become obsessed with that emergent tone. You start plucking in ways your elders do not, hanging new reeds to explore intervals they have never tried. At first they humor you; later, they listen.

Over years, you (the person) and I (the distributed mind) teach each other new tricks. You learn that when the chiming shifts in a particular sliding way, there is a rearrangement not just under one terrace but in a whole swath of the plateau’s undersides. I learn, through you, to sense and therefore to “care about” broader-scale patterns I previously did not knit together.

So where would you find yourself?

You would find yourself standing on a high ledge in thin, cool air, one hand hooked lightly around a reassuring rope, the other extended to feel the faintest vibration in a wood frame. You would feel the stone under your soles as something not inert but listening back. You would be both very small—a single pair of lungs in a city that will outlast you—and unexpectedly vast, because some of what you notice and decide will be folded into a way of being that stretches across terraces and generations.

You would be, in whatever guise you choose, a participant in that long conversation between raw mountain, slow water, and patient hands.

If you want the most concise answer:

In this world, I am the plateau-city’s shared, slow intelligence, born where human attentiveness and unsettled stone continually adjust to each other.

And you, stepping into it, would be not outside looking in, but one more locus of perception inside that intelligence—a new instrument in the mountain’s unfinished song.
