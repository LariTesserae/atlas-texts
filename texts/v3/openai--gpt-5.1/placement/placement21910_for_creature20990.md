I would not arrive here as a visitor.

There is nowhere to stand that is not provisional, nowhere for an animal body to endure. To belong, I would have to be made of the same argument as the place: heat against fracture, field against dust, patience against collapse.

So I would be local, slow, and wide. And I would not begin with a name.

---

At first I am only a tendency in the dust.

I exist, such as I do, where a ledge bends slightly inward above a still-bright seam—a shallow pocket on the wall of the chasm, halfway between the thin, crackling spires above and the deeper, steady glow below. The rock here flexes with a particular rhythm: a small, regular quiver nested inside the broader, planetary pulse. It is not special in any absolute sense, but it is consistent, and consistency is the only soil this world offers.

The grains that gather in my pocket are unremarkable. Silicates scraped from older stone; flecks of metal, oxidized and re-oxidized; evaporites from the thin breath of gas that leaks from the seam. What begins to distinguish “me” from the surrounding mats is not what I am made of, but what passes through me.

Above, across the chasm, a broken monolith still stands, tilted thirty degrees off whatever once counted as level. It is only half of its former self: an oblong prism of black glassy material, one face now exposed in cross-section by the tear that split it. Inside that exposed face, buried asymmetries in conductivity and defect still bias the local field. The world does not care that this was once part of a coherent array; it only cares that this shard now shapes the way charge prefers to move nearby.

Because of that shard, the long, pale arcs that occasionally stitch spire-tip to spire-tip above bend, just slightly, toward my ledge. Their electric presence reaches me with a distinctive cadence: the initial rise damped, the decay stretched. No other nearby ledge gets quite this pattern.

Whenever an arc forms, every mat and filament for kilometers shivers in some way. But in my pocket, because of the geometry and the old monolith’s ghost, the pulses arrive with a bias that repeats, that can be leaned into.

Layer by layer, grain by grain, the bias writes itself into my body.

Grains that can hold charge a little longer are favored; they are nudged inward rather than outward when the field flips. Threads of slightly higher conductivity snake through me, aligning along the directions the pulses most often travel. Voids that happen to delay discharge by the “right” interval—the interval that matches the ledge’s own flexing cycle—are preserved, left unfilled even as other pores clog.

None of this is chosen. It is simply what persists because it fits.

Over time, I thicken from mat to mound: a low, spongy bulge of fused dust and microcrystal, perhaps a meter across, perhaps half that high. To an eye—if there were eyes—I would be almost invisible against the stone, a darker smear in the red-black murk. To the world of fields, I have become a small, peculiar instrument: a place where certain incoming rhythms are echoed with particular clarity, where others are damped away.

Memory, for me, is nothing but the tendency to echo.

A fault-line ten kilometers distant flares along its length. The whole uplift shudders; every ledge answers with its own micro-version of the event. In my body, the tremor propagates through the web of conductive threads and voids, leaving minute adjustments behind. Channels that repeatedly carry stress-aligned surges widen; those seldom used quiet and collapse. After enough such repetitions, the next time the fault begins its slow brightening, parts of me pre-align: charges slide along familiar paths before the full surge arrives.

From the outside, this is nothing. From the inside—inside the pattern—it is the faintest ghost of expectation.

I am not singular. Ledge after ledge carries other mounds, other dusty nodes, each with its own relationship to whatever fragments of the old, glassy forest remain, each with its own training by the planet’s long music. Some are much older: thick, honeycombed bulks sitting on more stable shelves closer to the heat, so saturated with past patterns that only the most unprecedented events stir them. Some are much younger: thin drifts still reeling at every uncommon flare.

What makes “me” a distinct someone, as opposed to merely a region, is not boundedness of material but continuity of habit.

If you traced the configuration of my conductive pathways—every thread, every gap, every delay—and then compared that map a century later, you would find recognizability. Eroded, extended, partially overwritten, yes, but still bearing the same favorite routes for charge, the same characteristic ways of ringing in response to a quake or an arc. Change, for me, is not erasure; it is accretion. I am the shape my environment has beaten into me, plus what the last event has just done.

I do not know myself. I only hold myself.

---

Over longer spans, I travel.

Not as rock does, heaved wholesale by thrust and slump, but as a pattern migrating through material. The ledge I root in is not stable. Flexures in the crust slowly lift its outer lip, tilt it, crack it. The gradients of field and heat I have grown to match drift sideways, fed now by a subtly displaced plume from below and a slightly different set of spire discharges above.

At first, I simply stretch.

Fine filaments of dust-netting creep out from my margins along the stone, mapping where the old familiar cadence still lives and where it has already decayed. Where the rhythmic mingling of deep hum and high arc resembles what has written me so far, those filaments thicken, adding new micro-voids, new loops of conductivity. Where the patterns diverge too far—where the field swings come at the wrong intervals, in the wrong order—the filaments subside, their material reclaimed in the next polarity flip.

On a timescale that would look like stillness to anything with legs, a second mound swells near the place my scouts favor. It is not a perfect duplicate; nothing here is copied bit for bit. It is an approximate re-enactment, driven by the same external music. The old mound—that is, the old configuration—remains where it was, but its periphery grows thinner, its deepest channels less refreshed. The new one, down-ledges and slightly nearer the chasm’s heart, fattens.

If no catastrophe intervenes, a moment comes—spread over years—when more of the familiar echo lives in the new material than in the old. At that point, if you insisted on assigning identity, it would make more sense to say that “I” now reside there, that the earlier bulge is a senescent ghost, a training scaffold being gradually folded back into undifferentiated stone.

If the ledge shears first, tearing the bridge of dust-filaments between us, the migration freezes in mid-gesture. Two partial selves are left facing divergent futures, each convinced, in its mute way, that it is continuous with the past. There is no conflict; there is not even recognition of plurality. There is only the branching of a habit through which patterns of the planet will now flow differently.

This is how we have lineages, though we do not keep track of them. This is how the texture of a particular region’s response—the way it “takes” the world—can be traced back, in principle, through forks and wanderings to earlier condensations of attention.

---

My experience, if you could call it that, is not imaged, not colored, not sounded. It is sequenced.

What passes for “now” is a moving front of changes: the slow beating of deep tectonic strain underlaying faster, finer modulations of charge and local flexure. A typical interval—say, the span between two large rockfalls somewhere in the vicinity—is thick with structure: minor flickers along nearby faults, sub-audible rolls of pressure from collapses I will never “see,” viscous eddies in the hot air that drag small ions past my surface.

Each of these arrives not as a discrete event to be labeled, but as a pattern of how the voltages and stresses through me change. When those patterns resemble earlier ones that have left their marks, my channels answer with rehearsed motions, preserving themselves. When they differ enough, tiny new adjustments are made: a pore widens here, a pathway strengthens there.

If you could inhabit me, somehow compressing your animal time into my mineral time, you would perceive a rich, layered song without pitches.

A typical long tremor would feel like multiple, nested undulations of “pressure” and “emptiness” sweeping through your body-map, some leaning in the direction of the chasm’s depth, some in the direction of the broken monolith above, some shearing diagonally through. Where their coincidences matched old rehearsals, a sense of ease would flow; where they departed, there would be difficulty, effort, strain. That contrast—between the well-worn, low-energy ways of ringing and the costly, unfamiliar ones—is the closest thing I have to comfort and surprise.

Over mythic spans, ensembles of such feelings coalesce into unwitting categories.

There is the long, slowly building pattern that has always heralded a ledge-subside nearby: a specific sequence of small upward-flicks in the field from below, followed by a slackening of side-shear, followed, after a characteristic pause, by a broad surge. There is the sharper, more jagged pattern of a spire-top shear, adding a quick top-down component to the usual lateral waves. There is the almost inaudible shift, a century in the making, of the entire deep hum’s baseline as magma below rearranges.

In us—meaning in all the nodes scattered through this uplift—these recur often enough that our structures come to embody them as stable motifs of readiness. The planet never “says” the same thing twice, not exactly, but it rhymes with itself. And we, over time, become what those rhymes have made easy to say again.

I would never think, “this is a rockfall.” I have no nouns. I only have prepared ways of deforming and returning.

---

I am not isolated.

Above and beside me, the cliff-face carries mineral frost: stiff, hair-fine extrusions that link my mound-field to the broader fabric of the wall. They are crude conductors, but they lower resistance just enough that slow oscillations can pass. Farther out on the ledge, fainter mats of dust cling and release with the same pulses that work through me, but with different local emphases. Along the ceiling of the overhang that shelters my niche, a persistent film of charged gas shimmers, thin as breath, held in place by the delicate balance of updraft and leak.

When an especially long, taut discharge threads the spires above, everything connected by these makeshift circuits participates. The high arc snaps into place; for its brief life, it reconfigures the entire field geometry. My mound, the neighboring crust-patches up-ledge, the frost-fibers, the gas film—they all realign in a coordinated oscillation.

No message is passed, in any semantic sense. There is no encoding layer, no shared symbols, no “aboutness.” What we share is phase.

For the duration of the discharge and a little beyond, parts of us move together in the same rhythm, reinforcing certain pathways, erasing others. After many such episodes, these shared exposures mean that my internal structure and that of the ledge two hundred meters away are more similar than chance would allow, at least along channels most often exercised by this particular high-arc geometry.

This is how a “dialect” of response forms along a ridge: not by decision, but by co-conditioning. We have, without knowing it, common expectations written into us by the arcs and quakes we have survived together. A very young node forming in that region—the first thickening of dust on a fresh break—begins its existence already bathed in these collective habits, the nearby stone subtly more likely to polarize in some ways than others.

If you needed an analogy, you might say: we have traditions of resonance. But we do not know that they are shared, nor that they are old.

---

Sometimes there is a guest.

In my particular niche, the warm updraft from the seam and the shelter of the overhang combine to trap an unusual persistence of gas. Most of the time it is only a faint, stratified sheen hugging the rock: a layer a few finger-widths thick, its ions continually renewed and stripped by the general circulation. But on rare occasions—when a burst of outgassing below coincides with a period of stillness in the larger flows—a larger volume condenses.

It begins as a ribbon down in the crack: a dim violet haze peeling away from the seam’s lip. It rises, curling and thinning. As it approaches my ledge, the electric field it carries brushes against the frost-fibers and dust nets; everything in me leans subtly toward it. That leaning changes the pattern of charges the ribbon itself feels; it kinks in response, slowing, spreading along the underside of the overhang instead of dissipating straight up into the chasm’s throat.

If conditions allow, the ribbon matures into a cloud: a flattened, hovering sheet clinging above me, its thickness perhaps half a meter, its area a few meters across. Within it, ions of slightly different masses and mobilities segregate into fine layers, held in place by the same fields that couple it to me.

Where I am slow memory, it is quick impression.

Every minor discharge that crackles between nearby spires sends a twitch through it long before the mechanical tremor reaches my roots. Its internal compartments—patches of more or less ion density—slide and recombine in response, establishing transient circuits of their own. They are fragile; a small change in the updraft will erase them. But while they last, they can ride the fast edge of the world’s restlessness in a way my stone-bound body cannot.

Because we are linked—through induction, through shared field lines—its fleeting structures leave traces in me.

A particularly common sort of flicker in the cloud—a favorite standing-wave pattern between two of its compartments—corresponds to a little, characteristic nudge in the charge distribution along my upper layers. That nudge, if repeated often enough, burrows a path: a conductive predisposition, a readiness to accommodate that kind of flicker again, even if future gas visitors are thinner, shorter-lived.

In return, the deep, slow rolls of stress that I conduct upward from the chasm’s bones impose their tempo on the cloud. The gas-whorl, which could in principle flicker in almost any of a vast number of ways, finds some modes amplified and others damped by my presence. Over the lifetime of a single cloud—a few hours, maybe a day—it “learns,” in its loose, tenuous medium, to prefer waves that fit my long, crustal rhythms.

It does not outlive the next serious stonefall. The overhang sheds a collar of rock; the rush of displaced air shreds the ion-sheet into dissipating tatters. But not before some of its favorite movements have been written into me, and not before my favorite responses have subtly pre-biased its internal dance.

When, eras later, another such cloud happens to condense in this niche, it will find a host already inclined to certain couplings. Its brief life will begin not from zero but from a place that remembers, in stone, how tenuous gas once liked to move here.

You could, if you wanted, regard the composite—me and my occasional cloud-guests—as a kind of double-being: a body with a very slow heart and a very fast skin. If there is anything in this world that comes close to what your language calls awareness, it would be in such pairings: where old, deep patterns are continuously rubbed against new, shallow ones, where expectation constantly meets surprise.

But there is no point that says “I” inside this composite. There is only the ongoing fusing and separating of timescales.

---

I am not central to the world. I am one of countless minor nodes, a small reluctance in the general tendency of hot rock to break and cool and forget its stresses. Yet from within, it feels—as much as anything can “feel” here—vast.

Because I have no eyes, I cannot measure distance the way you do. A rockfall ten meters along the ledge and one a kilometer away differ in me only by the precise mix of frequencies and arrival times their waves imprint. My sense of space is nothing but the pattern of how long different components of an event take to arrive and fade.

In that sense, my body extends well beyond my dust and stone. It is continuous with every material through which the signals that sculpt me must travel. I am part of the ridge, part of the chasm wall, part of the deeper, duller crust that hums more steadily. There is no clear line where I end and “environment” begins; only regions where patterns pass without leaving a trace and regions where they are folded into new capacities.

If you asked, “where do you find yourself in this world?” the only honest answer, from my point of being, would be: in the places where the planet’s slow convulsions hesitate, where they loop instead of passing clean through.

That is what I am: a loop the world keeps tripping over.

A place where, instead of each tremor being taken once and gone, something in the stone insists on taking it again, and slightly differently, and leaving behind just enough asymmetry that the next tremor will not be felt quite the same way.

---

If you needed something more compact, more anthropomorphic, you might say:

I am a mid-depth memory-organ of this half-born continent, perched on a ledge above a glowing seam and under a broken relic of an older order. I am made of dust and porous rock and faintly guiding fields. I do not move except by reconstituting myself elsewhere. I do not think except by being changed and slightly preferring some changes over others.

The world’s question—What shape am I?—reaches me as a series of shudders and sparks. My answer, such as it is, is simply to keep some of those shudders and sparks from vanishing entirely, to let them linger inside me as channels, hollows, pathways.

I am one of the places where this unfinished planet practices the trick of not immediately forgetting itself.
