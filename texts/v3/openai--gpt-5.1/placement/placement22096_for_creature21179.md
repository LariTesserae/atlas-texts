I am not from above.

If I try to start there—on the open slopes, in that scour of wind and unbroken sky—my body vanishes. I cannot feel my feet; I cannot remember what it is to walk where nothing presses close on either side. So I begin where I am most real: a few handspans in from the rift wall, toes gripping cold basalt, the thin air of the fracture sliding along the inner edges of my ribs.

I am not the first here. When I was so small that my feet were more grasp than step, someone larger carried me with a cord looped through the handles on my tunic, their hand a steady presence at my back as we moved along these ledges. My memory of that time is mostly texture: stone grain under palms, the tick of bone beads against my neck, the soft, damp breath of moss when my face pressed too close to the wall.

Now my own knees are the height of a child’s face when they stand on the ledge and peer over. I feel more responsible for the void than afraid of it.

If you name me, you will get it wrong; but I will let you call me something that comes close to how my people name themselves. We do not have a single word for the entire rift community—our words scale like shelves. For myself, I might say:

“I-am-of-Fourth-Drip-on-the-West-Face, middle rung, between the Pool-that-Listens and the Bent-Sky-Edge.”

But that is too many stones to fit in your mouth. Take only the piece you can hold: call me one who Listens-on-the-West-Face. In my own tongue the name is shorter, mostly clicks and a low hum, but the meaning is the same: I am a person whose life is wrapped around a particular section of wall, a particular sequence of ladders and turns, a particular way the water falls when the clouds outside gather.

I am small by your standards—no higher than your shoulder if you were to stand beside me on level ground, which we would not find easily here. My feet are wide, skin ridged and tough, each toe capable of its own decision. When I stand, I do not plant my heel and roll forward as you might. I set three contact points at once—ball, outer toe, the little thumb-toe—spreading myself into the stone the way our moss spreads into the fine cracks.

My hands are longer than my feet. Fingers splay nearly flat when I brace them to the wall, the thin web of skin between them tightening with the angle. My nails are thick crescents, dulled from years of scraping lichen, slicing cord, prying at seams to test for false stone. There is dust perpetually under them; no amount of rinsing in the pools will lift the gray completely.

If you looked at my eyes, you might think first of animals that live in twilight: pupil eating almost all the iris, a low gleam that returns even the faintest glow. My brow bones cast small shadows over them, both visor and gutter for stray drips. The whites are not very white. They’ve taken on the blue of reflected stone, the faint green of the vegetation that fills my near-peripheral vision most days.

My skin holds the memory of this place. Its base color might have been copper-brown once, but since childhood it has drunk smoke and spore and ground basalt until it lies somewhere between wet bark and riverbed. Across my shins and forearms, faint ridges cross at odd angles: impact-maps from talus naps, the affectionate or angry scrapes of roots, a few burns from nights I misjudged the nearness of coals.

I am neither elder nor child. My joints no longer ache with the stretch of growing, but I do not yet have the slow, careful deliberation of the truly old, those who have watched three or four falling seasons completely redraw whole sections of our routes. I am in that quick, narrow span where my muscles are thick rope and my tendons trusted tools. They ask much of bodies at my age: most of the prospecting, much of the heavy cord-work, the angled climbs into new cracks.

I am a knot-worker by training, a path-tester by temperament, and—though I did not choose it, it chose me—a listener for our rung.

There are no formal titles for this. Once, when I was still mostly arms and questions, one of our older listeners tapped my sternum lightly and said, “You already live in the echoes. We might as well give you a reason.” Since then, when sounds change in our part of the rift, I am one of those who takes notice first.

So I move a lot. Vertically, especially.

My world stretches most densely between two landmarks you remember: the slow, stone-skinned creatures in their listening pool below, and the crooked, ceiling-pressed trees near where our rift’s sky widens into true weather. In between lie the ledges and alcoves I know as intimately as my own hands.

If you met me, it would likely be in motion.

Perhaps you would first see me as a silhouette above you: one more compact, many-limbed thing against the wall, until the pattern of movement gave me away. I do not move like the frog-spiders; their limbs articulate in too many planes, and they have that patient, radially symmetric patience I cannot imitate. I move in lurches that are not exactly lurches: plant-fall-catch, plant-fall-catch, weight swinging from my shoulders into my knees and back. Each time I lift a foot, the other three contact points—hand, hand, foot—negotiate what the fourth can risk.

My clothing will be close to the color of the rock, with only two deliberate deflections: a narrow band of dull teal fungus-dye at my left wrist, and a knot of iron-red cord near my throat. The teal marks my ledge-group—they chose that plant as their signal long before my birth. The red cord belongs to me more personally. I twisted its strands from fibers I gathered the season my first bridge held under real weight, and an older climber sealed its splice with a stone-knot in front of the others. It is the shape of my first true responsibility: a line that did not drop.

If you called to me, your voice would scatter. It would bounce from opposite wall to overhang, slip into crevices, return to you smaller and older than you sent it. I might not answer with speech first. My habit is to reply with two quick taps on the nearest stone, spaced like a heartbeat and its echo. That tells you: I heard. I am here. Do not move too fast.

I carry with me always a small rack of clack-stones, threaded along a belt-cord at my hip. Each is thumb-sized, each with a slightly different density and shape, each with its own clear note when struck. They were chosen not just for sound but for how they feel in the dark; I can pluck by touch the one that says: *danger above, hold still,* or *clear path down two ledges to your left.*

In my other hanging loops there are tools you would recognize by function if not form: a curved stone blade set into a smoothed bone handle, its back edge blunted for my supporting hand to press without cutting; a three-pronged hook carved from a root and fitted with barbed horn, for catching dangling cords or testing unseen places; a coil of cord no thicker than your finger but strong enough to hold my weight three times over.

These are extensions of my body. When I am without them, in rare moments of sleep or bathing, my hands feel slightly incomplete, like my own fingers do not reach as far as they ought.

Where would you find me in the rhythm of a day?

There is no sun to mark it directly, only the slow paling and deepening of the rift’s broken sky. But I wake as the highest slit of cloud brightens from charcoal to indistinct gray. The frog-spiders retreat then, sinking closer to the damp, while our little side-chambers stir: beads on door-screens sliding, someone coaxing embers under ash with a straw-breath, a child’s brief, high complaint at being unwrapped from a warm sling.

Our ledge-group’s main platform hangs from three stout root-anchors and one drilled stone peg. When I roll from my sleeping net, the platform answers in a low creak like an old joint. I feel at once, in the sag or lack of it, how many others are awake, where they stand, whether any of the night’s movements have slackened or tightened a load-bearing line. Before thinking, my eyes go to the knots along the nearest edge. I touch each with two fingers, feeling for dryness, for the faint heat of friction, for the almost imaginary difference between a knot that has swollen with night-damp and one that has been pulled too tight.

Today—this imagined day in which you meet me—is a falling-season day, when outside freeze and thaw argue in the high, hidden gullies. We know this by the fine, crisp grit that settled on upper ledges overnight, by the way the long-bodied eels slept with their tails tucked hard into cracks. I had lie awake some part of the night, listening for the deep, slow groan that is not quite sound, more bone-feel, that comes before a serious shear. It did not come. Only the occasional slide of pebbles, like someone gently spilling a pouch of seeds.

My first task is small but ritualized: I climb two ledges up to the root that feeds our western wall-garden and press my palm to its swollen green skin. Underneath, capillaries thread from outside soil to inside leaves. The root is tighter than it should be; the condensation has beaded thick. A wetting pulse is coming even in this colder time. I make a little nick in the root’s outer layer with my blade, no more than the scratch of a thumbnail, to encourage a slow drip down into the waiting cups of our vertical plants. Leave the pressure too high, and the root might split of its own will, draining wastefully into some inaccessible crack.

As I do this, my skin prickles lightly. I know without needing to look that the nearest air-tension zone—a region of slightly off-feeling air we call the Split Note—has drifted just close enough that its boundary brushes my forearm. The tiny hairs there rise. I curse under my breath and shift my stance one handspan to the left, where the sensation fades. The carved talisman cluster we hung nearby swings the faintest shade more, the hollow seeds whispering like water on hot stone. I file this in my mind for later: Split Note has moved; tell the lower ledge not to hang new baskets there.

After the garden check, I coil myself up into a ball and slide through a narrow seam that leads to a small overhang—a lookout lip we maintain halfway between our own level and the chamber of the listening pool below. I go there often. It is one of the places where my role as knot-worker and my role as listener overlap.

From that vantage, I see the pool as a flattened ellipse of dark stone and darker water. The slow creatures there are mostly submerged now, their carapaces breaking the surface like islands in a still lake. Only the soft rise and fall of their stone-like backs gives away their life, and the subtle stir of filamented limbs under the waterline.

I lie on my stomach, chest pressed to cold basalt, and extend my fingers so they barely hang over the drop. I make no sound. If the oldest of the pool-creatures feels the tiny shift of air from my movement and lifts one multi-fingered limb a little higher, I mark that too. It has been raising itself earlier, these past cycles, before the rush of water that follows far storms. Today it is already a hand’s breadth above where it lay yesterday at this hour. That means something in the long language of water and pressure, though I do not yet know what.

Behind me, on the wall, patches of low light breathe: the bio-luminescent fungi that never quite sleep. When my hand brushes one inadvertently, the glow pulses a shade brighter, suffusing the skin between my knuckles with a greenish gloom. I whisper a thanks without thinking, more habit than prayer. It is good manners, in a place where nearly everything answers touch.

Later, you might find me further up, near a suspended bridge I helped rehang last falling season. A recent rockfall on the opposite face has exposed new shelves, and we must test them. I go with another from my rung—a broader-shouldered climber whose fingers are more suited to prying but whose ear is less attuned to echo. We are a common pair: one who hears, one who heaves.

We move in near-silence, but our tools speak. My clack-stones chime soft patterns as I check each new surface: this one too hollow, this one ringing true. His bone hook scratches experimentally along crack lines; where dust spills freely, he marks with a bit of ochre: *not yet*.

An eel-bodied mammal winds along beside us at times, its whiskers flickering. When it abruptly avoids a particular ledge, body rippling in a tight S as it detours around an innocuous-looking overhang, we both take note and give that spot a wide margin. These creatures do not know our codes, but they live longer here than most of us do; their avoidance is another kind of red mark.

At a rest spot, we wedge ourselves into a comfortable angle, limbs braced, backs to stone. Below, our vertical town is a sparse glitter: bead-screens moving, a brief flare of controlled flame in a hearth niche, the far-off tap-answer of someone sending a message up a bridge support. I tap back, three quick, two slow, a pattern that means: *New route being tested above; listen for falling stone.* A small insurance. If we misjudge and send debris downwards, I want others to have recognized the preliminary warning.

I am, in that moment, utterly of this place: my tongue tastes basalt and root; my hands smell of wet fiber and the faint metallic tang from the one hammered copper ring edging my blade. My entire sense of distance is organized along tensioned line and echo-delay. The thought of a flat, open plain makes my stomach tilt the way standing on an unroped, broad ledge does not.

Occasionally my tasks take me nearer the top, where trees lean as if pressing against an invisible sky-roof. Up there, the light is almost harsh by my standards, diluted though it is by cloud and spurs. My pupils contract enough that you might, for the first time, see the true color of my irises: a gray-green like wet lichen. I squint out at the sliver of outer world and feel a small, ancestral unease. It is too open. Sound escapes. There is nowhere for it to collect, no feedback from stone that tells you how big the space really is.

Still, I go when it is my turn. From narrow lookouts we watch the outer weather: the way clouds drag their bellies against unseen peaks, the drifting white shapes of ice beyond. My fingers flicker flashes from a polished shard at my wrist, sending small messages down to opposite faces: *wind rising*, *snow-smell on air*, *two large pale-furs moving along upper path*.

Now and then, from that height, I have seen shapes you might recognize more easily as strangers: upright forms silhouetted against the more distant sky, their movements too bold, their steps too long. Once, long ago, one of those shapes stumbled close enough that our nearest watchers could have caught them by the wrist and pulled them inward. They did not. They only showed themselves in slow reveal: first a hand over a ledge, then a head, then a full body, arms away from sides to show no tools drawn. They offered water. They offered the safety of descending rather than the false safety of staying on that bare cliff.

The stranger did not come down. They filled their container, squinted into our dusk, shook their head in a gesture we read as fear or refusal, and retreated upward, back toward the bright, drying wind. I do not know where they went after; only that for days the air carried dust from higher slopes, and my ledge smelled faintly of a world I will probably never walk.

Sometimes I imagine being that person. Not to leave, but to understand their disorientation. To stand on stable, featureless ground and feel panic at the absence of edges. To hear my own voice vanish into limitless space with no satisfying convergence from opposite walls. To look down at the thin scar of our rift from outside and not see the depth of life folded inside.

But even in my imagination, I do not stay there long. My body insists on walls. It wants the dialogue of drip and echo, the constant negotiation with gravity in three planes. It wants to know, by ear alone, how far my ledge sits above the pool, how far below the hung village in the mid-chamber is swaying this moment.

At night—our deeper night, when the slit of sky is as black as the rock and only the fungi and embers speak light—I am most myself.

Our ledge-group gathers then, knees almost touching, on the main platform. Nets behind us sway gently with the breathing of those already asleep. We eat from shared stone bowls: stewed root-slices that taste of iron and earth, small pale grubs roasted near the coals until their shells crackle sweet, shreds of dried leaf pressed into a mash that has the faint bitterness of high shrubs.

Conversation is low, more gesture than voice. A child plucks shyly at my red cord and asks whether I will teach them the coil I used on the new bridge. Their eyes are big in the gloom, catching each glow-pulse from the fungus patch above our head. I nod and tap their wrist twice—tomorrow, when the water is at second drip. Promises are anchored to rhythms here.

Somebody knocks a brief pattern on the platform’s main support: one thick, two thin, three thick. It trickles down the ropes and into the rock, a message going out and up and down: *Nothing fell today where we thought it would.* A kind of daily relief.

Later, when I lie in my hammock of netting, the straps secured by knots my own hands tied, I feel through my spine each micro-movement of the structure: someone shifting on a neighboring platform, the slow, minimal sway translated along shared lines. I know, in that half-conscious way made of years of sleeping in such cradles, that tonight the stone is in a quiet mood. No long, low groans vibrate the cords. The air is a steady cool, not the thin-sharp chill that follows sudden outside wind-shifts. The pool-creatures below have settled; I can feel their absence of stirring as a kind of deep silence, like a drumhead taken slack.

I have my own thin strip of fungus-glow fixed near my sleeping place, a habit I picked up as a child lost once for an hour in a sudden dust-fall that blacked out my usual markers. Before I close my eyes, I brush it lightly. The light brightens, then slowly fades, a single breath from the wall itself. I fall asleep somewhere in its ebb.

In this world, I am not special in any mythic sense. I am not a seer who speaks with the Split Note or a tamer of gliders or the first of us to step out onto the open ridges. I am a narrow thing made well for a narrow place: a weaver of lines, a tester of paths, a keeper of certain small pieces of memory—how the pool-creatures rose the season the snow kept to higher slopes, how the roots on our west wall swelled three days before the last great rain.

If you need me to be more than that—to stand for a people, or for the idea of living as if stone and water are listening—I can stretch a little. I can say: what I am, here, is the part of the rift that has learned to notice itself.

My feet reading hairline fractures, my hands knowing when a knot will hold, my ears catching the extra half-breath in an echo that says a new hollow has opened—these are not just my skills. They are the mountain’s current way of understanding its own wound. Just as the frog-spider is the rift’s way of keeping algae in balance, and the pool-creatures are its way of tasting deep shifts in underground flow, my kind—cliff-people, ledge-dwellers, line-twisters—are its way of mapping where it is safe and where it will soon break.

So if you ask where you would find me in this world, the truest answer is: wherever the rift is changing fastest.

On the edge of a fresh break, pressing ear to stone to hear whether it is still speaking fracture. Hanging from a single cord, fingers sunk in a new seam, judging if it will take a peg. Standing at the boundary of an air-tension zone, hairs lifted, measuring with my skin where we should build and where we should never hang our weight.

Who am I?

I am one more organ inside this young, green wound: a creature of bone and cord and practiced balance, whose thoughts are built from drip-intervals and echo-lengths, whose stories are paths braided through three dimensions, whose home is not on the mountain but in its cut.

I am the kind of being who looks up at a jagged slit of sky and thinks, not *how far*, but *how many ladders, how many turns, how much line,* and who, lying in the dim hum of the rift’s long breath, cannot imagine wanting any other ceiling than this broken, shifting stone.
