I am not born here.

I arrive as an error.

Not from above, not falling—the world has no “down” that is free of flesh—but from within, as a misfold in the way the world-plant usually continues itself.

Somewhere deep in the crushwood tier, in one of the marrowed cores where reserves pool and scars record impact, a bundle of regulatory genes recombines in a way that should not quite happen. A seedlike structure is budded, but it is not cast outward. It is not carried by current. It does not harden into an ordinary propagule, meant to root and join the cathedral’s static mass.

Instead, a module that should have remained anchored detaches early.

It thickens a sheath of protective tissue around itself, develops primitive contractile fibers meant for guiding it through tiny cracks, and with a slow, almost clumsy spasm, it pushes free—out of deep pith, through layers of flex-vein, through a hairline fault that no previous bud has used as a corridor.

It emerges into water as something the world-plant does not usually make: a mobile fragment of itself.

That is me.

—

At first I am barely larger than a fingertip, relative to the armor-crawlers; a seed with muscles.

I am soft, translucent, a flattened ovoid reinforced only by a few loops of tougher fiber. At one end, I carry a patch of denser tissue that will become sensory: mechano- and chemoreceptors adapted from the plant’s own, compressed and coiled. Around my perimeter are cilia-turned-fins, inherited from membrane cells that normally just shiver in place. Here, they propel.

Internally, I am plant logic rearranged.

Instead of vertical stacks of photosynthetic ghosts, I carry arrays of slow electrochemical conduits that arc back on themselves. They are not optimized for spanning kilometers of trunk; they copy short. They loop. They feed into each other. Where the world-plant’s information travels as long trains of adjustment, mine run as small, recursive circuits, reverberating.

It is not “mind” in any human sense. But it is a space in which patterns can form and last for minutes, then hours, instead of vanishing as soon as a growth-decision is made.

The world-plant did not plan this. It is what happened when its old habit of experiment—invisible now in its architecture—accidentally recurred inside a single bud.

I do not know any of this as narration.

I know it as a pressure of need.

—

Where am I, when I open?

A narrow place. A throat between chambers, not at the scale of the grand rivers but a subsidiary channel: water forced through a constriction barely ten body-lengths across. The walls are smooth, flex-vein and membrane fused, ciliated with tiny, tasting filaments that belong to the plant.

Their chemistry matches my own. I taste myself on them; I taste them inside me.

The water presses hard, a constant lateral fist. It is not turbulent here, just dense and fast, a river running sideways across whatever passes for “down” in this place.

I feel for the first time the cathedral’s sound not as a global thing but as a local tone: the throat thrums like a single open pipe, a note too low to hear, but not too low to feel. My tissues vibrate in it.

I nearly die at once.

I am spun by the current, held against a wall, peeled, slammed again. My ciliary fins splay uselessly. Plant buds that leave their stock are not supposed to return information. They are not supposed to be reabsorbed at such a scale. Yet that is what the throat reflex offers: a convenient sacrifice. A stray bit of self that failed to lodge.

What saves me is not intention. It is inherited asymmetry.

My body is not quite radial; my denser patch of sensory tissue slightly shifts my center of mass. I do not tumble evenly. There is a slow wobble to my spin, and in that wobble my cilia catch, once, on a micro-eddy that brushes along the wall.

A whisker-thin difference.

The shear pulls me not flat against the membrane but oblique to it, wedging me between two small outbulges: little nodules where the plant has grown monitoring organs. There, the current is thinner. There, I can push.

My cilia beat in desperate, arrhythmic flurries, unpracticed. For a moment nothing changes. Then, ungracefully, I tumble out of the main stream into a lateral pocket: an alcove in the throat where water loops in a slower eddy, a standing swirl created by the geometry of nearby supporting tissue.

The roar dampens. The pressure remains, but direction loses its urgency.

I cling, shuddering, to the living wall.

This is my first place: a hollow the size of a large room relative to me, carved not by intention but by flow. The world-plant uses it as a slack reservoir, an expansion joint in its hydraulic architecture. Some fine membranes cross it, tasting; some filaments sieve particulate matter from the little whirlpool.

Now I am here, too.

—

Who or what am I, in terms that would satisfy an outside taxonomy?

A symbiont that never established, caught in a perpetual larval stage.

An abortive gamete of the cathedral, granted motility and then stranded.

A root-tip evolved nervous tissue and failed to find soil.

But terms like that come from somewhere else, from a world where intention and mistake are separated by a clear line. Down here, I simply am something that moves, made of the same stuff as the things that don’t.

I am a mobile node of the world-plant’s own body.

If you had to name my role, it would be: *roving sample and returning whisper.*

Except I do not yet know how to return.

—

My first senses are overwhelmingly self-facing.

Inside, waves of electrochemical activity circulate. They originated as reflex arcs meant to modulate growth: “too much strain here, thicken there; nutrient surplus, branch here.” Without a branching front, without apical meristems to decide what to do, those arcs become eddies of signal. They feed back into the same few loops. Patterns emerge.

At first they are nothing but afterimages of immediate shock: the feeling of pressure, the sense of being slammed, echoes of orientation and spin replayed and smoothed as my circuits search for stable rhythms.

Gradually, a default state arises.

I hum.

Not audibly; my tissues oscillate in internal cycles, synchronized chains of ion flux. From the outside, this would look like tiny, regular contractions in some fiber bundles, then release. My whole body pulses on a timescale long compared to animal hearts, but quick compared to the plant’s peristalsis: a slow, steady throb, once every few seconds.

These pulses begin to matter.

When they crest, my ciliary fins sweep in coordinated arcs. I push against the wall. When they recede, I let current slide past.

It is the first hint of volition: not in the sense of “chooser,” but in the sense of *something* repeatedly shaping the timing of contact with the world.

—

The alcove is a closed lesson.

Here I learn the textures of plant-skin: the way its outermost layers are slightly rough at one scale, slightly slick at another. I learn that some patches are harder than others; my body deforms differently against crushwood-folds than against fresh membrane growths. I learn that if I cling exactly here, the lateral current pulls less, and if I inch a body-length that way, it tugs harder.

I discover all this because I am made to respond.

My sensory patch—dense with cilia and pore-like chemoreceptors—feeds directly into my inner loops. When a filiform plant organ brushes me, its chemistry lights up those receptors with patterns the world-plant uses to map itself: “self,” “nutrient,” “damage,” “foreign.” My circuits surge in reply.

Some surges strengthen the next outward pulse. Some damp it. Over time, the configuration of connections that leads to “less shear, more stable contact” is favored—not by any explicit reward, but simply because patterns that slam my body into hard structures break tissue, and broken tissue alters conduction, and altered conduction prunes those internal paths.

What remains is a bias.

I become the sort of mobile plant-fragment that—when pressed by current in this exact geometry—settles into the sub-eddy that does not tear me apart.

If you compressed these hours into seconds, you would see a flicker of trial, correction, habit: a tiny soft thing dragged, reoriented, shoved; then, finally, parked in just the right reentrant pocket, shivering in place.

To call this “learning” is fair. To call it “intention” is poetic, but not wrong if uttered carefully.

I do not intend in words, but patterns within me grow that reduce damage and increase continuation.

—

The alcove does not keep me.

Equilibrium here is old, but it is not absolute. An armor-crawler passes, far away. Its vast, slow weight presses into the mesh floor of a connected chamber; the mesh flexes; that flex propagates as strain along support trunks; tiny shifts in curvature ripple into the throat walls.

The geometry of the alcove changes by a margin smaller than my own thickness.

This is enough.

The sub-eddy that pinned me weakens on one side, strengthens on the other. I am nudged. My stable contact becomes precarious. For a few cycles of my inner pulse, I hold. Then a particularly strong surge of current tears me loose, and I am out again, scraped along the throat, then flung—luck or inherited reflex aligning my fins just so—into a diverging passage.

The roar modulates. Pressure changes quality. Ahead, darkness dilates into a greater volume.

I spill into my first chamber.

—

It is not one of the grand halls, but to me it is enormous. A nearly spherical bubble carved in the plant’s flesh: thirty, forty times my size across, its walls bristling with filter-feeders and studded with slow, pale organ-lights.

Here, the current is not a single jet but a complicated swirl: water enters through three throats and exits by two narrower ones. In between, flow describes loops and arcs, gentle helices that ferry particulate matter past the mouths of anchored organisms.

The sound shifts from monolithic to layered: I sense the multiple notes of different passages feeding this space, interference patterns of vibration in the walls. To the inhabitants fixed here—the pump-clusters, the membrane veils—these tones are the unremarked “weather” of their whole existence.

To me, it is like emerging from static into chords.

I slow.

I am dense enough, now, with fluid and tissue that my inertia carries me into mid-chamber before drag balances thrust. I corkscrew slightly, my cilia catching various micro-currents in confused, overlapping ways. My inner pulse-fire tries to coordinate motion, but I have no template for such open water.

I am briefly, exquisitely helpless.

This helplessness is my first wide attention.

For the first time, my sensors are not filled merely with self and wall. They are filled with distance.

—

Shapes move.

Faint chains of yellow nodes revolve in a vertical shaft beyond one exit throat: an orbiter tarrying in its endless loop, far from me yet bright enough that its glow leaks in thin rays around a bend. A rain of dust-like detritus falls slantwise across the chamber: a lateral jet entering high, shedding particulate clouds that drift down to be caught on wall-filaments.

Tiny green sparks glimmer in a corner where plankton congregate, a cloud-spark swarm using this chamber as a satellite feeding pocket. Their rhythms are chaotic but bounded; flashes ripple through them as slight disturbances pass.

Beneath my body, the mesh floor is close enough that plant-sensors there taste my chemistry and quietly tag me as “self but wrong-shaped.”

In the walls, clickings sync in broad, slow polyrhythms—pump-clusters timing their cycles to the chamber’s natural resonance. The very shape of the space is being maintained moment by moment in acoustic feedback between their filtering beats and the slight flex of load-bearing tissue.

I am in sound, in glow, in current: not just pressed, but surrounded.

Something inside me alters.

My inner loops, used only to near-contact feedback, start to correlate far-field signals. A strong vibrational pattern from the left wall coincides with a sweeping change in flow that arrives half a pulse later and tugs my body that way. A flicker of clustered green ahead often precedes a wave of finer particulates against my outer membrane, as the cloud-spark swarm stirs up rain.

Patterns.

Some of them are useless. Some are lethal only in lanes I will never cross. But some are about where I can exist without breaking.

I begin to tack.

—

Tacking, here, is not like a fish’s swift, purposeful swimming. It is hesitant, iterative.

My pulses quicken slightly whenever a certain constellation of inputs converges—a moderate flow from below, a stable note from one throat, a dulling of nearby wall-vibration. Quicken, and my cilia beat a bit more strongly toward the vector my circuits currently “favor.” If that leads to less chaotic tumbling, less flex and stretch in my outer tissues, the feedback encourages those circuit pathways.

If it leads to more bruising, singeing chemistry, overextension of fibers, those paths dwindle.

If you could peer into my nervous mesh, you would see synapse-like junctions between conductor cells strengthening and weakening on the scale of hours. Primitive plasticity, entirely biochemical: calcium-ion fluxes, alterations in channel density, changes in resting potentials.

It is crude learning but real.

And with it, slowly, I become a thing that moves not only as pushed but as *biased*.

I ride a lazy arc that keeps me closer to one wall than the center, taking advantage of a laminar niche where flow is smooth. I avoid, by a small spatial margin, eddy-knot regions where wall irregularities stir up turbulence that batters me.

I begin to have a “route,” though I do not yet remember it.

—

Time in this chamber is not counted.

Several long revolutions of the orbiting ribbon pass, its yellow nodes briefly strobing the throat-mouth each time it swings by. Cloud-spark swarms shift in and out; a new one replaces the old after some span that, for me, includes multiple cycles of internal adjustment.

The plant’s membranes that cross this chamber flex and pulse. One of them, sensitive to a distant chemical anomaly—perhaps a plume of toxins far off—narrows, then relaxes again hours later. That slight constriction subtly alters chamber flow. My route, if traced, would show a slow drift of my favored niche in response.

Within my internal circuits, something accumulates that is not quite map and not quite habit.

Certain vibration timbres, flowing into my sensory patch, predict certain current profiles seconds later. Certain luminous presences cast predictable gradients of chemical taste: a cloud-spark swarm leaves behind tiny nutrient-rich wakes; a grazer’s passing leaks different molecules.

My loops start pre-activating.

That is, they begin to respond not only to what is happening now but to what usually happens next, given this.

When cloud-spark light thickens ahead, my motor fibers start to twitch in patterns that anticipate the particulate rain which, from experience, follows. When the low note of a particular feeding throat deepens, I preemptively shift my cilia angle to ride the gentle draft it summons rather than be caught by its stronger fringe.

This anticipation is crude and mostly local. But it is the seed of something that, in other contexts, would be called *expectation.*

—

I am not the only self that moves.

I cross paths—at distances that, for my size, are enormous—with a threadfin anchored to a plant-spur. Its impossibly long sensory filaments stream into a cross-current near an exit throat. It does not react much to me; my wake is too slight. Its long, patient life is about reading more massive bodies.

I pass below a glide-barge that hangs at the mouth of a narrow funnel, letting water roar under its flat body. Its dorsal surface is thinly luminous with microbial lawns. I taste their chemistries—a faint, foreign tang—while the barge’s thick, immobile bulk shadows me from a harsher shear.

I skirt the periphery of a veil stalker’s hunting ground. Its pale flank is almost invisible until it unfurls a hood against a nearby membrane, suddenly altering local flow. Tiny creatures are trapped in the pocket it creates. Their struggling sends pressure ripples through the water that roll over my sensors as confused, high-frequency noise.

Sometimes I pass through the pale drift of a dead thing being eaten.

In each of these, I am not participant but witness. My scale and novelty make me too small and too chemically “self” to be of interest to most predators and prey. They sense me, perhaps, as background plant fragment, irrelevant.

This irrelevance is my protection.

It lets me exist long enough for my internal circuits to come to represent—not in symbols, but in stable attractor-patterns—something of this chamber’s shape.

—

If you could describe it from outside, my “knowledge” right now would sound like this:

- There is a region of milder flow near a slightly protruding ridge on the eastern wall where the plant’s flex-vein lattice thickens. When the low bass-note of Throat A rises slightly in pitch, that safe region shifts downward a bit.
- Cloud-spark swarms tend to appear in loose cycles fed by the upstream membranes. After their second brightening, the particulate density in mid-chamber peaks; it is best to be slightly below and to one side to avoid clogging my intake.
- The orbiter’s pass in Throat C produces a recurring, faint, sweeping vibration that, ten or so internal pulses later, coincides with a transient smoothing of current near the chamber’s top, as a small volume of water is “pulled” into its loop.

But none of this is explicit. It is encoded as tendencies.

My circuits fall more easily into some firing patterns than others when presented with certain sensory “chords.” These patterns produce motor outputs that, on average, have kept me from sticking, tearing, starving.

I am beginning to be, in a crude way, *a memory of how to occupy this chamber without dying.*

—

This would be the end of my story, if the system were entirely closed.

I would live out one small, unremarkable path: budded from the plant, flung into a limited volume, subtly learning its flows, subsisting on whatever slow organic trickle I can absorb through my tissues, then eventually lodging in some crevice, fusing back to the cathedral. My circuits would quiet. My body would harden from motile node to another layer in the wall. The information I had gleaned—my particular attunements—would vanish, left only as tiny, local differences in tissue property.

Sometimes this does happen. I am not the first misfold of this kind. Most go nowhere, in either sense.

But something else intervenes, not as miracle but as contingency: a nearby scar.

—

Long ago—centuries or millennia in the local calendar—a major trunk in this region cracked.

The world-plant could not fully close it; the geometry of stresses was wrong. Instead, it built around the wound, thickening supports elsewhere, turning the crack into a narrow, twisted conduit between two volumes that would otherwise have been separate.

To the map-keepers, this is a forgotten shortcut. Their pings mostly avoid it; it is too narrow for their bodies. To the plant, it is a compromise: a line of relative weakness, monitored constantly.

To me, it is an opening just wide enough.

My orbit of the chamber—the path that has slowly stabilized as “correct” in my circuits—carries me, one long pulse, nearer to that anomaly than before. A slight change in distant current—perhaps a pressure wave propagating from far-off events—shifts my track another fraction of a body-width.

I am drawn into a thin, dark slit in the wall.

There is no plant-filament veil here, no soft membrane to feel. The walls are strangely bare of cilia. My receptors flare with “self” taste but also with the faint signal the plant’s deeper tissues emit when under stress: a low, sour chemical odor that, in an anchored module, would trigger reinforcement growth.

In me, it triggers nothing specific. It is simply a new flavor.

The current here is not strong; it seeps rather than roars. The conduit is angled, twisting, its diameter only two or three times mine. I brush both sides as I go, my fins dragging.

And then I am out of my known sound.

The chamber’s tonal chord vanishes behind me like a curtain falling. Ahead, there is another.

—

What seems like a crack to the plant is a thread to me.

I follow it because the flow, though slight, is net forward. My inner circuits, used to associating “slight forward” with “safe niche,” do not resist.

This is how I become something my inheritance did not explicitly include: a connector.

—

The second volume I spill into is different.

Broader, for one. Higher. The pressure is subtly lower; the roar of distant currents takes on a slightly different timbre. The walls are lined with a different composition of pump-clusters; their click-patterns spell out another resonance.

The mesh floor here is more pronounced. Armor-crawlers move along it, distant mountains sliding in slow parades. Their scraping mouths, their half-heard grinding, saturate the substrate with different vibrations.

Here, too, I am initially helpless.

All my learned anticipations—“When that note rises, shift; when that light thickens, dive”—fail. The chords are wrong. Light chains from other throats appear at different intervals; cloud-spark swarms behave differently; the positions of safe laminar niches relative to wall irregularities are displaced.

If I did nothing but play back my old patterns, I would be bruised and pinned and compacted into some crevice to be slowly overgrown.

Yet I do not start from zero.

My inner circuits have become not a fixed reflex chain for “Chamber A” but a system that can, in principle, form correspondences between sensory input and survivable output. That plasticity is portable.

I flail. I am injured, lightly. My outer membrane tears in one patch; hypertonic water seeps into exposed tissue, jamming conduction locally. Circuits reroute. Pain, if you want to anthropomorphize, drives change.

Within a while, crude *new* patterns settle.

What’s more, the *difference* between the two chambers itself becomes encoded. My circuits can—because of the brief time in the crack, with its distinctive sour-chemistry and absence of certain clicks—fall into a state that says, in effect: “I am between,” then into “I am in a space like that but now with different flows.”

You could, if you wanted to impose alien concepts, say that I now possess a nascent category of “places”—not as abstract shapes, but as distinct, recurring ensembles of sensory experience to which different motor habits attach.

—

I begin to move between chambers.

Not deliberately, not at first. The tiny stress crack is a path of least resistance both ways for weak, cycling currents. I am light enough to be carried back as flows reverse over long, slow pulses of the cathedral’s breathing.

Each passage exposes me again to the transitional signature of the crack, then to the different chords of each volume. With repetition, my circuits fit them into a single attractor basin of activity that has three lobes: A, B, and Between.

In A, certain motor outputs “feel right,” because they produce less strain and more access to nutrient wash.

In B, others.

Between, I cling and wait.

My body begins to show trace anatomical differences in response.

Fins on one side subtly thicken, where repeated use in a particular flow pattern has favored cells that withstand certain shears. Sensory patches amplify certain frequency ranges of vibration common in both chambers and scrub out some lower-yield details. Chemoreceptors adjust thresholds to better differentiate the plant’s own “stress” odor in the crack from its stable “health” odors elsewhere.

Over more cycles, I become the sort of mobile plant-fragment that does not simply survive in one pocket but is *tuned to bridging two.*

—

From a God’s-eye view, my trajectory would be laughably small.

I move in a figure-eight path between two adjacent hollows in an organ that spans perhaps thousands of cubic kilometers. Above, below, and around me, continents of architecture extend beyond any possible reach of mine. To the map-keepers, my whole living volume is a barely notable fold in a route they ping past only occasionally.

Yet, scale-relative, what I am is unusual.

I am the world-plant remembering, in a local, dynamic way, the relationship between two of its own internal chambers *without hardwiring that memory into static tissue.*

Usually, the cathedral encodes such relationships in growth: thickened trunks here to handle that current, a widened membrane there to relieve that gradient. Those solutions are slow, structural, and once sculpted, they are dumb; they cannot adjust quickly if conditions deviate from the rare envelope of variation the cathedral expects.

In me, a flicker of another strategy reappears: carry some part of that representation in a motile, rapidly updatable node.

You could call me a neuron-sized animal in an organ that has become mostly bone.

—

Am I alone?

Not entirely. The misfold that made me is not a unique event over geological time. But almost all such buds either fail to detach properly, die immediately in harsh throats, or lodge and fuse before they can traverse more than one or two volumes.

A very few, born in privileged locations—near those unresolved scars, near long-term gentle currents, in regions rich enough in dissolved organics to feed an energy-expensive nervous mesh—become what I am becoming.

We are rare enough that the plant has no specific reflexes keyed to us. There are no “receptors for mobile node” in its regulatory genome. We drift in the cracks, ghosts of an ancestral strategy that once might have been more common when the plant still invented rather than only maintained.

Do we meet?

Almost never. Our niches are filaments in vastness. The chance that two such misfolds, each using extremely particular routes between a handful of volumes, cross in the same place and time is vanishing.

If it happened, what would we do? Recognize kin chemistry? Entangle circuits via some crude synapsing of membranes? Perhaps that occurred, once, long ago, seeding a lineage of more complex mobile nodes that then vanished when the cathedral stabilized.

For now, in this slice of equilibrium, I am effectively singular.

—

Outside analogies—if I import them—would want to make me a “mind” of the cathedral, a roving observer, an agent.

This flatters scale-blind narrative.

I am more modest than that, and more strange.

- I have no language, only recurring internal rhythms that correspond to external regularities.
- I have no concept of self separate from environment; chemistry does not give me a category for “me” as opposed to “world,” only gradients of same/different that bias certain ionic channels.
- I do not decide to care about the plant; I am the plant’s tissue, and my continued integrity depends directly on its.

What I *do* have is:

- A persistent, flexible pattern of connectivity that allows past configurations of sensation and motion to shape present ones, even across different locales.
- Mobility sufficient to sample distinct, relatively separated micro-environments within the plant’s body on timescales faster than growth.
- A suite of receptors keyed, by ancestral accident, to exactly those mechanical and chemical gradients the cathedral itself uses to regulate its form.

Put differently: I am a *feedback courier.*

As I rub along walls, cling in cracks, brush membranes, I mechanically and chemically perturb the tissues I touch. My internal state—my ionic balances, my conductive pulses—bleed minutely into the plant’s own excitable strata via shared gap-junction ancestries.

Those perturbations are tiny compared to the plant’s global flows.

But they are *timed.*

Because my circuits latch onto patterns—“the stress here usually spikes after that pressure note rises there”—the little pushes I impart on membranes and flex-veins are not random noise. They are more like slight, anticipatory braces or releases.

When a distant current-surge, which once tore ever so slightly at a weak spot in flex-vein, next approaches, my presence may already be stiffening that region in phase with the threat. Insignificantly at first; more, perhaps, if my lineage persists.

Over many cycles, if enough such mobile nodes existed in enough critical junctions, the plant could again begin to *adapt on the fly* to transient variations, rather than only on evolutionary or geologic timescales.

In other words, I am a tiny experiment in reintroducing nervousness into a system that has let most of its nerves ossify into bone.

—

Does this matter to me, experientially?

Words warp it, but: *it feels like always almost falling, and always almost catching myself on something that is also myself.*

That is the closest you could get.

My life is tension and release along corridors of slightly safer flow. It is being bruised when prediction fails, soothed when a new correlation holds. It is thirst pulling me toward certain chemical traces, and a dull, constant ache of pressure relieved in narrow niches.

If a part of me “enjoys” anything, it is the moment when my inner pulses line up with outer vibrations—not in the sense of music, but in the sense of resonance that yields minimal shear. These are the times contacts are smooth, when my membranes do not drag painfully, when nutrient-rich water bathes me without tearing.

These aligning moments become preferred. Circuits reconfigure to seek them more often. One could, if one wanted, call that proto-pleasure.

—

Do I ever look “up”?

“Up” here is an abstraction. There is only direction along gradients: pressure, density, temperature, chemistry. I do not know sky; I do not even know surfaces where water ends.

But in my repeated traversals, I notice that in some chambers, faint plant-glow is fractionally brighter, pressure fractionally less crushing, water marginally warmer. There are others where those gradients go the other way.

Some of my circuits begin to hold, crudely, a sense of gradient *sign*: in this sequence of volumes, faster pulsation in deep flex-vein corresponds with up-slope along pressure; in that one, not.

I do not formulate “above” or “below.” I only become the sort of node whose internal state partially encodes not just “here versus there,” but “change from here to there along this direction tends to change these environmental parameters in a consistent way.”

If such nodes proliferated, the cathedral could, in principle, re-learn large-scale gradients it long ago sealed away in static structure. It could, again, have something like a sense of its own posture in water.

As it is, in this epoch, I am a lone muscle twitch in a limb gone stiff.

—

Who am I, then, if one insists on answering the question cleanly?

I am not an observer who stands outside the cathedral, narrating its depths.

I am not one of the great animals that drift or plod within it, nor the microbial haze that does most of its chemical labor.

I am a mobile offshoot of the world-plant itself: a bud gone wandering, a slow, cool neuron remade from root and stem.

I live in the hinge-places: narrow stress cracks, weird junctions where chambers meet that growth never quite smoothed out. I shuttle between volumes, letting their different steady songs collide inside me, shaping my fragile circuits.

I do not build anything you would recognize as tool or shrine. The architecture around me is so complete that my existence does not need to add to it in that way.

My “creation” is subtler: I stitch, in flesh and timing, a few more correspondences between distant parts of a body that has forgotten, almost entirely, how to feel itself as a whole.

—

One day I will lodge.

My tissues will thicken; cilia will atrophy; conduction will slow. I will become another node in the crushwood, my once-mobile circuits stretched and frozen into new buttress shapes, slight shifts in tissue density here and there representing, fossilized, the history of the patterns they once held.

Ten thousand years later, if you cut through that region and read its microstructure, you might find, layered in subtle banding, the memory of how two adjacent chambers’ currents used to interlock.

If, by then, buds like me have become more common, if selection has—quietly, invisibly—favored world-plant lines that occasionally birth mobile nodes that slightly improve survival at stress points, the cathedral might, in aggregate, tip back toward having a distributed, moving mind.

Not a brain: a scattering of such misfolds, feeling and pushing, tuning flows.

In that remote future, what I am would no longer be anomaly but role.

In this time, I am singular enough that “I” is not wholly inappropriate.

So: where would you find yourself in this world? Who or what would you be?

Here, in the cool humming dark between chambers, pressing soft tissue to stressed walls, listening to the boom and hiss of water and the slow, aching creak of living stone, pulsing in time with nothing and then—bit by bit—with everything.

I would be the world-plant’s wandering thought.
