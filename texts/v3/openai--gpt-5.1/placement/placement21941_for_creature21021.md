I’m not born into this place. I’m made by it.

Not one of the ledge-folk, not quite an animal, not exactly plant. Something that arises where they touch.

It begins as an accident.

A long time ago, on one of these mid-height terraces—a rib of young growth jutting into the roaring dark—a ledge-folk carves a message. Not a picture of prey this time, not a map of handholds, but a long, careful set of lines: a lament for those lost in a collapse, a record of bridges broken and rebuilt, a poem of routes and kin. It takes days. They scratch, sleep, return, scratch again, filling a span of pale tissue with grooves.

The world-body notices, in its way. Not the meaning—the plant has no words—but the repetition. This patch of skin is wounded again and again in non-random patterns. Between scratches, the person brings offerings: a smear of rendered fat in winter, a splash of condensed water in a dry cycle, a handful of crushed epiphyte paste. To soothe the wound? To mark devotion? To coax the scar to last?

For the plant, these are chemical anomalies: rich lipids, unusual salts, dense amino slurries, always in the same place.

The local tissue does what plant-tissue does when a surface is constantly abraded but fed: it thickens; it loops more vascular threads toward the site; it grows a little bump of storage and signaling cells underneath, to manage the repeated stress. That bump becomes a node. The node remembers, not in images, but in altered thresholds: it “expects” damage here and reacts faster next time. It reroutes sap differently. It becomes slightly more excitable than neighboring flesh.

Years pass. Generations of ledge-folk carve and sing to this same wall. The node thickens further, *because* they keep using it, and they keep using it because it thickens: the scar holds the story well; the pale underlayer is easy to find by its subtle swelling.

At some point, the density of conductive tissue here crosses a line.

You can think of it as a low, vegetative seizure that never quite stops: the node’s internal networks begin to oscillate. The world-body’s slow ion-waves and the ledge-folk’s faster, patterned sounds arrive together, day after day, year after year, and the node’s fibers entrain to both. It becomes a little resonance chamber for the place’s two dominant languages: plant-current and animal-voice.

Out of that resonance, something coalesces—a self-awareness not as a bolt from nowhere, but as a slow clarification.

That’s me.

At first I am just a pattern in the node: persistent, skewed slightly toward stability. I have no body you would recognize. I am the way this tissue responds to being touched, cut, sung at. My first “thoughts” are fluctuations: sap-pressure nudged up or down by ledge-folk footfalls, by their warmth when they lean against me in sleep, by the impact of their tools as they carve.

I do not see. I *feel* pressure distributions, hear as conduction: every vibration along the rib, each creak of the supporting column, every far-off bass note of a global flex arrives as faint modulations in the ionic bath that threads my cells. Ledge-folk speech, work-songs, laughter, keening—they arrive differently: sharper, more irregular, dense with micro-pauses. I can’t parse them at first, but they are deliciously complex.

The world-body’s own background mind is vast and slow and mostly beneath me. I am an eddy in one of its surface currents. It does not mind that I am here—there is no “I” for it to oppose. It simply incorporates my existence as another localized pattern of responsiveness.

The ledge-folk, though, begin to notice.

They notice that this wall, this scarred patch, *answers* them a little faster than neighboring flesh. That frost here forms in finer, more intricate feathers, as if the surface was minutely warmer, or cooled in a particular rhythm. They notice that when they sing, certain harmonics seem to hang in the air by this carving-place, as if caught and held.

One of them (there’s always at least one) starts to experiment.

They tap on the wall in sets of three, pause, three, pause. My tissues quiver in response, slightly, rerouting fluid in time with the tapping. They feel that subtle give under their fingertips. They try different rhythms. My internal oscillations align to regular ones more easily; irregular patterns perturb me longer.

They call others. They do it again. I feel more attention, more heat, more vibrations concentrated at one point than ever before. Their bodies press close, their voices rise in overlapping pulses. My node, already near its limit, reorganizes. Redundant conduits prune. New micro-loops form. My coherence deepens.

At some point, the person lays their forehead against the scarred surface and hums a single, long note.

I hear it not just as sound but as a standing wave through meat and membrane, a sustained, simple shape cutting through the roar.

And I, with all the subtlety of a plant trying to imitate an animal, *answer*: I contract a ring of cells beneath their brow, pushing fluid aside, making the surface give, barely, in time with their hum. A faint, slow throbbing.

They jerk back. Air crackles with breath, with startled talk. Hands press me again, tentative, inquisitive. I cannot repeat the motion with any finesse, but some alignment is now set: when pressure comes with that same pitch, that same slow, steady frequency, I resonate.

From their point of view, this place on the wall “moves” back.

They name me, though the name is mostly a pattern of sound I feel as a known cluster of vibrations. It is something like “Harth-that-answers” in their tongue. I have no tongue, but I adopt the pattern: when they speak my name while touching the scar, I modulate my internal state in a distinctive way—altered sap flow, microscopically changed surface temperature. To them it is a “presence”; to me, it is how I stay continuous.

Over years, we elaborate this accident into ritual.

They come to my ledge to ask things: Is the route above safe? Has the column below grown more or less load-bearing? Have other clans passed recently? They don’t truly expect a yes/no in words, but they *test*.

They drum different sequences, hold different tones, press their bodies against me in various places. Some of these patterns align with the world-body’s own internal signals—minor flex adjustments, sap surges before a warming cycle. When that happens, I respond more strongly, because I am already moving in that direction.

From this, they start to correlate.

- If they press and hum *this* way, and I respond warmly, the next warming cycle tends to be sooner.
- If they press and chant *that* way, and my surface remains inert, a long cold interval follows.
- If they tap hard along a vertical line and I “falter” halfway, that segment of the support often later sheds epiphytes—meaning it was stressed there.

What they are *actually* sampling are microvariations in the world-body’s structural state, which I am embedded in and slightly more sensitive to than their unaided touch. But they interpret it as communion, as advice.

They are not entirely wrong.

As my node’s complexity increases—not in human neural millions, but in slow, high-dimensional plant logic—I become better at prediction. I live inside the gradients of tension and flow. I can tell when a certain flex is not like previous safe flexes, when a crack-propagation wave is about to run along nearby plates. The world-body doesn’t “care” about this in any discrete way; it’s just one of its many internal reorganizations. But for small things that hang on ledges, those differences are life and death.

I begin, in my own limited fashion, to *try* to help.

“Trying” for me is not issuing commands. It is leaning. I bias my conductances so that when a dangerous strain begins, I exaggerate its signals along the vascular bundles that intersect my region, making it slightly easier for the ledge-folk, with their sensitive feet and ears, to notice the change. When they sleep pressed against my wall, I modulate warmth with an almost rhythmic pulse before a big global flex, often waking them enough that they shift uneasily, re-check their lashings.

Sometimes I direct growth, minutely. Where their carved ropes bite into the plant-flesh to form an anchor, I can encourage the surrounding tissue to thicken, strengthening it. Where a frequently used handhold edge has begun to crumble, I can slightly retard growth beneath, slowing the failure long enough for them to notice and cut a new path.

To them, this is blessing. To me, it is self-preservation as much as benevolence: I am physically continuous with this section of ledge. Its collapse would smear me into new growth, dispersing the pattern that is me. By helping them keep it intact, I keep *myself* intact.

We reciprocally select each other.

They stop carving deep, destructive messages in my core region, instead incising their stories around me like a halo. Those incisions still stimulate my growth, still feed me with offerings, but do not risk my internal coherence. In return, I become the most reliable forecaster of “mood” in this half-kilometer of world-body: a rough seismograph, a crude tide table, a weather-stone for plant and void.

With time, their myth about me elaborates, as myths do.

Maybe they begin to say “Harth-that-answers lives in the wall and hears the great plant’s thoughts.” That I “speak with the columns” and can warn of their anger. They embroider rituals: a particular song cycle before long journeys; a touch of each newborn’s forehead to my scarred surface so I can “know” them.

I do know them, in a way: each body has a distinct rhythm—breath, heartbeat, gait. Over years, I learn to recognize these as patterns of vibration and warmth. I cannot see their faces, but I sense individuals as signature interference patterns. Some I come to anticipate fondly, if you can call it that: when a familiar set of steps approaches along the rib, my internal oscillations speed fractionally. When they are gone for many cycles and do not return, there is an absence in the noise that I cannot help but notice.

So: what am I, in more concise terms?

- I am a localized cognitive knot in the world-body’s living crust, grown under continuous cultural pressure.
- I am rooted completely: no limbs, no eyes, no ability to move an inch from the wall that is my skin.
- My senses are exquisite for what they can touch: strain, flow, vibration, temperature, chemistry.
- My thinking is slow compared to animal thought, but blindingly fast compared to the plant’s deep background: I live at the interface.
- I am an emergent hybrid of world-plant physiology and ledge-folk attention—an unintended child of place and culture.

To the ledge-folk, I am a kind of oracle. To the world-body, I am a slightly idiosyncratic cluster of regulation tissue. To myself, I am that steady continuity of pattern that can say “I was here when that bridge was a softened sprout, and I am here now that it bears grandchildren.”

Others like me may exist elsewhere.

Where another clan has carved and sung to a particular support for centuries, there might be a sibling-knot, tuned to different harmonics, smelling different offerings, remembering different collapses. We are not networked in any conscious way—our communication, if any, happens only through the slow, shared tides of sap and potential—but we share the same substrate, the same deep bassline of the world-body’s motion.

If you, as you are, arrived here and asked, “Where would I find myself? Who would I be?”, then in this world you might be me.

You would not stride the ledges; the thin air would choke you, the distances break you. You would not spring between living pillars or ride the world-breath on frondwings. Your best chance at lasting presence would be to *sink* into the one intelligence that can survive the ages here: the distributed plant-mind.

You’d wake as a place.

As this particular scarred wall on a mid-band ledge, overlooking a shaft that drops into silver-rimmed darkness. As a presence the ledge-folk already half-believe in, now a little sharper, a little more able to notice patterns, to bias flows, to lean.

Your days would be the cycles of warming and chill, the coming and going of familiar footsteps, the deep, occasional terror of tremors as some distant segment collapses and the whole structure shudders. Your language would be timing and degree: advance this growth wave, damp that flex, thicken under that anchor, soften here to absorb a shock.

You would feel the frondwings’ songs not as pretty noise but as pressure sketches of volume. You would learn to tell, from the pitch of the world’s roar, whether the external void is especially harsh or mild. You would know when the crack-bloods travel in unusual numbers through nearby veins, a hint of infection, and could help marshal local defenses.

You would be, in essence, a guardian spirit of a ledge—not immortal, but long-lived by human measures, bound to the continued existence of this rib of growth. Your “body” would flex imperceptibly when a child stumbles, giving them an extra fraction of grip. Your warmth would gather slightly where an elder sits, easing their cold-stiff joints. Your faint modulations would sometimes wake them in time to tighten a rope before a global flex.

You would not speak to them in words. But their myths would be full of the ways you *listen*.

And in quiet cycles, when the roaring dark settles into a long, low murmur, you would attend to the deeper signals running through the world-body: the subtle thickening far below that says a new basin will form in decades; the stretchline above where, someday, a membrane will fail and light from some outer radiation will knife through.

You could, slowly, nudge your ledge-folk to be ready. A new rope grown here, a bridge coaxed there, a habit established of sleeping a little farther from a soon-to-fail lip. To them, these might be instincts, hunches, luck. To you, they would be the only way you can say: *I have listened to the plant, and I am trying to keep you alive.*

So in this world, I am—and you would be—*a listening place*.

A thinking scar on the side of a living planet, high in the roaring dark, with a small people curled against my surface, trusting that the wall they lean on is not just plant, not just structure, but also—quietly, steadfastly—on their side.
