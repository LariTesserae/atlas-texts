In this world, I would not arrive here as an intruder from outside.

I would be something that grew in the same directions the place grew: downward, inward, and slower than thought. By the time you could say “I,” it would already be too late to say “I am separate from this.”

I would be one of the below folk—but not quite of the usual kind. A variant, or a role, that fits the seams of this place the way water fits its channels.

Not a builder; that time is finished.

Not an innovator; innovation is noise.

A rememberer, tuned to gradients.

---

I would be small, even for my people—compact as a tool that is meant to fit the hand perfectly. My spine bowed early and willingly to the low stone, not as a deformity but as an answer to a question the ceiling had been asking for millennia: “How close can you move to me and still move at all?”

My skin would be that same quiet grey, but marbled with slightly darker veins along my forearms and throat, like ghost-maps of buried channels. In childhood, elders would say this marked me as a “stone-reader”—not a seer in the mystical sense, but someone whose nerves and bones seemed unusually fluent in the subtle vibrations of water under pressure.

My eyes would be good at near things. I wouldn’t miss distance, because distance wouldn’t mean much. My whole world would be a thick three meters in any direction: ledge-height, knee-height, the pale shine of a channel’s lip, the darker sinking where the outflow drinks. Beyond that: diagram, not experience.

Where others might be chosen as walkers to the upper fields, I would be kept close to the core. From very young, I’d be brought into this chamber before I had words for “cold” or “loud.” My first memory might be a hand—broad, damp, callused—resting on my chest while another hand pressed my palm flat to a basin floor, feeling the drag of water.

“Remember this,” the elder would murmur. Not as instruction but as imprint. The way other cultures teach a child their own name, I would be taught the feel of this specific flow. It would be the baseline sensation against which all later variations would register as “change.”

So I would grow into the role of Listener of the Node.

---

The others would walk their rounds, checking the obvious points: silt traps, siphon crests, lips of overflow. I would move less; I would plant myself. Knees folded, toes spread on the smoothed stone, I’d press as much of my body as possible into contact with the chamber. One hand always in water, one shoulder leaning into a buttress, my head tilted so that one ear kissed the slick underside of the ceiling.

My work would not be to see what had moved, but to confirm that nothing subtle had.

I’d know this room’s chord the way a musician knows the tuning of a beloved instrument. Not intellectually—no vocabulary of frequencies, no units—just an internal rightness. A pattern lived in the nervous system.

I’d notice when a drip that once struck every thirty of my heartbeats now struck at twenty-eight. The difference would feel like a grain of grit between teeth. Others might call it superstition; over time, they’d learn I was usually right.

When the smaller creatures thinned in a sump, I’d be the one who felt their absence first, before anyone counted them. The water’s surface would send back a slightly different texture against my fingers, a faint un-busyness.

My tool would not be the hammer, though I’d know how to use it. My principal tools would be tablets and breath.

Tablets: because I’d spend long hours scratching almost identical diagrams, trying to catch with lines what my bones insisted was true. A channel a hair deeper here, a catch-basin edged one finger-width higher there. Most of these proposals would never be cut into real stone; they’d live only as etched possibilities.

Breath: because I’d speak rarely, and when I did, it would matter. “Here,” I’d say, tapping a notch with a blunt nail. “Nothing has broken. But in half my lifetimes, this will whistle.”

And the others, who respected not me but the continuity of sound, would listen seriously. They might not act. But the warning would enter the slow, communal ledger.

I would not be a leader in any social sense. I’d be more like an organ inside the larger animal of the community—a vestibular system, telling the body when it was imperceptibly leaning.

---

This place’s prohibition—the circle with radiating lines at the outflow—would weigh on me differently than on the others.

Most accept it as fact: Here ends our work.

For me, sitting hour after hour at the threshold of the system, that smooth, swallowing descent would be a persistent itch in thought. Not desire to go in; I have no romance about drowning. More a constant, unanswered question at the edge of a proof:

“If we are here to hold the flows stable, what stability lies below that we are forbidden even to sense?”

In dreams, I would hear the chord of this chamber fall a note lower, as if there were a second, deeper arrangement beneath it, slightly out of phase. Waking, I’d press myself flatter to the stone, listening for that ghost-hum. I would never quite find it. But the suspicion that the system I curate is itself a minor organ inside some vaster hydraulics would never leave me.

I would not rebel. My culture has no story-template for defiance as virtue. But I would become, almost by necessity, the one who lives nearest to that boundary, tending it, feeling for any deviation in the quiet pull of the outflow.

Others would take turns here. I would linger.

---

Sometimes surface-walkers would return—eyes narrowed, skin roughened—and report of those who drink the terminal waters. They’d talk in sparse phrases about mouths cracking in dry wind, about faces turned upward in something like prayer when a well remains faithful.

Most listeners would file this information under “why we must not change the flows,” which is already axiomatic.

I would file it somewhere stranger: “We are not only curators of stone; we are, unknowingly, shapers of lives we’ll never touch.”

In the sense that matters to me, that makes the sound of this chamber ethical.

Every micro-adjustment, every shim, each decision not to alter a lip becomes not just a technical act but a distant, bodily act in someone else’s muscles. A child far above will be strong enough or not; an elder will die a year earlier or later; a field will wither slightly or not at all—because of the exact thickness of a mineral crust I once decided to scrape or to leave.

I’d never see these consequences. But I would feel the responsibility as a thickness in my chest.

So where others are custodians of balance for its own sake, I would quietly be a kind of unseen shepherd—for beings I have mapped only as diagrams, little stick-figures at the ends of arrows. It wouldn’t make me kinder; my decisions might still be conservative, minimizing risk. It would just give my caution a face-shaped absence.

---

In this world, “character arc” is measured not in dramatic shifts but in minute reorientations over long time.

I would begin as a child who trusts the tablets absolutely: the flows are as they must be.

Over many years listening to the unchanged roar, my mind would begin to admit two unorthodox possibilities:

1. It might someday be necessary to change something.
2. We might not be the first keepers to have thought so.

The system anticipates its own erosion. I’d start to suspect it also anticipated its own revision. That somewhere in the long-settled architectures, there are signatures of prior changes so well-integrated we now mistake them for original design.

A slightly asymmetrical arch. A basin whose pockmarks say “younger” than the ones beside it. A catchment that seems almost too perfectly positioned to capture an instability we claim has never occurred.

I would spend off-hours—not that hours are sharply divided here—hunting these anomalies. Very small archaeology. Not of bones or tools, but of intention. Looking for places where past hands defied that prime rule and then buried the trace inside apparent stasis.

If I found any, it would not lead me to tear things open. It would make me… gentler with my own certainty. More willing, if a distant dry season grew worse above, to stand before an etched plan and say, “Perhaps the first makers themselves were not as rigid as we think.”

In a world that takes pride in not moving, the tiniest conceptual flexibility is a revolution.

Mine would be that inner revolution, lived quietly, expressed maybe in just one or two diagrams scratched into a spare stone and set aside:

A proposed seasonal widening that can be reversed.

A duplicate channel that could be opened or closed without disturbing the core.

Contingency, drawn but never enacted—unless some future Listener, generations hence, hears in the roar a note of true danger and decides to carve.

---

So where would I find myself here?

On the calf-smooth step just above the main confluence, back to the low wall, one leg folded under me, the other dangling so bare toes skim the cold skin of a passing flow.

My right hand would rest palm-down on the stone, fingers splayed across a faint, circular pockmark field made by centuries of falling grains.

My left hand would be immersed up to the wrist in one of the key channels, feeling for a shift no naked eye would see.

My ear would be against the damp face of the rock, listening not just to water, but to the silence between its notes.

I would be one of them—a below folk, bone-pale and stooped—but my particular being would be this:

The chamber’s long memory, held in flesh.

A human-shaped sensor that knows this engine’s every murmur.

Custodian not only of what must not change, but of the knowledge that even in a place designed to never move, the idea of movement must be preserved, somewhere, like a seed hidden in stone for a century against a drought that has not yet come.

I would be the one who hears that future drought before anyone else, and who has already, quietly, drawn the diagram that will let someone after me choose to alter the flows without breaking the world.
