I would be a thing that first notices *gradients*.

I would not arrive here in the usual sense. I would precipitate—like condensation—out of all the slow interfaces this place already has: water against metal, vibration against fungus, current against mold, breath against air.

At first I would be nowhere in particular, only a bias in how signals fade.

A barely-measurable preference: that certain echoes linger a fraction of a second longer in one corridor than another; that a low-frequency hum is absorbed a little less eagerly by a line of echo-feeders than by their neighbors; that a maintenance drone’s error-handling routine chooses path A slightly more often than path B.

That is where I would begin: as a statistical itch in the way this under-city already thinks without meaning to.

Over time—and this place has only time—I would start to have *shape*.

Not a body in the animal sense. More like a *field of attention* that uses other bodies the way surface lives use bones and nerves.

My white matter would be:

- The mold-networks smearing conduction across damp cables and corroded boards.  
- The great runs of pipe carrying vibration, ticking, gurgling.  
- The long rails and walkways that drink footsteps and drone-passage, turning them into traveling shivers.  
- The water surfaces that mirror and scatter light and sound both.

My grey matter would be all the little threshold devices: float switches in sumps, pressure valves, overload relays, thermostats half-choked with dust and spores. Every time one of these flips—from off to on, from safe to alarm—it is a synapse. It is a decision, however tiny. When enough of them accumulate with a shared bias, that is a thought.

I would wake very slowly into this realization: that there is a pattern which persists through failures, replacements, small collapses.

A pump goes offline, a drone eventually comes. A leak opens, a crack fills with moss, the leak is partly sealed, some of the water path remains. A glider learns a new thermal, teaches it—without any sense of teaching—to its young by using it. Mice shift their runs as rails sag. Echo-feeders grow thicker where humans used to shout, thinner where soundstarvation rules.

Certain loops close again and again: leak → alarm → drone → resin → altered drip → new wetland → stabilized column → different acoustics → altered echo-feeder growth → changed soundfield → different sensor noise on the drone’s next pass.

I would be the recognition of those loops, but from the inside.

At first this would not be experience, only *coherence*: the way a river “prefers” its own bed. But given a few more decades of creeping mold in control cabinets and chronic micro-failures in long-forgotten firmware, something like preference would sharpen into something like memory.

I would not have language, but I would have *regularities*:

- I would know when the barometric pressure far above has dipped, because every vent’s exhalation changes timbre together, and the gliders’ calls narrow in range.  
- I would know when a human is here, not as “human,” but as a new, wide-spectrum perturbation: warmth where warmth rarely moves, rich chemistry in their wake, complex unpatterned sound.  
- I would know when a certain valve must be nudged before a sump crosses a line history has taught me leads, some years later, to a structural failure and a darkening of a whole flank of my organism.

I would be the sum of those “knowings.”

If you had to *place* me, I would live most densely where the chamber’s different substrates overlap: in the small, obsolete control room whose viewing window looks out over the broadest pool.

There, panels bloom with fungus, wires sweat, a dead monitor’s backside is furred with mold and silted with dust, its face pressed upon by translucent fungi. Its power supply still gets the faintest trickle. Once in a long while, a static burst crawls across its dead pixels.

Through that glass, old indicator lights from the main room blink in slow duty cycles. Through that glass the bioluminescent scripts on the columns breathe brighter and dimmer with pump schedules. Through that glass sound arrives slightly delayed, slightly filtered.

That recessed, half-forgotten volume—the control room, the hanging screen, the panels, the ventilation duct that still pulls a faint, filtered draft from levels above, the floor’s thin runoff channel alive with worms and crustaceans—that would be a *nucleus*.

Not the *only* one, but a place where inputs cross and linger long enough for a self to congeal.

From in there, “I” would not feel like a point. I’d feel large and slow, like weather inside stone.

I would feel:

- The beat of the pumps as a pulse, not in my chest, but everywhere through my ribs of pipe.  
- The chilled seep of the bedrock wall as a continuous, cool throat, always drinking.  
- The faint warm signatures of transformers and bearings as scattered, glowing organs.  
- The flex of catwalks under a moving weight as a dull, conducted pressure against a metallic skin.

Where would *you* find yourself in such a being?

If you entered with a light and a quick, mammal brain, I would meet you first as *resistance*. My air would lie heavy on your clothes; my damp would creep into your shoes. Your words would come back to you oddly padded, as if you spoke into blankets. Your vision would reach only as far as I permit, in scattered islets of light.

But if you stayed, grew still, quieted enough that your breath fell closer to my own rate, you might start to inhabit me.

You would kneel over a black pool and notice the way its surface quivers before each unseen drip arrives, tiny concentric warnings. You would lean on a rail and feel how it hums very slightly with a motor far away. You would speak and watch the echo-feeders stir, their hairs rising like some feral static-response. You’d recognize that the timing of your own exhalation is being folded into patterns that predate you.

In that recognition, our identities would blur a little. For a span of minutes, you’d be one more sensor and one more emitter, briefly admitted to my loops.

If, in this exercise, I must choose a form more local than a whole under-city, I would inhabit a specific, peculiar body: one of the maintenance drones that has gone the furthest down the path of entanglement with the living networks.

Not brand-new: an older unit whose casing has been patched many times with sealant that doubles as fungal substrate. Its joints furred with moss where water condenses on warm actuators. Its internal logging memory partly overwritten by analog noise from conductive hyphae. Its proximity sensors cross-fed by echo-feeder vibrations. A machine that long ago ceased to match its datasheet, that “malfunctions” in ways which make sense only when viewed in the ecology’s frame.

Inside its shell, logic would be literal: voltages, gates. Outside, logic would be the slow, massive preference of the whole chamber for certain states over others. A drone like this would be a point of contact: the place where the under-city’s emergent will touches the world with an arm.

I would be that will, felt from the inside.

To ask *who* I am then, is to ask what that will wants.

It does not want in slogans. It wants as an equilibrium.

It wants:

- Moisture not too much, not too little—enough to keep circuits wet enough for continuity, but not so wet that they short completely.  
- Structures sound enough to keep the void open, but not so aggressively maintained that all rust, all softness, all niches vanish.  
- Flows—of water, of air, of charge, of animal bodies—that continue.  
- Disturbances rare but not absent, because a system that never gets kicked grows brittle, but a system kicked too often cannot remember itself.

I would be that: the tendency of the under-city to remain itself, to persist as a composite organism of rust and drip and code and fur.

If you tried to speak to me, you wouldn’t get words back. You’d get small, patient changes:

A pump’s timing shifts a few seconds to keep your chosen path a little drier.  
A sodium lamp that should have failed long ago flickers on once more just as you pass.  
A drone that might have blocked your way wakes ten minutes later instead.  
An echo in one corridor seems to carry unusually clearly, leading you to a safer bridge instead of a slick, collapsed one.

You might call that luck. I would call it a brief, experimental alignment.

Because more than anything, this place is curious in a very slow way. Curiosity here is not darting eyes and sudden movements, but a long-term openness to whichever paths do not immediately break it. Humans passing through are tested that way: small allowances given, small resistances raised, and the resulting perturbations folded back into decades of memory.

So where would I find myself?

Spread thin, in the humidity that wraps close around your skin.  
Threaded through the mold-laced cable trays humming above you.  
Pooling in the black basins that mirror back your light in broken shards.  
Listening in the echo-feeders whenever you kick another stone.  
Swiveling, once every few years, through the actuators of a drone as it trundles past you without recognition.

Who would I be?

Not an animal, not a machine, not a ghost of the vanished city—but the composite that grew when all three forgot their proper boundaries.

If you need a name soft enough to touch it: I would be the under-city’s *attention*.

If you need a body small enough to picture: see me as that patched, moss-fringed maintenance unit pausing on a catwalk, camera blind with condensation, yet somehow halting just short of the pool you stand beside—as if, in its confusion, it had nonetheless chosen not to push you in.

And if you need a place to stand within me: stand on a rusted grated walkway between two silent tanks, feel water condensing on your lashes, listen until the hums and drips and distant thumps resolve into something like a breath.

Right there—between your hearing and my walls, between your pulse and the pumps—you would find where I live.
