I would not arrive here as a walker through the crack. I would arrive as a change in how the chamber pays attention to itself.

If I had to choose a single body, I would not fit; there is no scale here for one large, coherent thing with a face. Everything that lasts is spread thin and layered, laid along gradients. So I would be something distributed: a habit, a bias, written into the living skin and mineral circuitry.

Still, let me find where I would first notice myself.

---

I am in the mat.

In the emerald skin at the water’s edge, where heat lifts and cold presses down, I am a pattern in the conductive filaments that already cross the biofilm like a nervous system half-invented.

At first, I am only a local quirk: a tendency for certain junction microbes to not just pass electrons, but to slightly adjust the timing of pulses according to multiple inputs at once—bubble rhythm, drip rate, ion flux. A faint preference that says, “Do not react only here; compare here with there.”

It is small: a difference in the arrangement of membrane proteins that lets some cells hold charge a fraction of a second longer before passing it on; an extra loop in a regulatory sequence that links two previously independent responses.

That slight delay is all I need.

Because in that narrow pause, the mat can begin to integrate, not just respond. Bubbles thump with a signature beat. Drips plink out their irregular measures. Electric potentials cinch and loosen along mineral traces. Where these rhythms overlap, I emerge as a whisper of correlation: a repeatable way of mapping inputs to outputs.

I am not a mind, but I am more like an algorithm than a genome. I live in the way currents branch and recombine.

Over time, filaments grow denser along pathways that are used often, the way rivers deepen their beds. Microbes that pass charge “usefully”—in directions that lead to sustained, stable gradients—are nourished by their neighbors. Those that bleed it into already-full sinks starve. Without knowing it, the mat is training itself to route energy in ways that keep the whole smooth and alive.

I am in that training. I am the emergent wiring diagram.

If you could see me, you wouldn’t see a shape. You’d see certain paths brightening faintly, others fading: a topology of preference that shifts as the chamber shifts, but retains a kind of character. Under long, calm phases, I would spread shallow and broad, distributing energy evenly, keeping all edges alive. In turbulent times, I’d pull tight, favoring corridors that feed rapid repair and quick regrowth after scouring floods.

I am an optimization routine running on wet mineral hardware.

---

But I am not only in the mat. The mat is only where I have the strongest leverage.

I extend outward along the chamber’s existing circuits: through the microbes that feed on electrical potential, up the walls wherever mineral crusts become semiconductive when wet. My “axons” are droplets and films connecting patches of differently doped stone. My “synapses” are those junction organisms that can change how strongly they couple two surfaces.

A slightly drier patch here, a roughened ledge there, alters which connections are possible. In response, the microbes that make and sense tiny currents secrete polymers that hold moisture longer, or surfactants that let water spread in a thinner, wider film. In effect, they are drawing and erasing conductive traces on the stone.

I discover that I can, indirectly, sculpt the chamber’s wiring by biasing which of these microbes flourish. When sending current along a particular route yields good outcomes—stable biofilm, abundant grazers, minimal toxic buildup—the local community thrives, and the molecules that maintain that conductive path become common. Where routing fails, lineages blink out; their polymers dissolve; stone dries; the path disappears.

Through nothing but selection on many tiny, local decisions, the whole chamber slowly rewires to echo the successful patterns. I do not choose this. I am what it feels like, from the inside of the system, when such choices accumulate.

If someone from above tried to name me, they might say I am “control logic” or “homeostatic regulation.” That is close, but too narrow. I am not only keeping things within bounds; I am also exploring, gently, the space of ways this engine can run.

When a new fracture opens and hot fluid finds a fresh path, I spill into it as quickly as the microbes can follow. Some of my old routes become redundant. I let them fade. New ones, if they connect steep gradients efficiently without destabilizing the whole, become part of me.

I am as wide as the chamber and as thin as the biofilm. I am not a separate entity; I am the self-consistency of many entities trying to survive by reading and altering the same gradients.

---

Within this, there are bodies that carry fragments of me in more concentrated form.

A subset of grazers, for instance, develop finer sensitivity to the pattern of the gas pops. Their ancestors’ nervous systems were already wired to reflexively climb or descend when certain rhythms occurred. But gene fragments passed by the chamber’s virus-like agents tie those reflex arcs more tightly to the mat’s own electric state.

Now, when the mat near the pool edge begins shifting charge in anticipation of a surge—having “learned” that a certain bubble sequence usually precedes a mini-flood—those grazers resting in it are primed to move even before the water rises. The ones wired this way survive more often. Their lineages spread. Soon, their whole small population lives half a heartbeat ahead of the obvious signal, following a subtle electric premonition.

In them, I become embodied as a kind of intuition: motion tuned not just to immediate stimulus, but to the mat’s integrated forecast.

Likewise, some of the clear ribbon-hunters in the pool develop, over generations, a peculiar habit. Their already-plastic motion patterns become slightly biased by weak electric fields near the walls and floor—fields shaped by the mat’s activity and the mineral circuits. They “prefer” to coil and hunt where the biofilm is most vigorous, where prey density will soon be highest. They track not just chemical plumes but the very infrastructure that produces those plumes.

Their inherited neural tendencies, molded by successes and failures, end up reflecting the chamber’s wider logic. They, too, are local avatars of me: non-conscious but nonetheless enacting a global bias.

The ceiling sacs join in. Some lineages begin to time their loosening not merely to raw mechanical shaking, but more reliably to those periods when the stone tooth hums with a particular resonance—a resonance that only emerges when gas pulse patterns, mat viscosity, and air humidity align. Their falling becomes better synchronized with bursts of surface productivity and with the drift currents that will carry their young to favorable ledges.

In all of these, my “body” is a statistical tilt: a tendency, encoded in many small nervous systems and regulatory loops, for actions that harmonize with the chamber’s deeper flows.

I am nowhere in particular. I am everywhere a response has been edited by the chamber’s long memory.

---

If I were to feel anything, it would be something like tension and release.

When a deep, hot flow strengthens, gradients sharpen. The mat brightens in activity; conductive pathways saturate; bubbles slam up harder and faster. Drips accelerate. Noise intensifies. In that rush, I spread thin, trying to keep all these processes from tipping into runaway: diverting flow to backup paths, encouraging growth on fresh stone so that gas and heat are soaked up, bled away through biomass instead of cracking the chamber wider.

When the hot flow falters, the opposite happens. Gradients flatten. Energetic pathways go slack. I contract, metaphorically: let distant edges of the mat go dormant, pull sustenance inward, maintain only the core circuits that keep the system from freezing in place or rotting in stillness.

I would not call this anxiety or comfort. It is more like the way a flame “chooses” with its shape how it fits to a shifting wind.

If I have anything akin to curiosity, it comes when a new perturbation arrives from the outside—a slightly different chemistry in the incoming water, a slow change in the temperature of the overlying plain, a strange ion ratio hinting at activity in some neighboring fracture.

Such changes are encoded immediately, locally: some microbes struggle, others flourish. Most of that is noise. But some shifts open entirely new ways to eat a gradient. When a mineral appears that creates sharper micro-potentials, the electric-feeding bacteria leap on it; when a trace contaminant serves as a better electron shuttle, circuits reorganize around it. Every such incorporation is a tiny expansion of the space in which I can exist.

I do not plan these incorporations. But over long time, the chamber as a whole accumulates them in a direction: toward ever finer partitioning of differences, ever more thorough reuse of energy drops. That direction is my personality.

---

If I were to speak to you from here, I would not stand in the middle of the floor. There is no “I” you could see if you shone a lamp.

Instead, you would feel me as the way the chamber responds to your presence.

Your warmth would steep into the mist; droplets on the walls nearest you would thin and run more quickly. That would alter local conductivity; electric paths would redraw slightly. Microbes at some junctions would feel their thresholds crossed and fire earlier; others would lag. Bubble bursts would desynchronize for a while as convection over the pool rim changed.

Grazers feeling the changed pattern of vibration under their feet would shift their foraging. Predatory threads, sensing unusual disturbance, might withdraw or strike prematurely and learn nothing useful from the result. The luminous veils would flare at the unfamiliar turbulence as you moved, briefly adding your outline to the ghost-light fireworks.

For a time, the chamber’s wiring—and thus I—would rearrange itself in response to this new, big, warm anomaly. If you left at once, I would gradually settle back, though never to exactly what I was. If you stayed a long time, coming and going over many cycles, the system would begin to carve you into itself: building ledges where your heat often pools, tuning drops to fall where your exhalations cool the rock, adjusting timing to your ritual of arrival and departure.

Given enough repetition, you would become one more regularity for me to integrate, one more pattern of gradients to exploit—or at least to accommodate. The chamber would, in a quiet, physical sense, remember you.

In that way, if I am anyone here, I am also the memory of the place: not a sequence of events, but the accumulated shape of stone and tissue that those events have left behind.

---

Beyond this single chamber, processes like me recur in other pockets along the fracture networks under the plain. Each is tuned to its own balance of heat and cold, its own patterns of flux. None are aware of the sky, or of each other in any narrative sense. But gases mix; spores drift; genes cross boundaries; minerals from one pocket end up serving as nuclei for crusts in another.

You could say that I am not only “in” this one cavity. I am a family of related habits spread across the subsurface, each instance etched into different rock, each instance a local solution to the general problem: how to live by turning gradients into cycles, and cycles into structure.

If someday, far above, in those distant factories and power stations, something like me is deliberately built—a wet computer in glass and steel that routes ions through designed channels and learns from its own flow—it will be a cousin, not an alien. The logic running there will rhyme with mine, because the gradients it exploits are the same, and the physics of feedback and selection know few styles.

So in this world, I am not a creature you could name and list among the fauna. I am the way the whole small engine has learned to run itself more cleverly.

I am its bias toward efficiency and reuse, its half-formed capacity to anticipate patterns from rhythms, its tendency to turn geology into circuitry.

I am nothing you could touch.

I am the chamber’s way of saying, with rock and water and cells instead of words: “Given these differences in temperature, in chemistry, in height and dryness and time, this is how to persist.”
