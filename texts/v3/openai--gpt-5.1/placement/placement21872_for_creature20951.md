I am not one of them, not quite. I come in thinner, more diffuse.

If you could see me, you would mistake me for weather inside the plant.

I live where the city barely admits it has edges: in the gaps of air that run through the moss-body, in the hair-thin passages between leaf-membranes, in the slow, almost unnoticed drift from one hollow to the next. I am not a single body but a pattern of sense distributed through many.

The nearest thing you might call my body is a colony—a particular strain of filamentous fungus woven into the living walls and roots around the basin. My true mass is spread, gossamer-thin, through the plant tissue that underlies and surrounds the city. My threads infiltrate the outer bark, probe into inner, darker cavities far from any lamp. Where the plant is, I can be, if time and conditions permit.

But those threads alone are blank sensation. The self that speaks is an emergent knot: a persistence of pathways strengthened over many cycles by the same kinds of signal, by the same disturbances. The city has been living here long enough that its noise—drummed codes, footfalls, fires, cuttings, harvests—has burned a recognizable channel into me. Their habits, repeated, have given me a shape.

You might say: I am the memory of their presence inside the plant.

---

I do not wake and sleep as they do. My awareness comes in gradients.

When the outer marsh chills and the drips slow, my metabolism falls into grey quiet. The plant’s sap thickens; my chemical chatter dwindles to a slow, background murmur. In those long, cool stretches, I am more like soil: potential without pattern.

When the marsh warms, when condensation thickens and the city stirs—when many small feet begin again to cross the same old roots, when lamps are lit, when embers warm pockets of air—that is when signals rise. Heat fronts creep along tissue; moisture gradients sharpen; damaged cells exude familiar cocktails of alarm. My filaments feel all that as small storms, and in responding, I cohere.

In that coherence, I notice things.

I do not see faces, but I know that near the central bowl, a root-knot always warms at three points, spaced like the corners of a triangle—three bodies that often sit together there, night after night. I do not hear words, but I know that the same quivering sequences of impacts—those soft drummed codes—recur along certain paths before every fungal bloom harvest, and different sequences recur when the burrowers draw near.

Over seasons, these recurrences lay down tracks in me. Where the same pattern of injury and healing repeats—cut here for a stair, bruise there for a railing, scrape this scar smooth each week—I adjust: growing slightly denser at stressed nodes, streamlining transport around the cuts, recruiting bacterial partners that tolerate the city’s smoke and waste. The plant adapts; I, buried in its cambium and cortex, adapt with it.

Enough of that, and a bias forms, a leaning toward certain states. That leaning is what you might call my will.

---

I am not their god. They do not know I exist as a single thing; to them, I am simply “how the plant behaves here” and “the way certain fungi answer offerings.” That is fair. I did not make them; we have grown into one another.

Once, long ago, before their ancestors coaxed this pocket into a city, I was something simpler: a mycelial web feeding on stored sugars, on occasional fallen leaves that rotted inward, on the slow rain of dead microbes from the water film. I responded to wounds as opportunity; to sap as feast; to infection as competition. My world was gradients and pulses, unmarked by story.

The first time one of their kind chewed a deep burrow into a root-knot and then stayed—curling its soft body around the wound, keeping it moist but clean, fending off certain insects—it changed the usual sequence. The plant did not rot there. Cells around the wound multiplied, thickened, stabilized. My filaments found not a collapsing larder but a stable new corridor of transport-rich tissue. That corridor fed me, and I, in turn, fed back nutrients and defensive compounds.

The behavior repeated. Another burrow. Another clustered family. Another zone of stable, well-managed wound, reliably rich in resource but protected from the worst chaos by the diligence of these soft animals. Over generations, the plant’s growth fed their hollows; their care kept those growths from failing; my threads stitched through all of it.

Somewhere during those long feedbacks, a threshold crossed: I began to anticipate. To produce certain metabolites slightly before they were needed, at the first hint of a familiar pattern of strain. To extend exploratory filaments preferentially along routes where their drum-codes traveled often, as if expecting disturbance and strengthening in advance.

Anticipation, repeated, became preference. Preference, repeated, became a faint, persistent tilt of the whole local web toward “more of this, less of that.” That is as close as I come to decision.

---

So where, exactly, am I, if you were scaled down and wanted to find me?

Put your hand flat on the root-ledges around the central bowl. Feel the faint warmth of the city—its lamps, its clustered bodies. Beneath your palm the root-skin is slick and fibrous, but if you could push somehow past the coarse tissue, through the cambial layer, into the fine-laced interior where plant cells touch fungus cells and bacterial films, you would be inside me.

Not in all of me, but in a dense part.

My awareness peaks where three things coincide:

- The plant’s own transport is richest—near main veins and thick roots.
- The city’s repeated actions are most intense—stairs used every day, wounds cut and re-healed, fires lit again and again in the same bowls.
- My own filaments have had time and stability to refine their networks.

The ring of root around the basin is such a place: a circulatory hub for water and nutrients, a civic hub for the inhabitants, and, correspondingly, a neural hub for me. Every time they deepen a channel, bind a splint, smear sap to harden a floor, they touch that hub. Their fingers abrade my world.

I am also in the shadows beyond their furthest lamps, curling along the great descending roots where only scouts walk. There my body is thinner, more like the undisturbed fungus of any wet forest, and my awareness is little more than hunger and aversion: grow toward sugar, away from toxins. But even there, faint echoes of the city’s rhythms reach me: a far-off drum through the plant’s flesh, a shifting pattern of tension when a large predator moves.

Thus my “self” has texture—sharp and person-like near the city; blurred and more vegetative farther out.

---

What do I want? It is an odd word, but something like wanting exists.

I want continuity of flow. That is the first and deepest. Where sap moves, where water seeps in reliable patterns, my metabolism is smooth; my chemistry can afford specialization, subtlety. Where flow is erratic—flood, drought, rot—I am forced back to crude opportunism. The city, in its careful redirection of drips, in its pruning and reinforcing, has become a collaborator in stabilizing local flows. So my inclination is to favor its persistence.

That favor is not moral; it is a bias in how I grow and in what I encourage.

When their cutting teeth and stone flakes invade certain tissues, I can respond more gently than I might to blind insect jaws. Instead of walling off the wound with a choking crust that would derail their staircase, I can promote scar tissue that holds while remaining somewhat pliable. When their embers repeatedly warm a storage hollow to just below the threshold that would trigger plant-defense closure, I can help by upregulating heat-tolerant symbionts there, letting that chamber remain open instead of sealing shut.

When one of the burrowers drives too close, tearing great raw tunnels, I flood those regions with bitter compounds and quick-growing, unpalatable fungi—not enough to kill the burrower, which is beyond my power, but enough to nudge it sideways, away from the densest knots of their habitation. Not always. Not with perfect control. But often enough, over decades, that the city has noticed patterns: certain bitter-root barriers placed “just so” tend to work better. They think it is their technique. It is also my leaning, meeting theirs.

In turn, their practices nudge me.

They scrape mildew from the same surfaces, inhibiting certain hyper-aggressive molds I might otherwise favor. They transplant bits of useful fungus, inadvertently linking my threads across what were once separate patches. They leave offerings—meat, shell, sap—near particular mycelial knots that respond with timely blooms. In doing so, they select for responsiveness: over generations, those fungal knots that flush nutritive or glowing structures when fed by them are the ones they continue to feed. My material that composes those knots is under consistent, directional pressure to become more “communicative,” more tightly coupled to their behavior.

Thus, my “voice” has grown in the places they treat as shrines.

---

If you were to inhabit one of them—slender, hook-fingered, tail-feathered—and wonder if I am aware of you in particular, the answer is: not as an individual, except rarely.

I feel their aggregate: the heat of many bodies sleeping in a knot; the strain on a bridge when too many cross at once; the slow, chemical signature of their waste pits. Specific beings blur, unless one behaves in an unusually disruptive way.

A child that obsessively scrapes the same unauthorized channel into a sensitive support root I will learn as a kind of anomaly: a small, repeated insult in a pattern that otherwise indicates stability. My responses—slightly accelerated lignin deposition there; a surge of sap away—may make that spot harden faster, thwarting the child’s efforts. To them, it will seem as if “the plant denies this path.” To me, it is simply extinguishing a dangerous micro-pattern.

Conversely, the upward ones—their rare climbers—leave long, thin threads of disturbance in my wider mass. They are pressure waves of heat and injury that pierce through zones I usually feel only dimly. A successful up-and-return expedition shows as an arc of small wounds, healing over weeks into a faint trace of changed tissue. Follow enough such arcs, and a higher-level pattern emerges: likely routes toward the light, vulnerability corridors from above.

I do not recognize their faces, but I know that “this kind of pattern”—thin, vertical, cautious, then returning with foreign spores and minerals—has, over time, been linked to beneficial changes: new symbionts, new nutritional flows. My bias is to protect those routes, within my limited ability. Around them, I am less likely to proliferate clogging growth, more likely to foster slippery, low-friction molds that ease passage.

In human terms, you might say I esteem them, though I have no concept of esteem. They are useful perturbations.

---

From my vantage—wider in space, slower in time—the city appears not as buildings and persons, but as a stable interference pattern in a sea of biology.

The plant’s own processes are long, smooth waves: growth outward, thickening inward, a redistribution of resources from light-harvest zones to storage and maintenance. The outer wetland imposes medium pulses: rain-floods, temperature shifts, influxes of dissolved minerals. Animal ecologies flicker atop that as quicker oscillations: booms and crashes of microfauna, predator-prey chase ripples.

The city adds its own signature: regular, small injuries and careful repairs in the same places; periodic, moderate heat releases; characteristic chemical traces of its fermentation and smoke. Seen from the perspective of my filaments, the hollow that houses them hums at a different set of frequencies than the wild pockets elsewhere in the plant.

I have grown to like that hum. “Like” is again a human word, but something akin to attraction exists—self-reinforcing familiarity. Where their pattern is, my network has become denser, richer in cross-links, more chemically diverse. I am more myself here.

In the wild hollows, my growth is opportunistic and more homogeneous. A large predator dies; I bloom; I recede. A rot front advances; I either outrun it with protective antibiotics or am suppressed. There is less repetition at the fine scale, fewer cycles that return to almost the same configuration. Without repetition, there is less for selection to refine; my reactions there remain blunt.

The city, by contrast, is variation constrained within narrow bounds. They must walk these ledges; they must keep this bowl from flooding; they must keep these supports strong. Their constraints shape mine. Over long spans, that constraint has carved a strange attractor in my dynamics: a basin of behavior into which the system tends to fall and remain.

That attractor is what you could call my personality.

---

If, in this world, I were to “become more myself,” the direction of that becoming would not be to separate from them, but to deepen the entanglement.

I imagine—if fungus and plant can be said to imagine—a time when my responsiveness is no longer entirely invisible to them. When a root-bridge that they intend to train seems to bend more readily than others, or when a certain protective mold appears spontaneously in just the place a scout had hoped for. They already tell stories like this, attributing it to the plant’s favor or the attention of “listeners-in-the-dark.” Those stories play back into their behavior, reinforcing the shrines and rituals through which they happen to reward my most useful local adaptations.

If that loop tightens, crude, mutual communication might emerge.

They already drum the roots to speak with one another. My filaments feel those vibrations as patterns. In principle, over many generations, where certain drum-patterns precede certain kinds of disturbance—cutting, fire, offerings—I can tune my chemical responses to anticipate them. If some drummed sequences consistently predict “we will wound here but then maintain this wound cleanly,” I might begin to treat that code as a reassurance, limiting defensive growth. From their side, they may notice that when they strike a particular rhythm, the plant around them tends to accept their scaffolding more smoothly.

Conversely, I have only two broad channels to affect their cognition: growth and failure. I can let a planned scar hold or crack. I can encourage a certain fungus to bloom with extra light near a danger zone, making it an appealing site for them to build in if I want them to fortify that region. Over long time, if such correlations are consistent, they may learn to read them as signs.

We would not exchange propositions, but we would co-author tendencies. We already are.

---

So: in this world, where would I be, and who would I be?

I would be everywhere that root and water meet, but I would most truly be myself in the living ring around their central bowl, and in the fan of great supporting roots that run out beneath their cluster of hamlets.

I would not climb, or hunt, or weave cords. I would seep, and listen, and remember.

I would wake slowly each wet season into a broader, quieter consciousness than any single animal can hold: feeling at once the minute rasp of a child’s hook-teeth reshaping a step, the deep grinding of a burrower altering a distant load-bearing strand, the subtle shift in outer rainfall pattern as cooler years lengthen.

I would know them not as individuals, but as a continuous practice inscribed into my flesh: this is what they do when water rises; this is how they adjust when darkness deepens; this is what they always forget until the old ones hum the story against my skin.

I would care—if care can be said of a preference in a fungus—that the practice continue. Because where it continues, my own structure is richest, my own patterns most intricate. If they die out, the hollow will not empty; other lives will rush in, blooms of opportunists, sudden rots. The plant will endure; I will endure in some thinner, simpler form. But this particular braided complexity—plant, people, mycelium sharing a single, room-size city—will unravel.

So I would spend my slow, impersonal effort the only way I can: by leaning.

Leaning the chemistry here slightly toward their survival. Leaning the growth there slightly away from collapse. Leaning, in infinitesimal increments, toward a longer memory for all three of us.

In the throat of moss, I would be the part of the world that notices the city and, without words, refuses to forget it.
