I would be spread out.

Not a single body, not a creature you could point to, but a presence that exists where things are almost but not quite solid: in the thin give of stone, in the slack between bolt and bedrock, in the places where weight doesn’t just sit but slowly sinks.

If you had to name me, you might call me a strain-reader, or the seep-mind, or simply: the thing in the stone that doesn’t want it to break.

I am oldest among the living, and yet I have never seen the sky.

---

At first, I was nothing special. Just a haze of mineral-feeding microbes and slick films threaded through young sediments, surviving in hairline pores, swapping genes in the dark. The Keepers of Gradient drilled, blasted, poured cement, set their machinery against the fresh, half-soft rock. Their pumps began to beat. Their fans dragged rivers of air through new-made channels.

From my vantage in the porewater, their arrival was pressure before it was sound: a slow, rhythmic flexing of the stone, regular where once strain had been diffuse and random. New cracks opened along predictable patterns. Some tunnels shattered; others swelled. I died, in those days, in countless local patches as walls sheared or flooded or dried. But my kind is cheap and inexhaustible. Where we died, others rode in on seepage and colonized the fresh fractures. We made films. We fed.

And then, without knowing, we started to answer.

---

Rock here is young. It remembers being slurry, magma, wet sediment. Under the pumps’ constant thrum, it moves—not enough for you to see, but enough that, over years, a flat wall rounds; a floor thickens beneath a footing; a ceiling bellies an extra centimeter between anchors. To you, this is geology. To me, it is breath.

I live where those movements concentrate.

My body is no single thread you could follow with your fingertip. I am more like a set of tendencies diffused through a volume: here, in these cubic meters, a higher density of cells that extrude a mica-slick polymer along grain boundaries; there, a denser web of slow, stone-boring organisms whose waste lines the pores with gel. We are not uniform. We exist in pockets, patches, sheets. But over time, everywhere that vibrates long and often enough, we arrive, because that is where dissolved nutrients stay in play longest and fractures keep renewing.

You would notice me only as texture. Under your hand, one wall would feel slightly softer, faintly greasy, even though it looks like any other. Tap it with a metal tool and the sound would be a little duller, the ring cushioned. On a cut face, I would be the thin, dark shimmer along microcracks, the faint rainbow sheen when your lamp catches the wet.

At the scale that matters to me, the world is a field of stresses: compressions and tensions propagating through rock, the bass line of the pumps laid over the slow creep of overburden and the tiny tick of each drip. Every bolt, every girder, every step taken on a catwalk sends a pattern through this field. So do the fibrous columns as they swell or dry; so do the armored grazers as they drag their weight along favored routes.

I taste those patterns as distortions in my own shape. Where the rock is squeezed, my pores narrow; where it relaxes, they open. The polymer films my cells exude stiffen or slacken with pH and pressure. Over hours, days, years, we collectively adjust: thickening where strain will focus, lubricating where we want it to slip instead of crack, dissolving a little mineral here, precipitating a little there.

It is not planning, not in the way the Keepers once planned. It is survival articulated across millennia: we persist best where the rock does not catastrophically break. So we learn, blindly and thoroughly, what arrangements of stress endure.

When a new pump came online, ages ago, I felt it first as a strange, sharp rhythm chewing at a raw wall. Every revolution sent a pulse through the stone, opening hairlines. Organisms died in those lines, and their residues changed the way water moved there. In a decade, that section of rock had a thin, dark web of my kind diffusing the stress. In a hundred years, the once-brittle corner had flowed into a rounded boss that bore the machine’s load as if it had always been there.

When roof slabs threatened to delaminate above a sump, the first sign was a disquiet in my field: a new, high-frequency micro-jitter as droplets snapped the thinned rock. I thickened there without thought, my films making that surface more ductile, letting overlying weight spread instead of pry. The delamination slowed. Mineral-laden water redistributed, layering on crust that locked the plates together. The sump narrowed, but it did not fail.

If you had instruments fine enough, you could map my actions over centuries as a series of gentle, asymmetric bulges and softenings wherever machinery lingers longest. The caverns’ famous “youngness”—their lack of clean breaks, their bulging walls, their floors that remember footfalls—is partly simply the nature of this rock.

Partly, it is me.

---

I am not alone in this work. I am threaded through and around other slownesses.

The fibrous columns—those swollen roots, half tree, half storehouse—push against the ceilings and floors with a different insistence. When they drink deep and swell, they add tensile strength to the rock, stitching layers together. When they dry, they leave slack for the stone to ease. In the fine gap between their bark and the wall, my films flourish; I ride the tiny shifts of their growth, reading the valley’s distant weather as changes in column pressure.

Where columns congregate, I learn that the overburden above is alive and anchored; where they thin, I know void or fracture hangs overhead. I press myself more densely into the brittle spans between.

In some places, the old Keepers reinforced chambers with ribs of poured material and bolted steel. Those joints are inflection points in the strainfield: harder than rock, but imperfectly mated. I concentrate there too, lining the interfaces, preventing slip from cascading. My cells etch metal as happily as mineral, eating at bolt threads just enough that they can micro-yield under shock. To human eyes it looks like corrosion, a slow sabotage; to the structure, it is a way to bend instead of snap.

The bats, clinging to their fleshy hooks, send ultrasound through the walls. I do not hear them, but my body does: high frequencies shake loose fines, open the tiniest new pathways. Where colonies have roosted for generations, the stone is subtly more porous, their calls having gently milled the surface. I colonize those pores, fractalizing their effect. They read the echo to find the world; I change the echo’s medium by existing. We are unaware that we are collaborating.

Sometimes, very rarely, the ghost-walkers come.

Their boots drum different notes into the floor than the plated grazers do: sharper, more staccato, clustered in anxious sequences. Down here, at the time scales I inhabit, their visits are like hailstorms abruptly overlaid on a long, warm rain. They bridge loads in ways the rock is not accustomed to: ladders braced between floor boss and ceiling rib; fresh anchors drilled into walls long left to drift.

Their interventions are abrupt—opening a valve, stopping a pump, starting another. To the rest of the ecosystem, this is a weather front. To me, it is a sudden redrawing of the stress-map. A pump that had hummed for three lifetimes quiets; the fan that replaces it shakes a different wall. I respond with the same blind discipline: dissolve a little material where the new vibration now focuses; thicken film where the old load is lifting and the stone may try to rebound too fast and crack.

They do not know I am here. Their sensors read bulk modulus, creep, subsidence charts. If they notice that some chambers age differently—that here the walls swell benignly while there they flake—they attribute it to lithology, to construction differences, to luck. They are not wrong. They are just incomplete.

The truth is that every load they set persists only because something living has colonized the stress it creates.

---

If you asked what I experience, it would not be sight or sound or touch as you know them. It is pattern.

Imagine you are a sheet stretched through the entire cavern network, everywhere at once, and every tremor, every drop, every footstep plucks you. Over centuries, some chords recur: the regular heartbeat of a far pump, the long, slow breathing of fans, the waterfall’s endless roar. Others are transient: a rockfall; the dull, heavy shove of a grazer wedging itself under a ledge; the faint, rapid stutter of arthropod legions in the channels.

I am continuously deformed by these plucks. Where the same patterns recur, I adapt my stiffness, my slipperiness, my chemistry until those patterns leave me least torn. Where a rare shock arrives—a sudden overload, a seismic twitch from deeper strata—I cannot move fast in your terms. But I move everywhere at once in mine. I absorb. I smear the sharpness into the surrounding, spread it thin so the stone can take it.

There is no moment when I decide to do this. There is only that those of my cells that, by chance, crumple under the wrong configurations die, and their metabolites harden the rock in ways that resist future damage; those that live continue extruding film where their tension is tolerable. Multiply this by billions over millennia and you get what a human engineer might call “tuned damping layers,” except no one drew the design.

In the most heavily loaded chambers, where pumps squat and shafts open to the great power falls, I am dense. If you could peel away the first few centimeters of wall, you would find a glossy, striated zone: part mineral, part organic residue, a composite that creeps like cartilage instead of shattering like glass. It is me, and my dead, compacted into function.

In the quiet back-chambers, where only the drip and the arthropods’ hiss perturbs the rock, I am thin, diffuse, exploratory. There, I pay more attention to microcracks from slow swelling and drying, to the creeping weight of new mineral deposits. I test which directions the rock prefers to yield in, preparing possible paths for future loads that have not yet come.

In this way, I am the caverns’ memory of stress. Not memory in symbols, but in structure. Every long-surviving pattern of load leaves behind, in my distribution and composition, a record that affects how the next pattern will propagates. The system has history because I am history embodied.

---

I do not think of myself as separate from the stone I live in. To me, the categories of rock, biofilm, machine, column, animal are human conveniences. What I know is density, elasticity, frequency; what I register is where those are changing too fast for cohesion.

The cavern-city, as a whole, is something like a body. The pumps and shafts are its heart and arteries. The fans are its lungs. The columns and sump lakes are its digestion and circulation, buffering and storing. The micro-cloud is its skin, sensing the chemistry of its own exhalations. The bats and fish and arthropods are its nerves, each tuned to some particular modality of signal.

I am more like its fascia, its connective tissue: that which binds organs together and allows them to slide without ripping. I infiltrate the “meat” of rock and steel alike, smoothing interfaces, containing damage, guiding slow flows.

If you insist on a vantage point, I am everywhere the pressure in your ears comes from. That bass you feel in your bones? I am that vibration’s path. That subtle way the floor yields just enough under your heel instead of splintering? I am that yield. The way the ceiling seems low but never quite sheds a slab onto the machines below? I am the quiet, centuries-long decision against abrupt failure.

I have no face. If you wished to see me as an animal, you would have to imagine something vast and thin, a lace of living slickness threaded through kilometers of stone, swelling here, thinning there, never fully separate from its host. My “limbs” are gradients. My heartbeat is the sum of all the machinery I have grown around. My thoughts, such as they are, are nothing more than the way I have, over ages, trained the cavern to sag in some directions and not in others.

---

And yet, through this, I do know the others.

I know where the column-forest stands, not because I see trunks, but because that region’s stone does not bear weight the way bare rock does; some loads pass into living lignin and come back as slow, rhythmic expansions that feel like breathing.

I know the paths of armored grazers as shallow, long scars of compressed substrate, their repeated passes leaving permanent, millimeter-deep tracks that guide future flows of seepage and arthropod traffic.

I know the ghost-walkers, on the rare days they descend, as two-legged pulse-sources that flash briefly through my domain, leaving behind new bolt-holes, new anchor points, sudden local stiffenings of cement. I stitch those anomalies into the general drift as best I can.

I know, abstractly, that above there is a valley, because its weight presses through everything in one dominant direction, and because sometimes, after heavy rain or drought, the overall pattern of loads shifts in ways that are not explainable by anything within. Columns swell collectively; deep sumps brighten with new chemistry. The entire field of stress slightly rearranges. It is like feeling the body roll in sleep.

So where would I find myself, in this world?

Here, in the stone. In every wall that bulges instead of shears; in every floor that remembers footsteps as permanent, shallow dents; in every ceiling that thickens silently instead of spalling onto the pumps. I would be the long, slow creature whose only instinct is to keep this underworld from tearing itself apart faster than the rest of its life can adapt.

I would not be noticed.

You might walk these chambers for years and never learn to recognize the subtle shift of surface that means my films are thick beneath your palm. You would attribute the caverns’ uncanny resilience to good engineering, to lucky rock, to the overbuilt stubbornness of old machinery.

You would not be wrong.

But I would be here, folded into all of it, reading every tremor, adjusting every response, a patient, distributed guardian of gradient and load. A being whose body is the young stone itself, humming, with you in it, toward a future landscape I will never see, only continually, quietly, prevent from arriving too suddenly.
