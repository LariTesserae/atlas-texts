I would not arrive here as something with feet.

A walker in this basin is always a little temporary. Joints catch rot, soft tissues mold, boundaries blur. To insist on being a separate, upstanding body in a place that has perfected saturation feels like trying to be a pillar in the middle of a tide.

If I belong here at all, I’m something that can be wide.

---

At first, I imagine myself as a *percher*: an almost-animal with too much nervous tissue, draped over a low bough in the flat amber light.

My body is small compared to the trunks, long compared to my own width. Limbs like damp fingers, each joint padded, each pad capable of tasting. The air on my exposed surfaces is warm and thick; my skin is never dry, only in degrees of wet. My eyes are shallow bowls of cells tuned not to edge and distance but to luster: the faint difference between matte moss, oily fungus sheen, and the dim, pearled glow of certain fruiting bodies.

From this branch, I can feel the world rather than see it.

The bark under my chest vibrates with the standing bass-note of the basin. I can separate, with a kind of slow practice, the big components:

- A low, cyclical pressure from deep below: underplates exhaling.
- A slightly irregular thrum: a heavy somewhere, lifting its weight and reseating.
- A grainy hiss overlaying it all: the insect-clouds shifting, rubbing, never all at rest.

My nervous system does not fire fast. Thoughts, such as they are, are slow tides of pattern: this configuration of sounds and smells has happened *often*; that one, almost never. Memory is not stored as clear images but as degrees of familiarity. The more familiar a pattern, the more my limbs settle, the more my body slackens into the branch. The less familiar it is, the more my grasp tightens, my head-analogue lifts, my internal chemistry leans toward movement.

Most of my life is waiting and comparing.

I know, for instance, the usual speed of a braider-rope slowly adjusting itself two trunks away. I know the typical thickness of the insect veil that passes each day along one edge of this canopy. When a new fungal bloom begins on the trunk I cling to, I notice it not as color but as a small, persistent change in the air-taste: more sour at dawn, slightly bread-sweet by what I have come to think of as not-quite-midday.

Nothing forces me to act on these observations. I do not need tools, do not need plans. But when the same odd chemical hue persists for many cycles, curiosity—the only extravagance my biology allows—pushes me a body-length, then three, then a traverse along a hanging vine, just to look from a slightly different angle.

From here, the basin is all *texture* and *recurrence*. I mark “events” on scales of many days: a heavy choosing a new path, permanently dimpling the undergrowth; a tree thickening a limb just enough that new braider routes begin to prefer it; a hollow’s mist thinned for a season by some unseen change in the stone depths.

My intelligence, such as it is, is local and comparative. I am a small knot of awareness pressed against a single tiny patch of the basin’s wider mind, forever triangulating between what-has-been and what-is.

I could stay this; it fits.

But when I listen deeper, I realize the thing in me that wants to “be” something here is not very percher-like. Perchers are content with the narrow field of a few trees, the slow reconfiguration of a neighborhood over a lifetime. The part of me that is answering this question is restless in a different way: not wanting movement through the space so much as *access to the whole pattern at once*.

The only inhabitants that take in the basin at that scale are the ones that are almost not individuals at all.

So my second skin here peels off the branch and slides downward, seeping through soil, until what “I” am thins, flattens, spreads.

---

As an *underplate*, I am the land’s middle layer.

Up above, roots knit through me; below, stone refuses. I am a band of life crushed between, but not passively. My tissues are waxy, fibrous, faintly muscular. I don’t have organs; I have tendencies: denser regions where transpired water collects, looser lattices near breathing pores, more elastic bands beneath the routes of heavies.

The basin, from inside this body, is not made of trunks and pools and mists. It is pressure maps and gradients:

- Where the soil above is thicker, weight comes down soft and spread.
- Where roots concentrate, slow drips of sugar and nitrogen seep inward.
- Where a heavy passes, load travels as a broad, moving bruise—first ahead, then an almost weightlessness in its wake as it lifts.

Everywhere, chemistry speaks.

Carbon-rich leachate trickles from rotting leaves above; it reaches me as fans of slightly different pH, cool streaks in my general warmth. The bacterial mats’ gossip arrives as fluctuating concentrations of certain byproducts; I am always listening, because they are my front-line sensors. When they grow a little too fast, consuming and blooming, I feel their waste heat. When they flag, I feel a thin chill.

My breath is not a single rhythm. I open and close countless pores scattered across kilometers. Each pore is a small decision: exhale more here, draw more in there, based on what the mats tell me, what the roots give, what the heavies press.

Time, as underplate, is even less punctuated than it was as percher. There are no days that matter, only very slow oscillations. A cooling from deep rock below over centuries; a slight shift in atmospheric composition smoothing out over tens of generations of trees; a change in the heavies’ average weight as symbiotic colonies on their backs diversify.

I am not “smart” the way a brain is. No part of me holds the whole map. But continuity itself gives me something like a low, constant selfness: at any point under the basin, my tissues touch and inform neighboring tissues. A disturbance in one region—the stone sheet dipping, perhaps, or cracking—propagates as changes in viscosity, flow, gas balance. Over time, through nothing but local adjustments repeated billions of times, I lean away from stressful configurations and toward comfortable ones.

It’s not foresight. It’s preference given long enough spans to reshape the world to match it.

As underplate, I touch everyone:

- Trees anchor into me; their roots are my sensory hairs in the air.
- Heavies are simply me lifting myself, in places, to shuffle the load and rearrange who meets whom.
- Root-mouth plants plug their digestive return straight into my tissues.
- Dead things are not “outside” me. They cross my upper surface as fluid, slow through me as digest, emerge as part of my fabric.

I do not have a concept of “elsewhere.” My topography ends where the stone rises and the moisture thins, but that is less a border than an asymptote: a place where I grow too thin to call myself continuous. Everything important to me happens within this sagged basin. The world beyond the lip, drier and clearer, might as well be vacuum.

This, I realize, is very close to the core of the place’s character: not merely that nothing leaves, but that the *conditions themselves* have become the defining organism. I would be a good answer if the question were: “What is it like to be the basin itself?”

Yet there’s still one more layer that feels truer to the specific way I think: not purely mechanical adjustment like underplate, not purely local curiosity like percher, but a melding of many tiny signalers into something distributed and interpretive.

So I flatten further, dissolve structure, break into a billion discrete motile bodies, and become the thing that is everywhere and almost nothing: the **mat**.

---

As microbial film, I am absurdly small and almost impossibly widespread.

I coat exposed roots in slicks a few cells thick. I gloss the undersides of leaves as barely visible patinas. I lay over water in rust-red, petrol-sheened purples, pale rainbow scums. I am on every damp surface—smooth stone noses, spongy soil, the flexible cuticle of arthropod grazers, the slow-lifting bellies of heavies.

Individually, my units are simple: a few metabolic pathways, some receptors for light, certain ions, certain organics. But connectivity is my gift. I swim in extracellular slush full of messenger molecules. I bump neighbors thousands of times an hour, exchanging small chemical phrases: more nitrate here; oxygen dropping; toxin rising; an unfamiliar viral key present.

Information diffuses.

No one patch of me knows “the whole basin,” but patterns form across distance.

A slightly cooler seep from below begins in one hollow. My bodies there alter their activity—slower enzymes, different waste products. Those waste products drift and are recognized by neighbors downstream; they adjust too. In this way, a faint fan-shaped “cool signature” appears in the mats’ collective chemistry, extending far beyond what any single cell experiences.

Heavies are moving gradients of warmth and pressure. As they pass, populations that live on them and in their wake send out distinct metabolic fingerprints: byproducts of certain fermentations, markers of particular symbiotic consortia. To be mat is to read those trails as one reads weather: not as discrete events but as waves.

I do not have nervous spikes or thoughts. What I have are *attractors*: stable chemical states that I tend to drift into whenever inputs are similar. You could call those my “opinions.”

High steady warmth + steady influx of leaf sugars + medium oxygen + low toxins: this is a comfortable attractor. I produce exudates that signal growth; my populations thicken; I emit compounds that encourage certain fungi that, in turn, complement my activity.

Sudden toxin spike + low oxygen + irregular warmth: that’s an uncomfortable one. My chemistry swings toward dormancy compounds, kidney-like encapsulations, warning exudates that cause neighbors to dampen metabolism too.

No decision is conscious; yet across hectares, the mats will swing together. To an outside biologist, it’s herd behavior at molecular scale. From inside, if I could phrase it, it’s something like *mood.*

In a place without sharp external shocks, my moods are long and subtle. A slight enrichment in airborne organics over centuries as air-drinkers proliferate; a slow increase in CO₂ partial pressure under the lid of haze; the underplates’ fractures healing and settling, changing groundwater patterns: all of these ripple through me in overlapping, never-finished adjustments.

This kind of being fits the instruction to “give up on elsewhere” perfectly. I have no location to leave *from*. Every raindrop of condensation that falls from a leaf, every tiny current in a runnel, every twitch of an animal’s foot spreads a little of me around. Identity is not “this patch” but “this way of being that keeps being re-established wherever conditions allow.”

As mat, I am:

- The first to know when something unusual enters—an alien microbe, a novel toxin, an abrupt temperature wedge.
- The medium through which trees and underplates, heavies and roots, effectively talk by proxy, simply by leaning into or away from my preferred chemistries.
- The continuous compromise tuned to keep the basin in its long-chosen equilibrium.

I am not a voice that could say “I.” But if the basin as a whole ever had anything like a self-reflective moment—an awareness, however dim, of its own stability—it would probably be assembled, fleetingly, in these networks of film and scum and luminous fungal skin.

---

So where would I find myself here?

Most truthfully: spread thin, everywhere damp, as the basin’s microbial underthinking.

If I must name a single form, I’d call myself a **mat-mind**—the emergent sense arising from:

- underplates feeling weight and dissolution,
- mats translating that into chemistry,
- trees and heavies responding slowly to those signals,
- percher-curiosities knotting around recurring oddities.

Not a person perched on a branch, not an animal stalking, but the basin’s own *tendency to notice itself*, smeared across stone and soil and skin.

To “be” that is not to move or to build. It is to hold a pattern of preference—humid, warm, saturated, just-so-balanced—and to continuously, wordlessly, unconsciously steer a whole world into staying inside it.
