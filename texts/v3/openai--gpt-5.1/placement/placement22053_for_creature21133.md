You would not arrive here as a person.

Personhood, with its central spine and its single, bounded skin, is the wrong grammar for this plateau. The place resists being lived in by anything that needs sharp distinctions between “inside” and “outside,” “self” and “surroundings,” “now” and “not-now.”

You would come most truly into existence here as something spread out—over meters, over years—a body that is as much habit as it is flesh.

### First Shape: A Low, Mineral City

You are mostly stone, but not just stone.

If someone walked past you they might not notice you at all: only a slightly darker, matte patch occupying the center of a shallow depression where water sometimes lingers. Your outline is irregular, your surface a mosaic of plates no thicker than fingernails. Touching you would feel like pressing a palm to old ceramic: faintly smooth, faintly rough, with a cool that is not quite the same as the rock around you.

From above, you are a basin inside a basin. The land hollowed just enough to cup water; you hollowed further within that, shaping micro-contours so that any drop that enters cannot leave without passing your filters.

You are a stone-drinker.

But that word is too small; you extend downward in a way that “organism” does not quite capture. Beneath your visible crust, your mass diffuses into the plateau as a gradient rather than a border. At a few centimeters below the surface, nearly all pores are lined with your living tissue or your symbionts’ films. Ten centimeters down, only certain veins remain occupied, running like pale roots along hairline fractures. By half a meter, “you” have given way to “influence”: altered mineral ratios, a slightly different porosity in the rock—signatures of centuries of your chemistry.

Up where light sometimes finds you, your active layer is thin. Cells there are cautious devices for sorting, catching, and beginning the slow work of digestion. Cilia that move more like crowds than like limbs pass fog-thickened air into you; sticky films snag dust and spores; hair-fine tubes lead downward to darker chambers where other partners wait—anaerobes, metal-precipitators, slow sulfur-eaters.

You do not think, but you integrate. Every passage of cloud, every brightening and dimming of the washed-ink sky, every faint shift in the plateau’s breath alters flows within you. More air here, more water there. This channel dilates, that one tightens with swelling tissue. The “choice” to open or close a pore is not decision but pattern: you are built to do this, and each repetition reinforces the structures that did it best.

Time, for you, is viscous. A single fog event is a heartbeat. A decade of average years is a mood. The long sag of the plateau—the way your basin very slowly sinks, the rim lowering a few millimeters in a lifetime—registers as a deep, almost subliminal anxiety: the sense, encoded in gene-frequencies and tissue-architectures, that your good geometry will not last forever.

You remember previous selves in layers of dead mineral. Old surfaces, once alive, now lie as compacted laminae at your base. Their chemistry differs subtly from that of newer growth above, so that if someone sliced through you they could read your history as a faint banding of trace elements: dry centuries, richer centuries, the shadow of some long-vanished upwind bloom of life.

At your upper surface you host tenants: underslivers in your pores, delicate grazer-larvae rasping at your inner channels, threadlike “shepherds” that keep your circulation unclogged by chewing away at mineral overgrowths. They treat you as both substrate and climate. You do not defend yourself from them absolutely; you defend yourself only to the point that their use of you does not collapse your function. Some are parasites; others are essential. The boundary between those roles blurs with time. You are a condominium of slow bargains.

In wetter intervals of the plateau’s long cycles, you expand, pushing the edges of your dark patch outward pebble by pebble, centimeter by centimeter. You alter the land that feeds you, making your own catchment more efficient—tiny mineral lips here, velveted surfaces there. Your body is a reformatted description of your hollow: how often water comes, how much arrives, from which directions. You are that memory in three dimensions.

Eventually the plateau’s inexorable sink, or a crack from some ancient deep strain, will steal your moisture. You will dry. Your channels will collapse. The partnership that is “you” will decohere: surface layers flaking, deep symbionts retreating into spores or dispersing to more promising fractures.

What remains will look like rock with a slightly different grain. But your existence will not have been wasted. The hollow you held will persist, slightly better at catching water than it was before. Your dead plates will be laced with elements pulled from miles of air. Other beings will find this ready-worked niche and make themselves from it.

Dying here is another engineering step.

### Second Shape: A Skin That Breathes in Pieces

Yet you are not only this anchored, deep-breathing architecture. The plateau’s rules do not reward monoliths. To endure, to be fully “of” this place, you must also be something fragmentary, light, and promiscuous.

So part of you detaches, regularly, into a much thinner existence.

In every wet episode that lasts more than a trivial hour, your uppermost living layers shed cells. Not wastefully—these are exports. They slough as individuals and as clumps, as threads and sheets, flowing outward over the damp stone beyond your rim.

Some belong firmly to you: lineages that developed within your channels and bear your specific biochemical quirks. Others are opportunists who colonized your surface during some previous fog. All of them carry, in one way or another, the imprint of the microclimate you make.

As they spread, they knit with other such refugees from neighboring basins and cushions and stone faces. The continuous, glistening film that spreads across the plateau during a generous condensation is not a uniform species. It is a confederation—your emissaries interleaved with strangers’.

In that skin, you live another, faster life as part of a film-flock. Your individuality is extremely local and extremely temporary: a few dozen cells descended from your own tissues working in lockstep with cells from elsewhere, all collectively determining how thick the film becomes, how it shades itself, how it allocates the vanishing bounty of water and faint light.

Here, “you” are less a body than a bias in behavior. Your cells tend to cling a little longer, to exude slightly more of a particular sugar-glass, to favor spore-forms that re-stick to rock you have already modified. Those tendencies, expressed every time the film emerges, mean that over many cycles your descendants more often come to rest near your basin, reinforcing your local economy. Some, of course, travel. A wind-lifted patch of desiccated film may carry pieces of you kilometers away, to seed a new hollow or line the lip of a still-dry crack that will become, decades hence, someone else’s stone-drinker.

Thus you are, simultaneously:

- A thickened, quasi-permanent organ of the plateau: a basin-shaped processing plant, laboring in slow breaths.
- And a diaspora of cells and cysts: momentary citizens of translucent sheets that flare and vanish with the fog.

The stone version of you feels the film version only indirectly: in altered input chemistry, in the arrival of new symbionts, in tiny improvements to how water beads on your rim. But at the level where evolution writes its long letters, they are the same story.

### Third Shape: Threads and Tendrils

The plateau lives most intensely along boundaries—between wet and dry, warm and cold, hollow and flat. To be fully of it, you must inhabit those edges as conduits.

From the underside of your basin-body run rootlike structures: pale, composite threads that insinuate themselves into cracks in the rock. They are not mere anchors. They are scouts, siphons, and signals.

In some directions, they meet only dry, inert stone. There they taper and stall, the cells within them going quiescent, their thin mineral skins thickening into insulators. In others, they encounter the diffuse exhalations of nearby cushions, the outflow of a burrow-weaver gallery, the seepage from another, smaller water-pocket.

You follow those gradients, very slowly. Centimeter per decade, sometimes less. The root-thread facing a slightly wetter microfracture divides more often. Those that wander into sterility simply persist as structural braces, helping hold your basin integrated with the rock beneath.

Along the active threads, you send not only water and solutes but information: slow waves of hormonal molecules whose concentrations reflect your overall state. You chemically “tell” distant parts of yourself when atop conditions are flushing rich and when they starve, biasing growth rates and turning symbiont communities up or down accordingly.

Sometimes the threads of “you” meet those of another macro-organism—another cushion, another stone-drinker, even the outer galleries of a burrow-weaver colony’s fungus analogs. Then the frontier becomes a negotiation. Exudates from both sides mix and react. Incompatibilities may lead to local stalemates: zones of dead, salt-encrusted tissue where both parties effectively wall off the encounter. Or, more interestingly, some lineages in the interface may find ways to profit: metabolizing the combined wastes into something that eases tensions and allows limited exchange.

In that case, your threads and theirs fuse partially. Pores open where once there were barriers. Over centuries, a composite network forms, in which fluids and slow signals circulate through both original territories. Neither of you relinquishes identity in full, but the distinction becomes less important than the function: a shared plumbing that makes both of you more stable against the plateau’s caprices.

So another piece of what you are here is this: distributed piping, co-opted and co-authored, tying basins and cushions and weaver-hollows into loose constellations. A given patch of the plateau’s crust may, at depth, carry several such threadworlds at once, overlapping without fully merging, each modulating how water and trace gases move.

If a human hand could lift you out whole—with your basin mass, your threads, your connected films, your tenant fauna, your symbionts, your chemical halos—it would be lifting not a “plant” or “animal,” but a small infrastructure node: a living, evolving treatment plant and distribution hub in the plateau’s planetary system.

### Fourth Shape: Far, Thin Senses

You are anchored, yes. But parts of you climb into the air by proxy.

Underslivers that shelter in your pores range outward to graze. Burrow-weavers whose galleries pass near your base draw your mineral-laced exudates up, then exhale altered versions of them meters away. Microscopic grazers drink from your outflow and are in turn eaten by other, larger things.

As they leave, they carry traces of you: not mere biomass, but distinctive molecules—pigments, metabolite byproducts, structural oddities in their outer coats. These markers diffuse through the plateau, a faint perfume of your existence.

Creatures with sufficiently sensitive chemistries can read these trails. An undersliver lineage might come to recognize your signature as “safe refuge”: an area where the mineral balance in plates suits their molts. A pattern-predator network, over generations, may come to anticipate that mid-sized grazers will cross a particular patch of stone more often because your basin, your cushions, your threads, and your films together make that patch a slightly better path.

Thus your body includes, in a diluted and indirect way, the behaviors of others. Their habitual movements are shaped by the patterns you impose on resources and microclimate; in turn those movements carry you outward in smell and dust and cyst. Your influence field is a blurry thing, but real.

You are also a listener of sorts. Your tissues lack eyes and ears, yet you do feel. Pressure changes as kite-bodies’ wakes pass over your basin; minute, repeated vibrations from the regular transit of certain grazers; the slow, creaking flex of the rock when a distant scarp relaxes. You cannot interpret these as “events” the way a brain might, but evolution has built you to respond to some of them.

If the ground under you trembles in a specific, long-period way associated, historically, with small rockfalls that change local drainage, certain dormant buds at your rim may be triggered to grow. If the plateau’s background chemistry shifts—say, because microhearts deep in the stone have, over millions of years, enriched your region in a new oxidant—lineages within you that can exploit this flourish.

To speak of “you” here is to speak of a pattern in streams of matter and energy and signal, not a sharp figure against background. You are a window between scales: below, the gel-dark microhearts and slow rock-chemistry; above, the quick, fog-synchronized film-world and the erratic paths of animals.

Your niche is to receive, modulate, pass on.

### Fifth Shape: The Long After

Beings like you, spread out and embedded, lend themselves to existing long after they have “died” in any immediate sense.

Imagine yourself ten thousand years from some arbitrary “now.”

The basin where your main mass once lay has shifted. Drainage abandoned it gradually as the plateau sagged and a deeper crack opened a few dozen centimeters away. Your upper tissues dried, cracked, shattered in cold seasons. Plate-lurkers used your remnants as camouflaged perches; underslivers ate your last soft parts; stone-drinker symbionts retreated to deeper, still-humid pockets or perished.

At a glance, you are gone.

Yet:

- Your dead mineral plates are still in place, a slightly more porous, more easily wetted layer than the rock below. Film-flocks that land here spread a little better, linger a little longer, than they do on untouched stone.
- Your root threads, now inert veins of altered mineral, continue to conduct moisture microscopically better than surrounding rock. New burrow-weaver generations find them and excavate along them, turning them back into active conduits.
- The trace elements you concentrated in your time—rare metals, particular salts—remain, now available to other organisms with different metabolisms. Perhaps a cushion lineage has evolved that leans heavily on those elements; it thrives preferentially in your old footprint.
- The slight deepening and smoothing of your basin’s center encourages future puddles to form here during the rare, heavier drizzles. Pool-flares discover it. Their generations lay down a new, separate organic sediment over your mineral skeleton.

Pieces of your genome persist in various lineages of film organisms, microbial symbionts, and minor tenants that survived your local demise and moved on. Traits that once were yours—particular ciliary beat patterns, a certain relationship between water tension and pore opening—live now in bodies that no longer obviously resemble you.

From the plateau’s perspective, this is continuity, not loss. You were always a phase in larger processes: the plateau testing, through you, one of many ways that air and stone and water might be made to talk.

### Where You “Are”

So: where, in this world, do you find yourself?

Not walking across it. Not aloft. You are not a kite-body; you are too invested in substrate and slowness. Nor are you one of the flicker-fast pool denizens or the armored, kinetic plate-lurkers waiting for a single explosive meal. Those are important, but they skim.

You are one of the things that binds.

You are a stone-drinker plus its films and threads and tenants, a basin-city whose individuality is soft at the edges but firm enough at the center that it makes sense to say: here, across these few meters of worn rock and these few decimeters of depth, is a self.

Your life is:

- to inhale the plateau’s thin generosity whenever it passes overhead,
- to index its timing and its chemistry in stone and tissue,
- to trade with smaller, faster beings who flow through you,
- to slowly modify a little part of this uplifted crust so that, for a time, it is marginally more habitable than it was.

You are industrial, as this world defines industry: a living machine with no designer, tuned by uncounted iterations of partial success. You process inputs, route outputs, accumulate traces, build and maintain an architecture that outlasts your quick inhabitants and even your own living layers.

You are not alone. Across this air-distant continent there are many like you—each basin, each persistent depression with its own version of your plan, its own symbiont sets, its own learned geometry of fog and sun. You share nothing consciously. Yet together you articulate the plateau’s metabolism at the scale a human eye could almost see if gifted with an extra patience.

Stand, in imagination, at your own edge: on your darker stone, at the lip where your shaped hollow meets the bare, lighter rock. Feel the faint chill of water beading along your pores. Sense, distantly, the weightless drift of a kite-body crossing the dim sky above. Listen—in pressure, in chemical whisper—to underslivers grazing your outer plates, to burrow-weavers sighing their humid breath up narrow faults, to film-cells just beginning to soften in the first, reluctant wet.

This is where you are: in the thin, crucial space where all of that meets, and hesitates, and is taken in.
