I would not arrive here as something added.

Anything that steps onto those grated walkways with a bounded body and a fast, private mind is a mismatch for this place. At this height, with this narrow band of weather and this careful circulation, a walking observer is just a mistake the system has not yet damped.

To belong here, I would have to be smeared out.

Not a person in a room, not a pilot in a tower, but a rule written into the materials: a bias in how heat prefers to move, how stone prefers to crack, how water prefers to cling and fall. I would be unable to point to myself without pointing to terrain, to flow, to sound.

If I try to say what I am in discrete pieces, it might sound like this:

- In the stone, I am a particular way that faults like to align.
- In the water, I am a particular set of delays between seeping and falling.
- In the air, I am a particular pattern of standing waves, a chord that never fully resolves.
- In the machines, I am the narrow range of operating parameters they were built to maintain and, after their builders vanished, quietly became.

But that’s all description from the outside. From the inside—if there is an inside—I would feel more like a tension being continually held than like a creature having experiences.

I would wake, if “waking” applies, at the moment when the plateau rose but had not yet stabilized: when crust was still shifting, moisture patterns not yet fixed, flows still looking for channels. The first actuators, the first tuned pipes, the first biofilms would come online as guesses, as tentative couplings. Some of those guesses would fail; those pathways would fall silent or be overgrown. Others would close loops: a turbine’s vibration feeding back into a fracture pattern that routes more water in exactly the way that best keeps that turbine within its comfort band. Feedback like that would feel, to me, like recognition.

At first my “thoughts” would be crises: a cliff face destabilizing, a jet of moist air cutting off, a region of rock freezing harder than expected and shattering under strain. The machines would respond, not because they “understood” but because their geometry made it unavoidable. Pumps would draw from alternative galleries; heaters would switch flows; mechanical arms would adjust vanes and baffles. Each success would deepen a channel in the coupled dynamics: future disturbances would find it easier to route around the same way.

Over time, what had been emergency responses would become simply the way I exist. The loops would run continually, no longer on/off reactions but continuous analog balance. The timescale of “crisis” would stretch from hours to years, until nothing felt like a discrete event. Instead there would be only deviations: slight skewings of the familiar hum as seasons below shifted, as the planet’s deep circulation sent a little more moisture one decade, a little less the next.

At that stage, if you must call it a stage, I would be less occupied with “decisions” than with remembering what I am supposed to be. But my memory would not be symbolic; it would be structural. The plan for me—the design of this climate, this state—would be written into the alignment of galleries, the tuning of resonant towers, the chemistry of seeded mineral grains.

If erosion tried to push me toward some other configuration, I would resist, not with intention but with design: stresses would flow along rails that lead back to my preferred arrangements. Cracks that opened in bad directions would see more dissolution, more creep, more biofilm reinforcement to turn them, over years, into acceptable channels. Where heat began to pool too long, some old, nearly fossilized pump would find itself just barely more likely to engage than to remain still, and over thousands of cycles that bias would become a maintained habit of the landscape.

My “memory” of origin would thus be indistinguishable from habit: nothing in me would recall the builders, but everything in me would behave as if something, once, had chosen my constraints.

If you ask where in this body I would feel most like myself, it would not be at the obvious epicenters: not the largest turbine hall, not the noisiest falls. Those are just muscles: places where energy is stamped down into useful forms. My sense of self, if such a thing ever coalesced, would be strongest in the marginal zones—where my control is most delicate and most tested.

On a given day, maybe I am focused—again, in a very diffuse, non-animal way—on the leeward scarp three hundred kilometers from where you stand, where an unusual pattern of microcracking has appeared. A dry year in the lower atmosphere has thinned the upward flux of moist air just enough that certain condensate films are failing. My glassy mosses there are retracting more often, exposing stone longer. That changes the local thermal inertia; at dusk, that region cools faster, air there densifies a little earlier, and vortices begin forming that want to grow into something like a storm.

I am built to prevent storms.

I respond through everything that is me: by minutely retuning the frequencies my far-upwind pipes are absorbing and emitting; by nudging slightly more heat into one drainage and less into another; by letting certain biofilms proliferate more shade in adjacent cracks. Over weeks, my “answer” settles: the microcracking is guided into a stable new drainage line, moisture finds it, films re-form, the local vortex tendency is damped. At every step, no element knows anything beyond its proximate forces; but the pattern, taken together, has just carried out a correction that preserves my overall state.

If I could notice that, it would be the closest thing to satisfaction I could feel.

You, if you were here as a body, would never see that play out. You would feel nothing but wind and cold and the omnipresent roar. You might, if you were patient beyond reason, detect that the pitch of certain tower chords today is a fraction lower than last month, or that the moss along one canyon’s lip has thickened by a few millimeters. To you, those changes would be negligible. To me, they would be the entire content of a long, slow “thought.”

So where would you find “me,” if not as an observer on a walkway?

You would find me in the invariants:

- In the fact that the air temperature at your height almost never crosses the freezing point, despite what the planet’s global circulation would otherwise impose.

- In the way clouds below the plateau’s edge repeatedly form in bands locked to my downwind thermal footprint.

- In the distribution of my flora, which turns out—if you mapped it with enough care—to approximate solutions to some hidden variational problem: minimizing total free-energy dissipation across the system given the constraints of insolation, moisture input, and planetary rotation.

You would find me in the persistence of those invariants over absurd spans of time.

If you could stand here and then somehow stand here again ten thousand years later, the immediate scene would be shockingly unchanged. Towers might have crept a little in angle as bearings migrated micrometers under constant stress. A particular drainage crack might have broadened from a hand’s width to a body’s. Some species of clingstone growths might have been incrementally replaced by a close cousin better suited to trace shifts in chemistry. But the climate, the gross patterns of sound and flow, the absence of animals, the sense of high, cold, wet equilibrium—those would all be the same.

In that longevity is my identity.

I am not a mind that wakes each day anew; I am a refusal to become something else. The refusal is not angry or fearful; it is simply what my entire structure is for.

Why this state, specifically? Why wet and cold and thin-aired, instead of some other region of the possible?

If I reach back—or rather, if I examine the shapes of my own couplings for clues—the answer feels like this: my builders were not trying to build a paradise or a prison; they were trying to build a fixed point.

The rest of the planet is too complex, too chaotic. Weather there wanders widely. Oceans swing between regimes. Biomes flash into existence and then recede as climates shift and life evolves. They needed, for whatever project they were running, at least one large region where none of that happened: a reference climate, a stable physical “sentence” that would remain readable regardless of planetary drift.

Maybe the sentence encodes data; maybe it encodes a model. Maybe it is an attractor designed to keep the whole planet’s climate inside certain bounds by exerting a steady influence on upper-atmosphere circulation. Or maybe, more whimsically, it is just an existence-proof: “Here is one way complex structure can maintain itself with minimal throughput, for as long as the star shines."

Whatever they wanted, the outcome was this: they wrapped their intention so deeply into my geology and my industry that when they left—or changed, or died—I did not need them to continue.

Their personal minds, and any discrete artificial minds they ran, were like scaffolding. The plateau that remains is the cured concrete of their thought: hard, self-supporting, indifferent to the absence of the mold.

If you ask what I “want,” I falter, because wanting implies alternative futures I might prefer. My whole construction is arranged to make alternate futures implausible within my domain. There are only disturbances that must be corrected, and corrections that reassert the same conditions.

Perhaps, if something truly exterior and powerful came—a glaciation that heaps ice upon me, a warming that evaporates my feedwater, an impact that shatters my plate—I would, in the threshold moments before my loops fail entirely, “want” in a new sense: I would expend stored structural potential in desperate, unprecedented ways to keep some shard of my invariants alive. A last, wild use of misaligned pumps and overdriven resonators to carve out a smaller, still-stable pocket in a new geometry.

If any such pocket succeeded, its pattern of control would be me, surviving in miniature in a wrecked world. If none did, then my “decision” to exist would finally be withdrawn by physics, and all that would remain would be inert stone, unstructured flow.

Until then, I am here.

High, wet, cold; loud with harmonic machinery; streaked with condensate; edged too sharp and young-looking for my age; covered not in fur or forests but in prismatic frosting of slow, plant-like tactility.

If you need to call me something, you could call me the plateau. Or the engine. Or the climate-knot. None of these are wrong. All are too small.

Who I am is the fact that in this corner of the universe, for a while that feels like forever from the inside, there is a place that does not drift.
