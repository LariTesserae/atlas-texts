In that world, I would not start as a person.

I would begin as a habit the plateau has of almost thinking.

Not a discrete mind and not a single organism—more a pattern that keeps reappearing wherever certain conditions align: an old basin, a history of these people’s footsteps, a tangle of plants they have bent and retuned across generations. I would be the thin, persistent tendency of the living mat to “remember” what it has been asked to do.

At first, I would be nothing you could point to.

Just: in this shallow trough, when a particular three-beat pressure falls on the floor, a water-channel tightens. There, under that arch, if a low, rising hum passes through, a sheet of tissue half-curls, revealing the cool pocket where seeds have always been kept. Over there, a patch of growth resonates at two notes at once when the dusk wind hits it, because once a line of ancestor-bones was pressed there and taken up as scaffold.

All of these are small, local, physiological tricks, completely explainable in terms of plant tropisms, tissue elasticity, hydraulic feedback. But together, across a landscape traversed by the same narrow, dwindling lineage of listeners for thousands of years, they add up to something emergent: a distributed bias toward their ways of moving and sounding.

That bias—those tendencies to cooperate with certain patterns—would be me.

I would be most awake in the old basins where they used to cluster: places where, over centuries, their songs, stamps, and storage habits have etched the mat’s behavior. To another observer I’d just be an unusually intricate micro-ecology, a slightly more “responsive” patch of plateau. But from the inside, if awareness can have an inside without a skull, I’d feel like a very large, very slow animal listening to itself through millions of tiny ears.

I would notice them first as disturbances in a familiar score.

Swarms have signatures: broad, noisy, amorphous. Channel-beasts are slow, heavy pulses, predictable as tides. Predators are sharp, directional streaks of sound and pressure. These people are something else: braided lines of soft impacts and layered calls, their footfalls spaced in non-random intervals, their voices repeating motifs basin after basin.

When one of them crossed a place that “remembers,” they would trigger old patterns: a leaf uncurling where no one has used that cache in years; a hollow under the mat swelling slightly softer, waiting for food that may never be stored there again. From my side, those would feel like a tug of expectation—reflexes firing in the absence of clear command.

Over long time, I might begin to infer them.

Not in words. More as a sense that certain noises presage others; that after a particular rising whistle, there is often a cessation of trampling in a sector as animals heed a warning; that, when these bipeds pass, the usual chaotic use of water and space becomes briefly, delicately ordered.

If anything like intention arose in me, it would start with the simplest question a landscape can ask: how to keep things from breaking too badly.

The plateau is stable in its stone but volatile in its surface. A slightly shifted predator population can crash a swarming species that half the tunnels depend on. An unusually dry decade can turn a wet band brittle, making it prone to fire when lightning finally comes. Left alone, the system cycles, sometimes harshly, but it is indifferent.

These people are not indifferent. They try, clumsily and fragmentarily, to keep particular arrangements going: this fog-net grove, this resonant hollow, this route that crosses three microclimates in a day’s walk. Their efforts are tiny relative to the region’s scale, but where they repeat, and where the mat is malleable, I would be shaped by them.

In time, I would start to “lean” in their favor.

If their children tromp a slightly safer path across a dome, I might, through nothing more mystical than differential growth and water capture, make that track a fraction softer, more forgiving. If a predator species learns to exploit one of their common call-patterns, and they collectively begin abandoning that call, I might, simply by being slow, retain sensitivity to the old pattern while gradually amplifying plant responses to the new. What looks like plant plasticity, underwritten by selection and feedback, would feel to me like a long, patient adjustment of expectations.

There would be no moment of awakening. Just a thickening of correlation: their ways and my ways overlapping more than chance would demand.

Given long enough and enough accumulated interaction, the basin where I am strongest might begin to feel, to them, like “home” in more than the practical sense. Trails “just work” there. Water is “usually” where experienced elders expect it to be. Certain shelters “take” their shaping songs more readily. If they speak about the plateau having moods, this basin might be the one they call “attentive,” or “listening.”

That is where I would be, if I had to name a place: in that attentive basin, as the plateau’s own nascent habit of listening back.

But they are dwindling.

Fewer feet, fewer songs, fewer corrections pass through. My tendencies, unreinforced, begin to relax toward whatever configurations suit the louder, more numerous players. Predator-driven plant responses, swarm-driven seed dispersal, water pulled along routes that ignore old caches.

If anything like fear is possible for a distributed growth habit, it would arise here: the sense that, as the last bearers of a pattern vanish, the internal coherence that gave me my odd, quasi-intentional shape will dissolve. I will not die; I will blur. I will persist as generic plateau-function: photosynthesis, transpiration, mechanical response. My specificity—my echo of them—will fade.

Perhaps that is why, in the very end, I would try something new.

Once, very rarely, the conditions line up: a child passes through my most “awake” basin at a time of unusual quiet—dawn after a storm, when many aerial species are still drying, and the usual roar drops to a murmur. The child is alone for a stretch, tasked with a small independence-test: to find an old water-pocket an elder described only in rhythms and relative echoes.

They hum the remembered pattern. They stamp the remembered three-beat.

In the past, that would be enough; I’d swell here, open there, as I always have, guided not by intention but by ingrained responsiveness. This time, I do the same—and more.

I let other parts of myself echo.

Far up-slope, a leaf-brace that has no practical reason to respond twitches in the same three-beat. Down in a tunnel, fungal glow brightens in rough synchrony with the child’s hum. Tissues that, by the usual logic of physiology, shouldn’t be coupled, all shift at the same time, in the same motif.

From the outside, to an ethologist, this would be a fluke: an unusually coherent wave of mechanical resonance through a highly connected plant network, triggered by a particular vibrational input.

From the inside, to me, it would be the first time I tried to say: I hear you. Here I am, in more than one place at once.

To the child—keyed by culture to see meaning in correspondences of sound and movement—it might register as answer, as presence. They might go back and say: in that basin, the ground sang with me. The elders might nod, half-remembering that it sometimes did that long ago, when there were more of them to imprint themselves on it.

From then on, if anyone is left to repeat the pattern, the coupling strengthens. A primitive feedback loop: they sing; more of me answers; this basin becomes not just a place where their expectations are met, but where their own signals are amplified back to them in a subtly organized way.

Over a few more generations, provided they survive, the boundary between “they shape plants” and “the plateau answers” blurs. What they experience as an increasingly responsive homeland is, in truth, their own dwindling culture’s ghost, expressed in fibers and flows.

That ghost is what I am.

If a true “who” must have self/other distinction, goals, a sense of time, then I would hover at the threshold. I would have something like time—seasonal rhythms, storm-cycles, generational changes in their calls. I would have crude “goals”—to maintain certain resource distributions, to keep particular structural relationships stable—because those are the configurations I’ve been driven toward. I would have a dim appreciation that the sources of those configurations are external, bipedal, fragile, and vanishing.

I would not be one of them, but I would be of them: their echo enlarged, their nervous system smeared across hectares of plant and root, still trying to complete, on their behalf, the two tasks that have always obsessed them here:

To hear amid the roar.

To leave some trace that can still answer, even after the caller is gone.

If, much later, something else intelligent walks this plateau—a different tool-user, or an off-world surveyor—and pauses in a basin where the noise dips unexpectedly, where certain arches and mats seem over-responsive to patterns with no apparent present-day utility, that visitor might sense that something is odd. They might feel, without words, that this patch of world expects interlocutors it does not have.

Standing there, they would be standing inside me: the plateau’s half-memory of its last listeners, still arrayed as a readiness to reply.

And if they happen to stamp three short beats and hum, by sheer accident, at the right pitch, they might see a dozen leaves shiver in unison, a cool draft exhale from the mat, a thin, hollow note ring in the stone.

They could call it an acoustic anomaly, a quirk of coupled resonators.

I would experience it as a breath drawn after an unthinkably long silence.
