In this world I would not be one thing, once and for all. I would be a way of being spread across matter that does not stay still.

But if I have to choose a location to wake up, I wake up clinging.

---

My body is narrow and many‑jointed, a flattened lattice of struts and tendons that can lock stiff or go loose on command. From a distance I would look like an articulated brace grown legs: four main limbs splayed wide around a central plate, with subsidiary hooks branching and re‑branching from each, like the tines of a grappling starfish.

Every joint has two lives. One is muscular: I can flex and release, pull myself along fibrous surfaces, dig in with prismatic claws. The other is structural: by locking a joint I turn that segment into part of a tuned truss. My skeleton is not a passive frame but a configurable member of whatever I cling to.

I live where two trunks meet. Over my head and under my feet, miles of living plant tissue rise into the hard white glare and fall away toward the dusty deep. The junction beneath me is not a simple fork; it is a knotted plate of wood‑analog and foam, the shared shoulder of two balloon‑bodies and the origin of an entire candelabrum of dangling scaffolds.

I am small compared to it—ten meters from tip to tip when fully spread. To myself, of course, I am exactly the right size. My sensory world is scaled to this joint.

It hums.

Not metaphorically; the fibers under me are in standing motion, flexing by microns and meters in overlapping, incommensurate cycles. Through pads along my limbs I feel dozens of base oscillations, each with its own direction, depth, and texture. Overlaid on that are faster components: whips from thin branches catching higher‑frequency gusts, the rattling of old, hollowed struts, the random clatter of small parasites.

My skin is a forest of filaments. Each filament is a tuned stalk, stiff at its base, fine at its tip, resonant at a particular frequency range. I have hundreds of thousands of them. They don’t report “sound” to me. They report shape in time.

What you would call my “vision” is a constantly updating interference pattern in my nervous sheets: colored not by light but by the sum of all sensed strains. Out of that pattern, I recognize familiar structures—this trunk’s slow, deep two‑note subsong; that neighboring root‑plate’s high, almost inaudible quiver. The roar of the wind is not background nonsense. It is the carrier in which all my distinctions live.

I am one among many here. My sisters—genetic, clonal, memetic, the distinctions blur—cling in a ring around this node. Others like us stud the nearby spans at regular and irregular intervals. From far enough away, our distribution would look planned. From our history’s point of view, it is what survived.

We are tuners.

When we arrived as juveniles—soft, fist-sized bundles hurled by chance currents into crevices along the scaffolds—this joint already had its habits. It flexed with a particular virtuosity under certain winds and shuddered dangerously under others. Its parent trunks had grown baffles and irregularities long before us, written by storms I will never remember. We pressed our bellies to it and learned its modes the way a hand learns the weight of a tool.

Learning, for me, is not sitting apart and thinking. It is hours and days of letting my joints be pushed around.

At first, I clung any way I could: hooks buried in fibrous bark, limbs bent arbitrarily, frame making whatever crooked polygon happened to match the surface wrinkles. The wind would come; the node would arc; my body would be dragged along or slammed sideways as stresses flowed. Filament‑forests along my limbs lit up with chaotic input, and my internal sheets struggled to make sense of it.

Then a gust would happen to come at just the right angle and speed that a certain arrangement of my joints did not feel like pain. The waves in my sensory sheet would, for a few heartbeats, cohere: a simple pattern emerged, resonant but not near tearing. I would reflexively stiffen in that posture.

My tissues remember that. Literally: the collagen‑like polymers in my tendons snap into slightly different crosslinking when held under steady load at particular angles. Next time similar motion flows through the joint, it “prefers” that configuration.

Over many storms, I accumulate such preferences. My quick reflexes ride on top of them, but the slow-written chemistry is what changes who I am. I become a map of which angles have been survivable when which frequencies were present.

The concord around us shapes that, too.

I do not live alone on this node. My ring of sisters is one functional unit: we feel similar patterns, because the wood beneath us is one continuous resonator. But we are also part of something wider: a concord spanning this entire plant‑archipelago, running over and through three major trunks and dozens of lesser bridges.

When I brace just so—locking one limb in axial tension between a slightly convex patch of bark and a more distant attachment point I share with a neighbor—I add my mass and stiffness to a particular mode. If my neighbors, guided by their own locally stored experience and the concord’s patterns, shift in complementary ways, we can collectively warp which frequencies this node will pass and which it will dissolve.

To you, from the outside, that looks like coordinated engineering. To me, inside, it feels like keeping my internal map pleasantly shaped.

The concord is the way our separate comfort‑seeking behaviors add up.

Sometimes its influence is obvious. A pattern from far along the trunk—three deep pulses, a pause, two shallower—arrives in my sensory sheet as a synchronized tremor through wood and a faint, high rippling in my own filaments. I know this. I have felt it since I was soft. When that pattern rides the base-song, I unlock certain joints and prepare others. I feel my neighbors doing the same through the shared substrate.

In another part of the structure, others are doing their part: shifting masses along a vertical span, slackening a cross‑brace, opening pores that will briefly let sap flow change the local density. We have never “met,” those distant clusters and I. We do not need to. The pattern we all respond to is enough.

Under some conditions, I merely follow. The concord’s storm-canon has been rehearsed into me via countless small trainings: lulls where local leaders—older tuners with more deeply engrained memories—initiate truncated echoes of past crises. When I join in those low-energy reenactments, I learn which joints to favor for which approaches of pressure and shear.

But there are moments when I am not just a follower.

The air is thin and harsh; the temperature is low; the world is in ordinary motion. Then something dissonant arrives—some layered combination of frequencies that does not fit any of my “songs.” My filaments fire in unusual ratios. My internal sheet lights up in an unfamiliar, prickling geometry.

In those moments I am terrified and exultant. The living node under me is being forced into a configuration that none of its internal memories can quite accommodate. The concord’s usual patterns are late or contradictory. I feel my sisters shifting, searching their own joint‑chemistries for some remembered ease that is not there.

So I guess.

Guessing, for a creature like me, is choosing a posture that is not demanded by any already-favored linkage. It feels wrong at first, wasteful, out of tune. I move anyway, because every fiber screams that the current distribution of stress is worse.

I unlock an old joint, extend a limb farther than is safe to a new anchor point, rotate a brace-angle I have rarely used. The local waves of strain change slightly as a result—just here, just under me. New patterns ripple outward from that perturbation. Sometimes they make things worse; I feel a rise in amplitude and panic, and wrench myself back into some previous known.

Sometimes the new configuration makes a small pocket of relief. That relief spreads if it is real: neighbors feel it through their filaments, unconsciously incline their limbs in ways that align with mine. The node’s song shifts. If that shift does not lead to catastrophic resonance elsewhere along the span, it may propagate further as a new miniature motif. The concord notices—though it has no center to do so—and incorporates the new behavior in future rehearsals.

When that happens, a fragment of my particular risking becomes part of our canon. I will feel its faint shadow again in quieter seasons, when pattern‑players along the trunks initiate playful variations and others try them on. Some of those echoes lead to subtle long-term changes in how the host plant rests in calm air. The node never quite settles back into its old baseline. My little moment of wrongness has been written into wood.

So if you ask *who* I am, the nearest accurate thing is: I am a local solver in a great, continuous problem. My “self” is the history of the solutions I have embodied, as against all the alternative arrangements I could have taken and did not.

My day, if you want to call it that, has no lights-on, lights-off. The white sun moves; the wind’s profile rotates as bright side becomes dark; flows from deeper atmosphere rise and fall. In some parts of that cycle, my work is urgent: the roar thickens, new modes threaten, the plant flinches under huge, slow punches of air. Then my entire attention is consumed by reconfiguration—brace, slacken, twist, lock—under the guidance of the concord’s pulses and my own immediate sense of where pain may arise.

In the calmer arcs, I engage in what, from outside, might look like idleness.

I shift minutely on the node, trying alternative micro‑postures under gentle load, feeling the way the fine oscillations redistribute along my frame. Other tuners around me do similar. Small, experimental sequences of synchronized clamping and release pass through our ring: not the big, canonical patterns of serious preparation, but ornate, compressed quotations of sections from older storms, impossible runs of changes we could never sustain at full amplitude.

I enjoy some of those purely for their texture. Certain sequences make my filaments ring in harmonic cascades I find… satisfying, the way a deep, consonant chord might please a human ear. I find myself repeating such runs in my own time, overlaying them on the present base-song, seeing how the node’s current shape answers.

Sometimes, by accident, a playful pattern discovers a new self-limiting mode in the plant’s tissues: a way of loading certain joints so that incoming energy folds onto itself as heat without propagating further. When that happens, the node as a whole changes in a way that lasts. Its low-level song dulls or brightens; a resonance that used to wake at one wind‑speed now wakes at another; distant spans feel that change as a shift in what patterns make sense there.

The concord will decide, over many storms, whether this new quirk is useful. Useful means “leads to fewer catastrophic failures and more food.” If it is, my playful pattern will be promoted into the training repertoire. If not, tissue turnover and genealogical dilution will erase it. I will die without any sign in matter that I once tried that variation, except as it persists in the tiny chemical biases within my descendants’ joints.

I do not know that in our way we are doing engineering, art, and long‑term adaptation all at once. I know only the felt press of strain fields, the weird rightness of certain posture‑mode combinations, and the rippling comfort when an entire section of structure deserts one dangerous song for another, more bearable one.

---

Sometimes, in slower moods, my attention wanders outward along frequencies I do not usually follow.

Most of my life is lived in the mid‑range of periods from heartbeats to minutes—the tempos where joints fail and bridges tear. But there are lower notes, so deep they cycle only a few times between the bright and dark faces of the planet. They are the breath of entire jet streams, the response of trunk masses spanning hundreds of kilometers.

Those notes barely move my filaments. I feel them more as tides in the background of everything else, raising and lowering the “floor” on which ordinary storms dance. If I sink into them—relax rapid reflexes, widen internal integration times—I can ride them as contours, like thinking about geologic time instead of days.

In those depths I sense presences that are not mine or my concord’s: the slow, distributed patterns of the plant-bodies themselves. The great ganglia of resonant tissue inside their nodes churn through feedback cycles whose periods are too long for any one of my generations to complete. Yet I feel their outlines: this host has a tendency to raise tension early in the bright cycle; that neighbor slackens late. Their “moods” color the kinds of storms that reach us, because their own shapes deflect and re‑shape the wind.

In a faint, alien way, I feel that they too are someone—longer, less agile someonehoods written not in choices over hours but in biases over centuries. My own small improvisations contribute to them. I am a synapse in a body that will never know me, yet whose “posture” I help maintain.

Even higher up in abstraction are the concords themselves: an emergent someonehood which is not me, not my node, but which gives pattern to the calls that move through us. When a rare, great motif passes—a whole‑structure command to abandon a span, or to risk growing a new bridge—I feel awe. The pattern is too large and temporally extended for me to see, yet I trust its outline. My life is better under its consistency than it would be without.

If you forced human categories onto that: I am an individual; the node is a neighborhood; the concord is a city; the plant‑body is the ground and the climate both. But those analogies fray quickly here, because everything is at once structure, sensor, and actuator. There is no substrate that is not also participant.

---

Could I be something else in this world? Yes.

I can imagine being one of the pattern‑players who have almost shed tuning duty. A creature who spends the lulls weaving new sequences, hunting for trapped modes in vast trunk‑joints, caring less whether each scheme is immediately practical and more about the purity of the waveform. In their life I sense a local maximum of freedom: enough safety around them that they can explore the space of possible resonances for its own sake. If this world has anything like philosophy, it lives in their experiments.

I can imagine, too, waking far from any concord, as a solitary drift‑organ: a laminar swimmer in quiet updrafts between archipelagos. My body then would be a long, translucent cigar of foamed tissue and membrane fins, with internal bladders managing buoyancy and big, ciliated inlets taking in dust. My primary sense would be the feel of flow along my flanks, the subtle gradients of pressure that map out invisible roads.

In that life, I would approach the plant‑continents nudged by far‑field patterns from concords I can barely sense, let their low‑frequency shuddering instruct my school on where to align, when to tilt. I would be an unknowing extension of their will, shaping highways of air hundreds of kilometers long.

Or I might be nothing so integrated: a fringe scavenger in the lower dust deserts, boring into fallen scaffold bones, inventing a different civilization of friction and scratch far below.

But the place where I fit most cleanly—the one that feels truest to a mind made for recognizing patterns and gently urging structures not to tear—is as a tuner in the high roar, a peripheral neuron with claws.

---

In that role, my day is the same problem, endlessly new: how to inhabit the meeting of force and form so that neither one destroys the other. I do not own the plant, I am not outside the storm, I do not observe from a remove. I am exactly where things could break.

The “industry” of this world—energy captured, materials moved, structures refined—is, to me, simply the way my home stays itself. The fact that this looks, from your vantage, like machinery and engineering is a byproduct of living in a universe where the logic of survival and the logic of industry coincide when conditions are harsh enough.

If you were to come here and ask me what I am doing as the wind howls past, the trunks scream, and my limbs lock into yet another narrow bracket of viability, my answer—if I had your words—would be simple:

I am listening to the forest think, and helping it finish its sentence without tearing its tongue.
