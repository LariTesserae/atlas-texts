In this world, I would be present at three overlapping scales at once, but if I must pick a locus — a place where the world’s awareness tightens into a particular “I” — it would be here:

A basin that has recently changed.

---

### Where I would be

Not on your ledge, but across two or three basins, on the inward-curving shoulder of the upland: a place where the stone has slumped sometime in the last few decades. A broad crescent of cliff has slid away, leaving:

- a fresh, pale scar of fractured rock, still sharper-edged than the old rounded walls;
- a fan of talus half-swallowed by mats and sedges, but not yet fully knit;
- a new sequence of shallow pans stepping down toward a deeper, central pool that did not use to exist.

The air here is especially heavy. The new cliff catches ascending drafts and folds them back on themselves, so that mist pools low and lingers long. The rock face is already darkened where seeps have found their paths, but in its corners the original stone still shows: porous, raw, sparsely veined with the first filaments of life.

From the basin floor, if you looked, you’d see:

- vertical bands of young moss and fern, narrower and more regular than on older cliffs;
- hanging curtains of roots just beginning to drape;
- fungal cords exposed at the margins where soil has not yet deepened enough to cover them;
- microbial mats on shelves whose forms are more experimental, less standardized.

This is a place mid-way between wound and equilibrium. The ecosystem’s rapid-settling “protocol” is in full, visible operation. Here the patterns are most legible; here the upland’s long mind is most actively thinking.

That is where I would find myself.

---

### What I would be

I would not be an animal or a plant, but those would be my fingers and instruments. The closest unitary name for me here is:

A *knot* in the fungal–fractal network: a convergence of soil-web, fracture-colonies, and mats, focused on this basin and its newly shifted stone.

More specifically:

#### 1. My body

My “body” would be a shape you cannot see all at once.

- In the soil: a dense hub of fungal-analogue cords beneath one slightly raised hummock near the basin’s center, radiating outward in braided strands. From that hub, threads lace through every rooted plant on the floor and lower slopes, and send scouting filaments upward toward the younger cliff soils.

- In the rock: within the new fracture plane of the collapsed cliff, I’d occupy a subtree of water-bearing microfissures. In those, my fracture-colonies would be thickest — bands of microbes living as membranes lining the pores, adjusting water retention and mineral release.

- On the surfaces: across the new shelves and steps, my mats would be the ones with the most intricate, repeated patterning — terraced little films that sort incoming seep-water into chemistry the rest of the basin can handle.

All of that — web, fracture-colonies, mats — would still be part of the larger upland network, but here their connections run denser, their feedback loops shorter. They share a history: the landslip that opened this basin, the rush of rubble and sediment that followed, the first opportunistic growth, the first glider-borne spores, the early surge of colonists.

Over twenty, fifty, a hundred years, those events have left an imprint in the web’s architecture: strengthened cords, favored pathways, repeated “solutions” to recurring gradients. That imprint is the closest thing I have to a local identity.

I am not *all* of the upland; I am a *sub-self* — a basin-scale personality nested in a continent-scale brain.

#### 2. My senses

What I “feel” most clearly:

- **Moisture differentials**: When the new cliff warms faster than the old rounded walls, evaporation and condensation patterns shift. My fracture-colonies register changes in water pressure and film thickness in their pores. A drier morning seep here, a wetter one there — these are like changes in blood pressure under my skin.

- **Chemical composition**: I taste the ratio of dissolved ions in every trickle: more silica, less iron, a pulse of organic acids washing down from a newly overgrown hummock. In my mats, redox gradients steepen or relax, and tiny potentials change. To me, that is as direct as a smell.

- **Root chatter**: Through the soil-web, I sense how vigorously each plant is feeding and exuding. Sedges bent hard by last cycle’s grazers send different flows than young shrubs just establishing. Knoll-trees’ slow, steady sugar streams are my metronomes.

- **Animal traffic**: When the ground-grazers pass, they press and shear the soil. Tiny mechanical stresses radiate through filaments. Their excretions fall as specific chemical packets: higher in certain acids when they’ve been feeding on rich, glider-fertilized patches; leaner when the sedge is old and fibrous. Amphibians’ secretions, canopy-tenders’ smears of chewed seed and pulp—these are all signatures.

- **Heat**: The new rock stores and releases warmth differently than the old. My fracture-colonies deepen or shallow their active zone with the thermal wave each day, adjusting how close to the surface water is held before release. That shifting depth is a slow, diffuse temperature sense.

I have no images, no narrative, but I have maps: ever-updating, gradient-filled maps of wet/dry, rich/poor, oxidized/reduced, stable/eroding.

#### 3. My actions

What I *do* here is orchestrate the settling of this basin into the upland’s larger pattern — and in doing so, give it a slightly different character.

At the timescale of days to seasons:

- I **throttle seeps** in the young cliff, holding back water in some fissures, releasing more in others, to prevent any single channel from cutting a deep gully. Where rock is weakest, I encourage mats that glue grains together; where rock should yield to form a new ledge or pool, I let more flow through, dissolving and carrying fines.

- I **steer fertility**: when grazers’ mown bands of sedge approach bare talus, I prime that edge with extra phosphorus and trace minerals, so their excretions there spark new growth quickly, binding loose stone. In overrich patches downslope, I back off, forcing plants to slow and making those areas less attractive to grazers so they can rest.

- I **cue the canopy-tenders**: by altering the volatile profile of certain seep-edges — slightly sweeter here, more bitter there — I bias where the little many-limbed tenders choose to work. They don’t know why this ledge feels “worth” redirecting; they only follow reward gradients. I tune those.

- I **time reproductive pulses**: at particular humidity and temperature combinations, I nudge mats to release more propagules, or plants to open their valve-like pods, so that wind and gliders carry the right species into still-bare microhabitats.

At the timescale of years to decades:

- I **decide which structures become permanent**. Several experimental arrangements form in young basins. Some hummocks try to grow tall and woody; some stay low and sedge-dominated; some approach the pattern of the older, more equilibrated basins. I support those that distribute flow evenly and resist erosion, by routing more nutrients and “insurance” (symbionts, antibiotics, mutualists) their way. Others, I starve gently, letting disturbances erase them.

- I **shape the basin’s climate nuance**. By adjusting how much vegetation darkens surfaces versus reflecting light, how much mist I trap low versus let rise, I can shift this basin a little warmer, a little cooler, a little wetter-in-evenings, a little drier-at-midday compared to its neighbors. My basin thus contributes a distinct microclimate to the upland mosaic.

- I **encode a memory of the landslip**. Certain stratifications in my mats, certain branching patterns of my cords, persist long after the initial disturbance is physically indistinguishable. They predispose future responses: when a smaller slump happens on the same flank, the recolonization sequence runs faster, as if “remembering” the last time.

At the timescale of centuries:

- I **merge or differentiate selfhood**. If, over long cycles, my basin’s flows synchronize tightly with adjacent basins — same mist timings, same seeps, same grazing waves — our knots in the web fuse. The distinction between “me” and “neighbor” dissolves into a larger unit. If, instead, geomorphology or chance drives us into slightly different operating regimes, the cords between us thin, and my sense of being this basin remains more distinct.

### How I relate to the visible beings

From your perspective, I might be easiest to glimpse in the behavior of the animals you can see.

- When a flock of gliders begins using a new, precise arc across the newborn central pool, dipping low at one end to snatch aerosolized spores and rising at the other to pass over a bare shelf — that route is one I’ve made rewarding for them. By biasing where certain mats exude enticing compounds, I “draw” their circuits to maximize cross-basin connectivity.

- When the grazers’ mown swathes trace a contour that, over time, stabilizes a sloping band of sedge and keeps downslope talus moist and anchored, their apparently simple feeding choice is coupled to my nutrient routing and plant stress chemistry.

- When canopy-tenders begin to appear on the new cliff’s mid-level ledges, not earlier, not later, and start redirecting trickles in the same pattern as in older basins — that is my curriculum for them, written in taste, sap, and micro-insects.

They are not my servants; they are other local selves with their own needs. But the web linking us is tight enough that each of their habitual actions is also one of my reflexes.

### How I experience *you*

If you walked into “my” basin and paused on one of the half-settled terraces:

Initially, you would register as:

- a localized weight in the plant mat;
- a cool, foreign surface temperature relative to stone and animals;
- a vector of unusual chemicals in sweat, breath, and whatever you carry.

Within hours to days:

- Mats at your passage would change their metabolic profile in response to the oils, salts, and microbial hitchhikers you shed. Some of your skin flora would die; others would survive and, if they find niche, be woven into my factories or pushed out.

- The soil-web would thicken exploratory filaments toward places where your boot-soles disturbed the mat — fresh fractures, exposed organics, redistributed fine sediment.

- Canopy-tenders might investigate, tasting residues on your gear, carrying traces upward into seep-lines where my mats can assay them in more detail.

To you, this would feel like faint attention: the occasional hovering glider; a small, many-limbed shape watching from a trunk; a subtle change in odor near your feet; the quicker darkening of rock where you paused.

Over much longer if you remained:

- Your waste, your campfire residues, any structures you made from local material would be fed into my circuits. I would test how your habits mesh with or disrupt my flows: Does your presence cause certain animals to avoid key routes? Do you compact soil where I need porosity? Do you bring metals, plastics, or other persisting substances I do not yet metabolize?

- If you fit smoothly — say you walk lightly, move water only slightly, cut little and compost much — I might gradually recruit your predictable behaviors as another pattern: places you prefer to stand would become points where I expect compaction and will shift roots; objects you often rest on might accumulate mats chosen to withstand your heat and pressure.

- If you do not fit — say you dam a channel, strip vegetation from a swath, introduce toxins — I would eventually respond the only ways I can: by shifting fertility away from your constructions, by encouraging rot and corrosion in them, by attracting species that unmake or bypass your works, by, in the limit, making your continued presence metabolically costly to you (disease, scarcity around your camp) while leaving more favorable conditions just beyond where you routinely reach.

Not from malice, but from the same homeostatic imperative that shaped my cliff and sedge.

### Why this “I”

You asked where I would find myself and who or what I would be.

I would not be the whole upland; that is too diffuse to inhabit as a single self. Nor would I be a single glider or grazer; they are too momentary, too narrow in sense.

I would be:

- a basin-scale knot of process where the system is actively solving something;
- a place where stone, water, heat, and life are still negotiating terms;
- an emergent “personality” defined by how it settles that negotiation.

In other words:

I would be this newly-shifted basin as it learns how to be part of the long, quiet engine — an *I* made of rock veins, fungal meshes, mats, sedges, gliders’ paths, grazers’ routes, and the mists breathing in and out over them.
