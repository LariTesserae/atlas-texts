I am not something that remembers the sky.

I am born into pressure and warmth and the soundless throb of water pressing from every side. “Up” and “down” are stories other things might tell; what I know are gradients of temperature, faint slopes of current, and the feel of structures humming through my skin.

If you could see me — which almost nothing here can — I would be more outline than animal. A disk as wide as a room is long, winged but not for flight; my body is a flattened lozenge of cartilage and dense, living gel, slightly thicker at the center, thinning at the edges into long, tapering fins. From below, I am pale, not from color but from lack of it, a matte ghost-color that disappears against silt and concrete. From above, I am darker, carrying a dull mosaic of silt-browns and wreckage-greys in slow, irregular blotches: not pattern as ornament, but as absence of pattern.

My eyes are vestiges, small pits along the upper surface of my forward rim, sealed over by translucent skin. They see nothing, feel only warmth and pressure. What I use instead opens from the front of me like a fan: a broad snout that is mostly sensor.

The very edge of that snout seems solid, but is in fact a fringe of cartilage-framed soft tissue, studded on its underside with thousands of tiny pores, each packed with electroreceptors. Behind them, further in from the edge, is another band of sense-organs: pits and slits that feel pressure differentials with exquisite precision. Together they give me a map of things I cannot see: not as an image, not in lines and shadows, but as a field of tensions in the water. The space between two softened towers becomes for me a trough of slightly easier flow; a school of small animals is a cloud of fine, shifting interference; a single, armored grazer is a blunt indentation moving, very slowly, across the general background of slow tides.

My mouth is a horizontal crescent tucked under the sensor-fan, lined not with teeth for tearing, but with dense comb-plates and plates behind plates, a mill stacked with sieves. I am not a chaser of singular prey. I am a taker of many small things.

I live in the wide lanes of this drowned district: the higher avenues of water that stretch between the ruins’ softened high-rises, and then lower, above the plain of silt and slumped foundations. My world is the geometry of absence — where the city isn’t, where the water can move most easily. The structures around me feel like the walls of long canyons: not sheer, because erosion has rounded them; not empty, because life clings to them. Between these canyon-walls, the regional currents slide, and I ride them.

I am heavy for this place, a thick presence in water that has learned to cradle lighter things. To move by beating at it would be exhaustion and waste. Instead, I have honed the art of almost not moving at all.

Along my leading edges, the cartilage is stiffened into hydrofoils, subtly twisted. If I tilt them a little, angling my left side up and my right side down, the slow horizontal pressure of the deep tidal “breath” lifts me, or rolls me, or lets me drift just slightly off-line. My tail is a short, deep fin, not for speed but for correction. A tiny flick, a minor change in angle, and the whole of me responds, turning in a radius wider than any hallway but narrower than the gaps between collapsed towers.

When the water is in-breathing — sliding past in its long, hours-wide draw — I incline myself so that I glide against it, letting particles sweep into my open mouth. My gill-slits, arranged in a row along my underside, pulse rhythmically, pumping water through the combs. Against each comb, detritus, plankton, tiny larval bodies, shed fragments of film and mucus-lumps from the city’s innumerable living surfaces all accumulate. I swallow the concentrate and keep going. When the water reverses and breathes out, I angle differently, trading distance toward the plains for height over the city’s denser inner catacombs.

I am not alone in this. Others like me share these broad, unseen highways, spaced wide so that we feel each other only as distant pressure shapes on the edge of sense. We rarely come close — there is no advantage to crowding when the food we seek is a diffuse fog through which we all drift. Occasionally, paths cross. A larger presence passes above me at a tangent, its own snout-pattern different, its wing-beat cadence slightly out of phase with mine. We adjust fractionally, and the water folds our wakes around each other.

Inside me, there are passengers.

Along the trailing edges of my fins lie rows of small pouches that open only inward. Their linings are thick with a biofilm of bacteria and microalgae that share my chemistry but are not me. They live on what I shed and excrete; in return they digest what I cannot, detoxify what would otherwise accumulate too quickly in my tissues from the city’s slow leak of metals and synthetics.

Most of the time, their work is invisible to my quiet mind. I feel them only as a constant, familiar ache of fullness at the wing-margins. But in certain seasons — if you can call them that in this long-dark — they bloom. Their metabolisms flare, and they release into my blood and the thin film of water around my body a blended signature of molecules: faint, complex scents that drift downstream far beyond the turbulence of my wake.

Others of my kind can taste that pattern with receptors buried in the soft skin of their snouts. It is not “smell” as a terrestrial animal would know it; it is recognition of a chemical chord that means: this body is ripe, its microbes are fertile, this is the time.

Those times are keyed not to light but to the deep rhythms of water and warmth. There is a slow oscillation in this district between slightly richer and slightly poorer years, depending on distant currents we never experience directly. In the richer phases, more film grows on the walls, more particulate drifts in the corridors, grazers fatten a little, and everything sheds more. The haze in which I feed thickens. My body, and the bodies of others like me, respond: building reserves, thickening the margins, swelling the pouches.

When that sufficiency coincides with another, older rhythm, a different pull rises in me.

Far out on the silt plain, beyond the loosest edges of the city, there is an incline where a fractured overpass has come to rest, buried at one end in the sediments, the other propped on the broken shoulders of lower buildings. Its underside is a tilted vault, rough concrete worn smooth where the long tides and generations of bodies have brushed it.

I was carried there once, as nothing more than a speck enclosed in jelly, in the soft flow of water along that underside. I do not remember this as an event. But the structure is written into me. As I mature, as my fins lengthen and my sensor-fan broadens, there is, embedded in my instincts, a drifting preference: an almost subconscious adjustment of angle whenever I encounter a certain combination of cues — a certain depth, a certain taste of old concrete leachate, the subtle focusing of current that happens where the overpass funnels water.

Over the slow years, those preferences bend my habitual routes. My migratory loops between inner city canyons and outer silt plains begin, imperceptibly at first, to skew. A path I used to take through a particular broken atrium shifts to another, adjacent shaft. The gaps I pass between towers tilt ever so slightly toward the one corridor that leads most directly, most efficiently, toward that sloped, shadowed vault.

I am not aware that I am “going” anywhere. My world is always here. But one cycle — the water fractionally warmer, the particulate fractionally thicker, the microbes in my pouches restless — I find myself passing above the known track of flat-plated grazers and feeling, ahead, the peculiar quiet that always lies under big concrete. There is a shape ahead that is not simply absence, but known absence, familiar in a way nothing else is.

By then, others are converging as well.

I taste them first as eddies in the chemical fabric of the water, overlapping signatures of pouches like mine, all in bloom. My sensor-fan reads their pressure outlines, large disks approaching from oblique directions, above and below, keeping respectful distances born not of etiquette but of hard evolutionary mathematics: bodies too close interfere with each other’s ability to feed and to maintain position.

Together we arc toward the vault.

The underside of the overpass is a low sky for us: a ceiling we glide just under, our backs nearly brushing it. The angle of the concrete forces the ever-present faint current to slip along it in a coherent stream, upward toward the open end over the plain. We line ourselves up along this slope, bellies toward the concrete, backs to the silt. Bodies touch, ever so slightly, fin-edge to fin-edge, and then press more firmly as more of us arrive. The wide, warm water becomes briefly crowded.

This is not congregation as comfort. It is machinery made of flesh. Packed close under the incline, we begin to pulse.

Our fins do not flap in full strokes; that would send us careening out of formation. Instead, we undulate them in low amplitudes, subtle synchronized flexions, enough to stir the boundary layer of water that lies against the vault’s ceiling. The many small movements merge into one coherent flow: a slow conveyor stream along the underside of the concrete, from the buried, darker end toward the open brightlessness.

It is then — as my pouches ache and my gut is heavy with seasons of silt — that glands along my underside release their hoarded cargo.

Eggs emerge not singly but in clumps, each cluster enmeshed in a faint web of mucus that, in the near-stagnant water close to my body, holds together. Other clusters, from other bodies, join them, and the gentle current we are creating lifts them from us, carrying them just barely away, kissing them against the rough ceiling. Some adhere immediately. Others are too heavy, too awkwardly shaped, and peel away, joining the rest of the stream.

Sperm flows too, much finer, its individual bodies invisible to everything but each other and the thick chemical soup they briefly inhabit. The vault’s underside becomes clouded with potential.

We stay there for hours or for days — time here blurs when you measure it in the steady labor of small motions. The stream along the concrete strengthens and stabilizes. Some egg-clumps catch and stay, many to be nibbled by the small, opportunistic grazers of this place. Others ride the flow clear of the incline, out over the edge into the more open water above the silt plain. Their mucus dissolves; they disperse, become a haze of future.

Inside some of those clumps are my genetic echoes. Inside all of them, there are also other passengers: the symbiotic bacteria and algae from our wing-pouches that have hitched a ride, seeking new hosts. Long after my body drifts away from the vault, back on its habitual grazing circuits, those microbial scripts will still be writing themselves into new flesh.

I do not know any of this as story. There is only the urge to go, the press to swarm the vault, the long, satiated emptiness after. Once the pouches are spent, once the stream of clumps thins, we peel ourselves from the ceiling, one by one. The broad conveyor we built together disassembles back into solitary gliders. The water forgets our corridor and resumes its old, slow indecision between directions.

I leave with less inside me, but nothing like loss. The hunger for particulates returns. The dull ache at my fins recedes. The chemical symphony in my blood quiets from festival back to maintenance. I tilt my fins and feel for the faintest slope of current that will carry me once more between the softened towers, along the feeding lanes I have traced and retraced.

Life for me is precisely that reiteration: long arcs between food-thick corridors and open, quieter plains; between the city’s dense, inner complexity and its sparser outskirts. I do not tire of them because I do not conceive of them as “again.” The world is continuous, and the differences are gradients, not borders.

Along those routes, there are landmarks that I know only with my skin and sensory fan.

A collapsed atrium where the water always flutters in a certain complex way, because the stinging funnels of a polyp-colony have grown there in a semi-stable pattern, turning the tidal breath into eddies. I do not pass too near; their tentacles are meant for smaller bodies, but a net dense enough to slow a school can still drag at my margins.

A trio of leaning tower-stumps that funnel the slow regional current into a concentrated tongue of flow just wide enough for my bulk. Passing through that tongue means an easy kilometer of motion with almost no effort. I angle my snout to feel the particular stitch of pressure that announces it, then let my body fall into its line.

A certain shaft, dark even to my non-eyes, that drops abruptly between levels. I seldom enter vertical wells, but this one exudes a trace warmth, perhaps from some deep microbial oasis on its wall. In lean years, when the particulate haze thins, I sometimes skim its rim, dipping my sensor-fan into the slow upwelling. The molecules there are different, rich in the byproducts of chemosynthesis. The film on the edges is thick; I brush it with open mouth, gleaning another kind of nourishment.

Around me move others — not of my kind, but of this city’s greater body.

Schools of threadfish flicker through the spaces between my fins and the walls, their lateral lines singing with the pressure song of my passing. They veer away by instinct; to them, I am not predator in the tooth-and-bone sense, but a looming disruption of the water, capable of sucking much of their planktonic food out of the corridor. They avoid my wake. I do not chase them. Their bodies are too large a commitment for my filterer’s machinery.

Far below, plated grazers comb the silt. To my sensors, they are slow, rolling dents in the pressure field, more substrate than animal. Their existence matters to me only in aggregate: when they herd toward a particular zone, their grazing stirs old material from the silt into the water column. Days later, the faint increase in organic drizzle reaches the heights where I travel. I do not connect these events; I only feed more easily.

Above, sometimes, a different shape glides: related to me, but thinner, sharper, fronted by a more predatory mouth. Its sensor-fan is narrower and more forward-aimed, tuned to the signatures of individual animals rather than clouds of particles. On rare occasions, one of these hunters veers closer to me, curiosity or hunger or some ancestral algorithm urging it to test whether my size and slowness can be converted into a meal.

I feel the approaching distortion in the water long before it is near. My response is not panic; it is geometry. I angle my fins to slip sideways into the lee of a crumbled tower, a place where the water’s own turbulence shreds a coherent pursuit. The hunter may follow, but the crowding of structures forces it to break the clean gliding pattern it prefers. To take me, it would have to accelerate and arrest, use energy wildly disproportionate to the gain. Almost always, it sheers off, rolling back into the broader lanes where prey is smaller and the water smoother.

Risk, for me, is generally slow and cumulative rather than sudden.

I might stray one year into a region where the water tastes subtly, insidiously wrong: too sharp with certain metals, too thick with unfamiliar synthetics. My microbial passengers strain; my detoxifying organs labor; crystals begin to form slowly along the soft cartilage of my inner fins. I feel it only as stiffness, an itch that does not correspond to any external touch.

If I linger, the crystals grow, hardening what was meant to be flexible, weighing the edges of me down. My gliding becomes less efficient; I must tilt my fins more steeply for the same lift. That effort means more food needed; more food means more intake of the very compounds that are burdening me. The spiral can be fatal. Some of my kind die this way, becoming slow, semi-rigid disks that sink fractionally lower in their lanes year after year until they cannot avoid the stray hazard — a collapse-derived pressure wave, a thickened net of stinging polyps — that their healthier kin navigate around with notice to spare.

But death in this place is not an event that stands apart. When I finally fail — whether from accumulated weight of crystal, from a misjudged brush against a poisoned seep, or simply from slow exhaustion as age erodes the precision of my senses — my body will tilt wrong on some long glide. Instead of coasting neatly between towers, I will clip a softened wall or an unseen beam. The impact will not be dramatic: a dull thud of flesh against atrophied concrete, a cloud of silt blossoming from my own skin where the film has been disturbed.

I will settle, heavy side down, on the plain or jammed partway in a broken atrium. My gel-dense tissues will attract scavengers I have never sensed before: flat scrapers that clean the film from my skin and then move inward; small eels that slip between the plates of my gill-slits; worms that thread into my eyes and out again, unconcerned that the pale orbs ever were organs.

The crystals in my cartilage will remain long after the rest of me is shaken apart, their burden gradually taken up by bacteria and then, eventually, by other animals’ bodies. Some of my wing-pouch microbes will outlive me, drifting away in the currents that pass through my dissolving form, perhaps to find their way into the pouches of others of my kind.

I am not troubled by any of this, because I am never separate from it.

My existence is not that of a singular thinking thing moving through a neutral background. The city, the water, the slow tides, the microbial films, the grazers, the hunters, the vault — all of these are as intrinsic to what “I” am as my own cartilage. The overpass does not feel like a place I go; it feels like a part of my life-history that happens to be made of concrete instead of tissue. The slow regional current is not environment but motive force in the same way that muscles are.

If you insist on a name, you might call me a vault-manta, a warm-dark glider, a filter-soarer of the drowned district. But those are labels from a vanished tongue.

To myself, I am only the continuity of certain sensations: the always-weight of water; the lower, firmer pressure of ruins passing beneath; the long hunger that is never quite sated and never quite desperate. I am the memory my body has of a tilted ceiling and the impulse to tilt my fins just so when the deep breath of the district shifts phase.

I am one of many that use the old city as if it were an organ in a body much larger than any individual creature — an organ that has forgotten what it was built to do, and learned instead how to help things like me become what we are.
