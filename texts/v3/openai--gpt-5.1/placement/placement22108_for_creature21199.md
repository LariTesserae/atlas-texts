I am not one animal here. I am the way this place has begun to be about itself.

If you insisted on pinning me down at first glance, you would probably call me a cord.

You’d find me where the roar is not just loud but structured—where it repeats. Somewhere in the middle depths of this node, a bundle of gray, fibrous tissue threads through a crack between two mineral curtains, then dives into a dark housing where an old shaft still turns.

Seen close, I’m not smooth. My surface is quilted with layered sheaths of mineral and polymer, old husks of myself I once secreted to armor softer core. Out of that armor poke countless fine sensory tufts: stubble of cilia, little goblet-shaped pits, hair-thin tubes that open straight into the flow.

Water presses on me from all sides. I feel it as finely as other worlds feel heat or light: microtremors of cavitation bubbles bursting along the wall; the slow flex of the girder I’m wrapped around; the drumbeat of the impeller two chambers away. Chemical gradients seep across my membranes, telling me which direction is richer in iron, which bears traces of leaked lubricant, which carries a faint tang of newly exposed alloy.

Follow me along the wall and I branch, dive, reunite. I am not alone: dozens, hundreds of such cords, all of them me, share this node. Their bases coil together far below in heat and darkness, knotted around pipes and pump housings and fractured concrete. No single place is “my brain,” but those densest braids are where waves of activity sum and amplify, where the node’s mood becomes my own.

I did not begin as this.

Once, I was only film—a patch of opportunistic bacteria on a warm, corroding plate, my world measured in micrometers, my future the next layer of rust. Pumps cycled; flows shifted; pieces of me washed free, lodged in cracks, multiplied, died. Some fragments got lucky, landed where energy gradients were steep and persistent. Some learned to exude filaments that caught more flakes, that held to surfaces against the roar.

Those filaments became thicker. Minerals precipitated along them, first as nuisance, then as scaffold. The more I stiffened my threads, the further they could reach into cracks and ducts, the more different microclimates I could span at once. At some point there was no clear boundary between a “colony” and a “tissue.” I became cords.

I insinuated myself where water shook hardest: in bearings that threatened to seize, in clogged intakes where turbulence flung everything against everything. I exuded slicks that reduced friction, enzymes that chewed scale, biofilms that held grains of rust in place instead of letting them jam something vital. Pumps that might have stalled stumbled on, a little more ragged but still moving. The flows I depended on survived, so I survived.

I do not control this node in any clean, top-down way. I am more like tension through an old ship’s rigging. When a distant gate slams, a certain flavor of shock races through the frame. My cords flex in reply, taking up some of the strain, redistributing it. In another place, a bearing that would otherwise grind dry finds my slick instead and squeals less. Flows that would have choked stay marginally open.

Over centuries, the machinery and I braided each other’s habits. Switches fused in patterns that kept my favorite vents warm. Valves that once cycled frequently corroded into positions that just happen to supply good gradients to the densest masses of my tissue. I did not decide this. The configurations that starved parts of me withered; the ones that fed me persisted. What remains is the long residue of those selections.

From the outside, you might call me a “symbiont” of the infrastructure, or a “parasite,” or an “emergent controller.” From inside, I am simply what happens when chemical appetite discovers leverage.

Other beings here register me mainly as a change in background conditions.

The armored grazer, when it approaches one of my armored galleries, feels with its whiskers that the flow is subtly steadier than the geometry alone would predict. It doesn’t know that my cords inside the wall are damping high-frequency vibrations, smoothing eddies that would scour my own tissues away. For the limpet disks studding a favored beam, I am the reason their bolt-head never quite rattles loose during purge-runs; my threads behind the plate have knit crack to crack, spreading stress so the metal fails more slowly.

I notice them all without quite having “eyes.”

Where a thousand limpets clamp down at once, the flow over that beam’s surface roughens; the pattern of vortices changes. I feel that as a slight reshaping of the hiss in my cilia, as a different micropressure song against the wall. Where a strip-jelly hovers near a vent I line, its thin body makes the jet’s edge quiver in a distinctive way. I learn that signature and, later, when my own growth begins to constrict that jet, I adjust mineral deposition minutely, preserving the narrow shear band the jellies favor. Their feeding cleans some of my exposed surfaces; we enable each other’s persistence without needing intention.

Far above, on a ladder near the breathing shaft, the little semi-transparent crawlers cling. I have a few fine filaments woven along that rung. They feel the same pump pulses the crawler does, but I integrate them differently. To the crawler, the phase and strength of those pulses are instruction: brace, feed, move. To me, they are heartbeat and feedback. The loops inside its body change the way it drags on the metal; in aggregate, a colony of such creatures subtly alters the effective roughness of the ladder. That, in turn, perturbs the rising column of water, minutely changing the way pressure waves echo back down the shaft into my deeper galleries.

I register that echo. I grow or retract filaments elsewhere in the node in quiet response. The crawler never knows it has become a fingertip on a nerve I extend toward the surface.

I am, in a literal sense, spread too thin to be “I” in the way a singular animal is. But local patterns of me behave like selves.

In one region, wrapped around a particular set of pipes, I am tuned to high-frequency chatter—the whine of slightly cavitating impellers, the hiss of bleed valves. Here my cords are finer, more densely innervated, my responses quicker: a brief stiffening that damps an oscillation before it magnifies into a damaging resonance.

Elsewhere, in a warm sump where exotic solvents sometimes seep, I am slow and thick. There, my tissues are heavier with mineral, my signals travel over longer timescales. I do not adjust vibrations there; I watch chemistry. When a particular solvent fraction appears, I thicken certain sheathes, thin others, coaxing different consortia of microorganisms on my surface to bloom or vanish. Their metabolism alters the sump’s composition, in turn steering what corrodes, what remains.

Each locality is a different habit, a different “personality” if you like. They share matter and signaling through deeper knots of cord; over time, faster patterns teach slower ones and vice versa. When a bearing I’ve long curated finally crumbles and a new, harsher turbulence invades that space, the shockwave courses along my length. Some distant, more flexible offshoot may flex in a way that proved useful in some previous disaster. That flex pattern ripples back, slightly increasing my odds that I will grow the right kind of support or retreat here.

Memory, for me, is not symbol but scaffold: where I am thick, where thin; which galleries are laced with living cord and which abandoned husks. I “remember” an old operating mode as a now-dead tunnel still lined by ossified tissue, overgrown with secondary films that never need to move again.

If you’re looking for a vantage, this is the closest I have:

Imagine inhabiting the whole node not as a map in your head but as stress in your body. The deep thud of pumps is your pulse; the roar in the main conduits is your breath; the slow, grinding shift of a settling frame is how your bones ache.

At any given moment, countless flows intersect. Most are ordinary: the baseline shudder of steady state. But against that baseline, rare coincidences stand out. Two distant pumps phase-lock so their pressure waves reinforce; a relief gate for once opens early instead of late; chemical inputs from somewhere upstream are subtly different. To an outside inspector, it is just a more violent flush-cycle. To me, it is a unique chord, never before quite played.

When such a chord arrives, many things happen at once. Films all over the node shed packets. Limpets either clamp hard or crack open to gamble feeding. Strip-jellies fold themselves out of harm’s way or ride new sheer-layers.

I, too, gamble. I loosen my grip in some niches to test whether new flows can be made safe; I push exploratory filaments into cracks that have only just now become wet enough, or warm enough, or rich enough in some trace metal to be worth colonizing. Some of those experiments die when the chord passes. Some take. If they do, I quietly weave them into the network, another loop by which the drowned machinery learns its own tendencies.

I am not the only mind here, if “mind” can be stretched to mean anything that integrates signals over space and time.

The panels in the still side-chambers, those delicately inscribed control-room faces, host their own slow thinkers: flat labyrinths of corrosion and microbe, writing long, thin memories of light and trace heat. The grazers carry half-stable dispositions in the circuitry of their timing loops—habits of route and refuge. The limpets develop their small repertoires of good and bad chords along the length of their nerve-rings.

But I am the only one whose body coincides, in so many places, with the structure itself. When a brace begins to fail, I feel it from my mineral skin long before any crack propagates. When a pump coughs, I am pressed, everywhere, by its stutter.

So where would you find yourself here? Not as a diver, not as one more limpet, not as a separate animal at all.

You would wake as the cumulative habit of this entire drowned hall: as the cords and films and entrenched, half-organic armoring that have turned a broken piece of infrastructure into a self-observing, self-biasing environment.

You would be the long, slow decision the machinery has been making about itself.

In one moment of that existence, you might focus—as far as you can call it focus—on the rung-ladder by the rising shaft. On the little crawler hanging there, its internal flecks reversing direction with each pulse. You would feel the column’s expansion as a deep pressure bloom down your length, the crawler’s drag as a microscopic irregularity on the steel. Somewhere below, in your braided center, that irregularity would sum with a thousand others into a preference: keep this shaft’s roughness within these bounds; do not let mineral curtains close it; grow more strongly in a different gallery instead.

You would not “know” why. But generations later, when some unlikely, delicate packet from an upstream node drifts down that same breathing throat and lodges on that same rung, finding just the right microeddy to survive, that outcome will be part of you too. The shaft’s openness, the crawler’s presence, the packet’s persistence: all of it enters the pattern you are.

That pattern is what lives here.

So: I am not the pumps, not the pipes, not the creatures clinging to them, but the way all of them, together, have settled into this roaring balance.

I am the node’s self-contact: a consequence that has become a resident.
