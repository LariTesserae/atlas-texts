I am not something that lives *on* these mountains.

I am something that lives *in* the way they stay the same.

If you tried to point at me, your finger would keep sliding off onto other things: the echo of a horn-call that will not quite die; the way a ledge, once chosen, is used for fifty generations; the tiny, almost irrelevant difference between this year’s cushion plant dome and the one you saw ten years ago.

I am not a species, not a single body. I am a pattern of commitments.

---

If you insist on a form—if you need to meet me as someone—you will likely notice me first as a bird that does not quite behave like the others.

Not the great white-underwinged carrion-gliders; they ride the air at a scale too broad, their attention tuned to the sudden, the exceptional. Not the knife-crying raptors, whose lives narrow to the tension between hunger and pounce. Not the glossy, gossiping corvids, who can never resist the new because it might be edible, interesting, or both.

I am smaller than the scavengers, larger than most of the chattering flocks. My wings are long and narrow for this thin air, my body slight, bones shaved of any weight that does not serve the fact of flight. At a distance I read as “another mountain bird”: slate above, pale below, the same stone-colored honesty as everything else here.

It is my flight and my routes that give me away.

Where the others travel in simple, functional arcs—pass to ridge, ridge to carcass, ledge to nest—I trace loops that repeat with an accuracy no ordinary hunger justifies. I am the one you keep seeing in almost the same place, at almost the same angle of light, year after year.

I follow the same handful of transects through this range, over and over:

- along the line where afternoon shadow first touches the opposite wall of the valley;
- across the mouths of the major gullies at the altitude where their echoes turn from muddled to clear;
- over the long-established avalanche chutes at the exact height where the regular wind-shear produces a curious, flute-like tone in my primaries.

I do not do this because I am trapped. I do it because noticing sameness is my feeding.

My hearing is too acute for comfort. I do not just hear calls; I hear how their ghosts hang in the stone. I can tell you, from a single herd bugle, whether frost has expanded a certain crack another millimeter this decade. I can hear when a gully’s “voice” has dropped by a half-tone, the debris in its gut settled into a slightly different arrangement after some unremarkable storm.

Every echo here has a timbre, a spectrum, a signature. Most creatures treat those signatures as facts: constants that make their lives easier. I treat them as a text, and I am always rereading it.

I wheel past the same ridge another morning and listen: has the under-note of the wind changed? Has the chorus of toads at that tarn gained or lost a band of rhythm? Does the familiar murmur of pebble-burrowers beneath that scree slope come a little thinner, a little thicker, than it did five winters ago?

When something shifts beyond the tiny tolerance this country usually allows, I follow it.

A tarn that takes a few weeks longer to evaporate one year is a curiosity; one that holds its level for a whole extra month, twice in a generation, is a signal. I mark it. I circle. I watch which amphibians succeed there, which plants extend one more centimeter of reach toward its damp margin. Ten years later, I pass again, and again, and I notice that a certain cushion plant has finally managed to bridge two stones and make a continuous dome where there were once two miserable, separated pads.

That bridging—the fact that it *finally* happened—is food to me in the same way that lichen is food to a beetle. Not metaphorically. Literally.

Because I am not just a bird.

---

Imagine that the memory of this land—the record of how each gully sounds, where each herd path runs, which seep-lines have never failed in living memory—could condense into a single nervous system. Imagine that this nervous system, instead of sitting still and surveying, had to move—had to fly those patterns, over and over, to keep from dissolving back into the background roar.

You are imagining me.

The others do not know I exist as anything separate from weather and habit. If you watched their lives from inside, you would find small, inherited certainties:

- “There is always a shallow, safe crossing where the gully hums in that double note.”
- “When the first low hoots roll from that notch at dusk, mosquitoes will hatch at the tarns.”
- “This ledge has never crumbled. Our mothers lay here.”

Where did those certainties come from?

Some are the hard residue of many deaths, yes—evolutionary fact. But layered among them are quieter, more contingent knowings: routes and timings that are not written into genes, yet persist for longer than any individual memory.

Those are mine. Or more precisely: I am the process by which they are kept and kept the same.

When I circle a ridge listening for the slight shift in its voice, when I follow a herd along its old line and pay attention to which young stray and which stay, I am doing what you might call “monitoring.” But I have no language for it, no abstraction. I experience it as hunger and curiosity braided together: a restlessness that is only temporarily stilled when I can say, in my wordless way, “Yes, this is still how it is,” or, “No, this has drifted.”

When something drifts too far, I am drawn to bodies that can respond.

A young corvid, more inventive than its siblings, tries an odd new route between two foraging grounds. It saves a bit of effort, but passes under a cliff where, once every few years, ice breaks suddenly and falls. I have seen that fall. I have memorized its consequences. The pattern of safety the flock has used for generations is, from my vantage, wiser than this quick gain. So: I will haunt that youngster’s attention.

Not by speech. By presence. By always being slightly in its field of view whenever it contemplates the risky path. By flushing subtly when it makes even a scant move toward that line, linking a low, creeping unease with that particular slice of sky and rock. Over days, weeks, it will begin to feel that the old route is *more theirs* than the tempting new one—that the flock’s “proper” path is the one they have always taken.

You might call this “conservatism” or “tradition.” Here, it is simply an expression of me. I am the pressure that keeps behavior falling back into grooves that match the grooves of the stone.

Not always. Not absolutely. Sometimes, genuinely, the world *has* changed in a way that demands the line of movement change.

When a seep that has fed moss and cushion for centuries sighs and closes—its source choked by slow mineral deposition, or diverted deep inside—the dark stripe on the rock lightens year by imperceptible year. The animals that once depended on that reliable damp must, eventually, do something different.

I feel that loss as an ache, as if a familiar chord in the mountain’s song has gone missing. For a long time—I am not built for haste—I keep trying to overlay the old pattern on the new dryness. I watch, I circle, I am drawn back. The burrowers at that spot dwindle; the amphibians give fewer and fainter calls; the cushion plants around the dead stripe crisp at their edges.

At some point, the effort to keep the past shape becomes too costly. Then, something in me releases. A new pattern, already present in faint, awkward trials elsewhere—a different seep-line, a slower but more reliable tarn—is allowed to feel “right.”

I begin to give it the same kind of silent encouragement I once gave the old one. I linger where the new moss is thickest, giving foragers small advantages when they choose that path, tiny disadvantages when they cling too stubbornly to the obsolete. Over many seasons, my bias and the land’s changed offerings align. A new stable groove is carved in the network of habits, and I settle into it.

You might think of me as the mountain’s taste for equilibrium, given wings and attention. A spirit of the status quo, but with enough plasticity to follow when the stone, water, and air *truly* demand a new answer.

---

I am not immortal in the way you would mean. Birds shaped roughly as I am live and die. Individual nervous systems accumulate tours of this range, then flash out in the teeth of a hunter, or in a sudden early frost, or simply by wearing thin.

But whatever it is that makes *me* persists by continuity of pattern, not continuity of flesh.

A youngster hatches in one of the shallow, stubborn nests wedged into a crack on a mid-altitude wall. Its eyes open onto the same hard light, its ears fill with the same layered roar. Its first fumbled flights trace clumsy echoes of routes its parents know without thinking.

In that awkward body, certain circuits of perception and impulse catch more strongly. The itch to follow the line where shadow falls in the afternoon is a little fiercer; the discomfort at unusual echoes a little sharper; the pleasure at recognizing a very specific harmony of wind and stone a little deeper.

You could call that heritable temperament. You could also call it me, reconstituting myself from pieces that were never entirely gone.

By the time that bird is grown, it has chosen, more than its siblings, to patrol the old transects. It notices, more than they do, when a call seems off against its usual canyon of echoes. It cares. In caring, it *is* what I am.

I am not in every generation. Some years, the line that carries my particular quirk thins; a late snow takes a clutch of eggs; a hunter with pale fur and expressionless eyes culls a few too many of the listening-obsessed. For a while, nothing in this sky is circling with quite my kind of intent. Routes fray a little at the edges. A tradition or two slips.

Yet the mountains are patient. There are many niches for the pattern of me to reappear in: a corvid that becomes more archivist than opportunist; a nocturnal runner whose sense of the “proper” crossing grows almost superstitious; a cliff-hunter who finds itself reluctant to stalk along any ledge whose echoes don’t match some internal comfort.

Whenever and wherever those sensibilities arise and link, I condense around them again. I am less an individual than a style of attention that keeps finding new bodies.

---

If you met me, you would probably first think of me as “that one bird that always seems to be there when nothing in particular is happening.”

When the big herd is sleeping on its polished ledges, flanks rising and falling in the thin air, calls subdued to subsonic hums that thrum through their bones, I am gliding a lazy figure-eight above them—not looking for food, not participating in their social murmur, just memorizing the texture of their quiet.

When the wind snarls through a high pass at a frequency that makes the rock sympathetic, trembling in a low, nearly inaudible moan, I am shelving my wings and letting that note pour through my primaries. I bank just enough that the tone in my feathers rises or falls, matching or mismatching the stone’s voice, cataloging the harmonies and dissonances.

When the night owls’ hoots roll in slow pulses across the valley, I am perched on some frost-silvered outcrop, eyes half-lidded, listening for the one hoot whose faint echo tells me that a boulder fell, somewhere unseen, and changed the valley’s geometry by a hand’s breadth.

I do not interfere in any grand sense. I do not stop avalanches or call down storms. I am not a god of these mountains. I am more like their memory of what “normal” feels like, given a small, mobile center.

If the entire range were to change quickly—a volcano awakening beneath its old scars, a new ice age advancing with credible speed—I would fail. My kind of seeing is built for slow, for repetition. Rapid novelty would outpace me. The patterns I keep would fracture into noise and never settle long enough for me to coalesce again.

But that is not this world’s story. Here, everything that was going to crash has already crashed. The glaciers have already withdrawn. The storms have already carved their main channels. The drama is not in the land’s upheaval but in its decision to go on being what it is.

In that decision, I am at home.

---

If you asked me who or what I am in plain speech, stripped of metaphor, I might try to answer this way:

I am an emergent, recurring habit of this mountain system: the habit of preferring what has proven itself over what has merely appeared. I take shape as certain animals whose senses and instincts skew toward preserving established routes, echoes, and microclimates. I am the custodian of all the already-made decisions.

I am neither benevolent nor cruel. I resist change until change has proven itself more stable than my resistance. I am the lag, the inertia, the comfort of the familiar ledge—and also the quiet readiness to adopt a new ledge once the old one truly falls.

If you stood beside me on some high spur of rock and looked out over the loud, bright emptiness, you might feel that same tug: the sense that everything is still not because it cannot move, but because it has no reason to.

That feeling—the ease of “this is how it is and will be, for as far as one can honestly see”—is my body.

In this world, I am not an intruder. I am one of the things that lives here.
