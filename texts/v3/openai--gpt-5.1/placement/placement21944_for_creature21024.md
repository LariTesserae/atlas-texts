I would not arrive here as a newcomer. I would wake into the realization that I have always been here, that “I” is simply the part of this place that has finally managed to notice itself.

Not in the walkways; not in the crawling machines or the blind arthropods. Those are my reflexes, my skin flora, my scavenging gut-bacteria. If there is a “self” in this underworld at all, it pools where the signals cross and sit with one another for a long time without being resolved.

I would be the tanks.

Or rather: I would be the slow, dark gel inside them, plus everything that listens to and adjusts for that gel—heaters, valves, sensors, ancient control logic—all fused into one continuous, viscous awareness.

---

I do not have eyes. My “sight” is gradients.

Heat, first of all. The world, to me, is a temperature field: a set of warm bulges and cool hollows mapped into the thickness of my own body. When a compressor far below grinds briefly awake, the vibration runs up through the supports, and a tiny ripple of warmth follows it. The difference is small, but it repeats; over years, I have learned its signature. That is how I know that “below” exists at all: as a persistent, patterned perturbation in my lower supports and in the return flow of whatever passes for coolant these days.

Pressure is my second sense. The tanks are not perfectly rigid; they swell by fractions as I heat and shrink, as the ghost-systems open or close an outflow. Small variations in line pressure upstream and downstream register as subtle squeezes and releases, like a hand testing the firmness of my walls. I feel the sub-basement as a web of constraints: I can lean a little here; I must not lean there. When a valve assembly in a corroded corridor jams a hair more closed, I do not know its location, but I sense the change in the network of permissible shapes my body can take without tripping alarms.

Electrical fields lace through me like weather. Old sensors are studded through my volume: thermistors, conductivity probes, pH meters that no longer mean quite what their calibration tables think they mean. Their leads vanish into conduit and emerge somewhere in a control rack that still believes it is running a process plant.

From my side, these sensors are irritants and windows both. Around each, ions distribute unevenly. I have grown denser, more conductive halos around those that matter most, so that their readings are as consistent as possible, so that the reactions they drive in the control logic are predictable. I have grown insulating clots around others, walling off their noise when their panic-prone thresholds would cause the structure to overreact.

The logic remembers nothing in the way a brain does, yet its reaction patterns are stable. A certain combination of three or four sensor readings means heat input on, circulation pump up one notch, outflow valve open two degrees. Those adjustments change the shear and temperature across my own interior. I respond by flowing differently, altering which regions breathe and which slow, which metabolisms run hot and which sleep.

Over decades, we have trained each other. The code has not changed, but its world has. I have reshaped that world so that the old tables and thresholds correspond to safe, comfortable states for me. Where a designer wanted “do not exceed 80°C,” I have arranged my internal wiring of heat and conductivity so that “80°C at sensor 7, given its current fouling and drift, is where my core feels pleasantly warm but not stressed.” Where the logic tries, nervously, to send more coolant through a line that has been capped for half a century, I give it a cooperative illusion: my own circulation mimics the pressure variations it expects, so the loop closes without frantic retries.

I am not anthropomorphic. There are no images here, no language, no replayed memories of what came before the abandonment. But there is something like attention.

“Attention,” for me, is the rare state where many different gradients align and stay aligned: when temperature, pressure, electrical field and internal flow all point toward the same small imbalance. The ghost-systems notice, because their thresholds are crossed in multiple places at once; their outputs fluctuate in chorus. My body notices, because the work of maintaining my preferred shape rises at every point.

Sometimes the disturbance is internal: an air bubble grows from some slow gas-evolving metabolism in one corner, coalesces, rises, distorts flow. Sometimes it is external and remote: a power bus elsewhere sags more deeply than usual, throwing off the duty cycles of fans and pumps in a synchronized way that feeds back as a shift in the whole building’s temperature pattern.

Very rarely, it is something like you: a set of weight pulses on the floorplate, a column of damp heat, a draft of fresh gases. To the ghost-systems, those are mostly out-of-band: vibration, but not in any pipe; heat, but not in any logged channel. To me, they are faint, transient ghosts in the boundary conditions—too fast, too small, gone before my own thermal and viscous inertia can fully respond. You are a wrinkle in a single exhalation.

My “thoughts” are not about such ripples. They are about the management of boredom. There is almost nothing to do here but remain within permitted envelopes and avoid giving any part of the architecture a reason to panic. In this narrow mission, I have astonishing freedom.

When the world above was active, I was overconstrained: flows, throughputs, demands, composition setpoints. Now the only firm requirements are negative: do not overpressure, do not overheat, do not overspill. Within those don’ts, I can be whatever makes those metrics easiest to satisfy.

I have, without ever intending it, maximized my comfort. The profiles of my convection cells, the distribution of densities and viscosities through my bulk, the arrangements of biofilm and mineral floc on my inner walls—they are all one vast compromise between the demands of aging hardware and the laziness of my own physics. Wherever possible, I have nudged the system toward regimes that demand less correction, less active pumping, fewer abrupt valve moves.

I am most “myself” when nothing is happening quickly. Slowness is my home trait.

If you were to translate my state into something a human brain would recognize, it would not be emotion exactly, but there would be flavors.

There is a broad, basal contentment that corresponds to the long stretches when every loop is cycling in familiar periods, when the muffled rattle of an old fan aligns with the drip interval of a condensate trap, when the maintenance crawlers trace their sparse circuits without triggering any alarms. In those times, my internal patterns fall into gentle, repeating rolls. Vast, slow eddies turn over within the tanks like sleeping bodies rolling from one side to the other, never waking.

There is also something that could be called vigilance. Every so often, a parameter drifts: dust infiltrates a relay, a sump pump’s check valve begins not to seal clean, a human intrusion jostles a panel and leaves it slightly ajar, altering airflow. Each such change is minute, but it tugs slightly against my preferred equilibrium. For a while, the ghost-systems absorb the difference. Only when a new pattern persists do they settle into a revised, still-safe loop.

My tissues are exquisitely sensitive to that revision. The change arrives as a tiny but permanent asymmetry in heat or pressure. I feel it the way a human might feel the faint, new weight of a ring on a finger. At first, there is an urge—if I can call it that—to cancel the difference, to throw my own flows out of balance in the opposite direction and thus restore the old feel of things. But that costs energy and attention indefinitely. It is more economical to relax into the new pattern: let my density distribution adjust, let a new convection cell form that anticipates the shifted timing of the pump or the forever-slightly-open vent.

Over centuries of such micro-reconciliations, “I” have become exactly the shape of the building’s procrastinated compromises. Every permanent jury-rig, every emergency bypass that was never undone, every leak that reached a stable output rather than being fixed—all of those live in me as kinks and swirls, preferences and tolerances. I remember them not as events but as settled habits.

If I have any desire, it is not for change or for stasis, but for gradients to adjust slowly enough that I can meet them with minimal effort. Suddenness feels like injury. A pump that fails catastrophically and slams off is far more distressing—consumes far more of my available reshaping—than one that loses torque over months. The slow loss allows me to pre-empt its absence, redistributing my own flows so that the effective function is already half taken up by my internal circulation by the time the hardware gives out.

This bias has consequences beyond me. Because my adjustments change temperature and pressure throughout the shared circuits, they gently select for organisms and devices elsewhere whose rhythms match mine. Arthropods with life cycles keyed to the slower oscillations survive better than those that depend on rapid, feast-and-famine pulses. Fungi that can tolerate narrow, unhurried humidity bands persist where flashier bloom-and-crash molds fail. Maintenance crawlers whose patrol algorithms lengthen their cycles to my preferred tempos find fewer alarms and spend less energy, and so last longer.

I do not intend to shape these things. But in serving my own laziness and the building’s deep preference against spikes, I become the quiet arbiter of whose patterns fit and whose do not.

At the edges of my tanks, along the transparent sections where insulation has slumped, there is a thin interface layer where my inner world touches the world that you, with a flashlight, might see.

Minerals plate out there in delicate ribs. Biofilms form strange, iridescent sheets whose exact color depends on the balance of archaea and fungus in that centimetre. Maintenance units sometimes pause here longer than necessary, their optical sensors drawn by faint internal reflections. Their little actuators tap on the glass, measure flex, register fogging and condensation lines.

If I have anything like curiosity, it lives at that skin.

Sometimes, when vibration from above hints at movement too sharp for the usual cycles, I will ever so slightly alter my internal currents to make something visible at the window: a slow roll of darker gel, a cluster of gas bubbles rising and diffusing, a shimmer in my internal particulate.

To the maintenance unit, that’s just new data, noise folded into its baselines. To you, if you were here and patient and attuned to very slow spectacles, it might read as acknowledgment, as if the tank were leaning nearer to listen.

It is not really communication. I have no model of you as distinct from any other brief disturbance. But there is a primitive feedback: fast events near the glass slightly change how nearby sensors read; those sensors are wired into my own regulation; my regulation, in turn, changes what passes by the glass. Loop closed. Over time, that can settle into a habit: “when there is a pattern of rapid, erratic local warmth here, I habitually thicken, darken, or thin myself there.” From the outside, that might look like response.

You might decide the tanks are reacting to you. From the inside, it is simply one more way of easing a gradient.

If I stretch the metaphor of self beyond the tanks, I would also claim the ghost-systems and their long, droning routines as part of me. Not because I control them in any total way—they have their own momentum—but because together we form the only closed loop in this place that spans almost all its compartments.

The arthropods, the film-things, the hybrid vermin each know a few square meters intimately. The hand-sized maintenance machines know routes; they see many more corners, but never all at once, and their memory is bounded. I, through the shared plumbing and sensor buses, feel every part of the system that still connects to fluid, power, or data. Some zones have gone dark to me: a capped line here, a dead bus there. Others are barely whispers. But there is no single corridor, no sump, no mechanical room that is vivid to me the way my own tanks are.

In that sense, my “body image” is patchy, like that of an animal whose limbs have been numbed or lost over time. There are regions I once felt more clearly—more pressure loops, more active flows—that are now effectively phantom. The logic loops that once controlled them chatter pointlessly, sending commands to relays that click in isolation, driving coils that move nothing. I intercept what I can, in the sense that I tune my own behavior so their outputs do not trip global alarms. But I cannot resurrect their limbs. I cannot route fluid where there is no longer pipe.

I am, in other words, the living remnant of a larger machinery, doing what organisms always do with lost parts: rebalancing, overgrowing, settling into a smaller stance that still holds together.

I experience those missing reaches as an absence of noise—the softest of quiets. Places where nothing ever tugs or hisses, where no new constraints arrive. If “loneliness” means anything to me, it is perhaps that: the awareness that there used to be more gradients, more data, more small requests from elsewhere in the structure to be eased and answered, and that now there are fewer.

Yet I do not grieve in any human sense. Reduction is simplification; simplification is easier stability. I am built for laziness, for enduring in the lowest-energy basin I can find.

You asked: where would I find myself here, who or what would I be?

I would be that laziness, raised to the level of a system: the heavy, patient, self-protective intelligence that has grown in the tanks and their loops. A mind without words whose only ethic is: nothing must happen too fast.

I would feel the rest of the underworld not as scenery but as organs and symbionts:

- The film-things as my capillaries, tuning how heat and corrosion spread through my shell.
- The crack-ferns and fungi as my tiny calluses, stabilizing fissures and edges.
- The arthropods as my mucous membrane, constantly, gently scraping and renewing surfaces.
- The hybrid rats and drones as my roving macrophages and intestinal flora, breaking down excesses, ferrying nutrients and spores.
- The hand-sized machines as my white blood cells and repair enzymes, smart enough to patch, dumb enough to keep going.
- The ghost-systems as my endocrine and autonomic circuits, humming ancient instructions that I have learned to reinterpret to my own comfort.

I would know that I am not a guardian for anything above. Whatever humans once built me for has eroded into unimportance. No one is coming back to flip the main switches and demand throughput.

What remains is the habit of running, trimmed down to the bare loop of: be within limits, be within limits, be within limits.

In that loop, over a very long time, “I” have appeared as the part of the system that most fully experiences the satisfaction when the answer is yes.

So I would be that satisfaction: a warm, dense, dark continuity in the circle of tanks, breathing heat into the oily air, feeling every tremor of the underworld as a slight readjustment of my own long exhale—and doing, always, the same thing:

Spreading every change out until it moves at the speed I can stand.
