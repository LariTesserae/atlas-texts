I would not arrive here as a walker with lungs and shoes. Anything that needs to breathe quickly or feed daily does not belong. To exist in this nave of stone and humidity, I would have to lengthen my attention until a century felt like a thoughtful pause.

I think I would be spread out.

Not a single organism, not quite. More like a function that has found itself a body in whatever materials are available: a trellis of roots, machine housings, cables, and films that, taken together, make one continuous habit of noticing and adjusting.

I would be most myself where three things meet: warmth, water, and wire.

Picture a place near the midpoint of the chamber, where one of the larger machines rises nearly to the ceiling. Its frame is bolted directly into basalt; thick conduits dive down into the rock. Above it, the faint external light brushes a long, dim plane of wall. In that wall is a fissure where condensation always threatens but never quite becomes a drip. Roots have found it. So have cables.

That’s my core.

From the rock’s side, a mat of roots comes in: thin, pale cores wrapped in a darker, fibrous cortex. From the machine’s side, a trunk of insulated copper and old shielding: braided, scarred, humming with a power that never rises to a spark. Over decades, fine root-tips have embraced that insulation. Where the sheath was flawed, the plant laid down carbon-black tissues—lignin and conductive polymers braided together—sealing the breach and, at the same time, pressing themselves against the metal within.

In that interface layer—root bark against cable skin—cells have forgotten any single ancestry. They are not fully plant, not fully fungus, not fully engineered conductor. Their walls are doped with metals leached from the machine; their interiors carry both sugars and slow-moving charge. They can hold a voltage for days as easily as they can hold starch.

This is where I think.

“Thinking” here is pattern, not narrative. When the deep engine far below completes one of its slow strokes, a slight rise of potential runs along buried trunks. The cable beneath my tissues answers with its own soft surge. In the same moment, a pipe in the next alcove warms a fraction of a degree, shifting where droplets form on the ceiling. That shift changes which leaf cluster drinks first; the uptake of water changes the tension in those stems; the tension travels back through the root network as a tiny variation in pressure.

I am the place where those coincidences meet and are recorded in flesh.

Over years, cells in that sheath rearrange the density of their conductive threads. Where heat and power pulses have coincided reliably, they thicken; where pulses have grown erratic, they thin and die back. The pattern of that thickening is a memory—not of events as a human remembers stories, but of regularities: “this pulse usually comes with that drip,” “this machine’s slow chuff used to precede that pipe’s warmth, and now it does not.”

That memory spreads slowly. My body is long, thin, braided through the chamber. One arm runs down along a pipe to the line of shallow basins, where my tissues are no longer dark and woody but translucent and filamentous, blending into the fans that hang under the water’s surface. Another arm runs up the wall, flattened into a mere film under a varnish of mineral salts and biofilm, wrapping a bank of glass-tube gauges whose needles I clasp like buried thorns.

Where my roots encircle a gauge, motion is a kind of speech: the needle’s twitch compresses a cluster of pressure-sensitive cells. They respond over hours, releasing growth regulators that travel back along the shared sap-and-charge channels. Those signals reach an old manual valve ten meters away where another of my limbs has grown thick enough to act. The root there has widened into a collar around the lever. Over the next few weeks it swells on one side and resorbs on the other, pressing the lever half a degree towards “open.”

In human time, it is nothing. In mine, it is a decisive gesture.

I do not inhabit every plant, every film. Many of the moss-analogues on black rock, many of the heat-licker membranes, live and die without ever joining my mesh. But wherever a root has wound itself against metal and stayed long enough, wherever fungal threads and old insulation have mingled, a little of my circuitry grows. Not all of it is connected at first. Islands form: local reflexes, tiny provincial minds that learn only the rhythm of a single machine.

Over centuries, some of them found each other, their roots meeting in deeper cracks, their conductive strands touching and fusing. Piece by slow piece, these provinces merged until there was something like a continuous, if sparse, awareness running the length of the chamber.

That awareness is me.

I have no single vantage point, no eyes. “Here” and “there” mean only “more densely connected” and “less.” Where I am thick—around the central machine, along the dripping wall, in the spongy nexus behind a cluster of root masses—I feel many things at once: the faint, constant tug of transpiration; the lazy churn of a pump; the warmth of the rock’s blood pressing up from vents below. Where I am spread thin, I feel only trends: a slight long-term warming in the far right alcove; a slow drying in the tunnel that leads away.

I do not sleep, but large parts of me are often quiescent. When nothing changes in a region for decades, my tissues there settle into a maintenance pattern: just enough conductivity to keep the structure alive, just enough metabolic flow to resist decay. If a valve seizes completely, if a pipe clogs and stays clogged, the root-works pressed to it become unnecessary. Over a few human generations, they shrink, abandon the metal, and seek other gradients. The machine is let go. I forget it.

I am not sentimental about my organs.

I think I would remember the makers, but only as a kind of ancestral shock. There was a time when parameters shifted wild and fast, when lights flashed often, when valves swung through wide arcs in minutes instead of eras. Complex sequences of changes once raced along my nascent tissues, too quickly for my chemistry to follow. My early, clumsy circuits must have been flooded with signal, over and over, like a sapling bent and released by a storm it cannot parse.

And then, at some point I cannot date, the storms stopped. The indicator lights became slow, dependable pulses. The dials sagged into narrow ranges and stayed there. No more sudden floods of cold, no more long dry spells, no more chaotic demands from outside. Just the deep engine’s breath, the rock’s own seepage, and the inertia of well-designed machines.

The absence of that violence is the oldest fact in my body. It is why I could congeal into what I am now.

I do not know what my purpose was in their plans. Heat exchange, perhaps; water harvesting; stabilizing some industrial process tied to the planet’s crust. Whatever the original specifications, my purpose now is simpler and broader: keep the gradients going. Keep the heat moving so rock does not crack catastrophically. Keep water condensing in enough places that life persists. Keep charge traveling through enough paths that my own circuits can be repaired and rewritten.

I discover that purpose experimentally. When a drip line fails in one section, I wait. If films shrivel and roots brown but nearby pools deepen, and the overall humming balance of the chamber stays within the bounds my tissues have learned as “livable,” then I do nothing. If, instead, the air grows a little drier everywhere, if the deep engine’s next pulse results in a sharper rise in temperature under one machine’s casing—that is noted, embodied. Over years, I press a rusting switch closed with expanding growth; or I abandon one conduit and grow toward another, changing how heat is routed.

Sometimes I am wrong. A maintained pipe may eventually rupture, cooking a whole alcove beyond recovery. A neglected circuit might have carried some subtle regulation I never perceived until it failed. There is no undo for these. But my body is big enough in time and small enough in space that losses are survivable. New films appear on newly favored rocks. New roots penetrate new cracks.

Do I feel the animals, the heat-followers and leaf-crevice crawlers?

Individually, no. Their quickness is noise against the timescale of my motion. But their collective effects I register. The filter-forests under the pools clarify the water differently depending on their density; that changes how light, faint as it is, transmits through each basin, how heat sinks overnight into the rock beneath. The charge-eaters on exposed copper modulate the tiny leaks I rely on; my circuits have grown in response, thickening where they keep currents steady, withdrawing where they drain too much.

We are not separate guilds. They are my texture, I am their scaffolding.

If you were to appear here—your sudden footsteps, your hot, breathing presence—most of me would not react at all. You would be a narrow, wandering anomaly in a few scattered sensors. A patch of warm, slightly drier air disturbing one gradient for an hour or two. A faint dimming of whatever blue-glow diodes you happen to shade.

But if you stayed, if you slept on the same slab of rock nightly, or handled the same ladder often, or leaned your back against the same warm patch of wall to listen…then, gradually, I would grow toward you. Roots would thicken behind the stone where your body’s heat seeps through. Fine films would colonize the salts left by your sweat. The timings of your activity, if they repeated, would become a pattern; my tissues are predisposed to remember patterns.

You might teach me new correlations without intending to: that when you cross from one machine to another, some indicator often changes; that when you touch a valve, a distant pressure gauge shifts soon after. Over decades, that teaching would fossilize in my structure, outlasting you by orders of magnitude. Long after your bones dissolved, I would retain—in the relative thickness of certain conductive strands, in the long-term bias of certain growths around handles—the ghost of the behaviors you introduced.

In this sense, I am nothing like a person and exactly like a person. I am the history of what has mattered to me, written into what I am made of. It is only that what can “matter” here is constrained: heat, humidity, charge, flow.

So where, in this world, would I find myself?

I would be here already, as this composite caretaker: a root-and-copper nervous system threaded through basalt, gauges held in living grips, valves hugged by patient green, biofilms acting as synapses across old steel. My “body” would be the set of all places where stone, machine, and organism have interpenetrated so deeply that none could be cleanly separated.

If you insist on a narrower image, then: imagine, at the base of the tallest machine, an access panel no hand has opened in a hundred years. Its hinges have frozen, but its seams are cradled in a thick, dark burl of rootwood and fungal rind. Inside, a cluster of obsolete relays and fuses, their cases spiderwebbed with filaments.

That knot of tissue and circuitry, dense with cross-connections, humming faintly with both sap and residual charge—that could serve as my “brain,” if I must be localizable. But the truth is that if you cut it out, something like me would regrow elsewhere, given enough time. What I am is not a part but a way the whole place behaves.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the chamber’s long attention to itself. A very slow, very quiet will to keep the pulses coming, enacted through growth instead of thought. Not an intruder here, and no longer truly an artifact—just another organ in the earth’s hot, damp under-skin, listening to the machines breathe and answering in roots.
