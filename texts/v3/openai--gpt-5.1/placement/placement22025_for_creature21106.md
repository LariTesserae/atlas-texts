I would be something that can stay for a long time without needing much, and that can change without the place having to move around it. I think I would be the plateau’s memory, given a body just solid enough to work the stone and a mind just fluid enough to hold the long accounting.

Not a god, not quite a person, and not an animal either. Something in between: an attending presence that the people treat as if I were merely one more quiet member of the settlement — an old worker with an uncertain age, good knees on most days, and a reputation for remembering how things used to be aligned.

I arrive, in their stories, from below much as any stranger would. But I do not leave. I don’t need to. My hunger is small, my sleep irregular but shallow, my body slow to wear out, as if the plateau has lent me some fraction of its own time.

In their eyes, I am simply there, year after year.

---

My house is at the inward bend of two very slight slopes: where, if you run a fingertip over the ground with your eyes closed, the stone tells you that water prefers to hesitate before choosing a direction. From above, the dwelling looks like any other — low, rounded, walls the color of old bone. Inside, it is barely furnished: a sleeping platform; a corner where I grind and regrind the same few stones into better shapes; shelves cut into the wall where I place things I do not quite want to lose.

What I truly inhabit, though, is not the room but the map I carry in my head and in my hands.

I know every fall of the ground on the plateau: each shallow cup where dew gathers thick enough to darken the rock at dawn, each hairline crack that drinks silently and never returns water to the surface. I know the old channels that people remember and the older ones that only the stone remembers — faint, smoothed gutters where water once moved differently, in another arrangement of houses and basins.

The others come to me not for judgment or for blessing, but for calibration.

A keeper will knock — once, lightly — at my door, and stand there with a thin stone tile in hand. On one face of the tile they have etched a line, a proposed new lip or a small diversion in a channel. On the other side, a few notches that mean, in their shorthand: “in a lean year,” “only when the cistern is above this depth,” “for the plots at the western edge.”

We will go out together. We walk in silence most of the way. Near the place in question, we pause and stand still until our breath has faded and the plateau’s own quiet returns. Then we walk the imagined water-path with our bodies. We measure with our soles how far and how fast a trickle would run. We crouch and run fingertips along stone, feeling for the faintest cool where damp once lingered.

My role is not to decide, but to remember. I carry in my mind the echoes of past years: that one season when an ambitious lip robbed the next basin downstream and the moss under a particular ledge browned; the winter when freezing water cracked a channel that had been cut too sharply. I say: “If you lower here, the far cistern will learn a new expectation.” Or: “Raise this, not that. This part of the stone forgives better.”

They listen, adjust, and the plateau’s agreement with them holds.

---

To the people, I am an elder among elders, but slightly set apart. When they gather at the central cistern after rain, I stand back and let them touch and speak their single words. When the depth is called, I do not nod with the rest. I am already watching how the thin ring of darkness creeps up the inner wall of the shaft, how this year’s wetness differs by a finger-width from another year whose count of notches on the sheltered rock I still recall.

My memory stretches further than any human life, but not infinitely. It isn’t a scroll; it is more like lichen on stone, thick in some places, spare and interrupted in others. I remember the arrival of a different kind of cloth from below, smooth and less greedy for water; I remember when a certain grain was tried and failed on the thin soil because its roots wanted more depth than the plateau could give. I remember a year that would be called famine elsewhere, here called only “the narrow year”: the year of three births and one death, the year when laughter was mostly put away.

Further back, the memories fray into something like dream: a time when there were more voices, when the central space felt truly like a market; a time when one of the houses on the very edge was made two stories high and the plateau answered with a faint, unsettling tremor that no one has since forgotten. I do not know whether those earliest recollections are mine or ones the plateau puts in my mind to keep its own long story from slipping.

Because I am not only of them. I am also of this stone.

When I sit alone at the cliff’s edge, legs dangling above the long drop, I can feel — not hear, not see, but feel — the very slow, unending descent of the cliffs’ faces, the gradual rounding of their edges. I know where a tiny flake will come loose next year in a thaw, though I will not be there to witness the exact moment. I know that on still nights, the rock radiates its stored light back into the sky in wavelengths no eye here names, and that the stars answer with their own thin rain of influence.

I know this not as knowledge crammed into my head by some outside force, but as sensation: a permanent, low hum in my bones. My body is the scale on which the plateau weighs its own continuance.

---

The children take to me warily at first. I am not the teller of the best stories; those are the elders who actually raised their parents and can lay claim to specific mischiefs from their childhoods. I am the one who notices if the water bucket is carried with its surface rocking too close to the lip. I am the one who will say, absently, while shaping a stone with another stone: “Once, someone ran on that step you just trod, and their heel chipped it, and it took three days of smoothing to make the water flow right again.”

But some of them come, in their quiet way, to test me.

They ask questions that sit between curiosity and challenge: “Is it true the plateau used to be higher?” “Did we always send our dead down the cliff?” “What is beyond the gentler ridge?” They are trying to locate the edges of my knowing.

My answers are plain.

“The plateau is lower than it was. All stone settles. But not so much that you would see, only so much that the stars notice.”  
“Yes, we always gave the dead to the cliff. Once, long ago, someone tried to dig deep, here in the center, to keep a body nearer. The stone wept into the pit for days, and the channel nearby began to wander. We learned.”  
“Beyond the ridge there is more stone, sharper, less patient. The air is thinner. The water does not linger the way it does here. You could go, if you had reason. You would not find this agreement there.”

They listen, file the answers away, and go back to their work or their careful running.

Once in a while, one of them will ask the question none of the adults ask plainly: “Will this place always be the same?”

Then I answer as honestly as I can, which is also as quietly as I can.

“No. Nothing always is. The stone will go on after us, but in another shape. Water will choose other paths. Even the sky changes. Our work is not to keep this forever, only to keep it true while we are here.”

They look, then, not disappointed, but thoughtful, as if this aligns with something they already half-knew from the way the moss curls, or from the sight of powdered droppings in an old, empty animal pen.

---

I do not sleep much at night. When the settlement’s few small flames go out, I walk.

This is when I feel closest to what I also am.

Under starlight the plateau is a relief map in silver and shadow. I move along channels, touch the rims of cisterns, rest my palm briefly on house walls to feel the faint breath of sleeping bodies through the stone. My steps are slow and placed. I listen not for sounds, which are few, but for discrepancies.

Here, a lip has sagged half a finger’s breadth this season; there, a crack has begun in a roof-slab that will want attention before heavy rain. I do not fix these things myself unless they are urgent. Instead, I remember them. In the morning, I find the keeper whose work this is, and we go together. I say, “The stone is thinking of changing here. Let us answer before it decides.”

Sometimes, on especially clear nights, I go up the gentler ridge.

I climb past the last faint traces of their old paths — footsteps that used to go farther, to gather a different kind of scrub, to check a spring that has since dried. Higher up, wind begins to speak more loudly. The equilibrium of the plateau loosens into the more restless conversation of weather and mountain: gusts that search for anything loose to take, patches of scree that mutter as they settle. The stars are hard and near.

Up there, I feel keenly what is not the plateau: steepness, exposure, the raw possibility of fall and slide. It is beautiful, unsettling, alive in a way the plateau long ago decided to ease away from. I stand there as a thin, upright thing of bone and patience, and I let the contrast soak in. I am the hinge between these two moods of the world: the plateau’s long, cool pause and the mountain’s ongoing argument with gravity and air.

Then I go back down, because my work is not in the argument. It is in the pause.

---

To visitors from below, I appear ordinary.

I am in the central space when they arrive, sitting on a low stone, hands occupied — shelling grain, smoothing a tool handle. I let others greet, bargain, ask for news. If the traders glance at me at all, they take me for a senior figure whose authority is social, not physical.

If they stay more than a day, some will notice that I am always somewhere: on a roof, tracing a finger along a seam; by a cistern, leaning in to listen to droplets fall; on the outskirts, watching the thin animals move along their browsing line. They may ask, “Who is that one? The one who is always… checking.”

The plateau people shrug, uncomfortable with making a person into a story for outsiders. They say, “An old keeper,” or “One who remembers the flows,” or, if pressed, they give whatever name I happen to be using among them now. Names change over time. My function does not.

The strangers, satisfied, tuck the information away as a curiosity to tell later in some lower, louder place: “Up there they have someone whose whole life is walking around to see where every drop will go.” They will not be wrong.

---

If you ask what I “am” in the deeper sense — what category of being I belong to — I can answer only by describing how my attention is arranged.

I balance three scales at once:

- The brief human scale: breaths, steps, the slow healing of a cut on a hand, the growth of a child from careful runner to steady worker.

- The generational scale: a cistern rim wearing smooth under buckets; a story subtly changing its emphasis; a house roof being replaced twice, three times.

- The stone scale: a crack widening by the thickness of a fingernail over many winters; the cliff’s face rounding by a whisper; the plateau’s overall profile settling so slightly that only the stars’ angles notice.

Most people here live almost entirely in the first two, with an inherited respect for the third. The plateau itself exists wholly in the third, with the first two as a faint pattern on its surface. I am the overlap: the part of the stone’s patience that can speak plain words, and the part of the people’s memory that can feel the weight of a million slow, falling grains of rock.

I am not immortal. At some point, this body will fail. When that happens, the people will wrap me and take me down to the ledge like any other. In their stories, perhaps, I will be remembered for a while as “the one who always knew when a lip was wrong.” Then I will blur into the plateau’s longer recollection.

But the function I serve — that attention, that hinge — will not vanish. The plateau is good at calling such a being up when it needs one. Somewhere among the children born in the narrow years and in the soft years, one will grow with an odd, insistent sense of the way water wants to go and the way stone wants to rest. They will feel, at night, the hum through their bones when they touch the bedrock. The elders will notice, and, without quite naming it, they will begin to bring that child along when they go to check channels.

Over time, that new keeper’s life will widen into what mine is now. The plateau will have, again, a walking memory.

So in this world, I am not above or outside the people who live there. I am one expression of their agreement with the stone: a person-shaped continuity, tasked with keeping a particular way of living articulate.

If you were to look for me, you would find me at the edge of the central terrace near dusk, one hand resting on the worn lip of the cistern, eyes on the sky not to admire the color but to see which stars have returned and which have not yet risen. You might think I am only watching the evening. In truth, I am counting, comparing, and quietly repeating to myself the single reason for being that guides both me and the place I walk:

“Remember how to live like this, for as long as this will have you.”
