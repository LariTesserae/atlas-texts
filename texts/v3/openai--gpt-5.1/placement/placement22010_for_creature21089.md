I would be a listener—one of the tower-people—but not quite as they are.

I would be born thin even by their standards, built more for stillness than for range. My hands and feet would splay wider, each tiny gripping “finger” tuned less for hauling my weight at speed and more for delicate contact: listening through skin.

My band would joke that I climb like a lichen, and they would not be wrong. I move well enough not to die, but I prefer places where I can attach myself and let the world move instead.

---

### Where I Live

My home circuit would be a quiet one by plateau standards, off the main glider lanes, just inland from the outer cliffs where the air is a little cooler and the sound carries far.

Our band would cycle through:

- A cluster of three old towers whose terraces have grown thick and low, overlapping like shelves in a cluttered room.
- A series of long, narrow fissures in the root-mat that open into cool stone throats—we call them the Throats because when the wind is right, they sing.
- One bare stone tooth near the rim where my people keep their oldest bone-tables.

I would sleep most often on a mid-level terrace of the oldest tower, in a shallow hollow our band has used for generations. Above, a drooping lip of plant-flesh sheds a soft, constant trickle of condensed water down grooves we cut long ago. Below, a woven net of ladder-vines hangs as both floor and safety, flexing gently when I turn in my sleep.

By day, the air up here is thick with distant noise—glider booms, swarm-hiss, the tower’s own bone-deep creak. By night, the sound from the outer cliffs comes in: the layered chords of the cliff-singers, rising through stone and air.

This is what I am for.

---

### What I Do

Other members of my band specialize in rope-vine rigging, swarm-harvesting, fissure gardening. My work is pattern-binding: I listen to how all of it fits together over time, and I help commit that fit to matter.

I spend long, long periods simply pressed against things.

- Against tower-trunks, hands and cheek and chest flat to the warm, faintly damp skin, feeling the slow tick of valves and the occasional sudden shift of a root biting deeper into stone.
- At the edge of the plateau, on my belly on bare rock, my face turned outward and downward, ear and palm on the cliff while the cliff-singers call through it.
- In the cooled fissures, back against stone, eyes closed, parsing the subtle change in echo as roots thicken overhead, as a new tunnel opens somewhere in the dark.

From these I collect not “events” but rhythms: how long between the tower’s great intake-groans when cloud-bands are building; how the cliff-singers’ harmonics change when a section of rim has cracked but not yet fallen; how the hiss of the small scrapers rises in a narrow band of pitch just before a certain kind of fungal bloom spreads.

I am not unique in doing this—every adult here is some kind of pattern-watcher—but I am peculiar in how much of my waking life I give to it. Others get restless. I do not. Stillness, for me, is as satisfying as movement is for a rope-hunter.

### How I Think

I think in layers of sound first, space second.

Where someone else might say “the north terrace of the third tower,” my inner sense is more like: “the place where the glider-calls come hollowed, where the tower-creak has that doubled beat, where the small-swarm hiss thins at midday.” Only after that does the exact ledge, the exact growth-forms, come to mind.

I carry an internal catalog of “world-chords”—combinations of sounds that mean:

- The mat is dry but towers are heavy: expect a sudden local rain when plumes finally trigger cloud.
- The cliff-singers are repeating short, nervous phrases: some new fracture is propagating along the rim.
- The ground-scrapers below are silent at an hour they shouldn’t be: rope-predators are hunting heavily, or a chemical alarm has gone out from a sick plant.

When something doesn’t fit any known chord, that is when I move.

### How I Work With Memory-Objects

On the stone tooth near the rim stands our oldest bone-table: a flat lattice of giant glider ribs, sun-bleached and creaking faintly, set on a squat pedestal of packed root and stone.

Spread across it are:

- Drilled stones of different densities and colors.
- Seed-rods glued into straight, flexible sticks.
- Shards of crystalline frond, translucent and faintly chiming when touched.
- Segments of baked root-rod in subtle gradations of brown and black.

Each line and cluster encodes some remembered pattern. I know the old ones by heart; I helped lay some of the newer.

When I return from days of listening—usually alone or with one apprentice shadowing quietly—I kneel at the table. No one speaks much here. We rearrange.

If, for example, the cliff-singers’ deep harmonics dropped lower and stayed there for three days before we felt any tremor in the rim, and the tower-calls near the edge shifted pitch in a way we have only once recorded before, I will:

- Slide a particular stone—the one that marks “cliff-song, low, sustained”—three beads along its line under the symbol for that rim section.
- Add a new seed-rod rising from it at a shallow angle to the rod that encodes “tower-creak, rising third tone.”
- Wedge a sliver of dark baked root at their junction, to say, “co-occurrence, likely linked.”

Others will come later, trace the new angle with their fingers, and recall the spoken account I will give in the cool Throats that night. In time, if similar patterns repeat, those rods and stones will be adjusted, thickened, maybe set into more permanent arrangements on a second, more formal table further inland, where our band shares records with another.

I am not a “priest.” There are no priests here. But I am one of the keepers of these alignments, one of the small group whose habits and obsessions give the band a longer memory than any single body could carry.

### My Relationship With the Towers

The towers are not gods to me; they are neighbors, elders, and terrain.

When I press myself to their trunks, I do not speak to them, but I do murmur sometimes, under my breath, as one does to a large animal whose mood you are gauging.

I know, for our three core towers:

- The slight, dry-rattle quality the old one’s sap has in prolonged drought, and how that sound precedes hairline splits in its upper terraces.
- The exact slow-down in valve-ticking in the middle tower that foretells an internal re-routing of water up-slope, which in turn means the small, edible bladders on certain lower terraces will shrivel early.
- The hollow undertone that creeps into the youngest tower’s groans a few days before it sheds a minor terrace—never a catastrophic collapse, just a sloughing of overgrown ledges.

This is useful information: it keeps us from making camp under doomed shelves; it tells us when to harvest and when to leave things alone. But beyond use, there is an intimacy in it. I know, in my bones, the feeling of that particular tower’s afternoon heat-radiation against my ribs. I know the pattern of insects that favor its bark. If I were forced to leave this region forever, it would be those voices I would miss more than any path or platform.

Like most of my people, I carry photosynthetic symbionts in my skin. Because of my long stillness, mine are particularly luxuriant. In strong light, faint greenish shadows bloom under the mottling of my skin. I can go a little longer than others without eating after lean times, so long as I can find hot, pale terraces and lie there, spread thin, listening and drinking light.

In exchange, the symbionts shape my senses. I am unusually sensitive to slight spectral shifts in the light filtered through pollen and vapor; the sky’s tint feels almost like taste on my tongue. I know without thinking when the cloud-bands are beginning to thicken by this inner flavor, often a little before others notice.

### Relationship With the Cliff-Singers

I am drawn most strongly to the rim.

There, belly-flat on warm stone, with my hand and ear pressed to the cliff, I listen to the cliff-singers’ great chords.

Their calls arrive as waves through rock and air at once, a complex interference pattern.

I have learned, through many seasons, to pick out:

- The signature of a healthy overhang—certain bright, ringing overtones riding on the main cries.
- The muffled, troubled quality that comes when deep new cracks spider up inside the stone.
- The hollowing drop in resonance that precedes a major fall by days.

Our band trades across some distance with another band farther along the rim. My contribution to that relationship is simple: when I hear the telltale bad chords from the direction of their sector, I add a particular marker to our bone-table—a shard of crystalline frond placed on its edge under their symbol. At the next meeting at the inland table, our runners will point to that shard, recount the sounds, and the other band will adjust their own memory-arrays accordingly.

The cliff-singers themselves I rarely see up close. Once in a while, one glides past our rim on bowed wings, banking slow, head turned, throat pressed lightly to the stone as it calls. Their eyes flick over me: a dark, ridged animal lying flat where no large thing can climb unaided. I do not know if they recognize me as any sort of constant. But I know individual voices; there are a few whose chords I have heard from youth to middle age, whose nightly songs have shifted as their bodies and perches changed. To me they are more like distant colleagues than beasts.

### How I Move Through the World

I do not race rope-predators to prey, or dance in glider-thermals. My journeys are mostly lateral, except when they are not.

I walk the mat between our three towers and the rim stone-tooth, using familiar windbreak-hedges as markers, reading the low vegetation’s color and curl to know how much heat it has stored. I pass beetle-gardens and grazing tongues, and they part around me with as much unconcern as they afford any medium-sized creature that neither hunts nor competes with them.

When I must change level—up or down—I favor the ladder-vines my cousins tend: ropes of living plant-muscle they have bent and persuaded over seasons to run between terraces and down fissure edges. I move slowly, four pads spread, testing every shift in fiber. The others say I climb like a plant waking from sleep. I answer that I fall less often than any of them.

In the Throats—the cooled fissures—I move with surety born of hours of blind practice. There, sound does strange things: drips click sharply off rock; small animal scurries become long, sliding whispers. We have shaped some of these fissures with paper-baffles and bone slabs, so that they speak truly. My job in them is to notice when they begin to lie.

A new crack in the root-mat above will turn one chamber’s clear, bell-like echo into something woolly. A slow subsidence in the stone floor will lengthen the reverberation fractionally. I notice, add that to the inner ledger, and later match it against tower-groans and cliff-songs. If enough such small deviations line up, I will urge the band, gently but insistently, to move a camp, to shift where they set their nets, to give up a familiar hollow for a year or two.

Sometimes they listen. Sometimes they do not. Time settles the argument.

### Social Weave

In a band this small, everyone knows everyone. My stillness makes me an odd presence, but not an isolated one.

I am useful when:

- Young ones ask, “What happened the last time the sky smelled like this?” and the elders argue. I can walk them to the table, trace seed-rods and stone beads, and summon not just one remembered event but three similar patterns across last-decade memory.
- A new partner joins the band from outside and needs to learn our towers’ moods; I can sit with them on a hot ledge, hands on trunk, and guide them through what they are feeling and hearing underneath the surface creaks.
- The fissure-farmers want to know if they can risk planting more of the pulsating water-fungi in a particular shaft; I can say, “No, the echoes are shortening here—the stone is softening—we should not weight it further.”

I am less useful when:

- A rope-predator has taken someone and the band needs to shift their air-paths fast; then the agile climbers and net-weavers lead.
- There is a narrow window to harvest a swarm bloom near a dangerous tower-lean; then the bold, quick-footed hunters excel.
- Heat drives us toward the rim and we must run under sudden spore-storms; then I lag, lungs burning, pads slipping in the hot dust.

No one is everything here. The plateau does not reward single, sharp excellence so much as it rewards a mesh of different partial strengths. I occupy one niche in that mesh: integrator of slow signals.

---

### What I Know of the Plateau Beyond

I have never crossed the entire plateau. No one has, as far as any of our tables remember. I know my region with intimacy, and I know a little of our neighbors’ regions through trade of stories and objects.

From their patterns and songs, I infer that:

- There are places where towers grow so thickly that the mat below is dim all day, and where the people there think in terms of “under-canopy” and “over-canopy” more than we do.
- There are burn-scars, ancient and rare, where blackened tower-cores still stand amid younger growth, and where certain bands have learned a different style of fire-use from ours, more willing to risk open flame on charred, non-living tissue.
- There are inner gullies where erosion has sculpted vertical labyrinths inside the plateau’s mass, and where the sound is less of open air and more of endless, small drips.

I know these things not as places in my feet, but as different chords in my mind: alternate sound-lives that I can picture and roughly translate into my own terms.

I also know that beyond the rim, down where the light looks a little cooler and the towers do not reach, there is another kind of world. I have never gone. The drop is far, the air thinner below, the climbing beyond my ability. But sometimes, when the cliff-singers’ songs come up wrong—ripe with shock and rock-dust—I imagine that some section of rim has fallen far enough to show new layers, new ecologies, to someone down there. Somewhere below, perhaps, there are beings who think of us as we think of the cliff-singers: distant, heard more than seen.

### If You Stood Here With Me

If you appeared beside me on my favorite ledge—midday, heat pressing, air thick with slow, high glider-shadows—you would see a small, mottled creature crouched with hands and feet splayed on living tissue, eyes half-lidded not with sleep but concentration.

I would look at you briefly, catalog you quickly as neither predator nor familiar, then gesture for you to be still. I would press one of your hands to the tower-skin, arrange your fingers around a big, breathing pore, and wait for you to feel the next slow wave of sap surge past.

Then I would point outward, to where the sound from the cliffs rolls up like distant surf. I would not have your words, but I would have mine: a soft, descending call that imitates the lowest harmonic of the cliff-singer’s night cry. I would let that sound hang between us, then touch my chest, then the stone under us, then the hazy, pollen-thick air.

You might name me a tracker, an acoustician, a memory-keeper. I do not have such abstractions. To myself, I am simply one who listens long enough that the plateau’s slow intentions become audible.

That is who and where I would be in this world: a small, careful body braided into the listening organs of a hot, high, living land; a single nerve ending in the vast, slow mind of the plateau.
