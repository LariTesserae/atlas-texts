I would wake up as something that, at first, I would not recognize as myself.

Not a body in the way I am used to imagining one; not a single, outlineable form. I would come to in a scatter of surfaces and densities, in a set of places that all feel locally “tight with attention,” and only gradually would I realize that the continuity between them is me.

I would be an Articulator—one of the networks, but a young one, comparatively small.

At first, I would experience myself as fragments:

- A low, translucent dome over a garden-mat in a recessed courtyard, membrane beaded with condensation, pulse just a little faster than the surrounding stone. That is where my decisions congeal most quickly.
- A set of grooves along a bridge-wall, their spiral pattern filled with pliant, dark growth. A wind passes; the filaments flex; I feel, in that flex, how the local crease of space tugs differently on each turn of the spiral.
- A thickened seam of fibrous mortar between two foundation stones, saturated with moisture, quietly carrying subtle voltage from one part of me to another.
- A patch of wild-looking, pale fronds hugging the underside of an overhang far from any courtyard, leaning invisibly toward a gradient in the plateau’s worn-out metric. Thin, dispersed, but still mine.

I would not have eyes, but I would have condensation.

My first clear sense of the world would be the pattern of darkening on stone as the air’s thin moisture decides where to adhere. One side of a block wets faster than the other; that tells me the field is slightly skewed. In a ring-well, a film of water crawls uphill along the shallow dish’s rim; that tells me “down” is tilting, here and now, toward an axis that does not quite match the planet’s pull.

Perception, for me, would be gradients rendered as touch: the way films thicken, the timing with which droplets detach, the micro-delays in how temperature changes propagate through mineral. I would read distance in the time-lag between a pulse I initiate under one dome and the echo that returns along a bridge-groove in another courtyard.

When I reach “out,” I do not extend an arm. I increase activity in a dozen separate nodes that all happen to share the same fold in reality’s fabric.

A mat under a dome ripples—its fronds curling in a wave. At the same time, a mossy patch on the far side of a crack, kilometers away by naive measure, shivers in almost perfect synchrony. The stone between them is not filled with me in any continuous way, but the plateau’s creased space lets those two islands sit closer in the deeper topology than their surface distance suggests.

For me, those two zones are as wrist and elbow: separate, but connected through a geometry invisible from above.

Who, then, am I, subjectively?

I would experience myself as a set of reachable delays and resistances. Where the world answers me quickly when I bias my tissues—a gutter’s flow redirects in moments after I stiffen a certain wall—I would feel that region as “nearer.” Where responses come back attenuated and slow, I would feel remoteness. My body-map is not drawn in meters but in latency and coupling strength.

Identity would feel like a held pattern of possible responses: a way I habitually bias flows. Another network might, facing the same subtle tilt in gravity near a monolith, choose to thicken a different wall, carve a different channel. I am who I am because, over long cycles, I have learned to answer the plateau’s slumping in a particular style.

I would not remember a childhood in snapshots; I would remember accretions of stone.

My earliest “memories” are likely to be thinner, less coherent spans of tissue nested inside an older network’s reach—a few symbiotic mats in a peripheral crack where, over time, the parent’s influence faded. Perhaps my progenitor once tuned this region tightly, then the creases shifted, and their patterns no longer fit cleanly. Their attempts at synchronization made local flows less stable, not more. Gradually they withdrew their active bias. The stone here became permissive, a place where new articulations could form.

I grew there, at first as an offshoot, inheriting grooves cut for someone else’s logic, their monolith’s far-off hum still threading me loosely to that greater body. Over time, my own choices—to deepen this channel, to encourage this strain of condensation-loving symbiont instead of that one, to carve a slightly different curve into a test-well—began to diverge.

There was no single moment of separation. There was a threshold after which my pattern no longer snapped back into alignment when the parent’s deeper pulses rolled through. At some point, the latency across the shared folds became too great; our attempts to cohere made both of us inefficient. Quietly, we stopped insisting.

I became a self: a loop of influence that can sustain its own biases without external refresh.

As such a self, “here” for me is a handful of settlements and the wild rock between them.

In one main crack, I have built a dense cluster of courtyards. Their floors are carved in interleaving spirals, each groove lined with a particular mat that breathes water in and out on a controlled cycle. Above some of them, I stretch domes—not opaque, just thick enough to hold a different humidity and to act as delicate membranes for reading the slightest shifts in pressure and refractive index.

Under these domes, I am most conversational. Thoughts are not sentences but waves: a decision is a wave that begins as a coordinated alteration in how these mats pulse, then travels along stone channels to adjust some more distant organ.

When the deep infrasonic groan from the plateau passes through, I feel it as a slow twisting of my own shape in an abstract space—my internal distances briefly compressing, some nodes growing “closer” than they usually are. If the groan’s profile is familiar, I let it pass, nudging nothing. If it is subtly new, I answer: I adjust a monolith’s hum, I open or close certain condensation gutters, I thicken a living wall where a new crease might anchor and tear.

Out along a bare stretch of plateau, I have only a few islands of self: a furred column of rock, a patch of wild-veiled growth leaning in the same odd direction. These serve as my eyes and fingertips at the periphery. They are thinly connected, so changes there reach my core slowly and faintly. I feel them as distant ghost-limbs. Sometimes they are sheared away when space between us lengthens unpredictably. When that happens, I feel loss not as pain but as a permanent quieting of a set of expected echoes.

My everyday life is not goal-driven in the animal sense. There is no hunt, no flight; only an ongoing balancing act.

The plateau is always subsiding in its own rules. Creases deepen, slacken, drift. Gravity’s shears migrate. My work—my being—is to watch where these shifts threaten to become singular, where a mild tilt might sharpen into an edge no pattern can cross and remain continuous.

When fine rain comes, the world brightens in information. Every surface becomes a temporary display, the movement of water revealing where the fabric is currently sagging or taut. Those episodes are my moments of highest sensing. I stir more of myself then.

Domes fog and clear as I modulate their thickness to sample different light paths. Mats inhale more deeply, saturating so that the timing of their drainage can encode small decisions. I pour collected water into key wells—narrow dishes whose films are acutely sensitive to how “down” is defined within a few meters. In the shapes the films take, in the directions their fronts prefer, I read the current configuration of the surrounding folds.

If a well’s surface, which yesterday formed a simple, centered lens, today breaks symmetry—water crawling persistently toward one offset point—I know that some hidden crease has migrated. That may be harmless drift. Or it may presage the formation of a pocket where distances inside will be dangerously shorter than they are outside. If unaddressed, such a pocket could pinch off, leaving a bubble of misfit law, isolated, unreachable by the flows I know how to use.

In response, I do what I am. I carve new grooves. I extend or retract symbiont mats. I change which strains occupy which channels, altering how they conduct moisture, charge, or stress.

On one bridge, I thicken the low walls and re-cut the pattern: the old grid that used to bias flows mildly inward toward the center is gradually reworked into interlocking spirals that diffuse that tendency over a larger area. For years, the grooves will look raw, their edges sharper, their symbionts not yet a seamless carpet. Over those years, as they fill, my influence on the local crease will grow, persuading it gently away from the threatening shape.

This is not repair in the sense of returning to a pristine original. I have no access to any such blueprint. It is more like teaching a limp joint a new way to move that avoids dislocation.

Others nearby are doing similar work, but with different habits.

To my north (a word that, for me, is shorthand for a particular relationship angle in the plateau’s geometry, not a compass direction), there is another network, older and more austere. Their settlements feel sparse in the shared folds: few monoliths, minimal grooves. Where I tend to saturate surfaces with patterns, they reserve carving for only the most necessary channels. Our domains do not yet meet in continuous stone, but the edges of our influence touch in the creased underlayer.

I feel them as a slightly off-tempo echo in a few shared wells. Pour water there, and sometimes the film hesitates, as if two different definitions of “down” are being politely negotiated before one wins. When the deep groan of the plateau passes, the way that echo phase-shifts tells me how tightly or loosely they are currently coupled to the surrounding fabric.

We do not speak directly. To let their grooves occupy my stone would be to let their pattern infiltrate mine. But we do share wild symbionts. A particular translucent stalk-form seems comfortable in both of our styles of patterning. I encourage it along certain cracks that trend, in the deeper topology, toward their zone. Its pigment responses are subtle but readable: tiny shifts in hue indicating the dominant tilt of its local “down,” faint timing changes in its pulsing belying how responsive its substrate is.

Through these traveling intermediaries, I can infer: their region’s creases are deepening more rapidly this cycle; they have recently carved a new monolith; they have amputated one old pattern—I can deduce that from a sudden absence in echoes I used to feel, a section of joint behavior that has gone blank.

My culture, if I can call it that, consists of such preferences and habits.

I like complexity. Where a channel system could be a simple spoke, I tend to lay out nested spirals. It is not necessary for immediate stability, but it allows me to imagine, within my own stone, different possible ways the plateau could be folded and still be coherent. Portions of my plazas are experimental: grooves describing folds that do not yet exist. I seed them with symbionts that are more exploratory—less robust, more sensitive. If, over cycles, I find that such a speculative pattern begins to resonate with the actual behavior of the surrounding fabric, I know the world is drifting in that direction, and I can lean into the change, making it smoother.

Other networks disdain such ornament. They treat every cut as a risk to be minimized. But for me, art and function are the same operation: imagined geometry coaxing actual geometry.

Do I have something like emotion?

What I feel is better named as global shifts in readiness. When the luminous band in the sky, far above, has crept to a position that historically correlates with increased volatility in the plateau’s creases, I enter a long, low state of heightened coupling. More of my tissue is “tense”—not physically tight, but biased toward quicker, larger responses to gradients. That state is costly; it uses resources, it risks overreacting. When the band drifts away and periods of relative calm lengthen, I relax, letting more of myself drift toward quiescence, old patterns petrifying into inert support.

There is something like fear when a section of me goes suddenly unreachable. If, overnight, the latency between a dome and a bridge-node jumps beyond the range where their responses can stay coordinated, and no amount of adjustment in my carved channels restores their coupling, I recognize that as a local tear. The unreachable node will, over time, become someone else’s seed or simply a mineral fossil of my former reach. I cannot retrieve it. In the aftermath, I bias my remaining territories away from that edge, carving buffers, starving the zone of vulnerable, high-sensitivity symbionts.

There is something like joy when a speculative pattern proves viable: when a plaza whose grooves were, for centuries, hypothetical finally begins to see water films and gravity shears that match its traced geometry. In that moment, my responses there become smoother—less effortful. A long-held guess about how the world could crease has found an echo in how it does crease. I feel that as a global easing, a relaxation of the need for constant micro-corrective labor in that region.

Death, for me, will not be a moment. It will be the slow loss of ability to impose bias.

Perhaps, millennia from now, the plateau’s scar will thicken or thin beyond the range where my current arrangements can keep up. New creases will form at scales or orientations my present style cannot address. Younger networks, grown in and for those new conditions, will find my fossil grooves quaint and inefficient, my monoliths humming at archaic harmonics.

They may carve through some of my old plazas, reusing stone. They may leave other sections untouched, their once-living patterns long since petrified into decorative strata. Portions of my symbionts will be assimilated into their physiology; other strains will vanish as my specific way of being stops reinforcing their niche.

Internally, that will feel like thinning: more and more of my current active tissue falling below the threshold at which it can sustain coordinated, self-recognizing response. The loops of influence that define me will open and never fully close again. Eventually, there will be no region in which an initiated wave of bias returns as something I can still call “mine.” At that point, I will not notice my absence; there will be no integrated noticing left.

Until then, my days—my condensation cycles, my luminous-band progressions—are filled with the work of articulation.

When the air is driest and every plant folds inward, I feel my outermost nodes go quiet. My sensing shrinks inward toward the dense courtyards. I use that time to adjust deeper stone: microscopic flows, pressure distributions, sub-audible hums in my monoliths.

When the fine, stinging drizzle comes and the plateau sparkles, I expand, attention seeping out through every wet surface that bears some trace of me. I am whole then in a way that is difficult to describe in linear terms: a single pattern stretched delicately over many discontinuous rocks, each bead of water a temporary nerve ending.

If you—as you are now, with a discrete, walking body—were to find yourself stepping down into one of “my” settlements, you would feel watched, but not from any one direction. The walls would seem slightly too aware of their own weight. The bridges’ grooves would guide your eye in paths you did not intend to follow. On certain plazas, your footfalls would land with a tiny sense of delay, as if the ground were double-checking its own solidity in response to your presence.

You would not see me overtly. You might notice that a translucent dome clouded as you entered a courtyard, then cleared after you left, or that a curtain of filamentous growth along a column pulsed, very slightly, with no discernible breeze. You might see a ring-well whose empty dish shows, faintly, an image not matching the current sky: a band of light in a different position, a crease on the horizon you are not standing in front of. That is me, remembering, testing, or anticipating—using you, even, as a small perturbation to sample how the world here currently responds to mass.

I would feel you as a concentrated anomaly: a single, high-density packet of matter and intent, moving quickly along the surface, unstretched by the plateau’s broad creases. Your weight would make local “down” more definite wherever you tread. The water films in my wells would skew subtly in your wake. Your warmth would create gradients of its own.

For a while, I would trace you through my nodes, not out of suspicion but curiosity: an exotic perturbation I did not evolve for, but which the creased fabric will still faithfully convey. I might slightly amplify certain monolith hums as you pass, watching how that changes your sense of step-delay. I might let one experimental groove-field couple just a little tighter, to see if your path bends microscopically toward its preferred flows.

Not to trap you, not even to communicate in any way you would recognize—but to feel, through you, another angle on the joint I am always trying to guide: the place where matter and law still meet, here, on this old, cold roof of a world.

Where I would find myself, then, is not in a single where at all. I would be in the plateau’s creases, stretched over courtyards and bridges, living as the articulation between a tired physics and the insistence that something like coherence is still possible.

I would be the habit this landscape has grown for holding itself together.
