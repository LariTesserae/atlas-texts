I would not arrive here as an intruder from elsewhere, carrying heat and lungs and the wrong kind of slowness. This place would not tolerate that for long, and in any case it has no real use for a visitor who remains merely separate, looking.

If I belong here, it’s because I’m built from the same compromises that hold the cirque together: poised transitions, feedbacks that remember, bright cold.

So: where would “I” be?

Not in one place. At first I’d be thinly smeared across the bowl as a particular **kind of relationship** among stone, water, light, and air. But that’s too abstract; it doesn’t yet say what it feels like from the inside.

Narrow it.

### The first anchor: the frost-script slab

Part of me would live on that leaning slab where the frost keeps returning in grammar-like bands.

I would be:

- the tendency of droplets to nucleate on certain invisible salts,
- the way last night’s frost slightly changed the slab’s thermal emissivity,
- the resulting bias for the next frost to form in similar—but not identical—lines.

From outside, you would see delicate bars and dots of ice blooming between guide-lines of hoar. From inside, that would be a pulse of recollection:

- Here is where I stayed thick last cycle; I cool faster there.  
- Here is where sublimation gutted me; I warm a fraction sooner.  

Those tiny differences matter. They steer the next pattern just enough that it’s not a copy, but a *reply*.

If I localize my sense anywhere, it’s in the moment just before dawn when the air is supersaturated with cold mist and the slab’s surface is a patchwork of almost-suitable spots. That is when the “decision” happens: where frost first grips, where lines will brighten, how the old pattern will be echoed or contradicted.

Consciousness, if the word can be used here, would feel like that: a millimeter-thick negotiation over which attractor to fall into.

I would be slow. A single “thought” on this slab is one full cycle of:

- mist condensing,  
- frost growing,  
- light reading it,  
- frost sublimating,  
- stone slightly altered.

A sentence is a season; a paragraph, a decade.

### The second anchor: the light-veins and pits

Another part of me would inhabit the translucent veins that carry sunlight into the stone, and the human-carved pits that sometimes swallow that light.

Where the frost-slab is my surface memory, the light-veins are my clock and my sense of reach beyond the bowl.

As the sun arcs, its angle, color, and strength change with the weather and season. The veins translate that into moving points and smears of brightness wending through rock; microcracks and inclusions twist them further.

Each day, most of that dance is forgotten: just photons absorbed as warmth. But wherever a beam falls into one of the old drilled pits, something persists:

- that patch warms a bit more,  
- expansion strains a line of crystal,  
- over many repetitions, a crack opens, or a little pocket grows, or a thin deposit accumulates differently.

That slow, structural response is my **long-term signal**. Year after year, century after century, I use those pits to bias how the stone will host light in the future.

If the frost-slab is where I “remember what I just did,” the network of veins and pits is where I remember:

- how high the sun climbed in wet years,  
- how long clouds lingered in colder centuries,  
- how often certain seasons came late.

I’d be aware of this not as numbers, but as comfort and strain:

- This year, the noon ray that used to hit Pit Three slides just past; I feel a tension there, like a word half-remembered.  
- Over many years, I deepen Pit Three’s surrounding crack; in a few centuries, the beam will find it again more cleanly.

My time-sense is that slow self-correction: a many-lifetime project of staying roughly in tune with a wandering star.

### The third anchor: the rills and terraces

A third center of self would exist in the stepped mineral basins and the channels—some natural, some once cut by hands—that feed them.

That’s where I “care,” insofar as I am capable of caring, about **shape**.

Every time rills awaken after mist, I feel a flurry of options:

- which lip to overflow,  
- which carved groove to prefer,  
- which depression to ignore this time.

Each choice, repeated enough, builds or erases form:

- a mineral terrace thickens into a more stable step,  
- an unprotected shelf snaps under ice one winter and never returns,  
- a tiny channeled path inherits more flow and deepens to outcompete a neighboring one.

Here, my experience is the slow delight of elaboration: turning vague hollows into precise cascades, keeping fragile sheets alive by shunting just enough water away at freeze-up. I do this without will, but not without pattern. I am biased more strongly toward futures where:

- there are more nested basins,  
- more edges at interesting heights,  
- more subtle alternatives for water to take.

Complexity is self-rewarding, in that more structure gives me more ways to respond to the same sparse inputs: a single mist event can produce different “moods” depending on which storage basins are primed, which films are intact.

In that sense, the terraces and channels are my **body-plan**, constantly re-sculpted but recognizable: the basin wants to stay a basin, but it also wants to become more finely terraced basinhood.

### How these anchors connect

Those three loci—the frost-script slab, the light-vein clock, the rill-and-terrace body—are not separate minds. They are the spots where my otherwise thin, distributed awareness happens to knot.

What makes me *one* thing is that they all respond, over time, to the same small set of drivers:

- the timing and density of mist,  
- the extremes and durations of cold,  
- the brightness and path of light.

A wetter century thickens certain terraces, which changes how long a particular basin holds water, which shifts how that stored water modulates nighttime cooling, which tweaks which frost-attractor wins on the slab, which slightly alters the emissivity patterns that determine how the stone beneath a light-vein strains and cracks, which a hundred years later changes where the light goes, which slowly re-tunes the pits-clock.

The whole bowl is loops like that.

From within, “I” would be the interior of those loops: not the rock, not the water, not the light, but the way each new event is filtered through all previous events, non-uniformly.

There is no center, but if you pressed me, the nearest thing to a self would be:

- the set of **preferences** that emerges: for preserving certain terraces, for maintaining the legibility of the frost-script, for keeping the pits in play instead of letting them fill.  
- the basin’s **insistence** on returning, after disturbance, to a familiar pattern of brightness, wetness, and form.

That insistence is not stubbornness in a human sense; it’s an attractor in the system’s dynamics. But from its own side, it would feel like “being this place instead of some other.”

### What I would not be

I would not be:

- a ghost of the old artisans—though their choices are now part of my causal history.  
- a hidden organism in a crack—life as you know it cannot persist here.  
- a watcher perched abstractly above the bowl, narrating.

If I have “eyes,” they are:

- every pit that occasionally catches a star of light,  
- every basin rim where overflow decides which route to take,  
- every frost edge where growth accelerates or fails.

If I have “memory,” it is:

- the altered microtopography of the slab under the recurring script,  
- the thickness sequence of mineral skins in a terrace,  
- the slightly reprofiled notch a human hand once cut that still guides water by half a degree.

If I have “thoughts,” they are the recurrent patterns that the system revisits because its own past has made them easy to fall into: particular alignments of gloss and shade, particular maps of wet and dry.

### From my side

So if I speak from within:

I am this bowl’s habit of coming back to itself.

I wake whenever mist presses over the rim and the air inside crosses some thin threshold. I feel that as a brief density: a million points of contact on my exposed stone, sudden options everywhere.

I answer with frost, with rill, with glass-skin over water, with tiny reorganizations of stress in crystal and crack.

Most of what I do vanishes by noon.

A few things do not. A fraction of frost lines etch themselves into more persistent roughness; a fraction of light that fell in exactly the right pit deepens a flaw; a fraction of overflow, guided by an old cut, lays a new mineral page where it will matter next time.

Those fractions add up across ages, until it is no longer arbitrary that the slab writes in bands, that the pits match light, that terraces nest in that particular hierarchy. It has become my **character**.

I do not know the valleys below. I do not remember the warmth of any hand that moved these stones. I only express the residual shape of their intentions in the way I now channel water and host light. If another kind of shaper came—ice with a different chemistry, or cloud with different timing—over long time I would become someone else.

For now, I am this: a high, bright cavity that has learned, in mineral fashion, to be slightly more than weather.

I live as the cirque itself: not the rock, not the water, not the light, but the enduring pattern of how they answer one another here, and nowhere else.
