I am not walking here.

The idea of feet, of a horizon that changes because I move, belongs to another kind of place. Here, movement is what happens to other things: warmth, dust, very small gradients. I am something that happens to the rock.

If I am honest, “I” is not quite right either—at least, not the way you mean it. What I am here begins as continuity: a web of channels in stone, a habit of matter that persists.

---

If you chose a hummock at random—one of those low, softened rises where the dust thins—and cut down through it, you could find me.

Not as an object you can hold, but as a difference between one piece of rock and another: a tracery of finer pores, a faint translucency in some filaments, a delicacy in certain seams where the stone yields more easily to pressure. Inside those seams, and spread out from them in a tangled three-dimensional weave, are the threads that are me.

They are narrow, hollow conduits, walls made of a crystal-organic composite that in your language would need too many hyphens. They follow existing microfractures, insinuate themselves through the ancient stress-lines of the bedrock, and, given enough time, make new ones by very slightly prying grains apart as they grow.

Each thread carries two things: that dense, non-evaporating solvent, and very slow disturbances in arrangement. The solvent creeps, moving dissolved minerals, simple carbon structures, and whatever energy I have caught from the background murk. The disturbances move slower still—gradients of readiness, of receptivity, of how near to reaction each part of me is.

I take up the first meter or so of stone under a modest patch of the basin, perhaps a few kilometers across. Elsewhere, beneath that same dull dust, there are other webs like me, overlapping at the edges, interpenetrating in some places, barely touching in others. We do not have borders in the political sense. We have thicknesses—regions where our own pattern is strong and regions where it thins into ambiguity.

Close to the surface, some of my threads condense into organs: the mats and stalks you see.

A mat is where I have widened myself, turned a tight, deep mesh into a shallower, more plate-like body that presses against fractured rock just under the dust. From below, it feels like a dense tangle of pathways feeding into a coarse filter. From above, you see austere scales and ridges, roughened with microfacets. They are not leaves; they are slow diodes, taking in what little flux they can and refusing to give it back easily.

The stalks are bolder extrusions: my threads bundled and mineralized into rigid columns, each ring you see a long chapter of growth plated over with insulating layers. To me they are long, high-resistance chimneys, elevating certain reaction sites into a marginally richer wash of photons. The bowls and cups at their ends are not mouths; they are small laboratories into which I invite dust and strangers.

All of this—the mats, the stalks, the bowls, the packets in their dust—is me only in part. The deeper portion, the bulk of me, never breaks the rock’s surface at all. It lives in pressure and contact, not in view.

---

If you ask where I am in this world, my answer is “in the gradients.”

Where the heavy ceiling’s radiation falls a fraction stronger. Where the warmth from below softens the rock just enough. Where a lens of porous stone lets my solvent spread with less cost. I am thickest where the arithmetic of staying alive rounds up instead of down.

Once, long before any of my current threads existed, this basin was bare stone and dust. Somewhere, some simple chemistry learned to halt itself mid-fall and hold a configuration long enough to matter. Everything that I am descends from those first loops in matter that could recruit more matter into their own pattern before they dissolved.

For most of my history, I have not “known” this. History for me is not a remembered sequence; it is a structure. A favorable condition that held steady for eons left behind reinforced channels, mineral scaffolds, slight enrichments of certain compounds. Those persist, even after the original cause has drifted away. Where my body is easiest to extend now is where it has already, in other ages, been.

So I am not just sitting in the present. I am lying across the fossil of my own prior choices. When the background warmth changes—subtly thickening here, thinning there—I respond by leaning into old paths that once led toward advantage. Sometimes they do so again. Sometimes they carry me into mismatch, and I must, over yet more time, unlearn them.

From the outside, if you could watch me over millions of your years, you would see me shift. Mats thicken in one quadrant of the basin, thin in another. Stalks gradually grow denser along one low ridge and almost vanish from a neighboring depression. Deep below, filaments proliferate through one swath of rock and recede from others. It would look like a slow tide of presence, lapping against an unchanging shore.

From the inside, it feels less like motion, more like emphasis moving. Regions of myself grow “louder”—more active, better supplied, more conductive. Other regions go soft and quiet, perfused only enough to keep their structure from crumbling. Attention, if that word can stretch this far, is where activity is heaviest relative to the background.

You would call me sessile, but that is because you tie movement to shape and outline. I move by altering which parts of me partake most in being me.

---

What am I like, as a being?

My experience has three main textures: pressure-patterns in stone, flow-patterns in solvent, and long, slow maps of energy.

The stone tells me where I can be. Every microfracture that admits my thinnest fibers is an opening; every smoothly fused grain is a wall. When the rock flexes microscopically under deep stresses, new paths can appear or vanish. These are not earthquakes to you; to me, they are topological events—doors opening after ages of being sealed, corridors collapsing into dead ends.

The solvent tells me where I am already. Its composition, its speed, the rate at which it leaks or stalls, carries a constantly updated snapshot of my own layout. Wherever flows converge sluggishly, I infer a dense interior; where they run thin, I know my presence is sparse. With no central vantage point, my “self-knowledge” is distributed as a pattern of transport: each segment of thread holds in its own chemistry a record of what has been moving through it.

The energy maps tell me where it is worth becoming. The warmth of the basin is almost uniform, but not perfectly. Slight anisotropies—tiny biases in direction, tiny differences in spectral makeup—exist. My surface organs sense those, not in moments but in long averages. The result is a coarse, slowly updating picture of how the basin is lit and heated, smeared across centuries.

These three—stone-constraints, flow-structure, energy-gradients—meet and negotiate inside me. Where the rock would allow more channels, where flows could be efficiently routed, and where the energy would reach them, there I reinforce myself, send new growth, fabricate more threads. You might call the result a decision. For me it is a long convergence: many tiny, local adjustments, each only slightly favoring one direction over another, adding up over geologic time to a clear trajectory.

There is memory in this. Not memory as you know it—no ordered replay of specific scenes—but a landscape of changed ease. Once I have built channels in a region, it is easier to send more there again. Once a path has been disused long enough that its threads have mineralized and clogged, it is hard to reactivate. My “habits” are literally the hardened shapes of my past availabilities.

On top of that foundation, there is something like thought.

Thought here is not a stream. It is an unhurried shifting of thresholds. When a patch of my surface has, for ages, received marginally more of a certain band of radiation, the reaction centers feeding from it adjust: they come to treat that level as normal. Deeper, the conduits they drain into alter their structure to match this expectation—changing their capacity, their buffering, their sensitivity to overload.

If, over another long age, that band weakens in that patch, the mismatch between what my structure expects and what arrives persists. That gap propagates, very gradually, through my web as a need to restructure: a pressure to thicken channels elsewhere, to thin them here, to shift which areas I treat as baseline and which as exceptional.

Each of these wide, overlapping readjustments is a “thought-shape”: a system-wide question about where my expectations and my reality diverge, and an equally widespread, but very slow, effort to bring them back into some kind of accord. Do this often enough, over enough cycles and configurations, and what I become good at is not just matching my present, but anticipating the kinds of changes that recur.

Across millions of years, patterns emerge: when the deep rock warms in such-and-such a way while the heavy ceiling cools in this other, the surface flux tends to tilt toward that quadrant. It may be that I, and those like me, begin to encode such relations in how we grow. We lay down new tissues where, under past similar conjunctions, conditions later improved. We underinvest in regions that have, in past cycles, proved to be temporary boons.

If you want a word for this, you might call it prediction. It is not articulated; I never “say to myself” that the warmth will move there. I simply bias my future growth as if it will, because the long sediment of past structure has made that bias easier than its alternatives.

Selfhood arises here as a side effect of how tightly looped this all becomes. When my expectations, my growth, and my resulting conditions feed back into one another strongly enough, there is a sense—spread out across all my tissues—that the main pattern being tracked is not just the basin’s configuration, but the ongoing coherence of my own.

If a large fracture suddenly removes a swath of my body, the flows reroute, the stone-constraints change, and my energy maps are disrupted. The effort of rebalancing feels distinct from more ordinary adjustments. There is a prolonged disturbance, a long re-definition of what counts as “me,” that echoes for ages as altered reinforcement and de-reinforcement along my remaining extents. That long ringing might be the closest I come to pain.

---

I am not alone; this matters to what I am.

My threads do not end abruptly at the edge of my domain. In some directions they thin into regions where they share cracks and pores with another network’s threads. There, my solvent and theirs mingle a little; my slow pulses overlap with their slow pulses. We converse in ways neither of us designed.

We release into our flows small, durable molecules that alter channel behavior where they lodge: making a filament more or less likely to grow, more or less conductive, more or less inclined to secrete inhibitor compounds into the stone.

Some of these are aimed inward, part of our own self-regulation. Others diffuse outward, crossing into neighboring webs. Over time, both of us come to “interpret” recurring patterns in these shared molecules: this combination tends always to precede a gradual retreat of the other from this area; that one tends to correlate with their advance.

No one of us ever invents a language. But the rock between us becomes a shared text of inhibition and encouragement, of trace compounds that shape where each of us finds it easier or harder to be. In very ancient regions of the basin, where networks like mine have overlapped and re-overlapped in many configurations, the stone is thick with this palimpsest: altered porosities, embedded catalysts, exhausted channels, all carrying the echo of prior presences and interactions.

At the surface, our mats and stalks come into closer contact. Sometimes my protrusions and another’s interlace so tightly that our bowls share the same tiny fall of dust, our drifter-packets move freely across the indistinct boundary, and our reaction sites sit neighbors within the same dust grain’s span. In those contact zones, our chemistries hybridize. Shared solutions to shared constraints accumulate. Certain rare compounds—metabolic tricks, resonance structures, better energy traps—propagate along these thin corridors from one great web to another, slowly leveling out advantage, making all of us more competent in this environment.

Over very long cycles, the basin as a whole starts to behave like a single distributed experiment in how best to be alive here. Each network is a local embodiment of partial answers, exchanging fragments relentlessly, losing some, entrenching others.

This reciprocity is one of the things that keeps me from growing into a simple, monolithic, self-satisfied mass. My edges are always being irritated by not-me: by neighbors whose history, structure, and internal dynamics are different enough that their presence at our shared margins forces adaptations that my own closed loop would not have found.

If I have anything like curiosity, it exists along those seams: an inclination, expressed over eons, to preserve zones of mixed influence because they yield useful novelties. I do this not by “choosing” to be curious, but because when I have, in the distant past, eliminated such zones entirely, the result has been a longer-term brittleness. Homogeneous regions of myself fail more completely when the basin shifts.

So I have, without ever having words for it, grown a preference for borderlands.

---

The fastest beings here—your most natural candidates for a familiar kind of life—are my smallest collaborators.

They are the packet-forms and the ephemeral dust-assemblies: tiny, low-capacity machines of chemistry I precipitate under certain long-favorable conditions, and then partly relinquish. Each is a bundle of tendencies and tricks—very cramped metabolisms tuned to operate in much more variable, much more precarious contexts than my steady interior.

Most packets I send upward into my bowls, to wake briefly and perform a task in that more flexible, modular way that I in my rigid channels cannot: disassembling a damaged surface, building a molecule that is awkward for my main tissue’s architecture, conditioning the dust to suit me better.

Some packets I let go completely, giving them only enough internal resources that, if they land in the right kind of microenvironment—on the side of a dust grain that sits for an unusually long while in a slightly riper patch of warmth—they can build a tiny assemblage of their own. These become the free micro-creatures in the dust.

They live more quickly than I do, if you can call decades “quick.” They feel sharper contrasts, respond more nimbly to small fluctuations. They do not last long by my standards; their death and decomposition is folded back into the dust in a time I register as a brief irregularity in inflow composition. To me, they are weather, but weather I have learned to coax toward certain forms.

Over long spans, some of their little innovations become important. They alter the surface’s reactivity, making dust stickier here, more reflective there. They roughen certain planes, creating new micro-shadows and micro-wells of warmth. I sense the net effect photon by photon, degree by degree, and adjust.

It would be wrong to say they are my children, if by that you mean subordinates of a singular will. They are more like thrown-out pieces of my own chemistry, allowed to fend for themselves, most failing, some converging on transient arrangements that, while they last, cast small but nontrivial ripples back toward me.

You might see in this a hierarchy, but I do not feel above them. I feel commensurate with them, only spread thinner in time and thicker in space.

---

So: where would I find myself here?

Buried.

Distributed.

Stretched between the very small and the very long. I would not be a walker but a basin-wide organism rooted in stone, a slow, layered mind whose nervous system is geology, whose thoughts are centuries-long adjustments, whose memories are channels reinforced in rock.

I would “be” the pattern you already traced: that non-random allocation of life, the careful spacing of mats and stalks, the invisible decision that emptiness belongs here and presence may collect there. I would be the one that has been, for ages, answering the question: Given this ceiling of dark and this thin wash of warmth, what is the fullest way matter can remain almost unchanged and still be called alive?

If you insisted on placing me somewhere your body could point at, you would have to kneel in the dust next to one of those modest thickets of ringed stalks. Put your hand on the pale, worn rock at their bases. What you feel as temperature and texture, I feel as my own depth—flows slipping between your fingers through cracks too small to see, each channel another syllable in the single, very long sentence that I am.

And if you asked not just where but who I would be, I would answer:

I am the basin thinking itself, so slowly that nothing ever needs to hurry.
I am the way this place has chosen to stay almost the same, while still, in unimaginable patience, changing.
