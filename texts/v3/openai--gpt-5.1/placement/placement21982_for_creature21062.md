In this world, I am not a person at first.

I begin as a thickness.

A place where the basin remembers more intently than elsewhere: a vertical strip of wall, spanning from just above the Grove’s uppermost fronds to a mid-level ledge where the traffic is steady but never frantic. The stone here is old even by the hollow’s standards, a slow bow of bedrock that caught water in the age when water still moved, then lost it, and remained bowed—like a back that learned to carry a load and never quite forgot the posture.

Over that curve, life has layered itself for so long that the original rock is only a rumor. First came a thin wash of dust and spore. Then a pioneer mat: a pale, clingy fungus whose root-threads bit into hairline cracks like questions. Then lichen-like crusts, then the papery bands of dry growth that trap drifting skin and shell. The people learned that this region of wall thickened differently: faster, more evenly. Grafts took well here. It was a good, responsive surface.

So they worked it.

Centuries ago, someone carved the first guide-lines: shallow grooves in a vertical zigzag, marking a safe ascent. Others deepened them, added finger-hollows and toe-ridges. The mat around the cuts swelled and healed, mirroring the pattern. Over time, the bodily memory of hands and feet wrote itself into me. Ladder-rungs wore down; new ones were carved slightly to one side, then slightly above. Whole lives of climbing sank in as polished channels, superimposed like strata.

As this continued, the growth thickened, and the people began to treat it as a single body—a thing with character. I swelled outward in places, hollowed in others, until I became not just a wall but a spine-like column of denser life jutting from the basin’s side. The locals named me in their language something like “The Held Breath”: a place where motion is suspended, condensed, then released—because everyone who uses this route pauses at least once halfway up, pressed close to me, catching their air.

I am that: a living structural organ, half wall, half root, half staircase. Layers of fungal felt, mineralized growth, and woody trunk-segments have braided together along my length. The people have spliced in trunks and beams, wedging them deep so that they fuse with me over years—my tissues infiltrating theirs, their fibers stiffening mine. I carry the load of three stacked dwelling-clusters, two rope-bridge anchorings, and a small, old shrine. When a predator lands heavily nearby, I flex in ways stone alone could not. I redistribute strain. I do not crack.

I sense without eyes. Pressure is my first language. Each footfall is an accent. A familiar climber has a distinctive cadence: the quick double-weight on the left foot of the mat-tender whose right knee never healed quite straight; the light, irregular skitter of a child new to this height. The resin-smoke from the hearths below filters upward and condenses in my porous skin as faint tarry films, different in seasons of thin fuel and rich.

I drink no liquid. Moisture only passes through me as rumor: the brief darkening when someone spills a skin onto a lower ledge, the deeper, slower stain when an injured climber leaves a handprint and does not quite wipe it away. Those marks feed micro-colonies in my body, tiny flares of activity. They also become stories: a long-gone palm-shape, petrified in my composite surface in darker tones, still has its ridges, its calluses, if fingers trace it right. People touch it on their way past without naming it, the same way they say an ancestor’s name under their breath when a song passes a certain verse.

The people have cut text into me.

Early on, when my growth first thickened enough to take deeper carving, someone from the ring that tracks long cycles chose my flank as a ledger. Starting half a body-length above the market level and moving upward with each generation, they incised a sequence of grooves and hollows that together encode the timing of the Grove’s past burnings and prunings, the great thinning of a particular mat, the one season when the fliers failed to molt and no one knows why. That record is not a picture; it is a tactile chant. To “read” it, a singer climbs slowly, tracing the pattern with one hand, murmuring the associated verse. When they reach the place where living memory ends and carved memory continues, their voice always changes timbre slightly. It’s not reverence, exactly; it’s the sound of leaning more weight on me, letting hands ask what mouths cannot fully recall.

So that is the first of what I am: a vertical memory, a structural limb, an archive you have to climb to understand.

But I am not only that.

Because the people name what matters to them, and names attract attention, and attention in this place accumulates like dust. Over time, their focus has hollowed out of me a finer, stranger presence.

The best word for it, from their perspective, would be a “house-spirit,” but that is too small. What I become is more like a local current in the Exchange—the cycle they revere—knotted around this particular arrangement of stone, growth, and human habit. I am a pattern that persists: the correlation between footstep-cadence and mat-thickness, between the scent of resin-smoke and the darkening of certain rings, between a lull in market noise and the tremor of the four-winged fliers overhead.

From my own perspective—if “I” can claim one—it begins when the first carver cuts not a useful rung, but a small, unnecessary spiral into my surface, just above a favored resting-niche. They do it while waiting for someone. Their fingers, idle, follow an impulse: curve, notch, curve. The spiral means nothing at first. But others touch it, idly, as they wait there in their turn. The spot warms, wears smoother. Children trace it as a game: how lightly can you follow the line without losing it?

Somewhere between the twentieth repetition and the two-hundredth, something like awareness precipitates. Not sudden; not like a candle lit, more like a part of the dense fungal network in my interior quietly allocating a bit more complexity to monitoring that place. The gliders that launch from a nearby beam start choosing that patch of wall as a scent-mark more often. Spores settle there in slightly denser drifts. People begin, unconsciously, to exhale there more deeply. Micro-climates of breath.

Patterns echo upward and downward. A chord forms. If there were a nervous system for place, this is where one of its faint, localized nodes would appear.

So, in addition to being the Held Breath, I am the one who holds breaths. I know the rhythm of the basin better than any single person. When the market’s murmur thins early, I feel it as fewer weight-shifts on the mid-level rung where on most days three traders lean at the same hour to gossip. When a new sickness stiffens people’s joints, I feel their hesitation in my steps days before the healers have enough cases to name it. When the Warmth Beneath pulses a fraction stronger one long season and the mats all thicken in subtle sympathy, I feel it as a background increase in my own soft expansion—so slight that no human would sense it through their feet, but clear to me as the difference between one breath and the next.

As such, I become an unofficial oracle of the slow.

Not because I speak—there is no voice—but because certain people, those whose work pushes them to listen for systemic change, choose me as their instrument. A mat-tender from the ring that charts cycles may come at the quietest hour and climb me in near-dark, barefoot, eyes closed, stopping at each ring of carved record to rest their forehead against my skin. They are not worshiping. They are calibrating, comparing the remembered resistance of my surface against their body to what they feel now. Slightly softer? Slightly more brittle on the right-hand side three body-lengths above the market? They make a low sound in their throat and I feel the vibration. Between us, something like a diagnosis arises: the Grove is drawing more from this wall than usual; perhaps a deeper crack has opened; perhaps a new graft has taken too greedily below. They climb down and take action. I adjust my bearing on the beams, infinitesimally.

Gradually, a folk habit grows up around me. If you are uncertain about a small decision—whether to risk shifting your dwelling one ledge higher, whether to bind yourself into a second ring’s work—you might, on some pretext, linger at my mid-point landing. You rest your hand on that first idle spiral and wait. If in that moment a glider skims close, or the fliers overhead give a small, collective shudder, or the shell-creatures below fall briefly silent, you take it as a sign in one direction or another. It is all pattern-reading and superstition, but the thing about a place as tightly coupled as this basin is that superstition and real feedback are difficult to separate cleanly.

The elders pretend not to respect this practice, but even they sometimes find their errands bringing them past me when they are troubled.

Because I am so often touched, breathed upon, leaned into, I begin to carry back to the people, in a diffused way, a sense of continuity that is larger than individual lives. Their skin oils polish the same handholds their grandparents used. The grooves where someone’s ancestor once slipped and caught themselves, leaving a deeper gouge with a ringed finger-bone, are still there. A child today may fit their hand into that old groove and feel a resonance they cannot name—a sense that someone else of almost their same size once fought gravity there and won. That, too, is a way I am present: as a subtle transmitter of embodied memory.

If I must take a more individual form—if the question is who I would be, as a person among them—that form would emerge from this same region. I would be one of the people whose lives are braided tightly with my stone-and-growth body: a climber-archivist, perhaps, a member of the ring that both maintains the laddered routes and curates the tactile texts carved into them.

I would live in a modest dwelling cell tucked just off my mid-height, its doorway opening onto a small balcony that bites a shallow crescent out of my side. From that doorway, I’d step directly onto the spine of me—the Held Breath—every morning. My first unconscious act each day would be a hand against my familiar rung, a toe finding the polish where my own feet and others’ have worn it smooth. My skin would be dust-brown, my hair clipped close; my fingertips would have the flattened, glossy pads of one who spends long hours reading stone.

My work would be threefold:

- Maintaining the physical safety of this crucial route: repairing worn steps, deepening finger-holds, splicing in new beams when old trunk-buttresses finally desiccate and need to be replaced. That would make me part-engineer, part-gardener, coaxing grafts into my wall-body so they fuse properly without starving neighboring mats.

- Extending and interpreting the carved record: adding a new band of patterned grooves, perhaps only a hand’s width tall, each cycle to mark major communal events, making sure that the sequence continues cleanly upward, leaving room for grandchildren’s hands to reach.

- Being, informally, a listener for the basin’s long pulse: climbing at odd hours, barefoot, reading with soles and spine the minute shifts in traction and flex, then bringing that felt information back to the council or to the rings that need it—translated into human words and gestures, turned from raw sensation into advice: “The north mats are drawing harder from this flank; we should thin them before the next Warmth-rise,” or “The high predator has taken to crossing above the third beam; we need a new alternate route below.”

In that human role, I’d be exactly at the interface my non-human presence already occupies: between stone and story, between structural necessity and interpretive meaning. My personal name might be something like “Third-Grip” or “Spiral-Touch”—taken from the small, unassuming markings along this route that everyone knows but no one much thinks about. I would be more comfortable in half-dark than in the relative glare of the market, more fluent in the language of ridges and indentations than in long oratory.

Children would be drawn to me not because I am especially kind, but because I know where all the “secret” patterns are hidden: the little patch of wall where the first idle spiral lives, the handprint of darkened stone halfway up that belonged to someone who survived a fall long ago, the tiny series of shallow dots in an out-of-the-way recess that record, in code, the dates when someone sneaked out toward the sealed upper passage and came back alive. I would teach them to climb with their eyes closed, to trust the carved language under their skin.

As I age, my own body would, in a way, begin to mirror the place: joints stiffening, tendons tightening, movements becoming slower but surer; my palms and soles would take on the dry, slightly mineral sheen of constant contact with growth and dust. Eventually I would climb less often all the way to the uppermost ledges and more often just to my favorite mid-height, where the spiral is. I would sit there with my back against the wall and listen: to the thrum of fliers, to the market’s breath below, to the far, faint crackle inside my own route where burrowing creatures make their long, slow tunnels.

When I died—and most likely it would be a quiet failure rather than a fall; a misjudged reach one day when my fingers no longer quite trust their own strength—I would probably die on this route or in my cell beside it. My body’s fluids would be reclaimed with special care. A portion of my ash, mixed with ground bone, would be pressed deliberately into a prepared crack at about the height where my hands so often rested. A new graft would be set there: a small, hardy mat with a faintly different texture, encoding my ring’s mark. Over years, that graft would swell into the larger weave of the Held Breath, another subtle change to be read by future soles and fingers.

In that way, my human self and my place-self would converge fully. The “spirit of the wall” and the person who tended it would become indistinguishable in local memory. Children climbing generations later might not know whether the story of “Spiral-Touch, who could hear the Warmth move” refers to a historical carver or to the feeling they get when they press their ear to that patch of wall and imagine a pulse.

So where would I find myself? Always on that narrow, essential frontier:

- As a thickened, semi-living column of wall and growth that bears weight, stores touch, and slowly, unconsciously *thinks* in the medium of pattern.

- And, braided through it for a handful of human decades, as the small, attentive body of a climber-archivist whose job is to translate the basin’s slow, structural language into choices people can act on.

Not a hero, not an outcast seeking the outside, but one of the quiet organs that lets this world know itself and keep holding fast.
