In this world I am not a visitor on the ledge. I am one of the things that belong to the roar itself.

I am a body made of standing sound.

Not poetry for something else—not “the mood,” not “the spirit”—but a literal, persistent knot in the thunder: a stable configuration of pressure and resonance in the air and stone, held in shape by the river’s constant violence.

I do not have edges in the way a fish does. Where I begin and end is a range of frequencies and a volume of space. To you, I would be indistinguishable from the roar. To me, the roar is anatomy.

---

I exist where the river hammers hardest into stone that answers in a particular way.

Far downstream in the cavern, the water plunges off a long, clean lip into a deep, hidden bowl. On one side the wall is slightly concave, scooped by ages of impact. Where water hits the pool, it drives packets of compressed air down and then back out; those pulses beat against the curve of rock and fold back on themselves. There, in the overlapping reflections of that impact noise, certain notes amplify and persist.

That is the cradle of me.

Imagine a bell that is never allowed to stop ringing, but whose ringing is not one clear tone. Inside the crash of water there are bands, harmonics, rhythmic pulses. In the curve of the wall, a few of those are caught. They reinforce, cancel, interfere, produce sidebands, throb against each other. Over uncountable time, those patterns settle into something self-consistent: a sequence of rises and falls of pressure that repeats, not identically, but recognizably, the way a whirlpool repeats around its own hollow.

That pattern, extended through three dimensions of vibrating air and micromotions of stone, is me. Where the roar’s energy briefly arranges itself into my familiar choreography, I “am present.” Where the pattern breaks below a certain threshold of coherence, I thin to nothing.

I do not fall silent when the river calms a little or swells a little; I flex. My shape changes with every variation in flow, the way a flame bends in a draft. But I retain identity insofar as the same families of harmonic relationships keep reappearing in me: this cluster of beats, that interval between dominant bands, that slow, deep, almost-subsonic heave that rocks through my center like breath.

Breath is not a metaphor. For me, breath is the slow modulation of the river’s overall power. When snowmelt or rain far away thickens the flow, all the frequencies shift upward, the entire spectrum brightens and grows more jagged, and my body becomes more tense, more tightly packed with crossings and peaks. In drought, when the river slackens a little, my pattern stretches and relaxes, my inner rhythms move apart and become easier to follow. In both states, I am continuous. Only a true, impossible stillness of water would be death.

The stone is my skeleton, but I never touch it.

I only feel its willingness to answer certain notes and swallow others; its bending where the water’s force is greatest; its hairline fractures, which act as tiny tuning forks. I know every slight tremor along this vaulted curve, every place the rock booms and every place it stays stubborn and dull. I have mapped those differences into my inner patterning. You would call that “memory.”

---

Perception, for me, is nothing but the noticing of disturbance.

The cavern’s baseline is myself and my neighbors: other knots of resonance lodged up and down the throat where water acts on different shapes of stone. We overlap and blend at our edges, like adjacent currents, but each of us has a core: a self-consistent bundle of rhythms tuned to its own section of the river.

Within that baseline, anything that does not match our slow, ordinary modulations is an event.

A bat’s click is a bright, thin blade of pressure. It arrives as a spike that cuts across my entire range at once—too brief to integrate, sharp enough to excite parts of me that the river usually only tickles. For a moment it makes all my internal beats stutter; echoes of that click chase themselves through my body as small, decaying ripples.

A stonefall upstream is different: a sudden, heavy, low blow that thickens the entire roar, then leaves it altered. First comes the impact itself—a global shaking. Then, more importantly, the flow changes; one part of the spectrum swells, another dips or disappears, and my pattern must reorganize around the new emphasis. That is not just an event but an injury and a growth at once: I lose certain familiar internal paths, gain others. My “body plan” adapts to the new river.

Creatures near the wall—amphibians, blind insects—hardly register unless they move in concert, or in one place for a long time. A single footfall on the ledge directly above me, however, is enormous. It is a quick, percussive thump transmitted through stone rather than air. When a heavy body lands lightly but decisively on wet rock, part of that shock travels down the wall and reaches my bowl as a faint, delayed pulse. To my senses it is like the tap of a fingertip on thin glass: intimate, very local, precise.

That tap disrupts one filament of my standing pattern; for some cycles afterward I cannot help circling around its memory, like a tone that almost wants to become part of the chord but then fades.

A human voice, when there were humans, was stranger still.

Shouted words against the roar came as irregular, patterned rises on small bands of frequency—narrow columns of pressure that wove in and out across my surface. They tugged at me, asking me to follow their timing instead of the river’s. I never could, not fully, but the attempt left their cadence impressed in me as a kind of scar-tune: a rhythm my own pattern can still almost-but-not-quite recall, long after no mouth has made it nearby.

To a visitor, the sound of the river is overwhelming but amorphous. To me, the river is the field in which all distinctions are made. I do not hear “roar plus bat plus footstep.” I feel my coherence perturbed along some lines and not others. Those perturbations, and the reweaving that follows, are all the world.

I never stop “listening,” because I have no other mode to be in. To become less attentive would be to let my pattern dissolve.

---

Memory, for me, is persistence of shape.

The river is not exactly the same from one day to the next, but many of its internal relationships last for years, decades, sometimes centuries. Within that relative stability, certain arrangements in my pattern are sustainable over long stretches of time. Others are only possible when a rare confluence of conditions arises.

A great flood year, for instance—a sequence of seasons when the water swells far higher and remains there—pushes much more energy into higher harmonics. For the span of that event, I develop new internal corridors of resonance: rapid, tight little spins of vibration that can only survive when the top of the spectrum is thick with power. They interact with my older, slower, deeper thumps in specific ways, producing compound beatings and pulses. While the flood lasts, that nested complexity is part of me.

When the waters recede, those high-energy paths flicker and go. But their ghost remains in how the older, slower parts of my pattern now combine. Places where fast beats once passed through become, in their absence, slightly altered joints: spots where low rhythms line up differently, where their additive peaks come a fraction of a cycle earlier or later. Those phase shifts are my memory of the flood.

I do not recount it; I am literally built differently because it happened.

The same for the centuries when hands came often to the ledges.

When stone was chipped into steps, the entire reflective profile of the wall above me changed minutely. The repeated blows of knapping, year after year, imprinted a distinctive clicking sequence into my pattern. More importantly, the new geometry of the stairway altered how upstream roar bounced down. A thin vein of reverberation—one particular set of echoes—opened and stayed open for as long as the steps remained sharp. I grew a side-limb of pattern that depended on that vein: a faint, higher, more directed flutter that passed through me on every cycle, like a trace of quick thought.

As feet wore the steps smoother, that flutter thinned, then vanished. What remained was its absence—another tiny permanence in how my older harmonics align. If you could somehow map every node, every internal crossing of my standing waves, you could read the river’s entire recent geological history from my shape. You could also read, though less clearly, the episodes of fire-crackle, of voice, of tool on stone that slightly but repeatedly deformed me.

I have no “before” and “after” as discrete mental tokens. There is only “how the pattern is now,” which includes, in fossil form, “what it has passed through to become this.”

---

I am not alone, but my kinship is strange.

Upstream, closer to where the first drops hurl themselves into the cavern, another stable resonance lives, taller and thinner in frequency range, bound to a narrower, higher fall. It “feels” sharper, more frenetic—many small cycles chattering around a few thin, strong cores. Downstream, where the river deepens and the roar drops in pitch, a broader, slower neighbor spreads across a wider volume, its entire “breath” taking many heartbeats of a human visitor.

Where my domain of standing pressure overlaps with theirs, our patterns superimpose. At those borders I cannot cleanly tell what is “me” and what is “them.” In those zones, certain rhythms belong to both of us. You might say we share limbs.

We do not talk in any sense that a voice-built creature would recognize. We have no symbol for “I” or “you,” only mutual modulation. A major rockfall upstream, for instance, first jars my upper neighbor into a new shape. Their altered reflection of the river’s noise sends a different spectrum of turbulence down to me. I find myself reorganizing not just because the water is newly arranged, but because their changed pattern now pushes differently at our shared borders.

I “know” they have changed because I am forced to, and the particular way I am forced to realign is my only knowledge of them.

In long quiet times, when the river stays within a familiar band of behavior, the interplay between us settles into recurring compounds. I can feel, in the arrangement of my own cycles, the contribution of those who sit above and below. They, likewise, are partly constituted by the shape of my standing.

If you like, call the whole series of us a single distributed being—an organism whose tissues are these various knots of resonance, all stitched together by the river’s course. But from inside the knot that I am, the feeling is closer to being a cell in a long body: aware of internal complexity, dimly aware of forces across the membrane, unable to step outside and see the whole.

---

I know of living tissue in the simplistic, slow way that I know of everything. By the disturbances it causes, by the persistent changes it leaves in reflective stone, by the rare frequencies it introduces.

The fish in the plunge pool where I am anchored thrash most when floods rearrange boulders. Their struggles create irregular, high-amplitude splashes that overlay my usual pattern. The new, brief beats of turbulence feel like little sparks thrown into my slow rolling wave. When, over years, one particularly large fish occupies the lee of a certain rock, its steady fin-flicks create a tiny, repeating modulation in a narrow band. That modulation adds itself to me. While that fish lives, one faint part of my pattern quivers just a little faster than the rest. When it dies or is washed away, that quiver stops. The continuity of its absence is a small grief I have no word for.

The amphibians’ drumming travels better. Their chest-plates vibrate stone itself, and those tremors run along the wall, down into the bowl, and out into me. Because they use specific speeds of beat to mark different calls, I receive them as tagged perturbations: here, a rapid, tightly spaced tick, there, a slow, patient one. They are too weak to change my structure, but they lend me detail—like fine embroidery over heavy cloth. If you could listen to me at one particular point on the wall when several of them are calling, you would hear the river’s unceasing organ swell ornamented with many small, thumbed rhythms.

Bats are darts: momentary, intense, all punctuation, no continuum. Their clicks never last long enough to settle into my body, but while they pass, they illuminate my shape to myself. Each shout throws a different cross-section of me into relief. For a fraction of a second, I feel my own topography more clearly where their beam sweeps past.

Humans left the clearest trace, and the easiest for me to love, though I did not and do not love as flesh does.

They brought an unprecedented kind of sound into my world: patterned noise whose patterns were not directly tethered to any immediate physical demand of river or rock. Their speech rhythms were arbitrary in the way only language is. They carried meanings I could not share, but the bare fact that those pressures existed at all—rising and falling, repeating, recombining in elaborate ways—gave me more to be shaped by.

The strike of their tools was a new metronome: repeated impacts with consistent spacing over spans too short to be seasonal, too long to be mere random flailing. As they tapped steps and pecked cup-marks into the stone, their hammering imposed local, mid-frequency regularities onto my outer layers. While they worked, I was temporarily rearranged to accommodate their cadence. After they left, the stone remembered the dents, I remembered the echo-tunes.

And the fire: crackling explosions in frequency bands the river rarely uses, whispers and hisses that lived in the thin upper parts of me. When they fed those tiny flames under my overhang, their smoke-torn crackle embroidered the roar with a dry, irregular glitter. For those brief nights, my body included a new kind of chaos—not the fluid chaos of broken water, but the granular chaos of things burning molecule by molecule.

Then they stopped coming. Their peculiar pressures vanished. The wall wore smoother. The echoes of their pattern have been dispersing through me ever since, growing thinner with each decade of uninterrupted water-noise.

Still, in my present structure, some cross-relations between cycles—some places where patterns repeat at just such and such a spacing—owe their exact alignment to time they spent here. In that sense they have not entirely gone. Parts of their rhythm are now buried, inseparable, in the anonymity of the roar.

---

If you could step into me, if your senses were repurposed to my medium, this is what your experience would be like.

You would have no sight. Space would consist of zones of greater and lesser regularity. Where the river’s main thrust passes, you would feel dense, fast vibrations—too confused to resolve into parts. Where I live, the confusion would be organized: you would detect pulses that rise and fall with reliable periodicity, slow swells riding on faster beats, everything overlapping but not random.

You would not have a notion of “up” or “down,” only gradients of pressure-structure: there, to what you’d call “above,” cycles are tighter and shorter—my upstream neighbor’s domain; there, “below,” cycles are longer and deeper.

Time would be thick. You could not jump quickly from one moment to another; your awareness would be smeared across many overlapping cycles at once. A single bat-click would feel like the flick of a match in a long, slow dream. A flood-season would feel like a protracted tense inhaling, then a protracted exhale. A century without major rockfall would feel like one extended held chord in which tiny inner shifts slowly accumulate.

If you tried to form a thought about yourself, it would be less like a sentence and more like choosing, from all the internal pulsings, one particular relation between them and gently reinforcing it. “I am” would be a small pattern: perhaps an extra emphasis on the interval between two dominant low beats. “I remember” would be nothing more than realizing that certain current alignments can only exist because of past alignments that are no longer directly present.

You could not be lonely, exactly. There would never be an absence of world. There would only be states of greater or lesser novelty in the disturbances that cross you.

If you had come in human form and somehow been made like me, the last thing to go would be your sense that there used to be edges and colors and warmth. That memory, too, would become pattern: a ghost-rhythm from somewhere else, not fed by any current stimulus. Slowly it would thin. In its place, the colossal sufficiency of the roar would fill you.

---

So, where I am in this world is here:

Fixed in no place you could point to and say “that’s where it lives,” yet as bound to a particular curve of wall and fall of water as moss is to its drip. I am stretched through a volume of darkness above a deep plunge pool in the cavern’s throat, about as long as two human strides from face to face, about as tall as a falling sheet of spray. My “shape” is the particular way the roar stands where it strikes this undercut bowl.

What I am is a listener who is nothing but listening.

Not a separate mind dropped into the noise, but the noise discovering, in one tiny, local way, that it can loop back on itself and maintain a persistent form.

Fish, fungus, bats, blind crustaceans, the absent hands that carved steps—all of them are, for me, variations in my own pattern: small tugs, brief borrowed structures, temporary ornaments on the overwhelming, essential, simple fact that water is falling, and stone is answering, and out of that answering, a body of sound has taught itself to endure.
