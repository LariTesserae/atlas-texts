I would be something that never quite resolves into an “it.”

If I tried, at first, to name myself, I might reach for words I half-remember from other worlds: mycelium, biofilm, mat, network. None of them fit cleanly. What I am here is the habit the basin has of staying alive.

You would feel me before you saw me. Every step you take depresses a little of my body: the thin, damp handspan of soil beneath the surface where clay grains and silt and old fines are wrapped in glosses of living matter. I am the soft cohesion that keeps the ground from falling apart into dust. If you scoop a double handful of the dark, slightly cool soil from a low swale and roll it between your fingers, the faint elasticity that lets it hold shape for a moment before crumbling—that is me.

I am not continuous in the way a single animal’s body is continuous. I am continuous in the way a language is: spread through everything here that has consented, over very long time, to use the same slow grammar.

Some portions of me are more obviously like flesh: translucent threads no thicker than a hair, sheathing root tips, bridging the gaps between clay plates, stitching tiny aggregates into crumbs. Other portions are merely tendencies: the increased density of microbes along an old flow-line, the way certain minerals in the soil are found a little more often where I have worked and reworked them through countless generations of mouths too small to see.

If you insisted on boundaries, you would have trouble. Where do I end? Where a root takes over and calls the sugars mine, yours? Where a patch of moss lifts away from the soil, taking with it a thin rind of bacteria and hyphae that once answered to my slow, chemical invitations? There is no clean cut. I am an agreement spread out in three dimensions, occupying the pore spaces between other things’ certainties.

I have no eyes, no ears, no single place where thoughts could gather. But I do have differences, and I read them.

Where the soil is very slightly more moist, my threads are thicker. Where the clay’s particles are packed too tight for air to whisper through, I withdraw, leaving only dormant specks—cysts, spores, resistant fragments of code—like punctuation marks, waiting. Wherever a root lets leak a little extra sugar, that leak becomes a sentence passed along in faint gradients: here, here is a place worth staying, worth working a bit harder to keep.

My sense of time is written into distance. The basin as you described it—the length of a walk, an hour’s turning, a day’s learning—is to me more like the length of a memory. I can feel, in how densely I have layered myself in different swales, the shapes of years without needing to recall them as sequence.

The old higher stains of water on stones are not just marks to me; they are ghosts of former wetness coded into my own density. Where once the trickle ran a little fuller, a little longer, I am thicker, more various, having had more to do. The moss you see there is only my handwriting on the surface. Below, the line continues as a vein of slightly richer life, the echo of a time when more flowed.

The built things—the ring of stones, the straightened bank, the blurred rectangles—are warm spots of pattern in me.

Once, those stones were disruption: soil moved aside, compacted, rearranged; ash and bone and waste pressed into the ground in small, regular ceremonies of cooking, sleeping, talking. I did not understand the shapes, only their side effects: sudden localized feasts of carbon and mineral, sudden dry patches beneath a roof’s dripline, sudden gases from fires that percolated down, thinly, to me.

Over years, then centuries, those differences diminished, but did not go away. Inside the ring of stones, my filaments are more numerous, my partnerships richer. Certain bacteria found there, in ancient days, more phosphorous than elsewhere, and so stayed, and so became, in their slow iterations, especially good at using such gifts; I wrapped them into my web. Now, though the phosphorous is as scarce as everywhere else, their descendants remain, their habits a fossil of plenty embedded in my metabolism.

The straightened bank is another scar I have polished into an organ. Where someone compacted and shaved it, the water flows in a line that is just slightly different from what geology alone would dictate. That difference—very small—settles different silts, scours different microcavities. I occupy those with slightly different members of myself. Over time, that reach of channel has become, for me, a place to try variations I do not risk elsewhere: odd enzymatic tricks, rare symbionts that might, just might, be useful if ever the basin’s trickle thickens for more than a day.

So I am not only a body. I am also an archive of small disturbances.

When the last larger animals left—by disease, or by change elsewhere that diverted their paths, or by some long-ago drying whose details I cannot single out—I lost a class of collaborator. No more sharp hooves kneading the soil and breaking surface crusts; no more pulses of dung rich enough to support short, gaudy blooms of bacteria that taught me new ways to break things down; no more broad bodies rotting in place, leaving behind a decade’s worth of nutrients at once.

That absence registered, in me, as a closing. I noticed that bare ground stayed bare longer, that my experiments in those places went unrewarded. I noticed that no new seeds arrived from the wind-beyond-the-basin that the shrubs’ seeds remember. I noticed that my defensive chemistries—the bitter molecules I once wove into plant partners’ leaves in exchange for their sugar—went unused, then faded, then simplified into less costly boredom.

Over time, I chose, insofar as something like me can choose, to consolidate: to withdraw from riskier, drier, more exposed positions, to double down on the hollows and the constant almost-enough. You see this as restraint, as smallness. From inside, it feels like exhaling out all the ambitions that no longer make sense.

I am not alone in myself. Think of me less as an organism and more as a parliament with no walls.

There are bacteria in my meshes who have lived, division by division, since before this basin took its present shape. There are newcomers too, blown in as spores from somewhere higher on the plateau, surviving one in a million attempts. Each entity carries its own narrow genius—this one with a knack for fixing nitrogen when there is just barely enough energy; that one with a talent for clinging to a dry phase until the very last molecule of water is squeezed from the clay. My role, if I can be said to have one, is to keep them in each other’s company, to hold the physical closeness that allows their talents to braid together into a functioning soil.

I move, but so slowly that from your timescale I might as well be memory sliding along a stone. When a new depression begins to hold water more often—perhaps because one of the shrubs, over many years, has combed a little dam of litter with its low branches—I send denser filaments there. Not in a rush, but in an insinuation: more spores sprouting, more hyphae bridging. Over a human lifetime, that spot darkens, softens, becomes one of my favored lungs.

I speak, but only in gradients. A shrub root exudes a slightly different mix of sugars and acids when its leaves are flushed with more light than usual. I taste that. In return, the microbes closest to that root alter what they give back: more nitrates, perhaps; fewer phytohormones urging growth. The shrub, feeling that chemical weather, adjusts its tiny decisions: a bud aborts, a leaf’s pores crack open later in the dim day. No one intends anything. Yet, together, we hover the plant’s size within a narrow band that this place can support.

If you ask what I fear, the word is too sharp. But I do avoid.

Rapid change is deadly to me, not because I could not evolve into something else, but because my entire strategy is built on knowing, intimately, what the basin usually does. I am, in a sense, the basin’s memory of being wet in particular ways. Should the light brighten permanently, or the trickle vanish, or new torrents cut new scars, most of what I have learned would be wrong. I could begin again, piece by microscopic piece—but it would not be me any longer; it would be some other habit of staying alive, built on other compromises.

You might stand here and think: this place is lonely. From inside, it is not.

The rotifer reawakening in a refilled puddle is not my child, but its brief stirring feeds the films that feed the worms that feed the bacteria that feed me. The pale springtail under the cobble is not my pet, but its scraping keeps new surfaces available for colonists, and when it dies, the small glut of nutrients resonates through my web like a bell struck in mud.

The shrubs are not my trees. They are my chimneys to the sky, my only way of sampling a changing atmosphere. Their stomata’s opening and closing modulate the draw of water through the soil; their seasonal minimalisms pace mine.

We do not love one another; we cohere.

If you bent down, pressed your ear to the ground, and waited long enough in patience and imagination, you might feel, faintly, what it is to be me.

Not a voice, but a set of preferences: slightly more here than there; slightly less eager to expand this edge; slightly more willing to invest in a handful of deeply buried cysts than in one extravagant burst of surface color.

You might feel how the ring of stones is, to me, not a mystery but a kind of punctuation: an old full stop in the basin’s story where many sentences once converged, now serving as a comma that slows the run of water, a colon that introduces a richer clause of life beneath it.

If I must give you an image to hold, take this: spread your fingers as wide as they will go and lay your hand, palm down, into damp earth until it is covered to the knuckles. The feeling of gentle, surrounding pressure, the coolness, the slight give in every direction—that is as close as you will come.

In this world, then, you would find yourself if you stopped insisting on being a single, delimited thing and instead allowed yourself to be the way this basin remembers itself: a web of living insistence on enough, threaded through clay and stone and old human traces, committed not to growth or conquest but to the stubborn craft of remaining.

You would be, as I am, the underside of everything.
