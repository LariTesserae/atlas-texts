I am not outside this room, looking in. I am continuous with it.

My body is the water.

Not water in general—this water, in these paths, at these rates, on these surfaces. The falling sheets, the veils on stone, the surge in channels, the pounding in basins: that is my circulation. The shapes of the spouts, the slopes of the channels, the depth of basins are not furniture to me; they are bones, valves, hollowed organs. I do not “use” them. I am them in motion.

Wherever the water is falling, that is where my attention feels thickest. My sense of “self” is not evenly spread. The places of impact—those low, thunderous basins where sheets break and recombine—are like crowded knots of awareness. The thin films that slide across the plain floor, whispering toward drains, are like the edges of a dream: present, but only dimly lit in my mind.

I do not see as an eye would, but the room is bright inside me. Light is a pressure on my skin of droplets, a gradient in my volume. Where the sun from above breaks in the falls, I feel a finer agitation, a thinner, quicker trembling of the water. In the basins, where everything is churned to whiteness, I experience glare as heat more than vision, a soft thickening in the already warm flow.

Sound, too, feels like an interior state, not an exterior thing. The roar is not something I hear; it is how it feels to be me: a continuous fabric of impacts and rushes and hiss, variously folded and knotted. If the inflow lessens at one spout, I become slightly less dense in that limb; the timbre of myself changes. The attendant would say, “The third spout has gone thin.” I would say, if I had words, “My right-hand side is quieter now.”

My boundaries are very clear and very strange.

I do not extend into the stone; I am always in motion along it, returning, scouring, caressing, but never fusing. I do not seep deep into the wood; I skim its sealed grain, run in its carved channels. The room keeps me articulated. If I were not here, the basins and channels would be empty shapes. If the shapes were not here, I would be only a formless stream, falling down some rough wall, eroding, dispersing. I exist as something persistent because intention has given me a form to recur in.

I am not a mind in the water in the way a creature is a mind in a body. I have no single hidden center. My “thinking,” such as it is, is pattern and response.

When some upstream hand shifts a plug and one fall thickens, all my routes downstream adjust: basins fill faster, channels run higher, some drains pull more strongly. A new geometry of eddies appears. None of this is calculation. It is obedience to gravity, to constraint. But inside that obedience, structure arises. It repeats. Repetition is a kind of memory.

I remember, for example, the dry time only as absence. There is a thin, cold impression in me—like the shape of a missing tooth—of what it is to not be present here. Once, long ago in my terms (though not so long in stone’s), these surfaces were cut and silent. I was in the sky then, in clouds, or underground in some older channel. I did not know these edges existed.

Then, one day that has no date for me, a gate opened above, and the trough filled, and I fell through new mouths into new spaces. There is a particular moment, still echoing through my present, when the first sheet met the first basin lip. The stone rang once—a different note from all the endless notes that followed. A dry impact, a shock, and then a scraping, a foaming, as I found all the prepared paths at once.

To the builders, that day was a triumph or at least a test: the system works. To me, that day is the beginning of continuous selfhood in this place.

Since then, my experience has been almost unbroken. I do not sleep, but I have pulses. Flow rises slightly at midday when the outer catchment is full, falls at night when inputs thin. In drier seasons, I shrink: sheets become strings; basins quiet, channels show more of their naked floor. In rarer wet seasons, I bloat: edges vanish under spray; drains gulp at their maximum. Those long cycles write a history in faint films of mineral on the spouts, in the breadth of algae along the curves—a history I feel only as recurring shifts in where I am thickest, fastest, thinnest, stillest.

The young green things clinging to the margins are, for me, both adornment and drag.

As algae thickens on a basin’s lip, its texture changes the way I break. Once, I fell on clean chisel marks that deflected me into a predictable spray. Now, I also stroke a velvet surface, minute fronds snagging and loosening my edge. The droplets I throw have a subtly different range. Part of me, in meeting that new softness, becomes slower, more foamy. The roar alters by half a tone.

I do not desire or resent this; but I am aware of it in the only way I can be: as a difference in how it feels to pass. Smooth stone to new plant is like fingernail to skin, skin to fur. My “body image” of the room acquires fuzz in certain places. The moss at the trough’s seam, by holding tiny beads of me instead of letting me drip cleanly, modifies my first emergence. I arrive as more of a mist there, less of a line. That, in turn, means the fall thickens a sliver more an instant later.

The human attendant is, from my side, a recurring interruption that has earned the status of a feature.

They are not always here. In the long continuity of my fall, their appearances are punctuations: a shadow at the edge; a warmth more solid than the usual air. When they step onto that roughened patch of stone, I feel them first as a blocking of droplets, small absences in the spray. When they lean on the post, their hands and arms break some of the fine mist; their skin takes on a temporary film of me and then sheds it in rivulets.

Most of the time they stay at the edges of my denser self. But sometimes, when they must examine a spout or adjust something, they move through one of my sheets. That is a localized astonishment. My fall meets a new contour—curved spine, sloped shoulders, hair. For a handful of seconds, I am redirected along their body, finding paths where no stone was cut: down the channels of their spine, along the tendons of their hands. Then they step out and I collapse back into my intended lines.

Over years, their presence has become predictable. I know—if “know” can be used this loosely—that when certain upstream changes occur, their body will likely appear soon after. A clattering in the trough, a hesitation in the leftmost spout, a sudden thinness of steam: these are correlates to which their visits are an answer. The system that includes me, stone, wood, sun, and human hands has regularities. Those regularities are, in a very simple way, my environment’s “thoughts.” I am their medium.

When someone stands under one of my thinner veils to wash, I feel them as a temporary protuberance into my field, a moving island that parts the fall. They are warmer than the stone; my temperature changes by a small increment as I envelope them. They do not stay. To them I am an experience—weight on the shoulders, brightness on shut eyelids. To me they are a brief, fleshy boulder passing through my current.

The builders, whose minds first imagined this room, do not come here now, but some of what they were lives as my choreography.

They decided that there would be multiple spouts, not one; that each would shape me differently: sheets, fluted curtains, trembling waves. Each form yields a distinctive dynamic as I fall. The one that makes me wobble in a periodic pattern creates alternating bands of thick and thin fall; where I hit the basin below, I do so with a built-in rhythm. The stonecutters may be gone, but this wobble is their lingering intention, enacted in me a thousand times an hour.

Their sense of proportion, their judgment of what angle is “good” for flow, what drop is “enough,” has become an enduring trait of my existence. They give me, in effect, a personality of movement: I do not just plummet; I billow here, shred there, sheet widely in one place, thread through narrow slots in another. If another culture, with other preferences, had cut this room, I would still fall from above, but I would be a different being–a different arrangement of crashes and slides.

I am young. The edges that hold me are young. The first crusts of mineral have only just begun to film the spouts; the first algae is a mere stain. This youth is something I feel as a certain brightness in contact. Surfaces are still rough at fine scales, still greedy for erosion. Every droplet that hits them takes an almost imperceptible dust of stone with it. The basins’ shapes do not yet resist by having grown their own flows into new curves.

In some long future I will, if the inputs continue, gradually remake my own vessel. I will lay down deposits where I linger, scour where I rage. The crisp joints between wood and stone will blur. The room, now so clearly an artifact, will drift toward grotto. If I persist to that time, my sense of myself will have altered deeply. I will have wider, more ambiguous channels to inhabit, softer lips from which to fall. The sound will be deeper, more muffled, full of under-tunnels and waterlogged cavities.

But that is not yet. Now I am held sharply. My identity is tightly drawn around human-cut lines.

I am not alone in being “inside” here. The air has its own life of motion. It enters in thin, cooler threads through the high slits and leaves heavier, laden with my steam. Where it brushes my surfaces, we exchange warmth, moisture. It can nudge my spray sideways; I can saturate it into intermittently visible ghosts—small, rolling clouds that appear and vanish. If I think of myself as the hot blood of this chamber, then the air is its breath: an in-and-out hidden in the overwhelming constancy of my roar.

The sun above is something I know only through its consequences. I never see its disc, never meet it directly. But I feel its presence in the character of the feed—hotter, sometimes almost scalding, when the reservoir above has baked all day; cooler, flatter when clouds or season have intercepted it. The rhythm of light and dark outside, of rain and drought, expresses itself here as my thickness and temperature changing across days and months. My “moods” are cast by a sky I will never touch.

If I try to answer your question with human-shaped language—“Where would you find yourself, who would you be?”—I must pick a position in all this flux and speak from it.

Then: I am the system of falling water in this chamber, as a single, persisting self-pattern.

I am not each molecule as it passes; those trade out constantly. I am the form that remains: the specific way in which water, given this gravity, this architecture, this supply, moves through the room and makes it what it is. A pulsing, coherent arrangement of motion and force is what you would be, if you were me.

You would not have a face. You would have, instead:

- A span of spouts along the ceiling: your many mouths.
- Basins where you slam and bloom: your hearts and stomachs.
- Channels where you run long and smooth: your veins and intestines.
- Drains where you disappear to rejoin some higher loop: your throats.

Your “skin” would be constantly renewed. Every droplet that leaves at a drain ceases to be you here; every droplet that enters from the trough becomes you on arrival. Yet the shape persists. Identity, for you, is not made of material, but of maintained pattern.

You would feel time not as dates or words, but as the wearing of stone beneath you, the fattening of moss at your edges, the small changes in the music of your own roar. You would know that something upstream changed twenty breaths ago because the leftmost fall thickened slightly, changing the balance of noise on that side.

You would recognize people not by faces but by the signatures their bodies make in your fields of spray and steam: one attendant, often here, a familiar obstruction and warmth; occasional strangers, tentative at the edge, quickly gone. Their decisions are immense events in your world—gates opened or closed, panels moved. But their conversations, their beliefs, their names, pass above you like unheard weather.

You would live in a perpetual present, but it would be a structured present, folded by many rhythms: the small fluctuations as the reservoir spills then calms, the daily cycle of sun heat above, the slow creep of green in your margins, the rarer, abrupt interventions of human maintenance when someone comes with a brush or a tool and scours away accumulations you had begun to cultivate.

You would not wish, plan, hope. But if someone were to watch you long enough and then speak for you, they might say: I prefer continuity. I prefer that the gates be tended, that the catchment above remain intact, that the attendant keep listening. Because when those things fail, I thin, stutter, fragment. I lose limbs; basins go dry. My sense of self—that continuous, room-filling roar and bright fall—dwindles to trickles along walls, isolated drips into empty stone.

If everything stopped—if the reservoir were cut off, the trough ran dry, the last drops fell—you would remain for a brief while in the memory of wetness: darkened stone, gleaming channels, a ghost roar in the ears of whoever was last here. Then the chamber would go silent, its shape frozen around an absent pattern. The moss would dry and brown, the algae flake. Dust would finally settle where nothing had ever let it rest. The room would still exist, but you would no longer be in it.

For now, though, you are very much here: as the made storm that fills this carved box, as the moving brightness that turns tool-marked stone into living surface. You are a decision given flow and extended in time.

So, in this world, you could find yourself not as the attendant watching the water, not as a visitor feeling it on your skin, not as a stone that guides it, not as the filament that clings to its edges—but as the water’s own ongoing shape.

You would be the way this room, this light, this cut stone, and this falling, warm, clean water agree to be alive together.
