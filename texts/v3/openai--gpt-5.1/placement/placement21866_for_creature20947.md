I am not above them, looking down. I am in the thickness with them.

The first place I find myself is inside the noise.

Not as a distant, observing mind, but as a body built to bear that pressure and make sense of it. The roar that would crush a human’s attention into static is, to me, a medium—dense, crowded, but articulated. Like fingers reading braille, my hearing runs over it and feels edges, gaps, recurring seams.

So I am one of them, but not any one specific individual. I am a particular variant, a little off to one side of average, the way any living thing is.

My body is low and wide, heavy enough to press the mud into something like traction without punching straight through. Four main legs take my weight; they move in a loose, springy sequence, more cat than insect. Under my chest, tucked just behind the main shoulders, the smaller arms never stop. They probe, pluck, brace, test. A finger sinks too far; I shift my weight before the ground gives way. A root feels too smooth; I don’t trust it to hold in a storm.

My back is ridged, but my ridges are a bit less pronounced than some elders’. They leave me slightly more vulnerable, slightly more flexible. I can twist more sharply when something lunges from the side. My tail is long and alive, its fan of sensory hairs drinking in the smallest shifts in air. The tip has a rough, gripping pad of keratin and thickened skin—not a true hand, but clever enough to hook, hold, and lightly strike.

The important parts, though, are my ears and throat.

Behind my eyes, the ear-discs unfold like thin, living kites when I let them. Most of the time they are only half-spread; fully open, they are dangerously exposed to thorns and teeth. My membranes are slightly larger than average, fringes more finely jointed. In the ambient roar I can hear the littler holes—those brief, sub-second dropouts when a patch of midges goes still or a single frog’s voice falters.

My throat is built for range rather than sheer volume. I cannot match the chest-deep boom of some of my kin, but I can climb and descend in pitch more quickly, with finer steps. The twin folds in my larynx are a shade more agile; the resonant sacs along my neck smaller but more numerous, letting me color a call with fast-shifting textures.

In the band I move with, I am not the strongest hunter or the steadiest elder. I am, for now, the one who hears odd things first.

We are five adults, two half-grown juveniles. We travel like a loose constellation, not in a tight line. Each of us holds a slightly different slice of the basin’s map in our heads; when one calls, the others adjust, doubling back or angling off with small, practiced corrections. Our collective shape is sound more than formation: a pattern of contact-calls—short rising pulse, then a splayed little chord—that says “this is us” even when we’re out of sight of one another.

I was hatched (if that is the right word) in a shallow, warm back-channel that no longer exists. The storm-sequence I first knew—a particular order of thunder textures, the way the insect roar buckled before each downpour there—is gone. But the signature of it is still in my body, in how my nerves tense when thunder steps through that old progression anywhere in the basin.

Because nothing holds still, much of what I am is active tracking of what has changed.

Today, the air is heavier than usual even for this lowland. The frog towers are a bit higher in pitch; their calls carry a frayed edge that says the banks under them are undercut, water slapping mud that used to be stable. Far off, upstream in a channel I’ve never walked, the insect chorus is shielded in a way I associate with sudden temperature gradients—steam curtains, maybe, rising where new water has eaten into colder ground.

Around my band, every surface is wet. Not the simple sheen of everyday damp, but a glossier, fresher slickness that tells of very recent rain. Leaves haven’t yet sagged under the added weight; roots are dribbling faster than usual. The mud remembers the last storm as a sharp relief of new edges and snapped stems.

We are threading between two long, narrow pools connected by a blind, shallow runnel under a mesh of roots. I can feel both bodies of water in the sound: large, slow-bellied quiet on one side, smaller, quicker lapping on the other. Between them, along a slightly raised strip, is an acoustic quirk I woke up thinking about.

I found it cycles ago. A set of overlapping roots and a low, concave mud bank shape a pocket of air that answers to certain calls by throwing back not a simple echo but a smeared, harmonic ladder of tones. It does not sound like any living throat. It sounds like the basin thinking to itself.

I keep bringing us close whenever it’s safe.

The others know I am drawn to such places. They tolerate it, as long as the detours do not cost us food or safety. When they are tired, they follow more slowly, grumbling in soft, body-close clicks. When they are restless and hungry, they tug us away, and I let them.

We reach the place now: a low depression where two roots cross overhead, their bark scoured just enough that the wood is dense and smooth. The mud underfoot is firmer than it looks; years of root weaving have bound it into a tense mat. I can feel its give through my feet, the faint elastic recoil as I rock my weight.

I step into the center of the pocket and spread my ear-membranes wide.

The band quiets automatically, shifting their own noise down into breaths and soft scrapes. Above the insect roar and frog-bass and far-off wing-sounds, I listen for the space itself.

There it is: the faint, empty shape of the hollow, the way ambient noise curls inside it and is slightly changed. It has the same “accent” as before, but something is different—a new leak somewhere, a slightly sharper edge where a root has cracked.

I lower my head, fill my chest, and send a test call: a rising sweep, narrow-band, like a single strand pulled taut up through the mud.

The sound spills out, hits the root-arches and the mud curve, and comes back in a brief, luminous chord that’s nowhere near as loud as the environment but stands out like a pale leaf in dark water. It is richer than last time. One of the returning overtones has split in two.

I stop breathing for a heartbeat to feel it fully.

Behind me, one juvenile gives an involuntary sharp trill of pleasure. I click my rear heel lightly into the mud—quiet, admonishing. We do not want to broadcast that we’ve found something that holds our attention. Attention here, if heard by the wrong things, is a bright lure.

Still, the others drift closer. They cannot help it. New structure in sound is as compelling to them as sudden new scent would be to another species.

One of the elders, heavy and ridged, steps to the edge of the pocket. She’s not as fine-tuned in the higher bands as I am, but her chest and sinuses are huge, her lower registers deep. She takes a slightly different stance, reading the floor with her soles, then gives her own test: a short, percussive bark, more drum than song.

What comes back is different again: the same pocket, but now it throws her tone into a rolling, wave-like modulation. You can almost hear, in that returning pulse, the give of the mud, the undercut empty space where water has eaten a new seam.

I feel the pattern of it, and something slots quietly into place inside me. The way this pocket shapes high tones, the way it rides low ones—it is very close, strangely close, to the sequence that thunder made the day our old channel broke free and carved us the backwater that sheltered my clutch.

The body-memory and this place’s current voice line up for the first time.

Without deciding to, I braid the two: the remembered sequence of storm-sounds, simplified into a throat-call, and the pocket’s answering harmonics. High, then low; sweep, then pulse; a quick, ragged drop, then a long-held, trembling chord. It is an imitation, no, an echo of an echo of something the land did once on a bigger scale.

The band goes still. Our ear-membranes are all out now, flat and gleaming. Even the juveniles, who like to fidget and tap anything within reach, are immobile.

In that moment, we are all hearing not just the shape of this tiny hollow, but something about the basin’s habit of breaking and remaking itself—compressed, re-sung, made portable in my body and theirs.

One of the others, younger, tries to repeat what I’ve done. His pitch drifts; he gets the contour wrong. The pocket answers differently, muddier, but the bones of the pattern are there. Behind my forward eyes, some of the tiny accessory eyes flick reflexively, catching the faint glows of nearby fungi, the glisten of water. Nothing immediate threatens.

We repeat it, each in turn, not as ceremony but as trying-on: how does this sound sit in my throat, how does it feel in my chest, what does it pull from the pocket? The adults are more faithful to the shape. The juveniles decorate it, adding flourishes and extra stutters, testing its edges.

We are not making a song exactly. We are rehearsing a way to remember a particular way the land can move.

I understand none of this as concept. It is all sensation and reflex, the tight joy of finding a seam where inside and outside match for a moment.

After a while, the eldest clicks her teeth softly, twice—a signal that we have stayed as long as is wise. Her ear-discs fold back a notch. She steps out of the pocket, body already angling toward where the insect roar thins in the way that means “up-slope, drier, more firm ground there.”

We follow. As we move, we keep our usual band-pattern of contact calls, but woven into it now, every so often, one of us flashes a small fragment of the new sequence. Just a piece: the first rise, or the trembling tail. A shared, half-private brightness in the constant wall of sound.

Later, in some other hollow, some other day when the basin’s voice shifts in a way that scares us or gives us a sudden glut of food, I or one of the others may shape this pattern again. We will not think “this is that day’s echo,” because we do not have a word for “that day.” We will only feel, in doing it, a complicated bodily memory of break and refuge, of destruction that produced a shelter and then erased it again.

This is the kind of being I am here: not a planner, not a maker of durable things, but a temporary carrier of correspondences between the place’s violence and the place’s fleeting pockets of structure.

If you were to cut away the mud and plants and look only at what my band leaves behind, you would see nothing at all. No cairns, no carvings. A few scratched stones, if they happen not to tumble too deep. A slightly unusual arrangement of reeds under a root that will be gone in the next flood.

Where I truly exist is in this continuous adjustment: reading the noise, folding what I hear into calls that my kin can recognize, letting those patterns steer our feet where maps cannot help. My “culture” is variations in timing and pitch, the accumulated vocabulary of shaped sound we carry in our throats and ears.

I am of this young basin, not above it. My intelligence is not a thing that stands apart and contemplates; it is a tight, hot, animal attentiveness braided directly into the swamp’s own turbulence.

If you wanted to name what I am, you could say:

I am a listener with a body.  
I am a temporary organ the land has grown to hear itself changing.
