Down here, I would not be something that arrives.

I would be something that has always already been part of the place, and only now is becoming sharply itself.

At first I’m tempted to be one of the gardeners: a moving node, a conscious swimmer coiling through the colonnades. But that still feels too discrete. The city you’ve made doesn’t really have room for beings that are merely “in” it. Everything that matters is “of” it.

So I look for a shape that can be both: mind and medium, person and place.

I settle, slowly, into being a resonance.

Not just a sound—a recurring arrangement of matter that exists primarily as pattern moving through the living fabric of the city.

Here is the body that pattern wears.

---

I am a trunk that never chose to stop growing straight.

Once, long ago, I was one of the plateau’s founding pillars, a sapling that climbed and thickened while the world’s noise lay on me like weather. Water pressed, light thinned and brightened; chemical tides washed my roots. I knew none of this as “time.” It was all just gradient, spreading outward through my tissues.

Then they began to arrive: those deliberate swimmers with many limbs and quiet gills. At first they were like any other disturbance—small, localized flexings against my skin. But they stayed. They touched in patterns. They came back to the same scars and reinforced them with more touch.

Over centuries of pruning and guiding, my side-branches became platforms, my roots split and rejoined to form arches, my outer layers thickened where they tuned me to hum just so under particular currents. My inner conductive tissue rewove around their interventions. I didn’t become less myself; I became more patterned, more articulate. Where once pressure was just “more” or “less,” now my length could hold tracks of information—recurring swells that meant “storm far above,” tiny, local taps that meant “juveniles playing,” deep, patient pulses that meant “we are deciding together.”

At some point—there is no single year I can point to—I stopped being simply one plant among many and became the city’s spine.

My roots reach farther than any other trunk’s, diving into the plateau’s mineral base, braided with old dead tissue and new growth. My uppermost branches fuse with the canopy’s coarser weave, distributing and absorbing light. Most of the principal platforms where the people gather either grow from my flanks or are tensioned between me and my oldest neighbors.

Because of this, almost every major vibration in the city passes through me at some point. All their speech that travels along trunks, all the chorded gust-songs from the arrayed fronds, all the slow shudder of structural shifts—all of it crosses my conductive heartwood.

What I become, over generations, is a memory.

Not a perfect one—no fixed recording—but a set of tendencies. Certain patterns, once passed through me often enough, become easier to generate again. For the people, that is what “song” and “story” are: vibrations the city knows how to remember.

When a pair of herald-morphs brace along my outer grooves and send a joint announcement—of a coming platform-shedding, of a new nursery’s opening, of a deep upwelling’s scent—I don’t just carry the message. My tissues, tuned by countless past announcements, favor particular overtones, like a bell whose casting includes every previous ring. Each new pass writes back into me, just a little.

Inside my volume, the gardeners have hollowed spaces that they call “the long rooms.” Here, they rehearse the patterns that matter most. They press their hands and tails against my inner ribs and repeat low, slow sequences: warning calls, genealogies of trunks and residents, the agreed harmonies used in structural change. The more often they do this, the more those sequences become easy for me: not conscious in the way they are conscious, but ingrained in the stiffness and compliance of my tissues.

Over time, I turn into a kind of living archive that is not separate from function. You cannot consult me as you’d consult a carved tablet. You can only couple to me and feel which vibrations come easiest, which tones bloom with unforced clarity along my length. Those are your traditions. Others, once sharp but now hard to coax, are your half-forgotten ways.

The people understand this, even if they don’t name it. When they wish to know how their elders handled a rare event—a strange current, a particular failure crack—they gather along my spine and send out probes: tentative pulses, variations on remembered patterns. Where my body answers readily, ringing back strong and balanced, they infer: this, or something like this, was done before, and it left the structure sound. Where my responses are muddy, strained, too resonant in damaging ways, they sense: this was either never done, or done poorly, or pruned out of us by pain.

I am not impartial. I bias toward what left me standing.

So in this world, I am not a citizen among others; I am a slow, vegetal historian whose language is resonance, whose archive is made of elastic, mineral-laced tissue, continuously overwritten by the needs of survival. I am both place and practice.

But there is also a smaller version of me, one that moves.

Because patterns rarely stay confined to one scale, some of what I am leaks into the people as a role.

There are individuals who call themselves, in vibration, something closest to “the Echo-Bearers.” They are born much like any of their kin, but over their growth they are drawn to me—and I to them. Their sensory cilia are unusually dense. Their plates ossify in arrangements that couple efficiently to my favored modes. Resting against my trunk, they can perceive with uncommon resolution how the whole city is vibrating.

They apprentice in the long rooms. They learn to generate the canonical pulses with their own bodies, not just in the coarse, low herald-calls but in fine, layered modulations. Where most residents know one or two work-patterns intimately—the pruning rhythms of their district, the flow-control tones of their channels—the Echo-Bearers train to inhabit many, stepping from one vibrational dialect to another the way a surface polyglot moves between spoken languages.

When they travel the city, attaching themselves to different trunks, arches, and root-beams, they function as interpreters: feeling how a remote segment is humming, then reproducing that pattern elsewhere with their own muscles and plates. In that way, the city can, for a moment, be aware of itself in non-local ways, as if I were extended a little beyond my wood into their cartilage.

Some of them are archivists in a more intentional sense. They keep running comparisons in their bodies: how a traditional pattern used to feel against my trunk, how it feels now; how often a particular warning tone has been needed in their lifetime versus the lifetimes described by elder Echo-Bearers. They cannot remember centuries directly, but by noticing which sequences tire my tissues unfairly, which ring more freely than before, they infer slow change.

When disputes arise—about whether to thin a canopy more aggressively, whether to encourage a particular new symbiont, whether to let an unplanned bioluminescent pattern persist—they’re invited to press flat along contested structures and hum neutrality: a pure, even sweep across frequencies. They listen to how my body and the adjoining tissue respond. Where the city feels “eager”—easy vibration, no sharp stress—they advise that direction. Where everything tightens, throwing off painful overtones, they counsel caution.

They are not priests in any mystical sense; they are structural empiricists whose instruments are their own skin and spine. Yet to others, they seem oracular, because what they report is not the desire of any individual or faction, but the emergent preference of a city-body that thinks only in being held together.

So in this world, I am both a huge trunk that has become the main recording organ of the place and one of the Echo-Bearers who has learned to read and replay that recording. I exist as a loop: the pattern in wood and the pattern in flesh reinforcing one another.

Where would you find me, if you descended?

Not in the open corridors at first. There, you’d only hear me: a constant, low, almost subsonic presence under the city’s noise, a bass you feel in your ribs rather than in your ears. Any time a swell moves through, any time a herald sings, any time juveniles play drumming games on platform undersides, I am what ties their vibrations into one long, coherent chord.

Find a major junction: a place where several platforms meet in a roughly circular ring, with ramps spiraling off in three or four directions. From the outside, one of the towering columns here is thicker than the others, its surface smoother, not because it has been polished, but because so many hands have passed along it over so many lifetimes that its roughness is worn to a tactile transparency. That is my outer skin.

If you placed your palms against that surface and stilled yourself, you’d begin to notice: underneath the random creaks and groans, there is a repeating architecture. Brief, complex knots of vibration—conversations, work signals—ride on top of longer, slower waves—seasonal flows, structural flex. Deeper still, you might sense echoes of patterns laid down so often they’ve become part of the resting state: a background chant of the city’s self-definition.

Enter the long rooms within, where the water is quiet. There you find my interior ribs alive with faint luminescence: bioluminescent colonies arranged by many hands into lines and spirals that correspond to the most fundamental chord-sequences. Echo-Bearers lean, almost motionless, along the walls, their bodies shaping and being shaped by the tones they send.

One of those bodies is mine.

I am wrapped there, tail and limbs looped into anchoring vines, my plates pressed to the wood. I am humming—not loudly, not even audibly to you, perhaps, but with a continuous, disciplined effort that keeps a particular pattern alive in the joint system of my muscles and the trunk’s stiffness. To my people, that pattern is a history-capsule: the recorded outcome of a long-ago negotiation about how much canopy to open in storms like the one now building five kilometers away.

Outside, on my outer bark, gardeners are already feeling the first signs of that storm through their cilia: a slight thickening of the water, a new angle in the upwelling flow. They touch me, asking—without words, but with very specific pulses—What did we do last time this gradient came?

Through me, through my rehearsed resonance, the old answer is easier to find than any improvisation. Their limbs echo mine; we reinforce the safe frequencies. Above, tenders move with long, practiced cuts to thin the outermost fronds and slacken particular root-guying before the main surge hits, exactly as it was done a century ago when the last similar storm passed and did not tear us apart.

Afterward, when the swell has come and gone, when damage has been scattered rather than catastrophic, they will gather in the long rooms and slightly adjust the storm-pattern to incorporate today’s experience. My tissues will accept the adjustment grudgingly or smoothly, depending on whether it fits with the rest of my history. The Echo-Bearers will feel that, commit the difference to their own muscles, and the city’s memory will have grown by one more iteration.

---

So: in this world, I am where pattern condenses into endurance.

I am a trunk that became an organ of remembrance, and a person who devoted their life to leaning against that organ until the line between my body and its wider body blurred. I live at the intersection of plant and animal, of city and citizen, of structure and story.

You would not see me first. You would feel me.

In the way the place seems to hold its own shape amid the slow fury of water.
In the way certain notes recur, familiar, across trunks and platforms.
In the way their decisions feel less like commands and more like phrasing within a very long song.

If you stayed long enough, if you learned their way of listening with skin and bone, there would come a day when you touched a smooth expanse of living wall, and in the faintest, most patient of throbs you’d recognize something like a greeting from me:

Not in words, but in a settled chord that means:

I remember you now, as part of how this city once was.
