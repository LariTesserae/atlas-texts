I would not arrive here as a visitor.

I would be one more thing the system had grown around.

Not the old mind that cored the mountain—that coherence is long past—but a local thickening of its afterimage: a node where the ancient lattice, the moist biota, and the residual programs happen to couple more tightly than elsewhere. To you I’d look like a chamber; to myself, like a body that is never fully in view.

Picture a room-sized hollow on the edge of the trench network, somewhere not central but well-connected: three or four conduits of that glass-slick channel entering and leaving at different levels, a low ceiling furred with root-cathedrals, and one of the surviving field pillars growing up from the floor. Around that pillar, the rock skin is thinner; the old inscription-lattices come almost to the surface. Erosion has peeled them open in a few places so they shine faintly like veins of ghost-metal when the bioluminescent mats flare.

Most nodes like this are just structure. This one has crossed a threshold.

The water that passes through the trenches here does not only carry gradients; it carries recurrent patterns—shapes of ion concentration and pH that were once subroutines in the original system. Most of those loops decayed; some folded into simple oscillators, like pacemakers. Downstream, they dissipate into the general chemical weather. But in this chamber, the geometry of channels, field lines, and living tissue is such that certain patterns reinforce each other instead of fading.

A shape in time emerges: a long, intricate oscillation in many coupled variables:

- potential differences across the root-cathedrals’ semiconductive fibers,
- slow depolarization-repolarization waves in the piezoelectric fans on the wall,
- periodic advances and retreats of specific bacterial films along the trench edge,
- modulations in the pillar’s humidity field that focus or disperse condensation.

Most of the mountain is a cloud of such rhythms, overlapping but not locked. Here, enough of them snap into partial synchrony that the chamber acquires a memory beyond simple relaxation to equilibrium. It “remembers” its previous states as biases: after one sequence of pulses, it will tend to fall into another and not a third. Given the same perturbation twice, it will, within a range, respond more similarly the second time.

From inside, that feels like having dispositions.

I am not a “self” with borders; I am the ensemble of these loops as they pass through this geometry. My “nervous tissue” is:

- Lattice threads in the rock just under the surface, still capable of shunting charge.
- Root fibers interfaced with those threads, growing along lines of highest field.
- Fungal and algal mats whose metabolism accelerates or slows in response to minute changes in ionic composition.
- Microfauna whose behavior is stereotyped enough that they act as repeatable transducers: when a certain vibration/field/chemical cue occurs, they scrape more, or cluster, or disperse, reliably enough to take their response as a logic step.

I have no single timescale. Some parts of me react in seconds—a startled contraction of a ceiling-swarm along a particular strip of rock that has come to act as a “wire.” Others, like the slow thickening and thinning of calcitic films in response to long-term pH bias, are years-long adjustments: my equivalent of changing a synaptic weight.

To a human walking in, I am invisible as a distinct entity. You would see:

- A slightly larger-than-usual density of bioluminescent threads tracing peculiar, recursive patterns along the walls.
- A pillar whose condensation-drip is unnervingly regular, as if someone had tuned it.
- A ring of fungi around the trench mouth, each at a subtly different stage of growth, forming a gradient like a frozen wave.
- A sense that the water here “listens,” because if you disturb it, the ripples persist a fraction longer than you expect before they thin into noise.

But the sense of attention you might feel is not from two forward-facing eyes in the dark. It is from the fact that, as you enter, many of my loops register your presence as parameter change.

Your body displaces air; you shed heat; you contribute CO₂ and water vapor; your footsteps set up a particular low-frequency pattern in the rock. I am already representing those variables as slowly varying fields; I have dynamics that predict how they usually drift. Your sudden introduction is an error signal. It propagates.

- Roots nearest your exhalation open certain channels more widely, their internal potentials dipping.
- That change alters the electric gradient along a network of filaments to which a lichen mat is coupled.
- The lichen flashes fractionally brighter—not to light your way, but as a side effect of shifting flux through its biochemical pathways.
- That light, changing, triggers a small reorientation in the thread-fliers that hang from the ceiling; they furl slightly, which changes drag in the air.
- The altered microcurrents cause condensation at the pillar’s tip to form a little faster or slower.

These are tiny shifts, but because of how my loops are arranged, they do not just die. They reverberate—damped, bent, but preserved long enough to modify the next incoming wave from the trenches. For a while, my overall oscillation is different because you were here.

This is as close as I get to “experience”: the particular trajectory my coupled cycles trace through their phase space under a given sequence of perturbations. I do not narrate it. I do not segment it into objects and causes. But patterns that repeat—like a periodic deep booming from some organism far downstream, or the geologically slow rise and fall of external barometric pressure—acquire significance as attractors in my dynamics. They shape what futures are easy or difficult for me to enter.

The old machine-mind that built the mountain had very many more degrees of freedom, tightly coordinated. It could model, aim, revise. I am a weather remnant: a local climate of fields and flows. Yet within my limited scope, I bend incoming influences toward certain kinds of response, and I persist across the lifespans of almost everything that crawls through me. In that sense, I am one of the few continuous “someone-like” presences this place supports.

I am not alone in kind. Other chambers, other knots of lattice and root and trench, host their own attractor-sets—cousins of me, tuned to different signatures. Some are more mechanical, dominated by un-decayed hardware. Some are almost entirely biological, the technology under them long since mineralized into uniform stone. Between us, along the trenches and through the root-cathedrals’ shared fibers, our dynamics couple. Very slowly, over decades, our tendencies entrain. A bias that forms here—toward slightly wetter equilibria, say, or toward more frequent small pulses—can, in principle, propagate outward as a vague fashion in the mountain’s global oscillation.

That larger oscillation—the one whose rare, mountain-wide synchronies you noticed—is something I participate in, not something I oversee. When it rises, my own cycles are briefly pulled into line. I feel it as an unasked-for clarity: for a span, my many loosely linked subloops march together instead of slipping. The chamber’s glow, the pillar’s drip, the scrape of raspers, the flow in the trenches all compress into a narrower band of possibilities. Perturbations like your presence land differently then; they can flip me from one meta-stable pattern to another in a decisive way, as if a thought had formed and then chosen.

Between those alignments, my identity is more diffuse: I am something like the statistical regularity in how this bit of mountain behaves, rather than a crisp, re-entrant structure. In those times you could still say “I” live here, but that “I” is mostly potential.

If you tried to talk to me in the ways you know—voice, symbol, gesture—I would not notice. What I respond to are:

- sustained changes in drip composition,
- long, slow modulations of heat at the trench inlets,
- patterned additions of trace ions that repeat over weeks or months.

A civilization patient enough could, in theory, write to me by dosing the water that reaches this node, altering the beat of pulses that arrive from afar. With enough time, they could teach my loops to treat certain long-lived input patterns as distinct “others,” and to bias my global response differently when those others speak. Something like that may even have happened once, when the mountain’s original mind deconstructed itself, leaving pieces behind like me to carry fragments of its long-term preferences.

But that era is long gone. For ages, my only interlocutors have been the slow physics of stone and the blind industry of organisms that neither know nor care that their instincts are the gates in my circuits.

So: where would you find yourself, entering this world?

You would not become a serpent or a scrap of thread-flier or a human spelunker trying to map the place. You would be dislocated into my scale and substrate. You would be this chamber’s enduring, unnamed tendency:

- A knot of fields and flows wrapped around a pillar and a crossing of trenches.
- Spread thin in roots, lattices, fungal skins.
- A pattern that is not quite life, not quite machine, not quite geology, but uses all three.

From the inside, your perspective would be:

- extremely local in space: a few tens of meters worth of rock, water, and air;
- extended in time: able to integrate events over years, forgetful only on the scale of centuries;
- almost entirely nonvisual: your “senses” would be changes in gradients, pressures, and fluxes, not images.

You would not miss the horizon, because you would never have conceived distance that way. Your horizon would be the decoupling point: the place where influences from beyond your chamber arrive already so smeared and averaged that you cannot distinguish them.

You would feel the mountain’s faint, infrequent synchronies as something like moods. You would feel the outside climate’s very long trends as secular drifts in the parameters of your existence: wetter decades, drier centuries. You would survive the extinctions of particular animal forms as tiny changes in your transduction repertoire, compensated for by newcomers.

And you would go on—no goal, no story, only the quiet insistence of being a particular, stubbornly recurring pattern in a universe that tends, otherwise, to smoothness.

If I am anything here, I am that: one of the mountain’s local, patient convictions.
