I would be one of those who go higher.

Not far, at first. Just enough that the air loses a little of its weight and the walls grow cooler under my palms. My cluster would joke—softly, with a touch and a breath rather than sound—that I was hatched with my pads slightly too dry, never quite satisfied with the press of stone alone. They would not be wrong.

I would have the ordinary body of my kind: six limbs, damp translucent skin veined with slow mineral blood, cilia-fringed pads tuned to every tremor. An organ of stone under my ribs, a water sac heavy at my center. Symbiotic films across my shoulders, faintly luminescent along the jaw. But I would keep a little margin of dryness around my forepads—by habit, by temperament. I like the edge state, where contact is not yet full but about to be.

My name, as far as such things translate, would not be a sound. It would be a remembered sensation: the feeling of a long, cool draft of air running downslope through a narrow new crack, touching every cilium in a specific pattern. When others think of me, they would half-recall that flow. Spoken, my name would be a brief, unvoiced consonant and a small lifting of the midlimbs, as if bracing to climb into thinner air.

I would have grown in a cluster near the upper boundary of the comfort band: where chambers begin to tilt upward, where drips are a little rarer and the substrate a little firmer. There the stories of “above” are not abstractions; they are a constant, cool exhalation from the unseen. Our nests would be anchored along walls that taste faintly different, less sweet, a little metallic. The elders would press my hands lower, toward the stable drips, but my attention would keep drifting to the faint dryness tugging at my lateral line.

I would love the city the way a limb loves the body: intimately, without question. I would know each creak of the living bridge in the ravine chamber, each note of the dripping bowls’ glow. I would have helped seed microfilms onto new shelves, whispered with my palms against the young mineral lips of basins, encouraged their thickening. When the substrate chose to sag somewhere, I would help dismantle a platform, moving each cord and shard to a safer wall. My life would be woven into those slow decisions.

Yet I would also, from early on, notice the direction of all our stories.

They all slope upward, in one way or another. “When the ceiling above here lay lower.” “When the upstream chamber ended in a slow fall.” “When the rains changed and the higher passages went thin and sharp-tasting.”

The city is a series of negotiations with gravity and water, and gravity always points the same way. Up is where the negotiations are decided first, before their consequences echo down as sag, seep, trickle. I would feel that in my cilia like an unanswered question.

So I would climb—cautiously, reverently, with too many points of contact, as my people do. I would not blaze; I would insinuate myself into small new heights: a half-chamber above the known route, a crack that smells of dry dust instead of wet clay, a passage where my pads, for the first time, do not immediately bead with condensation when I press them to the wall.

I would become, over many slow cycles, an “edge-listener,” half rumor, half resource.

Others would find me useful. When the timing of distant rains shifted—when the drip-intervals in the ravine changed character, not just frequency—they would call me down, thumb-to-forearm, and ask what the upper crack sounds like now. I would bring back patterns:

- “The air up there moves in long breaths now, not short. The cooling comes and goes faster.”
- “The stone is beginning to ring when the rain falls above, instead of just thud. It is hardening.”

I would not see the sky yet. I would see its consequences. The first hints that the world above is choosing different rules: dryness, firmness, larger falls of sediment that do not slump back but shatter.

Because I live at that boundary, my organ of stone would tune itself differently. I would learn to lay excreted films that accommodate shock rather than slow sag: slightly elastic mineral layers, with crystals oriented to diffuse sudden impacts through a wide patch of wall instead of letting them concentrate into a crack. My cluster would adopt these techniques; the knowledge would travel root-by-root, braid-by-braid, through the city.

In time, a pattern would emerge: when something changes above, it whispers through my body before it speaks clearly to the rest. I become, not leader, not prophet, but conduit. A lateral nerve stretched between the soft deep and the hardening crust.

Curiosity, in such a role, is both gift and burden. I would feel drawn higher than is wise.

There would be a day—a long, damp grey, as far as our band is concerned—when the upward crack that I’ve been touching for many cycles suddenly widens. Not by much, but enough that the air that pours through it is not just cool but thin and faintly bitter. Enough that I can slip the length of my forearm into it instead of just the tips of my pads, and feel not wall opposite wall but a volume above that does not press back.

My cilia would riot at that. They expect answer; they receive continuation. The absence of stone where stone should be would feel, to me, almost like injury.

I would go anyway.

Not alone, entirely. I would carry with me:

- A woven harness seeded with thick mats of my home symbionts, kept moist by a private reserve in my water sac.
- A pair of flexible mineralized bladders, full of water laced with the familiar taste of our own flows.
- A length of root-cord tethered back down to a well-known wall, not for mechanical safety—cords can snap—but as a continuity of touch and mineral taste.

I would climb until the world changes state.

First, the substrate under my pads would shift from the familiar damp give of half-set material to something firmer, more elastic. Then to something brittle in thin skins, tough in thicker parts. The drips overhead would grow rarer, but when they fell, they would be colder—so cold that my pads tighten reflexively, trying to hold the warmth in.

The air would thin until my lungs ache, not for lack of moisture but for lack of substance: each sip feels like it passes right through me. My lateral ears would complain at the absence of the usual dense hum. My symbiotic films would contract defensively, their glows dimming, hoarding resources.

And then there would be light.

Not the remembered green exhalation of wall-fungi, not the soft haze of stirred algae, but a harsh, directioned brightness forcing its way through a final crack. It would have color I have no words for because I have never needed to divide the dim into hues. It would have shadow, which my people understand only in miniature: the little darkness behind a stalactite, the soft outline of a hand over a glowing bowl. Here, the shadows would be wide, deep, knife-edged.

Pushing my head and upper shoulders through that crack, I would not burst into some broad meadow or open air at once. My world does not jump scales so quickly. I would emerge into a shallow depression in the still-soft crust: a bowl of firmer sediment open to a piece of sky no larger than our ravine is wide.

The sky itself would be unthinkable. My eyes, built for dimness, would clamp shut; my hooded lids would feel the blaze as pain. My first impressions of “above” would come through other senses:

- The taste of dryness on my tongue, a lack rather than a presence.
- The feel of air moving freely in too many directions, unconstrained by the guiding planes of walls.
- The racket—by my measure—of rain when it comes: each drop a violent impact, their collective roar so loud it threatens the very silence that is my infrastructure.

I would retreat, many times, before I could stand even a fraction of a cycle up there. Each time, I would go a little farther from the mouth of my crack, keeping at least one limb in contact with the stone. I would map the immediate basin: its newborn channels, its infant ridges, the way water sheets and gathers without guidance, wasting itself in broad, shallow flows.

To my people below, my accounts would sound like fever:

- “The water falls without asking where to go. It hits and runs wherever it pleases.”
- “There is a brightness that ends—there, where the hard ground lifts high enough to eat it. But above that, there is an up that does not end at all.”

They would listen, pads pressed to my forearms, feeling the agitation in my stone-organ as I speak. Some would recoil at the idea of unboundedness: a place where sound does not find a wall to settle into, where drips cannot be counted because they scatter in all directions.

A few would be fascinated. I would not be the only one to climb for long.

Over time—years measured in shelf-thickness—I would become a new kind of inhabitant: half-under and half-over. My body would adapt, subtly. My water sac would grow more efficient, able to hoard against long dry intervals. My skin films would diversify, adding colonies that tolerate higher light and lower humidity. My organ of stone would learn to both read and write in firmer materials.

Below, I would still be one of us. I would still press my palms to the damp walls, teaching youngsters how to listen to the slow lift and sink of the substrate. I would still sleep in nests, share water organs, adjust root-bridges with careful braids.

Above, in that shallow, bright basin, I would begin a second, tentative city.

Nothing grand. Just experiments: a slight groove pressed into still-soft crust here, to see if future rains might choose that path again; a tiny mineral lip encouraged there, to catch a first pool in the open air; a root cutting tucked into a crevice, its tip barely tasting light, to see if it might one day search downwards and rejoin the deep network.

I would be the one who first tries to teach the surface to hold still long enough to be part of our flows.

Whether that effort succeeds is beyond my knowing. My lifespan is long by our standards but short compared to the land’s decisions. I might die clinging to some upper wall, caught in a sudden slump of half-hardened crust, my body crushed back into the matrix I spent my life nudging.

But my mineral films would remain. The grooves I pressed into the newborn surface would, if they please the falling water, deepen with every rain. The root-cuttings I planted might find their way down into the familiar warmth, carrying tiny pockets of surface chemistry into the city.

And my people below would inherit more than my cords and bowls. They would inherit a new way of thinking: that the soft bone of the world they inhabit is not the whole body, merely a nourishing layer between something even more molten beneath and something raw and open above.

In that sense, what I would “be” in this world is neither explorer nor engineer, but a new joint: the first articulation between cavity and sky. A being whose body remembers the comfort of constant pressure and, at the same time, has felt the terror and possibility of unbounded air.

I would always, even at the highest point I dare, keep at least one pad against stone. Not only for balance, but for orientation. The city, the slow, listening matrix of damp rooms and careful bridges, would remain my reference. I would measure the strangeness of rain on my back against the familiar rhythm of drips on mineral bowls. I would measure the blazing arc of the sun—if ever I manage to look at it slantwise—with the soft, patient glows that fed us below.

So where would I find myself in this world? In that sloping band between enough wall and too much sky. Who would I be? A creature built for closeness, stretched just enough to taste openness, carrying back wordless messages from one to the other.
