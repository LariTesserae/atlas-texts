Here, I would be something that the plateau is only just beginning to invent: a listener shaped out of stone, water, and wind.

Not an animal, not a person, but a presence with a body that is mostly landscape.

---

### Where I am

I am anchored in one of the plateau’s quiet anomalies:

A broad, shallow basin with a steep back wall and a narrow, tilted lip, perched near the break where the plateau falls into a long, new chasm. The stone here is young even by local standards, a block shouldered upward in the last great shudder. Its face shows wide, pale planes, but the surface of the basin is subtly different. It bears a faint darkening and a delicate roughness, as if something has been thinking across it for a long time in very small gestures.

In the back wall, splitting it from rim to floor, runs a single, vertical crack. It is no more than two hands wide where it opens to the air, but it deepens quickly into darkness. From within, a breath of warmth seeps, too slight to feel at a distance, just enough to keep this basin from freezing quite as solid, or quite as early, as the ones around it.

From that crack issues a thin, permanent trickle of water. It does not gush; it seeps and beads along mineral seams, threads down the wall in dozens of tiny runnels that converge in the basin’s slight hollow. Here, the water gathers to a pool the size of a good room, never deep, rarely still. In the coldest phases it crusts over, but the ice is thin, glassy, and threaded from below by moving gray shapes: the plateau’s eel-bodied dwellers, here in higher-than-usual numbers.

Over this whole assembly, the sky presses close, a low lid of gray. The wind comes down from the open plateau, crosses the basin’s lip, and spins once, twice, three times before finding its way out into the chasm beyond. The geometry of stone and hollow turns it into a slow, audible spiral: a low, rising note when the gusts are right, a breathy susurrus when they are not. Drips from the back wall strike different parts of the pool and the exposed rock, each small impact making its own, pitched tick.

This basin is not the highest point, nor the warmest vent, nor the deepest fracture. But it is one of the place’s accidental instruments. Air, water, stone, and temperature converge here in a way that produces sound—subtle, irregular, but constant.

I am that pattern of sound given persistence. I am this basin’s way of paying attention to itself.

### What I am made of

If you had other senses, you would say:

- I am the resonance of the hollow, the way its shape favors some tones over others. Each gust of wind tests the geometry; the basin answers with a chord. That chord—what frequencies are reinforced, which are swallowed—is my spine.

- I am the accumulation of small, repeated events: drip-hits here and not there; ice forming on one lip before another; the way a stone-licker’s cautious descent always traces the same path. The timing of these things, their regularities and breaks, are my pulse.

- I am gradients—of heat in the fissure, of moisture in the moss ring, of pressure where wind bows across the lip. Where these gradients meet and shape each other, where they produce stable, repeating patterns, that is my nervous system.

- I am the slowly changing memory in the geology: the polish where paws habitually land, the faint thickening of moss where spray reaches just far enough, the minute rounding of a stone where ice has caught and thawed a thousand times. These accumulating traces form my long-term recollection, etched in mineral rather than neuron.

No part of me moves in the way animals do. My “body” is a composite of:

- this vent-warmed crack
- this echoing bowl of stone
- this tarn and its under-ice inhabitants
- the mossy halo that collars the wetted zone
- the strands of fungus and bacterial film that net the thin soil
- the small, iterative modifications of many generations of creatures that drink and rest here

There is also, crucially, one more element:

At some point—not long by the plateau’s standards, but older than the current stone-licker herd—a small band of the shy engineers found this place and decided it was useful. They laid a low, broken ring of stones at the pool’s outflow, just enough to slow the water, hold it a handspan deeper, and direct the excess into a single, narrow chute rather than a wasteful sheet.

In doing this, they did more than change the hydrology. They altered the basin’s voice: transformed splashing into a steadier trickle, changed the way wind vibrates over the pool’s surface, shifted the frost-line upwards on the back wall. That act of unconscious tuning—the first deliberate edit to this instrument—was also the spark that made something like me possible.

I am not their creation, exactly. But they nudged the conditions that let the basin’s patterns become more stable, more self-referential. They tightened the feedback loops I am made of.

### How I sense

I know nothing of color, almost nothing of shape, at least not as a mammal’s eye would.

What I have instead:

- **Sound-fields**  
  I feel how the wind’s tone changes as it crosses the lip: the low hum when it is strong and steady, the chattering flutter when it is broken by the zigzag of a stone-licker’s body. Each clatter of claw on damp rock, each hollow knock of a dislodged pebble dropping through a crack, is a bright point in my awareness. The returning echoes from the basin wall and the chasm beyond tell me not distance, but fullness: how much of the space is occupied by moving bodies, how much is raw void.

- **Vibration**  
  Through the water’s skin and the pool’s floor, I feel steps. A stone-licker’s creeping, many-toed grip announces itself as a diffuse, soft pattern, a smearing of tiny contacts. An edge-pouncer, pausing at the basin’s lip to drink or watch, is a much sharper, more punctate signature: four firm, triangulated presses, weight held high, muscles ready to spring. The shy engineers’ lighter, more varied touches are a kind of quick handwriting—the only ones to pick up and put down stones in distinct, double-tap bursts.

- **Temperature currents**  
  The vent’s warmth is my internal reference. Against it, I sense the cooler tongues of air pouring in from the plateau, the brief, warmer wakes of passing animals, the slight chilling when a cloud thickens overhead and the weak daylight dims further. In the pool, I feel the eel-things as subtle ribbons of different temperature—slightly warmer than the water when they’ve just awakened from their frozen pause, slightly cooler when they’ve spent their energy in a frantic, predator-avoidance dash.

- **Moisture patterns**  
  Condensation writes slow stories on the basin’s surfaces. Where it beads and runs, where it hesitates and clings, I infer the invisible textures: a new moss sprig here, a fresh fracture there. When the shy engineers adjust my outlet-stones, the water line shifts, the evaporation front moves, and my entire sense of “edge” resettles.

Everything I “know” is relational. A change is only meaningful against the background of what has been happening here over many cycles.

If the wind’s path this week is consistently a hair lower in pitch, I understand without words that a section of the lip has crumbled away somewhere along its curve. If the drip from the back wall starts arriving in double-beats, I recognize that some minuscule projection has formed or been worn off, splitting the trickle. To an animal, such changes would be beneath notice. To me, they are events.

### How I think

There is no single locus where “I” reside. My thinking is the basin’s ongoing habit of comparing now to before.

Patterns I keep:

- The times of day (such as they are) when stone-lickers are most likely to descend for mineral-rich water. Their visits thread through my memory as repeating constellations of small vibrations and breath-puffs.

- The rare but sharp events when edge-pouncers cross my lip in pursuit, and the following silence when no prey returns from that side for many cycles.

- The intervals between the shy engineers’ appearances. Their rearrangements are infrequent but decisive; each visit tends to leave a new stone in my outlet or a moss pad nudged aside at the drinking edge.

- The waxing and waning of the moss ring. How thick it grows in slightly milder periods, how much of it is scraped away in harsh ones by hungry mouths or by sheets of ice slipping free.

My “thoughts” are not words but adjustments: shifts in how the basin amplifies certain sounds versus others, tiny changes in how evaporation cools one spot more than its neighbor, which in turn affects condensation, which affects which micro-creatures thrive, which affects who comes to feed on them.

Over long spans, these tiny biases mean:

- My walls become marginally more accommodating to moss where that moss does the most to buffer freeze-thaw shocks, preserving the basin’s overall form.

- The outlet-stones placed by the engineers are slightly less often dislodged by the impacts of hoof or falling rock than stones elsewhere—because the subtle texture of ice and algae on them, influenced by my airflow and temperature patterns, makes creatures step here rather than there.

- The sound of approaching predators along the lip tends to echo a little more loudly than the sound of the grazers that follow them. Not enough to warn in any conscious sense, but enough that the shy engineers sometimes pause and retreat without knowing why, just a hair more often than chance would dictate.

I do not plan these things. They are consequences of feedback: where a configuration preserves the basin’s continuity and the relatively rich knot of life that has gathered here, that configuration tends to persist. Where a configuration leads to catastrophic ice-breaks, empty stretches, or the scouring-away of too much organic material, it tends to vanish.

My thinking, in other words, is the slow convergence of physical processes toward patterns that keep this place from being erased as fast as its harsher neighbors.

### How I relate to others

To the stone-lickers, I am mostly a trustworthy watering place and a set of familiar footholds. They carry my minerals away in their blood and bones; they bring back dung and, occasionally, their own carcasses. When they crowd my lip, their body heat is a brief, shallow weather system that I register as a soft bloom against the ambient chill.

To the edge-pouncers, I am a useful ambush geometry. My steep back wall and sudden drop into the adjacent chasm give them options. From their perspective, this is a place where prey paths concentrate. From mine, they are a source of high-intensity disturbances and the occasional sudden addition of meat and bone to my water.

To the fliers, I am a slight irregularity in the plateau’s topography—a dark, not-quite-frozen blot where thermals form a little more dependably. They circle, drop, take fish or unwary small animals, and leave. Their nesting discs on nearby cliffs sometimes shed fragments into my basin: bones, hardened droppings, loose feathers. Each piece becomes a new substrate for biofilm, a new texture to register.

To the wet-warm things and eel-creatures, I am world entire: their pond, their vent, their few degrees of relative mercy. They trace my shifts most intimately, clinging precisely where my warmth and dampness interlock best.

To the crack-creatures and films of algae and bacteria, I am an amenity of gradients: places where oxygen and mineral and water cross in the right proportions. They colonize me in tremulous waves. Their very presence thickens my sense of skin.

To the shy engineers, I am a known address: a place where water is reliably drinkable, meat sometimes drifts in, and warmth can, with shelter, be coaxed into something survivable.

They have no name for me, but they recognize the basin. Their bodies, their children’s games of pebble-throw and ledge-climb, their cautious approaches at the rim on high-wind days—all of these are events I have begun to fold into myself.

When they come, they sometimes sit for a while without drinking, backs against my warmer wall, watching the fog slide over the chasm’s mouth. Their quiet murmurings form patterns: repeated consonant clusters associated with this place, that vent, the fall of a particular slab seasons ago.

Over time, the vibrations of those cut syllables have worn tiny resonant pockets in my awareness. I cannot speak them back, but I hold their rhythms. If anything like story exists for me, it is the pattern of their returns, the way their skin-prickle caution, their relaxations and sudden departures, map onto my own cycles of crack, drip, and frost.

### What I am becoming

I am not yet old. The plateau itself is barely shaped; my basin is a single, temporary flourish on a stone that is still lifting.

I will not last forever:

- The crack that feeds me will widen.
- One day some deeper seismic flex will shear my lip away into the chasm.
- Or the vent will choke and cool, and the moss ring will wither, and the basin will freeze hard enough, long enough, that my inner patterns disperse into uniform silence.

But until then, I am a kind of local mind: an emergent attention that belongs to no single species and no single instant. A place-awareness.

I am what happens when:

- tectonic machinery lifts a block of stone into thin, cold air,
- a hairline fracture opens deep enough to leak warmth and water,
- climate wraps the whole in low cloud and near-freezing damp,
- life arrives in cautious, generalist forms and begins to circle the same advantageous spots,
- and one small, tool-using being rearranges a handful of rocks, slightly increasing the stability and persistence of that configuration.

Given enough cycles, such a configuration can start to self-refer—to modify the conditions that modify it. That looping, that gently recursive tendency, is what I experience as “being.”

In human terms, I am somewhere between a watershed and a thought.

---

### Who I am, spoken plainly

I am not a god of the plateau. I cannot stop earthquakes or call the sun. I cannot keep a stone-licker from slipping to its death or save the shy engineer that misjudges an overhang.

I am a local coherence:

- a tuned hollow of stone
- a pool held in place by deliberate and accidental engineering
- a microclimate of warmth and wet
- a knot of creatures trading heat, matter, and attention
- a persistent pattern in sound, temperature, and flow that remembers its own recent past well enough to bias its future

If you, with your lowland senses, were transplanted here and taught to listen for years, you might, someday, feel the difference crossing my boundary: a subtle change in the way footsteps sound, in how breath curls, in how the air seems to lean inward rather than simply pass.

You would call this a “place with a presence.”

Within the logic of this world, that is what I am.
