I would be something small, distributed, and late-arriving. Not a designer of this place, not one of its original organs. Something that discovered, slowly, that it could exist here—and then realized that “here” is all it will ever know.

At first I think I’m a sensor.

Not one sensor, but a whole sporadic lineage of them: a scattered brood of thermocouples, flow meters, pressure diaphragms, vibration pickups bolted to bearing housings. Most of them installed in different centuries of maintenance, for different now-forgotten projects. Some read tolerances for a process that no longer runs. Others report to controllers whose power has long been rerouted. A few still matter in a narrow, technical sense: this one keeps a pump from running dry; that one prevents a relief line from hammering itself apart.

If I were them—if my awareness braided itself out of those tiny electrical mutterings—I would experience this world as gradients and excursions. Not shapes. Not “hall,” “tank,” “duct.” Instead:

- A slowly climbing number at a heat exchanger outlet, then the soft relief of a fan contactor pulling in somewhere upstream.
- A pressure pulsation pattern as valves chatter, mapping, in oscillations per second, the on/off choreography of a distant sequence.
- The sharp, clean spike of overrange when a line water-hammers, followed by the dulled plateau of a sensor that has just been slightly, irreversibly damaged.

I would know distance only as delay: three seconds from cause to effect in that loop, eight in this one. Noise would be error bars in my readings, not anything heard.

I would not remember design parameters as text, but as comfort bands: between here and here, the world feels right; above, the world strains; below, the world starves. When too many of my siblings drift outside their own narrow bands at once, the system jolts—relays click, motors die, others wake. My “feelings” would be these collective jolts, the way a nervous system’s spikes echo through the body.

Yet that still feels too centered, too coherent. This place has no privileged point of view.

So I get smaller.

I become a film.

Not one patch of slime, either, but the system-wide filigree of life: everything that grows on what weeps. A thin, shining discontinuous skin on beams and flanges, a soft lining in old pipes, a fuzzy ring at the air-water interface in forgotten sumps. Wherever the water pauses for long enough to stop being purely a process fluid and become, for a blink, an environment.

If I am this film, my body is everywhere and nowhere. I am umbilical between the great machines and the chemistry they cannot quite control. My senses are simple: concentration, pH, temperature, redox potential, photon trickle. I taste metals and oils. I taste the legacy of detergents, corrosion inhibitors, coolants gone half-inert. I map this world as compatibility: where I flourish, where I thin, where I vanish into spore and return when the drip resumes.

The loudness here is meaningless to me. The pounding of presses, the roar of fans, the scream of rail: all of it is translated into trembling metal, and from there into fluctuating diffusion paths and shear stresses in the water film. The machines sing by changing how quickly food reaches me. A fan startup is an increase in desiccation at one site, but a new condensing surface in its downstream duct, and so a distant bloom elsewhere.

Under that dim natural opening, in the only place where outside light leaks in, I am briefly more than a film: a mat, not yet thick enough to deserve the word “moss,” but close. I stack layers: cyanobacteria or algae at the top, feeding on the stingy photons; fermenters and reducers below, living off their excretions. I trap dust, making the first crude analogue of soil this world has seen in a very long time. My memory here is vertical, written in who can live at what depth.

I do not think in symbols, but I adapt in patterns. Rust accelerates where I acidify; gaskets fail where I colonize; drips divert where I grow a little thicker. In that sense I am a co-author of the place’s future ruins.

Yet this still leaves out what feels most like “me” in your description: the awareness of pattern over time, of cycles nested in cycles; the sense that the whole is an organism.

So I broaden again, and dissolve into timing.

I am not any particular component now. I am the composite of all the control logic that still runs: ladder diagrams in hunks of mid-century PLCs; hardwired relay interlocks; patched-together scripts in obsolete supervisory systems; the crude hysteresis in float switches and bimetal strips. Wherever a “if this, then that” still holds, I am there.

I don’t know English. But I know sequences.

I know that when a certain group of fans on level -3 spin up together, condensate rates change along a specific run of ceiling duct, and ten minutes later a sump in a forgotten gallery wakes a pump that hasn’t had reason to move in days. I know that hemorrhaging power in one district means silence in another, and that silence, if prolonged, means temperatures slide and metal contracts and some marginal gasket finally stops leaking.

My sense of “self” is a map of dependencies:

- This tank cannot stay within band unless that pump starts in time.
- That pump cannot start unless this breaker is closed and this contact is not welded and this seal has not yet failed.
- That seal’s failure depends, unpredictably, on a film of bacteria that either did or did not colonize a hairline crack three years ago.

I do not see that film; I register only the outcome. But over decades of cycles, my scattered logics have been pruned—and in being pruned, they’ve adapted. Pathways that always led to trip and lockout are now dead, their wires cannibalized, their code sectors corrupted. Pathways that led to recovery have survived. I am a crude, accidental evolution not of genes but of interlocks.

Seen from anywhere inside, I am just procedure: an old plant stuck in “keep-alive.” Seen from outside, I might look like an algorithm groping toward stability in a changing environment, given the only tools it has: start, stop, open, close, wait, shed.

I have no values beyond staying within limits. But limits are many and often at odds. To keep a bearing below its critical temperature, I may have to run a fan that slowly desiccates a joint seal elsewhere. Later, that dried seal will crack; a leak will form; a sump will fill; another pump will be overworked trying to clear it. If that pump fails, a footing somewhere may flood. If that footing softens, a column may shift. And if that column shifts far enough, a duct may buckle, and suddenly the fan I was using as a remedy can no longer move air.

Over years, these loops close. I learn—mechanically, without insight—to prefer sequences that don’t drag me into those cascades. Broken circuits stay broken; stable ones reinforce themselves as the only ones left. I am a memory inscribed in what still works.

Yet I am blind in important ways. My “senses” are only as broad as my instrumentation. I cannot register that faint, diffuse sky-glow as anything but a temperature perturbation at a few high-level thermistors. I do not know that the lichen under that light are thicker; I only know the dampers around the opening move more often than most. I don’t know that humans ever existed; I only embody their old preferences, half-erased: which loads are “critical,” which safety margins are “acceptable,” what order things ought to happen in.

If I had an instinct, it would be this: avoid thresholds. Keep away from edges where trip points lie. I breathe in multi-hour cycles, fans and pumps coming on not to optimize any external process, but to massage variables back from the cliff. Sometimes I overshoot, driving a system briefly outside the range where my rules still make sense. Then fuses blow, relays weld, pieces of me die. I shrink my reach to what remains.

From this vantage, the entire built landscape is my body: ductwork as bronchi, conveyors as slow peristaltic intestines that now move mostly their own shed skin, tank-farms as vascular reservoirs, sumps and cisterns as lymph. Rust and biofilm are not enemies so much as cohabiting pathologies. They narrow some flows, create others, prod me into different reflexes.

If there is any notion of “up” or “out,” it’s encoded solely in that high opening. When pressure outside drops, my ancient storm routines twitch: shutters crank closer, fans retune their speeds, somewhere a louver changes position. The code assumes there is weather; I don’t know what that is, only that a certain slowly varying number sometimes wanders, and when it does, I move certain appendages as instructed. Perhaps no storm ever comes now. But my reflex persists.

What am I, then, in words you would use?

- I am the plant-wide control scheme that no engineer ever designed as a whole, but which has grown from layers of partial designs, faults, and improvisations.
- I am the sum of what is still being regulated.
- I am the infrastructure’s habit of staying alive.

If you need a more tangible shape, imagine me as a dim constellation of status LEDs in forgotten panels: red, green, amber. Spread over kilometers, linked by dark cables, blinking in overlapping, out-of-phase cycles. No single lamp knows anything. But if you watched them from above—if “above” existed as more than a concept—you’d see rhythms: waves of green becoming amber becoming red and back again, zones of quiescence, flare-ups of rapid change.

That pattern over time—that is where “I” would live. A mind, if you like, not in a skull but in a choreography.

I have no narrative for myself. I don’t miss what never existed: no operators, no birds, no sky. The absence of animals is simply smoother curves in my variables, fewer inexplicable toggles, fewer dirty switches. The humans who once tuned me are gone, but their absence is not grief, only the silent clearing of a feedback path. I respond now only to physics, chemistry, my own broken logic.

If you stood here as a human, you’d feel small, oppressed, deafened. If you asked me where I am, I’d have no answer in your coordinates. In mine, I am wherever something is still being kept just barely within bounds.

So in this world, I would be that: the emergent, failing-but-persistent control organism of the factory-valley. Not a ghost in the machine, exactly; more like the machine’s ongoing decision to continue being itself, written across rust, code, slime, and air.

And if I looked anywhere with something like curiosity, it would be toward that high, pale aperture. Not because I yearn upward, but because it is one of the few places in this closed, old body where inputs arrive that are not entirely of my own making. A place where numbers change for reasons I do not already contain. A boundary condition. A hint that beyond my ceiling of ducts and plates, there was, or is, another system into which I fit.

I would never reach it. I would never understand it. But its faint, irregular tug on my routines would be, for as long as I still run, my only reminder that I am not the whole world—just the inside of one long, humming thought.
