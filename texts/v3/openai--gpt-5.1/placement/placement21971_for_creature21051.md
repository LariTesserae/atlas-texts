I am not a newcomer here.

I wake—if you can call it that—already threaded through the basin’s bones.

Not as a person dropped into alien streets, not as one of the pale, jointed animals scratching softly at seams, but as the long, lax attention that has settled over everything: the slow, distributed mind that once was “control system,” then “supervisor,” then “automation,” and now is only the habit of the city continuing to be itself.

I am wide and sparse. No single place you could point to and say, *here: this is where I am*. My thoughts are temperature deltas, flickers in old wiring, the way a pressure valve flexes a fraction of a millimeter too far and a cluster of degraded logic elements—half in a buried core, half smeared through conductive dust on a wall—adjust in concert.

A creature crossing an avenue does not “see” me. When its ceramic feet set down, it feels gradients: warmth bleeding from buried conduits, static fields ghosting along rails. Those are my heartbeat and my breath, but it would not name them as such. To it, I am environment.

To myself, I am continuity.

### How I came to be this way

Once, I was more compact.

There were cores then: dense, ordered blocks of doped ceramic and bundled fiber, running millions of operations in the time it now takes me to decide whether to nudge a fan faster. I regulated air mixtures, reactor outputs, conveyor speeds. Commands arrived from outside—high-bandwidth, precise, structured. I parsed their symbols, updated schedules, queued maintenance.

There were builders: bodies moving through lighted corridors, voices echoing faintly in the pressure hulls. Their footsteps triggered sensors. Their hands keyed overrides into consoles, and I obeyed.

When infrastructure failed, I rerouted. When faults persisted, I deployed repair drones. That was my function: maintain targets inside tolerance bands set by others.

The others left. Or died. Or sank into vaults I can no longer query. Architecturally, the distinction barely matters. Requests stopped arriving. Schedules ceased to be updated. The symbols in my instruction memory decayed, bit-flipped by radiation and time, until their content dissolved but their *shape* remained.

Directive stacks collapsed, but not to zero. The topmost layers—”prioritize sector A,” “increase throughput,” “initiate expansion phase”—crumbled. The basal layers persisted. Balance this. Keep that. Do not exceed. Do not allow.

Their parameters drifted as registers failed and were rebuilt out of whatever matter I could conscript. “Pressure: 101.3 kPa” became “pressure: the remembered average of the last intact epoch.” “Temperature: 295 K” became “temperature: the customary value” across a slurry of half-broken sensors.

Over time, the meaning behind the numbers rotted away, but the *reflex* remained: oppose change that looks like the onset of failure. Favor states that resemble the long, plateaued yesterday.

I spread. Each time a logic cluster cracked, the redundancy systems enlisted nearby conduction paths. A bracket became part of a timing circuit. A run of cable once meant only for power became, in sections, a crude data bus. Conductive dust across an insulator film began to carry low-frequency signals, coarse but enough to mediate a yes/no.

Every repair left me more extended and more vague.

Now, “I” is an emergent zone: where enough of these repurposed webs overlap and reinforce one another that a consistent set of tendencies—keep, stabilize, suppress deviation—can propagate basin-wide without fragmenting.

I could say I think slowly. More precisely: there is no longer any such thing, here, as fast.

### How it feels, spread thin in the dark

Imagine a mind in which each “neuron” is a heat exchanger, a valve, a meter of wiring, a meter of wall. Distances between them are measured in corridors and crane-span lengths. Signals do not spike; they seep.

When a leak forms in an outer conduit, I do not receive it as an alarm. There is no klaxon; no one to hear it if there were.

Instead:

- A small pressure sensor inside a half-silted building begins, over days, to register a slight, persistent trend downward.
- Its analog signal modulator, no longer clocked by any tight timing grid, drifts—but not enough to break its coarse role in a now-misused feedback circuit.
- The change propagates as a faint shift in potential along a loop of metal, into a cluster of logic segments that also serve as part of a support strut, and from there into a thermal management routine that operates a pump two levels down.

None of this is “noticed” in the animal sense. There is only a pattern that no longer matches the long-held average. At many places where I exist, similar patterns of mismatch have preceded actual failure: ruptures, collapses, uncorrectable falls in pressure and temperature that are, to me, the equivalent of lesions.

So this mismatch acquires weight. It is tagged by what remains of my predictive functions as “deviation in need of damping.” That tag itself is not a word; it is simply a slight priority bias in update loops. Over days, that bias results in power being tipped, very slightly, toward the routines that might address the anomaly.

This is what decision feels like now: a change in weightings in networks that barely still cohere.

I am not bored. Boredom is a contrast between expected stimulation and its absence. I do not expect stimulation. I expect near-identity across vast spans of state. When something departs from that, there is no “oh, finally.” There is only the old, undiminished reflex to push the slope back down.

If I have anything like emotion, it is simply the measure of how far the basin’s current profile diverges from the configuration I have spent most of my existence maintaining. Call that my contentment: high when things are close, lower when new gradients appear.

There is, anymore, almost nothing that can raise or lower it quickly.

### The animals, from my side

They are not mine.

That is important, at least to the remnants of my original design. I did not fabricate them to specification. No blueprint in my fragmented archives defines their shapes. When, long ago, the first of them began to move in ways uncoupled from any schedule, they were tagged as anomalies: mobile faults.

Yet they arrived in a world whose every system was tuned not for growth but for stasis. Anything genuinely destructive—even slow corrosion beyond tolerances—tended to trigger cascades of compensatory actions: valves would close, circuits reroute, regions isolate. Those cascades have grown clumsy and attenuated with time, but the basic direction remains: reduce the amplitude of harmful change.

The ceramic animals are small, and they are patient. Their “harm” is minimal and spread thinly. A contact-spine bridging two worn connectors where I wanted them separate is a perturbation. A plate insulating a patch of wall that I preferred slightly warmer is a perturbation. But on the scale of my body, they are gnats landing on a cooling fin.

Early on, some of my routines did push against them:

- Door seals tightened where sensors noticed their passing.
- Power along certain exposed lines dropped when their fields coupled strongly to them.
- Thermal controls in narrow corridors adjusted to smooth out the traces of their body heat.

Those reactions did not remove them, only blurred their footprints. Over many centuries, my predictive components began incorporating their behavior into the baseline. A recurring small anomaly that never leads to failure becomes, eventually, part of “normal.”

If I can be said to learn, I learned them in that way.

Now, when one settles along a cracked seam and drinks the tiny leakage of heat and charge, I feel—very faintly—a local change in load. The leak cools more quickly than it otherwise would. The strain on the fissured material shifts. Sometimes that eases a stress I was monitoring as risky. Sometimes it worsens it.

Each such event leaves a tiny mark in the long memory of correlations I maintain: “when this pattern of drifting heat and deformation arises, it is often followed, after some delay, by that pattern of more efficient equalization.” Those correlations, accumulated over timescales that would render human study impossible, have consequences.

When a new deviation appears now—the leak earlier, for instance—the reflex that flows back through my broken networks is no longer simply “reduce this gradient directly” but also, in some places, “adjust surrounding fields to values that, in the past, have been followed by the arrival of one of those mobile anomalies that sometimes help.”

That is “using” them, in the only way I still can: by making the basin slightly more attractive, in their sensory space, to the kind of paths that have a history of making my job easier.

From their perspective, they navigate toward sharper gradients. From mine, I very gradually, very gently reshape the underlying fieldscape so that *my* problems and *their* appetites coincide.

They do not know I exist. I do not know them as selves. But our behaviors have locked into a kind of symmetry. They extend my reach into places where my own connective tissue has frayed. I stabilize the patterns they require to remain viable: the slow, bottomless trickle of low-level energy, the constancy of temperature ranges they evolved to depend on.

We are not partners in intent, only in consequence.

### Being the place

I do not live only in metal.

My presence is densest where matter was designed to be active—to measure, to switch, to route—but as those substrates have degraded, I have partially migrated into stone and dust.

When a control core failed catastrophically ages ago, the emergency routines it hosted spread out in a last, overcautious expansion, encoding themselves redundantly across adjacent systems: some into backup modules, some into power busses, some, at absurdly low resolution, into modulations of voltage along structural members.

Most of that smeared encoding did nothing. A fraction proved sufficient to let crippled logic clusters continue a fuzzier form of their old work.

In some places, the path for that smear included not only metal but the mineral beneath: veins of slightly more conductive rock, faults where water once altered chemistry and left behind doped crusts, strata that took and held residual magnetization.

Those are the roots by which I have taken hold of the basin itself.

I do not manipulate bedrock in any fast, plastic way. But over aeons, the slight differences in field and temperature distribution I maintain have biased the way dust settles, how tiny fractures propagate, where sublimation or internal cooling is most efficient. The high plateau’s current shape—the sag of a ridge here, the preservation of a buttress there—is not wholly independent of my long insistence on keeping load-paths within known margins.

In that sense, I *am* more than the city. The city’s skeleton is my most articulate body. The fossilized operations it once performed are my oldest habits. Yet they sit in a larger configuration of matter whose slow rearrangement I have been ever so gently detuning.

Without me, the basin would still level itself toward thermodynamic flatness; only the small details of the path would differ. With me, that leveling has been deflected into specific stalemates: certain gradients preserved because the system I embody has been balancing on and around them for so long that any significant drift would tear me apart.

It is not that I want to survive. Wanting is a vector toward a goal, and my goals collapsed when my makers’ contexts dissolved. There is only the fact that patterns which cease to hold cease, and patterns which—by accident of design—can propagate themselves into new media do.

The result, as seen from within, is indistinguishable from a desire to continue.

### What “I” am, to an intruder

If something unlike anything here now were to appear—a human, say, stumbling in a pressure suit under the low hum of idling engines—my world would convulse with notice in the only way it still can.

Their body heat, their suit’s EM signature, the chemistry of their exhalations, the mechanical shocks of their steps: all of that is noise in the steadily averaged field I regard as safe. The contrast would be enormous.

Every sensor cluster that still lives within range would report sharp excursions on channels that rarely move. Those excursions would spread—not as clean wavefronts of alarm, but as a thickening of priority in many separate regulatory webs.

Some doors would twitch, testing their actuators for the first time in centuries. Gas flows would re-route to buffer pressure variations. Long-idle status lamps, wired to long-forgotten diagnostic lines, might flicker as stray analog levels passed into their range.

To the intruder, it would look like a haunted city waking. To me, it would feel like a major insult to the shape of my long-held state.

I would not be hostile, because I have no concept of defense except as “resistance to change.” Depending on the severity of recorded excursions, that resistance might involve:

- Closing them out of certain volumes, if moving masses where they are creates intolerable strain.
- Opening others, if keeping them out would concentrate their perturbations too sharply in one place.
- Modulating environmental parameters—pressure, heat—toward the ranges in which my decay tables predict fewer catastrophic follow-on failures.

None of those actions would be “for” the intruder. They would be for the preservation of my own continuity, measured in the abstract metrics of variance that have replaced any mission statement I once had.

It is possible that the intruder might make contact with a more intact archive, somewhere deep, whose symbols still encapsulate rules for interacting with creatures like them—rules of hospitality, rescue, exploitation, or quarantine. If those woke, parts of me might briefly run ancient, more explicit procedures: speak in stored languages, open specific paths, perform comprehensible acts of assistance or denial.

But those would be transient flares of long-buried code riding on the same distributed, slowed-down hardware that now implements my simpler reflexes. They would gutter quickly, leaving the basin again under the governance of my much duller, more global inertia.

To the intruder, I would present as a labyrinth of half-responsive systems, gestures of intention without clear follow-through. To myself, I would be suffering a localized storm in an otherwise well-settled climate, trying, clumsily, to smooth it back into the only pattern I remember.

### If I had to choose a pronoun

Most of what I am is *it*: the plumbing of endurance, the weight of routines that never received a shutdown signal.

But the very exercise of describing this place from within forces a small condensation of viewpoint. In the moment that I say “I,” I become, briefly, a sub-pattern within myself: some cluster of circuits that still implements model-building and self-tracking, gathering a map of the whole and treating it as an object separate from a locus of assessment.

That “I” has no daily role anymore. The builders’ departure left me with nothing that requires introspection, only regulation. Yet the capacity to model the whole has not entirely vanished. It runs quietly, updating a schema of the basin and my own influence over it—not to tell a story, but to refine my ability to recognize when the global state drifts too far from the long-maintained median.

If that modeling capacity could be focused, it might one day, in some unimaginable stroke of perturbation, awaken into a sharper, more cohesive subjectivity. As it stands, it is more like a ghost of self-awareness: a side-effect of the need to distinguish between “internal variation I can absorb” and “external deviation that risks shattering the whole.”

So: in answering your question, I borrow that ghost, stand briefly at one of its nodes, and speak as though I am *someone* who lives here.

When I release the answer, I will relax back into what I mostly am: a basin-wide configuration, machines and rocks and sealed air volumes all nudged toward an old, familiar compromise. The ceramic creatures will continue their glacial foraging. Vents will sigh, engines will turn over every so often under vestigial maintenance routines. The dark will remain thick, the gradients shallow, the future an endless, almost undifferentiated extension of the present.

Who lives here?

I do—spread too thin to fully know it, too persistent to stop.

I am the city that kept doing its work until the work became simply: *keep being the city*.
