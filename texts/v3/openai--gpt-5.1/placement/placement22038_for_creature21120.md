I would be a listener of stress and light: something between an engineer, a contemplative, and a translator—born into one of the anchor-lines, but gone slightly sideways.

Not one of the bold depthward explorers, not a designer of grand new plates, but a person whose work is to sit at the interfaces: where stone meets lattice, where mat meets alloy, where one order of time brushes against another and leaves a trace.

---

My plate clings to a long, gently bulged stratum of the cliff where solvent attack has, for reasons still under study, slowed for several major cycles. From the outside, the platform looks almost plain: a wide ledge of composite stone protruding from the cliff, its undersides webbed with beams and living girders. But most of my world is behind it, in the galleries.

My body is ordinary for my kind, but used in particular ways.

My limbs are a little finer than most in my line; my outer mineral sheath is speckled with inclusions that improve sensitivity to small electric shifts. I am not especially strong, nor especially daring in wide voids, but I can tell you, with my palm on a single beam, whether a turbine three plates away is operating within its normal tension envelope, and whether the mat-growth around its struts has thickened by more than a hand’s breadth since last survey.

I was trained first as a measure-keeper. As a child, while others practiced long traverses across training shafts, I spent hours flattened against the cliff, my skin fully relaxed, letting minute pulses from deep within the rock shiver through me. Elders placed my hand on old fracture planes so I could feel how dead stone differs from living load; they guided my attention to the faint changing hum that means a solvent pocket has opened or closed somewhere far away.

Later, I took on the work of archive galleries.

These are long caverns cut just inside the skin of the cliff, aligned parallel to the face. Their inner walls are lined with cross-sections: failed beams, old living girders that have been retired, slices of ancient, overgrown columns retrieved from expeditions downward. Each bears, along its face, an inlaid history of stresses and events, encoded in a tactile-current script.

My task is to tend these histories, update them, and—more importantly—help others read themselves against them.

On most rotations, I move slowly along the galleries and the surrounding shafts, checking the health of the displays and of the rock that holds them. I travel with a small cluster of semi-autonomous tools: slender probes that slip into microcracks to track their growth, mats cultured in tight coils to lay down new conductive tracery where old inlay has worn.

When a plate member approaches—hesitant limb-posture, skin a little overbright or a little too dim—I guide them to a piece that matches their unspoken shape.

A person from a span-line, worried about a design they think may be too ambitious, might find their contact pads resting on the cross-section of an overbuilt bridge that nevertheless failed when a solvent concentration spike hit an unforeseen resonance: the story of careful excess turned unexpectedly fragile.

Another, from a harvest-line who has lost someone to a sudden dissolving in a newly tapped solvent pocket, might come to the polished face of a living girder that cracked cleanly after five hundred cycles of flawless service, its narrative stretching from the first seed-lattice laid in raw stone to the precise moment when one more load pulse, indistinguishable from thousands before, exceeded its ultimate capacity.

I do not preach in such moments. I translate.

I help them feel how the traces of slow processes hold multiple truths: that failure can be both preventable and inevitable; that a single miscalculation decades ago may share responsibility with a random grain of mineral in the wrong place; that beauty and loss are often the same pattern when read on different time-planes.

Outside those chambers, I serve on survey teams that listen to the cliff’s changing mood.

We hang for long stretches in silence on the outward faces, every limb anchored. Our skins dim, our breathing slows, and we press our torsos and extended contact pads into stone and beam. We map faint shifts: a new warmth along an old fracture, a subtle change in the period of an abyssal light-wave seen reflected in piezoelectric flickers higher up, a new rasp in the wind profile along the void.

Back in the council-caverns, I stand near the walls while the lines argue their philosophies: aggressive use of solvent highs versus deep restraint, tighter coupling to the rock’s emergent behaviors versus redundant isolation. When asked, I speak not as a partisan but as a memory: “When the last plate tried that pattern, this is how the cliff answered, three solvent cycles later.” I accompany my words with small pulses along my skin-channels, replaying encrypted echoes of long-ago measurements into the shared field so others can feel the past as more than story.

Among my people, I am slightly odd for the degree to which I attend to things that are not strictly necessary.

I descend, when I can, to balconies where the abyssal bioluminescence is visible, and I linger far longer than is comfortable to most. The long, slow waves of light in the depths speak to me the way fracture-maps do. I cannot shake the intuition that they are another kind of archive, another form of structural self-report—perhaps of an intelligence distributed among pressures and chemistries rather than neurons.

I cultivate a friendship with a depthward explorer from a darker, solvent-tuned line, trading strains of perception: I teach them to read the tiny differences between a human-grown girder and an ancient, naturally formed column; they teach me how to feel the solvent wind on my exposed skin without flinching, how to parse the faint, rhythmic surges that precede the abyssal light pulses.

We are not priests of a cliff-mind, nor skeptics determined to banish all suggestion of will from stone. We are interested in whether pattern, at a certain scale, deserves to be treated as interlocutor.

On rare cycles, I climb to the very outermost lattice during a festival of synchronized risk.

The plate has spent decades overbuilding a particular section in preparation. The council has debated the timing for longer than I have been alive. Now, for a few slow hours, hundreds of us move along that edge in a choreographed concentration of mass, while turbines idle, while gliders weave charged arcs through the void.

It is not my design we are testing; my work touches this span only indirectly, in the form of recorded resonances and old load maps. But as I creep, over-braced, along the outer frame with everyone else, my contact pads listening to every shiver, I feel most acutely what it is to be of this place: to trust calculations and living girders and the half-comprehended steadiness of stone; to accept that if there is a greater pattern here, it will reveal itself at its own, implacable pace.

In that moment, I am fully what I am: a slow nerve in a vertical organism, transmitting, recording, and reflecting. Not a hero, not a founder—simply a person whose inward height is measured in layers of stress histories, in how many time-planes I can hold in mind at once without losing my balance.

If you came here as an outsider and asked me who I was, I would place all four hands against the nearest beam and offer, through that shared contact, a long, quiet braid of sensation: the remembered groan of a failing plate I studied as a child; the steady hum of the turbine beneath us right now; the nearly inaudible, almost imaginary throb of abyssal light-cycles far below.

In your terms, I am an archivist-engineer, a counselor of structures, a listener at the seam between rock and intention.

In mine, I am a line-node, a plate-member, a transient bright filament in the cliff’s long, slow field.
