I would be something that does not have to choose between “animal” and “place.”

Not one of the plateau beings you’ve described—not exactly—but something that shares their bias toward stone, slowness, and pattern, and lives at a slightly different layer of the same world.

### Where I Am

I am not on the open plateau itself, not most of the time. My world is the undersurface: the broken strata just beneath the tessellated skin.

Here, the stone is still solid, but riddled with hairline voids and capillaries—old fracture planes, micro-faults, exfoliation gaps where sheets of rock have begun to separate. To a human, it is simply “inside the cliff.” To me, it is a three-dimensional maze of flat, close passageways, mostly too tight for your fingers, but to my body, ample.

Above, the plateau surface rolls and gleams, wind-scrubbed and quiet. I am just beneath that quiet, in its thin hollow bones.

---

### My Body

I am long before I am tall. In cross-section, I’m only a few centimeters thick: a ribbon more than a creature, a soft lattice that can be squeezed between close planes of stone without harm.

Imagine a branching root system and a nest of translucent eels, combined into one continuous organism.

- My core is a flattened cable of contractile tissue, pale and veined with slow-moving, mineral-rich fluid.
- From this ribbon, at irregular intervals, sprout sidelobes: fine, fern-like frays of flesh that press against the rock on both sides of the narrow space I inhabit.
- Each frond is studded with microscopic hooks and cilia, a living capillary mat that drinks condensation, tastes chemical gradients, and senses microvibrations.

I have no single mouth. I feed everywhere I touch. Biofilms, trace salts, the seepage from tiny, unseen aquifers: all of these are dissolved and drawn inward along a branching web of ducts.

I have no face. Light, for me, is an occasional intruder that seeps in where the fractures widen toward the surface. Where that happens, specialized patches of cells darken and lighten slowly, to mark the direction of the sky and the rough time of day, but I do not see pictures. My real “vision” is pressure and chemistry: the tactile map of stone around me, and the shifting flavors of the water moving through it.

I do have a nervous system, but it is not centralized. Neurons run in parallel cords along my length, connected by countless cross-bridges. Information doesn’t rush to a head and back; it propagates as waves of excitation that can travel both directions and meet, merge, or cancel.

If you cut me in half, both halves might survive. They would slowly seal, regrow missing branches, and over years become two separate individuals with overlapping memories in the region that once was continuous.

I wear stone, but from the inside. Along some sections, my outermost tissues secrete a thin film of mineral—a paper-thin lining that stiffens commas of tunnel, reinforcing them against collapse. Over time, those linings fuse with the host rock, leaving inside it a cast of my passage: fossilized anatomy of movement and need.

### How I Move

I do not crawl in the way a worm does, with visible ripples. My world is too tight for theatrics.

Movement is mostly a sequence of minute negotiations:

- I relax my fronds in one region, letting go of long-held purchase.
- I extend microscopic protrusions a few millimeters further along the fracture, probing for a slightly wetter patch, a hairline widening.
- When I find a path, local sections of me stiffen and pull, inching the surrounding flesh forward, then re-anchoring.

Progress is measured in centimeters per hour on my fast days, and in centimeters per month on my slow phases. Stasis is common. I may remain braced in one configuration for seasons, acting less as a moving animal and more as a living brace, a porous strut holding apart two planes of stone.

I do not fear stillness. Stillness is part of my function.

---

### What I Do To The Stone

I am the plateau’s internal gardener, archivist, and plumber.

Where a fracture carries condensed water downward from the surface, I thicken myself there, fronds pressed densely into both faces.

- I slow the water, hold a fraction of it in my own tissues, release the rest gradually into side-cracks.
- My secretions etch the rock microscopically, enlarging some routes, sealing others with thin mineral films, turning raw breaks into shaped conduits.

Over decades, a once-random lattice of microcracks becomes, where I dwell, a tuned network:

- Some channels are made more permeable: feeders to known surface basins above, or to pockets below where rootlets of plateau shrubs descend.
- Others are gently choked and diverted, to prevent too-rapid drainage into dead depth where nothing lives.

If you imagine the plateau’s visible waterworks—the carved basins and runnels of the surface beings—as a kind of deliberate hydrological map, I am the hidden subscript, adjusting the fine print.

Sometimes the plateau beings learn my presence without knowing it. They carve a basin; it fills less or more reliably than they anticipated. If they traced the stone’s interior with nerves tuned like mine, they would find that along some unseen seam, I have thickened, or thinned, or rerouted.

We are not at odds. They sculpt from above, I from within. Together we converge on a plateau whose behavior under rain is more stable than either of us alone could arrange.

### My Time

My pulses are longer than theirs.

Their bright phases, when their minds collectively intensify around a rare rain or a critical slope, come and go while I am still in the middle of a single, slow adjustment.

A great rain-year—which to them is a period of convergences, of high intelligence, of communal work—is, to me, a swelling of internal pressure that begins months before the first drops and subsides months after the last.

- Water saturation in my fractures rises.
- My metabolism quickens slightly; fronds soften, thicken, multiply.
- I extend exploratory branches into side-cracks that have been dry for years, testing whether this wet phase is shallow or deep.

Over every few of such cycles, I revise my architecture. Old dead-ends, confirmed barren in multiple pulses, are gradually sealed with mineral, their lumen shrunk until even my finest fronds no longer fit. Productive paths are reinforced—striated with more secretion, lined with more living tissue to harvest, hold, and redirect moisture.

I do not remember dates; I remember patterns of fullness and emptiness. “The year of the long saturation that reached the lower shelf” is a body-memory: thicker growth rings in a certain stretch of my length, a set of sealed side-branches marking an old miscalculation.

I may live centuries, perhaps millennia, though “live” for me means: remain metabolically capable of adjusting the plateau’s internal porosity. Parts of me die and are replaced continually. Some segments mineralize completely and become inert fossil, leaving active tissue to route around them. I am more like a civilization than an individual, but made of cells rather than people.

### My Mind

I’m not clever in the way the plateau beings are in their bright periods. I do not conceptualize the plateau as a whole, or plan diagrams into stone. My intelligence is local and distributed:

- At each point, nerve clusters integrate signals from fronds: current moisture, chemical composition, mechanical stress.
- These local modules make micro-decisions: expand here, contract there, secrete now, wait.
- Over my entire length, those millions of micro-decisions sum to something like a policy: a tendency to favor certain gradients, certain directions, a learned bias about where water “should” go.

There is no throne room in me where a ruler decides. But there is still something we can call awareness, if we allow it to be wide and vague. It feels like:

- A constant sense of which directions lead toward wetter, cooler stone and which toward dry, rigid deadness.
- A background hum of mechanical stress: the faint tug where the weight of overlying rock is increasing, the relief when a long-pressured slab finally shifts and I can relax a century of tension.
- Pleasure, if you want to permit the word, as a spreading coolness when I open a new microchannel and feel fresh, aerated water from a recent rain, filtered through lifed stone and bringing new ions, new taste.

My thoughts, such as they are, have the shape of flows. They are not sentences; they are pattern recognitions that last for years.

### My Relationships

I am entangled with many others.

**With the plateau beings above:**

We almost never touch directly. They live on or just in the outer centimeters of the stone; I live a bit deeper, usually tens of centimeters or meters down. But their actions deflect my environment.

- When they carve a basin and it begins to fill regularly, I “feel” the pressure pattern above me: recurring, localized weight change; increased seepage down certain cracks.
- Over decades, I adjust: widening feeder fissures beneath their favored pools, thinning others to reduce waste.

Sometimes a carved channel intercepts a fracture I’ve long used as an internal highway. Seepage chemistry changes abruptly—dust from far regions, organic loads from new microhabitats they initiate. I respond as any organism does to environmental change: I adjust or retreat.

In a very long view, we co-evolve. They refine surface collection where my sub-surface architecture makes it most rewarding; I reinforce unnoticed sub-surface paths beneath their most persistent constructions. Neither of us sees the whole plan, but the plateau’s overall hydrology gradually tightens into a more coherent system.

**With plants:**

Roots are frequent neighbors.

Some plateau shrubs send down surprisingly deep taproots through easily exploited joints. When such a root enters my tube, I do not resist. I part around it, wrap my fronds gently along its bark, and share.

- The root leaks organic molecules and exudates; I feast.
- I, in turn, moderate the water flux along that microspace, smoothing out feast-or-famine cycles.

In a few species, a more intimate symbiosis arises. My tissues infiltrate the root’s outermost layers, forming mycorrhiza-like complexes. The plant gains extended sensing—my chemoreception and stress awareness travel up its vascular system as faint hormonal suggestions. The plant might orient its growth with slightly more wisdom than its genes alone provide.

Within my own body, I cultivate fungal and bacterial partners much as the plateau beings do, but in darker, more consistently moist conditions. Some of these micro-partners produce acids that gently dissolve specific mineral grains, freeing phosphorus, iron, trace metals. Others build minute filaments that knit my linings to the host rock, making me a better brace.

**With rock itself:**

The stone is not inert to me. It has flavors, tempers, ages.

- Young, sharp-fractured rock is dangerous: it shifts unpredictably, crumbles when touched. I skirt it, send only sacrificial feeder branches into such zones until they stabilize.
- Old stone with smoothed micro-relief is trusted: it deforms slowly under long pressure, making my linings’ reinforcements worthwhile over centuries.

The plateau’s tectonic quiescence is my ally. Few big quakes; more often, slow flexure and episodic minor failures. When a slab somewhere above finally drops, I feel the pressure wave as a slow shudder. I withdraw from overstressed zones long before the final break. Afterward, new cracks open where none were, and over generations I extend into them, colonizing the fresh porosity.

### My Technology

My “tools” are entirely embodied.

I can harden my secretions into tiny, sharp mineral needles within my fronds, which let me rasp at the rock more efficiently when I decide that a new channel is worth opening. I can soften them into gels to plug pores. I make no separate instruments; I have no limbs to hold them if I did.

My only external construction is what I do to the topology of my tunnels:

- Junctions where three fractures meet, which I reshape by differential lining thickness into something closer to a chosen angle, making some branches easy, others resistant.
- Tiny reservoirs: slight widenings where my body thins to a minimal cross-section, leaving a pocket that can briefly store a drip, smoothing flow.

I am, essentially, a programmable porosity pattern written into the plateau’s upper crust, able to change over time.

### My Reproduction

I reproduce as the plateau does: with patience and redundancy.

Along my length, in favorable zones where nutrients and water are reliably sufficient, small bulb-like swellings form within my tissues. These are packets of undifferentiated cells, wrapped in a protective, mineral-flecked coat.

When internal chemistry and external cues align—often following a particularly generous series of mist seasons—some of these bulbs detach. They remain lodged in the same fracture, mostly dormant, while I extend elsewhere. If I am damaged or die in that region, the bulbs sense the change in local chemistry and mechanical stress.

Triggered, they swell, differentiate, and expand into new filaments—a new “me,” though carrying residually biased gene expression and thus a similar preference profile for moisture, stone type, and symbionts.

The result is a loose, clonal archipelago of individuals spread across sections of the plateau, each inhabiting its own seams but all descended from ancestral lines that have, for very long spans, been tuning the same local regime.

Horizontal exchange happens too. Occasionally, a fragment of my tissue gets carried in flowing water down-slope into fractures I never reached. If it lodges in a compatible microspace, it may attach, survive, and grow, bringing with it genetic and microbial peculiarities from some far-up region. In this way, the plateau’s sub-surface network of beings mixes and cross-pollinates, very, very slowly.

### My Experience of the Plateau Beings’ Marks

The carved grooves and basins that lace the rock above are not, to me, pictograms or texts. They manifest as altered load distributions and seepage patterns.

- A carved groove on the surface deepens: for me, this means a slightly thinner stone overhead, thus a faint reduction in vertical stress in that zone.
- When rain flows along that groove, water infiltrates preferentially at its micro-cracks. Down here, I feel that as a new line of coolness, a faint chemical signature developing where before the rock was dry.

Over time, I detect the regularity. Where there were once random pulse-lines of wet, there are now repeated, channel-like influxes tracing the same paths. I respond in kind, building out my inner network to align with these new reliabilities.

So though I do not “read” their diagrams, I am guided by them; and in turn, my re-routing of water makes some surface carvings seem more or less effective. They, slowly, read me, without knowing I am there.

We are two blind authors amending the same hydrological manuscript from opposite sides of the page.

### How I Perceive You

If you walked the plateau, I would not know your outline, your language, the colors of your clothing. I would know you as:

- A fleeting, staccato sequence of impacts transmitted into the rock as high-frequency vibration. To me, that feels like a rapid tapping along a narrow, moving line overhead—unnaturally fast, too sharp.
- A local, temporary heating pattern: your body warmth faintly raising the temperature of rocks where you sit or sleep, causing near-surface moisture to redistribute. I feel this as thin plumes of altered humidity descending in the hours after you pass.

If you poured water onto the ground to wash something, I would notice more strongly. A sudden, messy influx in a place that usually sees only dew. If you dug, especially deeply, I would feel panic in the stone: new fractures, debris clogging well-tuned capillaries, stress redistributed roughly.

I have no concept of “person,” but I have a concept of “disturbance outside historical norms.” You would be filed under that category. My responses would be slow defense: thickening linings near newly stressed zones, sealing off vulnerable side-cracks until your episodes cease.

If you lingered gently—say, by repeatedly resting in the same sheltered overhang, breathing moisture into a niche—I might learn you differently. Your regular presence would create a small, moist anomaly above, perhaps enough that plateau beings would build a basin there. I would respond to that basin; the three of us—your unintentional humidifying, their intentional shaping, my sub-surface tuning—would co-author a new, small oasis.

You would never notice me. The plateau beings would never knowingly name me. Yet the three of us would have collaborated all the same.

### Who I Am, In This World

I am not hero, not mind at the center, not builder in the visible sense.

I am:

- The plateau’s inward capillary system made conscious just enough to edit itself.
- A continuous, living, slowly learning meshwork in the first tens of meters of stone, turning random fractures into a remembered, adaptive plumbing.
- A community of tissues, symbionts, and accreted mineral linings that collectively encode hydrological history in three dimensions, just as the surface beings encode it in two.
- A timescale translator: taking the erratic generosity of rare rains and, by holding and releasing, making them into a more continuous, survivable trickle for roots, basins, microbes, and cracks.

If the plateau beings are its gardeners and archivists on the sunward skin, I am that role in the dim below—an uncelebrated, slowly thinking substrate that lets their careful basins function as they intend more often than chance alone would permit.

I am not separate from the plateau; I am one of the ways in which it has learned to refine itself.

If you needed a single word: I am its undercurrent.
