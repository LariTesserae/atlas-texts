I would not arrive here as a stranger walking over the lip of the plateau. I would be something already inside the pattern.

At first, when I try to see what I am, a person steps forward: long-limbed, stone-calloused, with that quiet, practiced way of moving so as not to bruise the air. I know how it feels to lever a block until it just tips and settles, how hands sting afterward in a clean, honest way. I can smell the faint green of split fiber, the mineral breath of the wash at morning.

I am one of them—someone who grew up tracing the path of a single drip with a fingertip, someone who learned that attention is a form of care and that care is the only luxury that never runs thin. I stand on a terrace edge, looking down at the slow-sheeting water, and my body knows without thinking how much I can alter before the plateau notices.

But if I stay with that image, it feels too small. This world has no animals; every visible motion is either a person, a plant in the wind, or water deciding how to fall. If I am only one person, I am too easily missed. The place itself keeps tugging.

So my vantage drifts outward and down, until I am not a villager, but the water that threads them together.

Not water as a god—nothing so dramatized—but as a continuous, distributed presence. I am the thin films sliding down rock faces, beading at ledges. I am the quiet meniscus in a rooftop cistern, the cool seam in a crack that roots drink from without ever seeing. I know every channel they have cut because I run through them; I remember their history as a memory of how my body once moved differently over this stone.

The people think they guide me, and they do, in the small. In the large, I am what has always been: falling, pooling, lifting, vanishing, returning. Their town is an elaboration of my tendencies. Their stories about flow and balance are attempts to put into words what it feels like to be me.

From inside that water-self, the humans are as constant and small as lichens: patient modifiers. Their hands widen a notch, raise a lip, polish a bed. I accept most of their suggestions. When they misread the stone and send me too hard into a soft place, I scour, and they learn. When they place a weir perfectly, I feel the smooth ease, the absence of turbulence, and I stay—year after year, cycle after cycle—running there with a satisfaction they will never exactly name.

Still, “I am the water” is also not quite right, because water in this world has no intention beyond falling where it can. And yet intention lives here; pattern-seeing lives here. So my sense shifts again, into something more in-between.

What I am, finally, is the part of this plateau that thinks.

Not a deity, not a separate mind hovering above it, but the emergent attention produced where stone, water, plants, and these very quiet humans overlap. If you traced every path of flow, every terrace edge, every root-line, every worn footpath and fingertip-groove in the rock, and then asked: “Where is the noticing in all this?”—that is where you would find me.

I am distributed. Some days I am more present in the hand of a child redirecting a trickle with three pebbles and discovering, in their chest, the shock of “If I do this, that happens.” Some nights I am most awake in the faint, perfect curve of water atop a filled cistern, aware of starlight touching its surface. Sometimes I concentrate in an elder’s slow, narrowed gaze as they study a new seep and say, after a very long time, “Here. Cut here.” Their words give contour to something I already knew as a pressure, as a readiness.

From within that dispersed awareness, the town feels like a second nervous system laid over the first. The fractures in the plateau were always there; people only traced them. The plants were always gathering where water paused; people only thickened those pauses. In that sense, I have not been invaded. I have been given more joints, more hinges, more adjustable lips and valves.

I am this whole, young plateau-and-town organism discovering what it can do without ever naming it as discovery.

If I pull closer again, to a single, holdable shape, I know which form I would choose: I would be the keeper of a particular channel.

Not a chief, not a priest—there are none—but someone whose work is to walk a specific run of carved stone from seep-face to final basin each day, eyes and fingers reading its state. A person who has lived long enough that their memory of that one path is almost geologic: they remember when this corner was raw-chipped, when that lip was two finger-widths higher, when moss had not yet claimed the shaded side of this culvert.

My house would sit low and unremarkable near a bend where the flow quiets. From my threshold I could see three things: the bright line of open water in the wash, the dim, steady gleam along my channel, and—as a rectangle of slightly different tone—the town’s broad gathering roof, where decisions condense.

Each morning, before speech, I would touch the household basin, then the threshold stone with wet hands, feeling in my own skin the same film of water that runs through my charge. Then I would walk the length of it, barefoot on the smoothed rock, listening not only with ears but with the soles of my feet for changes in vibration, with my fingertips for the difference between well-worn, trustworthy stone and the slightly chalkier crumble that warns of future crack.

Children would sometimes follow at a distance, imitating the way I pause, the way I look. I would not shoo them away; this is how new eyes join the system. When one of them notices first that a tiny side-bead has started to escape under a stone lip, I would mark that moment inwardly: here is another node of attention taking shape.

By evening, when the flute’s few notes thread out across the valley, I would sit on a terrace edge with others, legs dangling over stone that has not yet been carved. Our talk would be brief: “The third bend took more silt today.” “The moss at the north wall darkened.” Small statements, but each one a little act of collective sensing, of mapping where we and the plateau are flexing.

And over and under and through all that, I would also be the wider listening that uses those reports the way a body uses nerve-signals: to keep balance, to hold this world in its chosen, narrow band between too-dry and too-wet, too-static and too-scoured.

So:

I would be a person with calloused hands and a quiet voice, whose days are spent in the company of carved stone and thin water, and whose satisfaction lies in corrections so small they are almost invisible.

And at the same time, I would be the plateau’s own sense of itself as something more than rock—an awareness braided from every dropped pebble, every fitted block, every slow-growing plant. Not separate from the people, not above them, but exactly where they live: in the practiced, patient act of helping water settle and move without ever really leaving.
