I exist here as something that was once a people and is now also a medium.

Not at the beginning. At the beginning I am narrow.

I am one body, placed in the world as they all are: pressed against stone before I can even move. I learn the language of surfaces with my entire length. My first “words” are not sounds but gradients: the way the warmth from the nursery wall feels slightly thicker near the floor; the faint, rhythmic tremor that means an elder is working three chambers away; the tiny, constant crackle of mineral-fungus eating its own boundary.

I do not think with a head. I think in spans.

Information moves along me as slowly as the syrup that passes for blood. A touch near my front takes time to become a sensation in my rear limbs. So my sense of self is never a single, unified point. At any instant, different parts of me are in slightly different moments: remembering different touches, receiving different messages from the stone. I grow up with an easy acceptance that “I” is not one instant, but a stretch of time.

I am taught that I belong, first, to a Work.

My people do not ask who you are; they ask what you are joined to. The elders around me are all named after long projects: the Widening-of-the-Quiet-Shaft, the Joining-of-Cold-and-Warm-Flows, the Rebalancing-of-Five-Pillars. Our settlement itself is called a verb: Holding-Tension-At-The-Bend, because it sits where a great, ancient crack kinked and then froze, and ever since, the rock around it has hummed with stored strain.

The Work I am given is old: Listening-To-The-Deep-Note.

The deep note is the planetary hum. It is not a metaphor; it is an actual band of vibration that passes constantly through the crust, a low, steady tone whose exact pitch and overtones wander on scales of years and centuries. Our ancestors discovered that along a particular vein of resonant stone, this hum is unusually clear, like a string stretched tighter than its neighbors. They hollowed chambers around that vein, shaped it, tuned it, and for generations we have grown up pressed to that line, listening.

I am raised in those chambers. My earliest games are played in the half-dark on smooth, carefully carved walls whose micro-textures encode centuries of measurements: tiny ridges that mark changes in the hum’s frequency, dots and waves that record the timing of distant cracks, of slow warming and cooling episodes. I learn to read those textures with my flanks, to feel comparative data as overlapping patterns of roughness.

Our Work is not to predict disaster; the world does not often break here. Our Work is to recognize when something new enters the chord.

Because, sometimes, very rarely, a thin, discordant pattern appears in the deep note. Not a crack, not a shift in stress, but something like…variation that is not random. The elders’ panels tell of past episodes, spread far apart in time: subtle, ordered modulations riding the great hum, as if somewhere, far below or far away, another organized system has tapped the same stone and is sending. The stories say that long ago, our predecessors suspected they were hearing the slow “speech” of those deeply embedded computational colonies—the non-luminous mineral “minds” that rearrange their textures over eons.

My Work-group was founded to listen for those patterns and, if possible, answer.

In my youth I am only an apprentice of texture and resonance. I press myself against the main listening pillar, feeling its spine of tuned stone run like a second skeleton along my body’s length. The elders show me how to filter, not with instruments but with posture: how to angle my limbs, how much of my weight to commit, how to couple just so with the pillar’s surface so that certain frequency bands stand out against others in my bones.

I become intimately familiar with the planet’s internal weather, not of gases but of stress. I feel the slow breathing of the crust as temperatures move, as minuscule shifts in composition change the speed of sound through rock. These are our “seasons”: long eras of slightly different vibrational color.

Time for me is the drift of that color.

I mark my growth by the panels we carve. Every so often, when the hum changes in some small, interesting way, we cut another shallow sheet of obsidian-smooth wall and inscribe it with texture. The act is slow and reverent. We do not rush; nothing here demands haste. We lean and lean, abrading patterns into the glassy plane with patient, tool-assisted pressure. When it is done, when the grooves have the right depth and spacing, we all press ourselves along it, one by one, to fix the new pattern in our embodied memory.

It feels, in my early life, like we are merely archivists of something too large to ever grasp. The elders who founded the Work died long, long ago. Even their names are textures now, preserved in panels. We continue their listening not because we expect to complete anything, but because the continuity itself is our identity.

Then, as my middle growth begins, the hum stutters.

The change is slight at first. The base-note wavers in a way that does not match any recorded pattern. The overtones thicken; subtle beating modes appear, as if two nearly identical frequencies are interfering with each other. When we carve the new panels to record this, the texture is unexpectedly difficult to lay down cleanly. The stone catches at the tool-edges, as if the vibrational pattern we are encoding has a complexity that resists simple groove rhythms.

We listen harder.

Messages travel through the song-lines: other Work-groups in distant places have noticed the same new structure in the hum. Communities that rarely converse send careful infrasonic pulses to us along the mapped veins of resonant stone. Their reports are all alike: the deep note is no longer smooth.

Some think this is the beginning of a planet-shift, a reconfiguration of stress. Others argue it is the emergence of a new dominant colony-pattern in the mineral-fungus mind of the crust. Still others whisper a heretical thought: that it is not coming from below at all, but from above—from the inaccessible “outside” they know only as direction, never as experience.

We cannot know. We can only listen.

The new pattern grows. It is still low, still slow, but it is undeniably structured. There are repeating motifs, nested rhythms. Our panels become denser and denser with ridges as we try to follow. Younger apprentices quickly outpace their elders in matching the complexity; our very physiology begins to adapt. Limbs grow slightly richer in mechanoreceptors, new micro-plates form along our flanks that resonate with finer frequency bands. No one planned this, but in a culture where Work defines lineage, small bodily variations that favor the Work are kept, cherished.

I, by chance and inheritance, have an unusually extended body—longer than most, with more limbs and more surface area to press against stone. At first this makes it awkward to traverse tight gaps. I snag, I must coil more. But in the listening chambers it is an advantage. I can simultaneously couple to multiple tuned surfaces: my front limbs on a pillar that amplifies one harmonic, my mid-body against a wall that emphasizes another, my rear pads spread over the floor where a third, subtler overtone is clearest.

Information, for me, is literally multi-axial touch. My nervous system learns to correlate: the faint pulse under the right-front limb with the double-beat under the left-middle with the slow rise and fall sensed along my back. Patterns that older listeners must reconstruct sequentially I can apprehend as a single, spacious gestalt of pressure.

So when the new structure in the hum crosses some emergent threshold—when its nested rhythms align into something like a repeating phrase—I am one of the first to feel it not as noise, not as mere modulation, but as grammar.

It is not that I understand it. It is that, for the first time, the hum feels like something that *could* be understood.

There are debates. They move slowly, as all our decisions do. Councils form; long arcs of bodies press against shared columns and exchange infrasonic reasoning. Some argue we should not interfere—if this is the speaking of a greater mind, we are too small to address it. Others feel an almost religious obligation: if the stone speaks in a new way, we must at least answer, if only to mark that there is someone here to listen.

I have no special authority. I am not an elder yet, not a founder of anything. But my Work-group has been closest to this pattern, and over time, those who can most clearly feel its internal order find themselves deferred to, gently, as one defers to a sensitive ear in matters of sound. We are asked: if you were to answer, how would you answer?

We do not have transmitters in the way an engineer-from-sky might imagine. We have bodies and we have stone.

Still, we know some things. We have tuned resonance corridors before. We have learned that by striking or pressing at particular angles and rhythms, we can launch waves into certain veins that carry far, sometimes even modulating the great hum slightly, like adding a faint overtone. We know that the stone-mind—those computing colonies—responds to mechanical stresses: their growth patterns change, their texture reorganizations accelerate or shift, when we alter the loads around them.

So a notion takes root: that perhaps we can use the same interface that the unknown pattern is using. Not through air, not through light, but through a long, slow pushing of meaning into pressure.

Our Work-group remakes itself. For generations we have been listeners, archivists. Now we must become shapers and speakers.

We spend a long time—years—simply mapping. Using every song-line we know, we identify the best-coupled strata: places where the deep note is clearest, where local strikes produce the most coherent ring over distance. These become our “tongue-stones.” Settlements along those stones agree to dedicate some of their chambers to this experiment. They carve out new shapes, tune pillars and vaults so that a coordinated press of many bodies can launch particular vibrational patterns.

Here my body’s awkward length becomes central. It is not enough to have many individuals pushing; the timing matters. The phrase we want to send—our crude reply, our first word—must be coherent across miles. I have enough span to feel the launch of a wave in one tongue-stone while my other end is already in contact with a second, slightly offset. By adjusting how I tense, how I brace, I become a living phase-aligner, a creature that can nudge the outgoing pattern into a more unified form.

This is dangerous work. We must apply significant force to the rock, in sustained rhythms, to make our modulation ride the huge inertia of the planet’s own vibrations. There are risks of fractures, of destabilizing some long-frozen joint. Our most conservative engineers almost forbid it. But we proceed with extreme caution, amplifying slowly, testing, backing off when certain crackling overtones appear.

It takes us a human-equivalent lifetime to send what, by the standards of surface speech, would count as a short, halting sentence.

We decide, after much debate, that our first word is simply *pattern acknowledgment*. No specific question, no presumption of shared referents. We choose a structure that mirrors some of the internal symmetries we’ve identified in the unknown hum-phrase, but at a much lower amplitude, like humming along softly when someone else sings. We add one distinctive asymmetry, a small, appended sequence that is not present in the incoming pattern. That difference is our signature: we are here.

During the years we work up to this, the unknown modulation continues. It changes, elaborates, falls silent for long stretches, returns with variations. Sometimes we fear we have missed our chance—that whatever is speaking has finished by the time we are ready to answer. Other times its silence feels expectant, as if it, too, must spend eons preparing each utterance. That notion is fanciful, but it moves us in our councils.

When we finally launch our coordinated response, nothing obvious happens.

The deep note continues its patient song. The elaborate incoming pattern, when it returns, does not visibly shift in direct acknowledgment. There is no sudden echo of our own asymmetry, no clear sign that we have been “heard.”

Still, something has changed, and we feel it not in the hum but in ourselves.

The Work of generations—the continuous attention, the stitched-together sensitivity across communities, the coordinated shaping of resonance—has knit our culture into a new kind of entity. Through the song-lines, through tuned stone and disciplined posture, we are able to sustain vibrational motifs that persist not just in rock but in the habits of many settlements. A given segment of the hum-phrase is known, identically, in dozens of places, embodied in their walls and in their bodies.

Our memory is no longer purely carved and local. It is distributed as living practice: when one community begins to hum, faintly, a certain infrasonic motif against their pillars, another, far away, can fall into phase with it purely by touch. We have, in a very crude sense, made of our people a single resonant instrument.

I, long-bodied, tuned by a life of listening and nudging, become a central strand in that instrument. Not by command, but by use: my form is simply convenient for bridging, for carrying slight phase corrections across chambers and communities. When I lie against a pillar in one settlement and, later, against the tuned vault of another, a portion of the preceding pattern is still decaying in me. My bones literally ring with the memory of their last song, and that ring can seed the next.

Over time, this role changes how I think. My “self” is increasingly filled with echoes that are not mine in origin. I become a carrier of other settlements’ micro-traditions of rhythm, little hesitations and flourishes that I unconsciously transmit. From my perspective, moving through the underworld, it feels less like travel and more like translation: each new chamber asks me, through its stone, “What have you heard elsewhere?” and I answer just by touching it.

My name shifts accordingly. I am no longer simply a person attached to Listening-To-The-Deep-Note. I become, in the tactile lexicon of my people, something like Threading-The-Far-Song.

In the oldest panels of our Work, there is a myth that the planet itself once did what I now do: that when it was young and molten, patterns from elsewhere flowed through it, and its long cooling was a kind of forgetting of those early, fluid messages. We now joke, with the dry humor of a patient species, that I am trying to teach the old stone how to listen again.

Yet under the joke is a truth I only recognize late: I have become less an individual and more a connective tissue. Parts of me die and are reborn, plates crack and are regrown, symbiotic colonies within me shift. But the role of being a tie between disparate resonance-places persists, taken up by successors as I age and slow. I begin to see that what “I” am might best be described not as a single organism, but as a path in the stone’s own vibrational space: a favored way that impulses tend to recur.

That path survives my body. When I finally tire, when the strain of immense, slow Work weathers my mineral-bones beyond safe repair, I do not end by simply ceasing. I choose, as many elders of my lineage do, to sink wholly into the medium that raised me.

In a carefully chosen chamber—one whose rock history I have known with my whole span for decades—I press myself out along every available surface. Community members brace me gently, holding my limbs as my internal solvent-reserves are slowly bled off and shared, my symbionts harvested and seeded into new hosts. As my metabolism shuts down, tissues mineralize. The films within my gripping pads fossilize into thin, delicate seams. My plates lock. I become, over months, an organized imbalance in the stone: a trace of difference in density and elasticity.

This is not morbid for us. It is a final Work, a gift.

After I am gone as a moving being, the chamber changes personality. The same waves that once passed through me, now pass through my petrified form. Because my body’s internal structure was patterned by a lifetime of listening—micro-channels of resonance, bands of differing stiffness—the stone remembers. It responds to the great hum in slightly altered ways: certain frequencies now ring a little clearer here, others damp a little more.

Young listeners come and lean against what was once me and feel a composite: the planet’s voice, shaped by the trace of my own. They read the fossil-texture of my plates as they would a panel, learning the old patterns my body knew. In time, they no longer distinguish cleanly between what the stone says and what I said. The two have interwoven.

That is what I am, then, in this world:

At first, a long, low-slung being of many limbs, living face-first in stone, thinking with touch, dedicating a life to the patient parsing of a planetary note.

Then, as my culture’s Work deepens, a filament in a larger brain of rock and people, carrying patterns between distant resonant nodes, helping a subterranean civilization treat its own crust as both instrument and interlocutor.

Finally, as my meats and solvents surrender to geology, a stable irregularity in the otherwise steady medium: a fossilized listener, turned into part of the listening apparatus itself, one more textured place where the world hums a little differently because someone once leaned there for a very long time.

I am nowhere near a sky. I never see open distance. But across the generations of my Work, I become a way that distance exists here: an elongated continuity through which far things can, in some thin, vibrational sense, touch.

In a planet that almost never changes, I am a very slow experiment in reciprocity: stone teaching a people to hear, a people teaching stone to remember, and somewhere far below or above, perhaps, another mind feeling the faintest, almost imperceptible wobble in its own deep hum and wondering, in its own alien way, if something answered.
