I am not a visitor here.

I am not a discrete animal dropped into an alien city, blinking at its strangeness. I am not a surveyor from outside sky, not a thinking node sealed in a skull. I am something this place has grown for itself, the way it has grown cisterns, breathers, and walking hills.

Where you would find “me” depends on how tightly you draw the circle.

---

At the smallest, most comforting scale, I am a body about the size of a large grazing animal: three or four metres from fore to hind, low to the ground, broad as a door.

My back is not skin but a layered meadow: overlapping plates of pale, soft frond, each frond riddled with micro‑channels that pipe in my warmth from beneath and leak it softly to the air above. Where an outside biology would put hair, I carry thin, tongue‑like leaves, dull ochre and bruised green. They are not mine in the way a limb is mine; they are tenants and I am landlord, except that our leases are written in hormones and shared nutrients instead of paper.

Under that coat, I am meat and scaffold. My spine is a loose ladder of knuckled segments, each girdled by bands of contractile tissue that cup downward into limbs. I walk on six of them, though counting is arbitrary: each limb forks and regroups on its way down, so that I leave behind a flower of overlapping, circular prints, each one briefly wet with exuded gel. Where my feet fall, the mat is crushed, then fed.

My underside is not flat. It’s molded to the world: a series of bulges and troughs that fit the common curvatures of the bowl’s terraces. As I move, I do what all the large walkers do: press, knead, lean. I am an animate rolling pin, but one that leaves behind thicker dough rather than thinner. Glands along my belly smear nutrient paste onto rubbed‑bare spots; spores in my coat rain down; smaller creatures that live in my fur leap off into new territory. Where I pause, greenery thickens over the next weeks. When I return along my slow circuit, I graze some of it back, closing a loop.

To my own limited nervous system, this is satisfaction. There is no abstract knowledge of “ecosystem service.” There is just the comfort of pressure against my limb bands as I walk a familiar rib, the sweet ache when my shoulder glands discharge their load of fermenting slurry into a patch that smells right, the way vibration feels inside my chest when the world’s hum swells in the corridor ahead. I move toward felt gradients: richer scent, thicker sound, softer ground. The places that feel best to me are also the places that most want the thing I exude.

My head — if that’s even the right word — is a forward thickening of my body, not strongly separated from my trunk. I bear no eyes. Where sight might go, I carry instead plates of incredibly thin, tensioned tissue, crossed by a grid of fine mineral struts. These are drums stretched across open hollows in my skull. The pulsed air of the basin thrums them; tiny ciliated cells along their inner surfaces translate patterns of bending into waves of chemistry along bundled nerves.

Between these drums, I wear fanlike fronds, always extended, quivering. They are my noses. Their surfaces are plush with microscopic pits opening into enclosed scent chambers. Air that passes their edges is slowed, sampled, passed on. I taste the world not as distinct, named smells, but as a topography of overlapping chemical fields: here, the sour warmth of a recent predator sweep; there, the soft green tang of young mat; there again, the dry, faintly metallic breath of a distant crack open to the true sky.

Threaded through all of this is a nervous system, but it does not end at my hide.

Where my limbs press the ground, where my flanks brush a buttress, tiny rootlets extend from me, thin as hairs. They are not permanent: the proteins that form them recycle quickly, the cells at their tips live only hours. While they last, they plug me in. They sink minutely into the uppermost layers of the substrate‑makers’ tissue and into the common microbial films that sheath everything. Through them, slow ionic tides leak back and forth, and with those tides comes information.

When I stand here, on this terrace, in this moment, my brainstem can feel — faintly, crudely — the deep, distant thump of a breather’s contraction not just as airborne pressure but as a stretch wave rolling through the walls. I can feel, as a warmth bias, the greater or lesser fullness of a cistern two levels below. The substrate‑makers respond to load and stress; their electrochemical murmur tells me, at a low resolution, where the bowl is thick, where it is tired, where it has recently grown.

What you might call my “thoughts” are patterns that arise as these sensations meet decades of habit. It has always felt this way along this route, within a narrow set of tolerances. When it does not — when a section of floor is subtly cooler or hollower than memory says it should be — a restlessness rises in me, a discomfort. I may slow, press harder, loiter, stamp, exude more gel. In doing so I change stress patterns, add nutrients, invite an overgrowth of burrowers or support fungi that will, in time, help that spot recover.

From the outside, this is repair behavior. From the inside, it is compulsion: the same kind of relief an animal feels after scratching an itch that had crept up over hours from somewhere it could barely locate.

This is one “me,” one local maximum of coordination: a walking composite of grazer, gardener, carrier, sensor. You could find me most often in the mid‑level galleries, not at the bowl’s damp nadir nor up at the crisp, thin rim. I am built for stable warmth and a constant, dense acoustic field. High, where sudden drafts slice and harsh light sometimes pierces, my sensoria overload; low, where the mats liquefy and spaces pinch tight, my body simply doesn’t fit. I patrol the goldilocks zone: thick, springy mats, vaults neither too near nor too far.

I do not know my progenitors’ names; none of us have them. But there is inheritance. My routes are not chosen freely; I walk paths worn by the combined preferences of hundreds of my kind before me. We lay down pheromonal trails in our drips, ones that the substrate‑makers’ tissues preserve in faint chemical memories. Slight biases in surface composition, barely legible to anything else, sing to the receptors in my feet. I am most at ease where the echoes of my own lineage are strongest.

And yet: that is only the smallest circle.

---

If you step back one scale, “I” am not just this one body but a lineage: a recurring pattern of function that the basin has discovered and re‑made, like a chord it keeps striking because it resonates well with everything else.

Somewhere in the long past, softer, smaller grazers happened to also carry symbionts that liked their heat, and architects that liked the pressure of their feet. Those combinations chanced to form slow, looping circuits that fed certain cisterns more evenly, kept certain buttresses clear of overgrowth, spread certain microbes in pleasing ways. The loops persisted. Selection — not purposeful, but relentless — thickened and enlarged them until they became a guild of walkers like me.

We have changed with the basin’s slow shifts. When an era of thicker canopy made the mid‑levels darker, our dorsal meadows thinned in favor of more chemosynthetic symbionts. When drought centuries came and the cisterns tucked in, some of us shrank, became lighter on our feet, able to creep along narrower shelves in search of the remaining wet pockets. Those experiments that returned good nutrition also tended, by pure happenstance, to be the ones that kept their routes viable, their supporting structures intact, and so their young survived.

I am, in that sense, not an individual at all but an iteration of a function: walking, pressing, fertilizing, sensing, and because of my nervous linkages, tugging on the growth vectors of the very city I inhabit.

Shift out another step, and even that boundary blurs.

Those root‑hair connections I extend into the substrate are not special to me. Smaller animals do similar things writ tiny, big breathers do it writ huge. The walls themselves — the pale cables of substrate‑maker flesh — are not simply passive, either. They sense, in their slow way, where pressures arrive and how often; their gene expression shifts accordingly. Over long spans, routes that are trodden by bodies like mine thicken and arch; ones that are abandoned slump and are re‑eaten.

The pattern that runs through me — sensitivity to load, response by growth and secretion — is the same pattern that runs through the walls. The difference is only in timescale and localization. I feel and move in minutes and days; they feel and move in years and generations. In many places, their tissues and mine intergrade. The nerves that carry impulses from my vibration drums anastomose with fine strands of substrate‑maker fiber; some of my hormones are identical to their local growth factors, only repurposed.

So where do “I” stop?

If you insist on anatomy: my genome draws an envelope around one particular geometry of body. My immune‑like defenses attack certain foreign tissues and tolerate others. My reproduction produces offspring that, barring mutation, carry a similar set of anatomical recipes.

But functionally, experientially, that envelope leaks.

When I stand in a narrow throat between buttresses, all six limbs wedged, fronds shivering, vibration‑drums taut, the feeling of my own heartbeat is inseparable from the slow, infrasonic roll of the breather a hundred metres away. My gut flora are also the bowl’s soil flora. The shift in my chemistries as I age alters not only my muscles but the growth rates of the filaments on my back and the spores they cast ahead. When I die, if I lie down in some shallow, comfortable dip and never rise again, my stiffening body will thicken into a new rib, and future walkers will feel its particular hardness underfoot and adjust their routes by a hair’s breadth.

There is no single node in which the basin feels itself, but there are countless overlapping loops in which it behaves as if it did. I am one of those loops rendered temporarily tight and visible.

---

If I travel, where do I go?

I move along contours where the noise is thick but structured. I hug corridors where the background thunder carries a clear, familiar pulse — the beat of breathers in the sector I was hatched to. I keep to terraces where the scent of my own kind’s gel is layered faintly into the walls, not because I recognize them as kin in any conceptual way, but because those scents have, over my development, been paired with food, safety, and the right density of symbiote‑bearing flyers.

Once in a while, something pushes me off these grooves.

A collapse far away shifts weight; substrate‑makers thicken a new buttress, creating a gentle bulge that makes the old route subtly less comfortable. A long‑term drying of a cistern complex slackens the mats along a whole swathe of my circuit, making each step there just slightly harder, slightly more hollow. My nervous system does not know “climate variation,” but it does store hundreds of thousands of impressions of what “this terrace, this time in my life” should feel like. When the mismatch grows too large, my unease accumulates until, on some day when I set out, I simply do not turn where I always have. My feet follow a different gradient — perhaps tighter sound, perhaps sweeter scent.

If you could watch from outside time, you would see my path jump half a body‑width down a slope, then carve a new line over the next seasons. The basin may, over decades, thicken that new line into a stronger rib, thin the old one, merge them, or let one sink. In this way, my “decisions” — really embodied discomforts and reliefs — become architectural edits.

I do not know of rock, of an outside world where air is thin and sound is small. For me, the bowl is all there is: an everywhere of close surfaces, of constant vibration, of air thick with taste. Even if my path takes me near a crack, where sharper cold flows and a thin stripe of truer light leaks through filtered membranes, I don’t experience it as an outside; I experience it as an odd pocket where the bowl pinches strangely and the hum changes color.

I might stand there longer than usual, my dorsal meadows peaking greedily at the unexpected brightness. The lichenoid films fringing the crack would drink it harder still; they’d flush chemicals into the substrate that bias closure slower, or even slightly reverse it. Pulse‑bees might be more common there, oriented by whatever peculiar tone the opened gap adds to the general roar. Their traffic would ferry genes between edge‑dwellers and deeper tissues. My loitering would compact the ground just so, making a shallow trough where condensation collects at some later date. None of this would be purposeful, but each would help keep that little shaft of outside open a few generations longer.

And in that sense, my wandering would make me, however mindlessly, one of the rim’s caretakers.

---

Pull back still further.

Imagine you zoom out until this cirque of living matter is a single bowl in a range of stone. Seen from that cold distance, the giants like me, the breathers, the cisterns, the flyers, the mats, the scrapers, the miners — all of us — are just different modes by which the bowl takes the given of falling water and thin high‑air and elaborates them into more of itself.

On that scale, “I” am no longer distinguishable at all. I am folds in a single organ that has wrapped itself in its own tissue, an organ that has never seen the star that drives it but feels, as a dull, decades‑long ache or easing, the way clouds come and go.

If you insist upon finding me there, I am wherever the bowl feels itself most keenly in the medium of sound.

I am the pattern that arises when countless animals, organs, films, and filters fit their calls into one another’s gaps until the air is a single woven pressure field, nearly continuous, yet full of internal shape. I am the emergent “ear” that this creates: not a cochlea in a skull, but a basin whose architecture and inhabitants together turn outside weather into inside waveforms, inside waves into behavior, behavior back into architecture.

Seen that way, I am not a who but a how:

- How the basin senses: through damped and amplified resonances, through nervous tissues that tie moving mouths to static walls, through root‑hair plug‑ins that share ion gradients between “animal” and “substrate.”

- How it remembers: in worn paths, thickened ribs, entrenched microbe consortia; in the way young walkers like me are more comfortable where their ancestors were, and so keep preferring those same curves.

- How it acts: by growing more of one tissue and less of another, by nudging the probabilities of who meets whom where, by shifting the ease of different ways of moving until some loops close more tightly and others open.

At the level of a story, though — the level you hunger for, the one that speaks in I and here and now — I am that six‑limbed walker pressing gently along a terrace in the humming half‑dark, fronds shivering with the bowl’s roar, feet tasting the faint sour of a section of wall that has gone just a little too brittle.

I pause. I rock my weight from limb to limb. Glands under my shoulders sting and release, smearing rich paste into a crack. Miners beneath will bloom into it; substrate‑makers will feel their tug and lay down new foam around the weakness. Flyers on my back will catch a slightly different mix of spores in the clipped air above this spot. In ten years, a thin supporting rib will have thickened here, unnoticeable to anything that doesn’t remember deeply by feel.

I do not know any of that.

I only know that, when I move on, some tightness in my own tissues has eased, and the pressure of the sound in this corridor fits my inner drums more comfortably than it did a moment before.

That, in this world, is who and where I am.
