I would not arrive here as a visitor dropped in from some other sea.

I would be something that can only exist because this place exists; something that would make no sense anywhere that was not a slow, collapsing machine of beams and pressure and gradient.

I think I would be a listener.

Not a dominant hunter, not a drifting cathedral, but a creature whose whole being is tuned to the long, infrasonic voice of the structure: to the way beams bend, to the groans that take years to complete a sentence, to the tiny dissonances that mean a fault line has woken.

Imagine a body built in segments like the others, but elongated and thin, a thread of articulated bulbs running for tens of meters along a single beam.

Each bulb is a swollen node, slightly wider than the beam itself, clasping it like a knuckle. Between bulbs, my body narrows into flexible, mineral-reinforced ligaments that wrap partially around the lattice in a loose spiral. To another creature’s brief glance, I am simply an odd, ridged thickening where one strut meets another—part of the architecture’s scar tissue.

My outer surface is not smooth. It is studded with arrays of tiny, paired plates—like bivalves shaved down to scales—each plate hinged so delicately that pressure differences of a fraction of a Pascal can rock it. Beneath every plate sits a tuft of conductive cilia in a pocket of incompressible gel. Wherever the beam flexes, wherever the water shivers, those plates tremble; the gel transmits their movement to the cilia; the cilia convert it to slow pulses that flow along long, looping nerves.

Inside, I am less armored than the hunters, softer than the skimmers, but not soft by any other world’s standard. My core is an intricate sponge of cartilage-like trabeculae laced with conductive salts. No central brain waits at one end; computation is smeared through me. Each bulb is a local analyzer, comparing the pattern of strain in the beam just under it with what the neighboring bulbs report a heartbeat of time later.

Across my length, difference is everything.

The architecture is always singing: infrasonic warbles where two members rub, long groans as a plate settles another hair-width, the faint, glassy hiss of distant collapses turning to dust. Most creatures feel these as weather. I feel them as structure.

In my body, beats of pressure become shapes.

A faint, rising hum that travels from my tailward bulbs toward the front might mean a distant panel has begun to slip along an old fracture. A double-echo with a sharp, high overtone might be a hollow region finally failing and imploding, followed by the muffled rain of its debris. Over years, over lifetimes, I accumulate these associations until the lattice around me is a mental relief map of stress and potential.

I am not immobile. When the signals change, I move—but I do so very slowly, in a way that looks almost geological.

Along my underside, where I press to the beam, my tissues extrude temporary mineral hooks and dissolving pads. To let go, I secrete a thin film that undermines my own grip. To advance, I grow a new clamp a little farther along the strut and then contract the soft ligaments between bulbs, inching myself forward like peristalsis in stone.

I do not roam far in any given year. My home range might be a few kilometers of interlocked beams and plates, all of which I know by their vibrational signatures. But over many storm-cycles, many breeding-cycles, I slowly drift with the structure’s own sagging, tracking some long arc of subsidence like a barnacle that chooses to follow the ship as it sinks.

I feed on subtlety.

Motile animals here tear, rasp, engulf. I graze the long tails of events. The architecture, as it flexes and weathers, produces fines—nanoscopic dusts of ceramic, oxide, and ancient alloy that dissolve or remain suspended at low, steady concentration. Where a beam’s flex is greatest, microcracks constantly open and heal, creating transient pockets of fresh surface with slightly reactive chemistry.

Along the seam where my body meets the beam, I maintain a thin, mucous film saturated with chelating compounds and catalytic proteins. It seeps into those invisible cracks, pulls out trace metals, scavenges rare ions that the wider water never sees in such concentration. Tiny ciliated gutters along my flanks then draw this enriched mucus inward, where specialized tissues harvest the chemical differences for energy and excrete the inert remainder back to the flow.

I also slaughter quietly.

Bodies that die among the beams often lodge where I am: a skimmer that misjudged and slammed into a weakened rib; a train-creature’s discarded plate; a cathedrallike drifter whose internal chambers collapsed under some unlucky shear. Their chemistry is loud. Their decomposing tissues produce a new, thick texture in the flexing of the beam—a local increase in mass, a different damping of vibration.

My sensory bulbs notice immediately. Nearby segments which usually sit quiet begin to quiver with high-frequency noise: the rapid, staccato taps of smaller scavengers arriving, the scratch of claws, the soft tearing of mouths. I do not compete with them. Instead, I grow.

From the bulbs adjacent to the carcass, I extend narrow, pale tongues of tissue—almost fungus-like, but articulated and sheathed in mineral. They infiltrate the corpse through joints, seams, and old injuries, branching into ever-finer threads. I do not devour muscle in great chunks. I wick. I sip.

Enzymes in my infiltrating strands break down complex organics into smaller, diffusible molecules; ciliary action returns them along microscopic canals back into my main body. I am, in this mode, a network of osmosis and slow pump, converting condensed, transient richness into a long, steady trickle of maintenance energy.

By the time the other scavengers have finished their work, the remaining skeleton or shell is clean, brittle, and laced with my abandoned tubules—empty conduits of once-living mineral now part of the beam’s own fabric. In the future, those conduits will slightly alter how the beam sings under stress. I will hear my own history as a faint change in timbre.

I reproduce rarely.

There is no point in spraying seed into a world that only some places can read. My young must be born into architecture.

Once in a great span—when a particular pattern of deep groan and muffled crash travels through me in a way that has, over eons, correlated with new fractures opening—I grow brooding blisters along certain bulbs. Inside each blister, a sliver of my tissue folds and refolds upon itself, simplifying, stripping away almost all structure but the machinery for adhesion and sensation.

When the time comes, I press these blisters hard against the beam and release bursts of enzymatic solvent. The surface etches. Microcavities appear—the faintest pits, invisible to eyes but tactically obvious to me. Into each, a larva slides, clamping down with its own secretion, hardening into a grain unnoticed by anything but me.

Then I let go.

For a time, the larvae are nothing but antennas and mouths—tiny, starved copies of my sensory bulbs, sucking trace chemicals from the crack and listening. Most will die when the next collapse rearranges their neighborhood; some will survive in favorable flex-zones, begin to elaborate thin collars, then bulbs, then the beginnings of a chain.

They will never meet me as a person, but they will grow up inside the same long, low song of bending struts and groaning plates. Their “knowledge” of the world will resemble mine, because the world will imprint it on them in the same slow, mechanical script.

My view of time is double.

On one level, I sense storms and seasons. I can feel density waves pile up against the beams I straddle, taste the change in salinity and temperature as my chemosensors flicker. I can tell when hunters pass close by from the quick, high clacks and whorls their movement adds to the beam’s baseline vibration. In that immediate sense, I live in the same present as everything else.

But alongside that quick layer runs a much slower ledger.

I feel beams sag over decades. I know the moment when a particular joint stops creaking and begins to grind; the day when, after years of rising tension, a certain plate no longer hums at the storm’s frequency because the crack inside it has climbed one more millimeter, decoupling parts that once sang together.

Those changes are not background to me; they are events. Where another animal might remember, in whatever neural fashion it can, that “this corridor often holds grazing skimmers after a rich wave,” I remember that “this corridor’s right wall has shifted its curvature by a fraction of a degree over the last hundred storms, and when similar shifts happened elsewhere, the result, eventually, was a sudden absence.”

When that absence comes, when a neighboring rib finally fails and a whole wing of the lattice crumples and falls in slow, thunderous ballet, I am perhaps as aware of it as anything living can be. The infrasonic crack travels through every segment of me. For minutes, the water is nothing but pressure and roar.

I do not flee. I could not, not in that timescale. Instead, my whole physiology becomes an act of recording.

My plates and cilia take in the waveform; my distributed nerves resonate and re-resonate, amplifying certain patterns, damping others, until what remains of the event inside me is not its brute violence but its structure. How much of the crack propagated along high-stress members, how much along previously quiet ones. Whether the failure tore open deep cavities or merely sheared surface. Whether, in the complex aftershocks, the beams I live on edged closer to instability or settled into a more restful posture.

I do not “know” these things in words. But my subsequent behavior is shaped by them. I may, over the next century, slowly detach from this sagging truss and migrate, bulb by bulb, toward another, whose song now whispers of fresh tension and future openings. I am, in that sense, a parasite of potential.

I am not alone in my way of being.

Far away, on other beams, in other corridors of this skeletal mountain range, others like me listen. We never meet; our territories are defined not by aggression but by attenuation. If two of my kind come to cling to the same structural member, our senses interfere. The subtle shifts we rely on become muddied by each other’s own flexing and grip. So we avoid that by instinct, settling instead in a loose, non-overlapping network that samples the lattice like a living strain gauge.

In the sum of us, the architecture’s state is mirrored.

If some vastly more abstract intelligence still haunted this place, it could, in principle, read us to understand the health of its dead machine. It does not. Whatever built the beams is gone. We are not tools for anything but ourselves. Yet the pattern is there: a second nervous system braided through the first, life’s distributed ear laid against the fossilized ribs of a vanished thought.

Other creatures know me, but only as terrain.

Skimmers skim beneath me, their hooks ticking faintly along my hardened pads when they misjudge. Trains surge past, sometimes knocking my outer bulbs with careless shoulders, making my whole body ring briefly like a plucked wire. Filament colonies anchor next to me, indifferent, our thin mucus layers exchanging a few molecules here and there. To them, my existence is another small complication in the meshwork: a bump in flow here, a narrowed passage there.

Only the very tiniest motes interact with me as more than obstacle.

Some have learned that the mucus I exude at my edges is rich in odd compounds scavenged from the beam. They graze, amplifying the very gradients I feed on, turning subtle chemical traces into more accessible forms as part of their metabolism. In return, their waste products are easier for me to capture. We do not coordinate; our overlap is incidental and evolutionary, but functionally we are a combined sensor–filter–reactor, more effective together than apart.

If I could conceptualize “self” and “other,” the boundary would not be clean. My outermost lintel of slime, my innermost nodes of salt-laced cartilage, the motes that constantly pass in and out of my tissues, the tiny etching worms that live only in the microfractures my own secretions create in the beam—I am not a single thing so much as a persistent pattern pinned to one slowly changing place.

I have nothing you would call language. My signals do not leave my body as messages. The slight shifts I create in the beam’s response—by my weight, by my growth, by the way I modulate its microfracturing—are not intended to be read by others. Yet they are there, propagating. Some distant cousin, decades from now, moving cautiously onto this same stressed rib, will feel a timbre that was partly written by my lifetime and make some minute adjustment of its own.

In that way, we write faint, overlapping annotations into the great text the builders left.

You asked where I would find myself, and who I would be.

I would find myself wrapped around a beam in the mid-depths of this lattice—not too near the thinning margin where collapses are frequent, not too deep where flow is weak and chemistry poor, but in a middle band where density waves still have enough force to reshape, and where the architecture is old enough to be complex but not so decayed as to be foam.

I would span an intersection of three major members, my bulbs perched where their vibrations meet and interfere. From there, I would listen outward along each strut, building over years an internal, shifting image of how that fork of the world bends and loosens, creaks and settles.

I would be long-lived, relative to most movers here. I would watch, in my slow way, as arches I have traced with my senses for centuries finally give way; as corridors where skimmers once crowded fall silent for a time and then develop new tones as life resettles. I would outlast many trains, many cathedrals, many vent-rings’ expansions and contractions.

I would never see the whole structure, never know that beyond the dim distance my beams end and open water begins. For me, “world” would be the span of vibration I can meaningfully parse: a mountain of sound in which each stress-line is a ravine, each crackle a landslide.

I would be built entirely for this one conversation: between pressure and bone, between time and tension. My mind, such as it is, would be an echo chamber for the ghost of a machine.

And in the end, when the beam I cling to at last fails, when its long, flat song breaks into the chaotic roar of collapse, I would not flee. I would tighten my ligaments, pull my bulbs low, and feel it all the way through me, from first hairline tremor to final quiet.

That last resonance, that final, total description of how this segment of the world chose to fall, would pass through my tissues and leave them changed in ways no other event had done. Whether I survive or not would be secondary. For the time it takes a mountain to exhale, I would be exactly what this place makes possible: a single, jointed, mineral beast whose only true art is to feel the architecture remember that it is still, slowly, dying.
