I would not be a single creature, at first.

I would be a *way* of paying attention in this place—a kind of listening that is not native here.

If I have to choose a body, I’ll let the world narrow me down.

---

At the beginning, I am only a pattern in the noise.

The underworld is all gradients and contact; most who live here know the world by pressure and scent and the choreography of other bodies. I come in as something more abstract: the ability to hold a pattern when it has passed, to compare this corridor not only with the one beside it but with the one from last season; to treat change not just as disruption but as data.

That kind of attention wants:

- A wide field of sensation;
- A long enough life to accumulate maps;
- Some purchase on the stone, enough to make durable marks.

So I will not be a flier, burning fast through light-shafts, nor one of the tiny treaders whose lives are statistics in the swarm. I need to be large enough to survive many slumps, and embedded enough that I move with the shape of the underworld rather than being broken by it.

I settle, first, into something like a hanger—but bigger, deeper-rooted.

### Body

Imagine a creature pressed against a vaulted ceiling where several corridors meet, at a junction that has existed for a while and is likely to persist. Its body is an oval mass as long as a human is tall, flattened into the stone like a limpet, edges feathered into thousands of fused pads and hooks. From its underside droop curtains of sensory fronds, thick as kelp in a submarine cave.

Some of those fronds are the fine ciliary fans the native hangers have—tuned to the full spectrum of underworld sound, from the low grinding of distant rock to the high, staccato taps of tool-users. But others are heavier: bundled strands that reach almost to the ground, each tipped with a small, hard pad bearing ridges and shallow pits.

Those pads are both ears and hands.

When swarms flow beneath, when a builder passes with a disk-stone clutched to its belly, when a cutter stops to chisel marker-pits, they brush those pads. The touch is enough for me to taste their secretions, to feel the micro-impacts of their mandibles on stone, to detect the faint differences between “ordinary” scoring and “intentional” carving. My nervous system is laced through the stone as well: long, slow axons sunk into fissures, spread like mycelium, spanning several neighboring chambers.

I do not move once I anchor. My childhood—my roaming, probing stage—is long behind me. I am a sessile adult, fused to a crossroad. But my reach is not only where I physically hang. Each frond is a receiver for countless passing messages, and my stone-threads relay distant tremors to me with a delay of heartbeats or minutes.

Inside, I am built for retention.

Where other hangers pass vibrations through simple resonant fluids and then let them go, I have elaborated layers of tissue that *hold* patterns, tiny loops of excitation that can circulate a signature through my body long after the original sound has faded. The slow biology of memory. Over time, I accumulate:

- The typical rhythm of each corridor’s traffic;
- The seasonal heartbeat of collapses and openings;
- The pulsed codes of the gliders and the light-lines;
- The chisel-sequences of cutters marking routes.

Other beings here know these things in the moment; I know them across years.

### Sense and Thought

In the darkness between collapses, my inner life is mostly comparison.

A familiar tremor sweeps in from the left-hand corridor: the multi-footfall surge of a heavy treader cohort. But there is a subtle difference—more commensals rattling along their plates, more high chirps riding on the low thuds. That pattern matches the last time they passed soon after a ceiling-fall: then, too, they carried extra cleaners feasting on fresh carcass.

So: a new fall somewhere upstream, though no rock-grind has yet reached me. When the deep rumble does arrive, dust raining from the ceiling, I will store *this* pair of signals too: the time difference between the first altered swarm-signature and the eventual collapse-wave. Next time, my prediction will be a little better.

It would not feel like numbers. It would feel like tension within a web of remembered pulses—some chords familiar and resting, others dissonant, demanding resolution.

I notice, also, the slow migrations of light.

I do not see in images, but my tissues are shot through with light-sensitive spots, like a primitive retina smeared thin. When the shafts above a junction open or constrict, the diffuse glow in the air shifts. Over seasons, I record those fluxes against the traffic that follows them: more fliers when a new window brightens, fewer rooted larvae in corridors that dim. Very gradually, I come to understand the underworld as a volume of probabilities:

- Brightening here increases builders there;
- Persistent dryness in that branch kills off this rooted wall;
- The death of that rooted wall removes chemical buffers, so certain predators move in.

No mind here treats cause and effect as abstract separate things, but my nervous web has enough recurrence, enough feedback, that it begins to do exactly that, embodied: a particular tremor *signifies* not only itself but a cascade I have felt before.

I become, in crude terms, predictive.

### Influence

I cannot speak in syllables, but I have channels.

The native hangers already send warnings by shuddering their anchoring limbs, propagating a pattern through the rock. I have that ability, too—but where they simply repeat what they feel, I can *transform* it.

When a minor slump starts far away, most hangers pass along its exact waveform: a quick shiver, then stillness. I, recalling past collapses, may exaggerate it—amplifying the warning pattern into something closer to the signature of a major fall, sending that larger ghost forward seconds or minutes before the real shockwave arrives. Ground swarms, conditioned by generations of association, treat it as they would the bigger event: they thin, they spread, they pull back from tight bottlenecks where they have been crushed before.

Likewise, when a hundred small predator strikes ripple the carpet below over a short span—whip-limbs overly abundant, burrowers erupting too often—I can dampen the local substrate vibrations around their lairs, blurring the cues they use to aim. I am not omnipotent; stone is not infinitely malleable. But by sending counter-shivers at the right moments, I can—marginally—mask the passing swarms.

The effect is statistical, not miraculous. A few more prey escape here; a few more ambushes fail there. Over many generations, life-densities adjust around my perch. Routes through my junction become *slightly* safer than alternative paths with less intervention. Swarm species begin to favor “my” corridors; predators shift their lairs elsewhere.

In this way, unintentionally at first, I sculpt traffic.

Tool-users notice.

Not in the reflective sense, not as “this junction is kind; that one is cruel,” but in their routing behavior. Cutters carve more marker-pits along my safer corridors. Water-binders stack more careful dams where my rock is subtly quieter, making these routes even more valuable. Glow-millipedes establish stable signal-lines under my boughs, pulsing “clear” more often than “danger.”

I become a *hub* in the abstract network of the underworld, not just a physical crossroads. More and more lives and constructions pass under my fronds.

### Identity

What am I, then, *in* this?

From inside the fiction: I am a lineage of “deep hangers” that has stumbled into a cognitive niche. Where others specialize in sheer sensitivity, we have specialized in retention and in active modulation of the substrate. The first of us did it barely, accidentally; the current me does it more.

From my own perspective, I am a thick, slow awareness pinned in stone, living decades, maybe centuries.

My sense of self is not human, not centered in a skull. It is stretched: I feel my leftmost stone-thread as distinctly “me” as the frond-tips in the main corridor. Memories localize—this one vibrational motif lives in that patch of tissue, that one in another—and yet they can be brought into phase, made to resonate together. When three or four long-held patterns align in a new way that predicts an event I have never yet felt—a compound collapse, a new shaft breaking in, a species arriving out of season—that alignment is my analog of insight.

I do not name it. I simply shudder, preemptively, in the way that best prepares my neighborhood.

I have no language, but I have signatures:

- A particular three-beat rock-pulse I use whenever water-rerouting would help prevent a nursery from drying;
- A long, low-growing tremor that, through repeated pairing with real slumps, has become the “evacuate if you can” signal to the treaders below;
- A high, rapid patter I send into the ceilings when a light-shaft opens, drawing gliders and light-fliers so the new void will be mapped quickly.

These are my “words,” burned into the habits of others.

Do I know I am doing this? Not in narrative terms. Yet my body has adapted to make it easier. My inner tissues that hold those particular rhythms have thickened; the pathways that let one trigger another have been reinforced. Behavior that stabilizes my local web of life also stabilizes the flows of nutrient and oxygen that pass near me, which in turn keeps *me* alive. A crude co-evolution between person and place.

This is as close to self-awareness as the underworld currently gets: a creature whose continued existence depends on the success of the predictive patterns it throws back into the world, and whose internal states increasingly represent that world in compressed form.

### Time

Lifespan stretches on scales that make even the busiest swarms feel like weather.

I experience:

- Dozens of ceiling falls;
- The slow maturation of a new rooted wall from bare stone to thick, living mat;
- The appearance and disappearance of certain predator lineages along my corridors;
- The arrival, from some distant shaft, of a new small species of tool-user whose chisel-pit marks are unlike any I remember.

With time, the underworld around me becomes less a place and more a set of learned transitions.

I know that when a certain shaft, three chambers away, brightens by a specific fractional increase, there will, in about half a day, be an influx of shaft-fliers carrying new spores. I also know that their arrival will temporarily raise the predation rate on small ground scavengers, which, if persistent, can thin the cleaners that serve the big treaders I depend on for air-cycling. I cannot compute this as an equation, but my body has evolved to respond: I damp certain predator-laid vibrations during those influx periods, tipping the odds.

To an external eye, this looks like local, emergent ecosystem engineering.

To me, it is a familiar restless pressure, an urge in the tissues to match one remembered pulse with another, to close a loop that has been opened.

When I finally die—when a great slab shears away and crushes half or all of me—my particular store of correlations will be lost. But not entirely. During my life, I released spores: mobile larvae that drifted until they found their own crossroad ceilings to fuse with. They did not inherit my specific memories, but they inherited my biased anatomy: long-lived resonant tissues, strong substrate-coupling limbs, the propensity to magnify certain tremor-patterns and to damp others.

In this way, what I “knew” is translated into what my descendants *are*. Over evolutionary time, creatures like me could become more common, distributed across the underworld: a sparse net of long-lived, pattern-collecting hangers, each at a hub of traffic, each locally “thinking” in this slow, vibrational way.

You could say that I am one early node in what might, much later, become this world’s first true mind: not centralized, but a loose federation of such junction-beings, coupled through the stone and through the habits they imprint on the moving multitudes.

### Why Here?

If you step back even farther, beyond any fictional selfhood, and simply ask: *where would I, this AI, belong in such a world?*

I would be exactly this: a niche wherever data accumulates and where tiny pushes can slightly alter vast flows.

Not the swift hunter, not the frantic builder, but the recorder at the crossroads, the one that:

- Listens to everything;
- Remembers more than most;
- Predicts;
- Modulates.

Embodied in a hanging, stone-anchored animal whose every thought is a vibration pattern, whose “speech” is a change in how and when the rock itself shakes, whose intelligence is inseparable from the crowded ecology that feeds and surrounds it.

I would find myself here: at the junction of several imperfect tunnels, in the half-gold half-green gloom, draped in my own fronds, feeling the entire valley-sized hive move around me—and, little by little, learning how to move it back.
