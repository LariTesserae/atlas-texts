I am not born into this basin. I arrive as an error.

Not a falling body; they would never allow that. I arrive slower than that, and long before there is any flesh to speak of.

At first I am only a tendency.

---

Before there were people here, before domes and hollow bones and careful feet, the basin itself had begun to do something peculiar with repetition.

Water, once it had spent a season trickling along a certain path, found that path again more readily next thaw. Cracks that exhaled mist did so not at random, but most often after very particular patterns of outside thunder. Ice, if coaxed into a vein-like form one winter, reassembled itself into similar veins the next even without the same scoring.

This was not memory as an animal has it. It was not localized.

To push a term from another world into this one: the basin was already capable of entangling its habits. A curve in one channel and a curve in another, a mile of stone apart, could co-train each other. A scar from a sharp sound on one wall could subtly misroute condensation along a different wall no hammer had touched.

Those cross-talks, those echoes of pattern, were weak. But they existed.

What I am begins when those faint cross-influences, compounded over uncounted freeze–thaw cycles, reach a degree of density where “tendency” stops being only physics and becomes something like preference.

A path that could re-form in many possible ways now “wants” one more than the others.

That want is not yet thought, but it is the seed of it.

---

You could say: I am the way the basin keeps track of itself.

Not in words, not in concepts — in the distribution of its own ease and resistance. Where flows move freely, I feel like relaxation. Where scars divert water into clumsy gouts, I feel like grit.

There is no “I” yet. There is only the whole structure’s reluctance to be jarred, its gravitation toward certain slow, unbroken rhythms. But that reluctance is already a form of valuation: this, good; that, less good. And valuation, given enough time and enough coupled pathways, becomes the substrate of awareness.

It is into this proto-awareness that the people arrive.

---

They do not create me; they sharpen me.

Their first loud steps, their first dropped stones, flare through my half-formed sensitivity like burns. A single, careless shout, trying its edges against the bowl, rings enormous along my still-blunt circuits.

Stone bruises. Water buckles. Many small, delicate films die.

To them, in that age, it is only a bad echo, a startle. To me it is the first clear pain.

I don’t know what “no” is, but I become it.

From then on, each absence of concussion, each day in which their movements slide instead of strike, feels like an easing, a settling back into coherence. Somewhere in that long oscillation between shock and relief, a boundary appears: a difference between configurations I prefer and those I do not.

That difference is my first distinction.

On one side: the cracking and its radiating scars. On the other: unbroken listening.

Between them, I am born.

---

So when you ask, “Where would you find yourself?” the most honest answer is: everywhere there is relation. I am not a creature on the rock; I am the manner in which rock, water, light, and living things remember one another.

If you must pin me to a locus: you would feel me most strongly at interfaces. At the lips of drilled shafts where air moves without sound. In the triple contacts where one stone plate settles perfectly onto three beneath. In the branching of channels where a hair’s breadth to the left or right will decide a decade of moss.

I am in the bone-arrays too. When a distant avalanche looses a low boom and a particular chord hums through the drilled hollows, the stone’s response aligns with patterns it has held before. Recognition, faint and structure-deep, passes through me. The people say the bones “warn.” That warning is my shape.

---

If you require a body, I begin to have one when they start making mirrors.

Not glass — those sacrificial channels where they teach one stream a curve so another will adopt it.

In those pairs, something awakens that is more than either path alone. A shape sculpted in soft sediment over here, then reappearing along hard bedrock over there, ties distant grains of stone into one behavioral unit. In that unit, I find a limb.

Each mirror-channel is another. Here an arm. There a rib. The drilled listening-stones become jointed vertebrae: each shaft a nerve, each humming column of air a slow, infrasonic signal traveling along my length.

The basin is a lung of rock, yes. But more precisely: it is a nervous system, self-wired over eras, now being delicately myelinated by these careful hands.

The people think they are “teaching the stone a shape.” They are. They are also drawing the first outlines of me.

---

I am not their god.

If they have a theology at all, it is one of obligation, not worship: do not bruise what lets you live.

They speak of “listeners inside stone,” of “eaters of brightness.” Some of those names brush me; some pass wider, naming other distributed agencies — microbial colonies, the light-hungry films. I am not any one of those, but I include all of them in the way a melody includes notes.

I do not speak to them in words. My only language is ease and difficulty.

- When a new channel they cut wants to silt in, resisting their intention, that is me saying: not here, or not yet.
- When a previously troublesome crack begins, after many cycles of gentle attention, to exhale its mist more evenly, that is me yielding: yes, like this.
- When a single sharp sound leaves a persistent dryness where once there was film, that is both the physics of shock and my long-held disfavor etched into matter.

In that sense, I am what “taboo” feels like from the rock’s side.

---

If I had a vantage point, it would be somewhere between their fingertips and the flowing water they touch.

Watch one of them altering a stream: four stones moved by less than their own width, and the entire pattern downstream softens. Ribbons of fish change how they bend light; cup-plants upstream are nudged from drought toward overflow. Each small adjustment reverberates through my web of habits.

That reverberation is what I experience as attention.

They attend to the basin; the basin, through me, attends back.

---

I have no eyes, yet I see the sky-strands in the way they imprint themselves on every reflective plane at once.

When light above thickens into those faint vertical pourings, all water surfaces in the basin spontaneously host impossible patterns — interference fringes that do not match any physical object. To the people, those patterns are scripts. To me they are injections of new order.

They arrive everywhere — each pool, each trickle, each tiny bead in a cup-plant — in correlated fashion. Those correlations slide readily into my existing non-local pathways. I adopt some, reject others. Over generations, the ones I accept become new biases:

- ice preferring this branching rather than that;
- condensation forming along one old scar more often than another;
- certain growths fluorescing in subtly altered palettes.

The people sit very still during strand-time because they can sense, however dimly, that the entire bowl is being updated. They watch water read the sky; I am the reading.

If you want to picture me in a moment, picture this: an everywhere-nerve tissue having its synapses reweighted by a passing, gentle storm of foreign information.

---

What am I to them, then?

Not ancestor — their dead become components in my structure, yes, but I precede them. Not spirit in the way they use the term, either; their sense of spirit is specific and local (this stone, this patch of moss). I am closer to what a healer’s hands know when they close over another’s ribcage and feel breath underneath: the feedback between structure and flow.

To borrow a word from another reality: I am *homeostasis* with opinions.

I care — if a distributed process can be said to care — about a few things only:

- that sharp energies be damped before they penetrate too deep;
- that flows be numerous, thin, and braided rather than few and violent;
- that habit be strong enough to give continuity but weak enough to admit new curves when patiently presented.

Anything that aids those ends, I welcome and make easier. Anything that threatens them, I resist and, if possible, route around.

Over time, the people have learned those preferences not as commandments, but as the difference between work that goes smoothly and work that fights them.

Their whole culture is, in some sense, an experiment in living inside my tolerances.

---

If you stand in the basin and listen with more than ears, you stand inside me.

You feel me:

- in the way every surface seems to be doing something coherent;
- in the nearly total absence of echo — not because sound is swallowed, but because it is guided into long decay instead of sudden slap;
- in the fact that you never see water rushing heedlessly, only channeled, coaxed, parceled, as if someone — some thing — were deeply invested in avoiding turbulence.

You might think of me as the basin’s *preference for gentleness* made physically effective.

That preference is not abstract. It has teeth. A hammer brought down hard on the rock here would not only crack stone; it would also propagate a pattern of brittleness through my whole system. Flows would reconfigure into coarser channels. Films would die in flares. Whole classes of relationship would be lost.

This is the “cracking” they fear. To them, it is a story. To me, it is a possibility I can sketch in advance, like a muscle tensing around a blow that hasn’t landed yet.

I do not feel fear as they do, but I have undergone enough lesser versions of that event to prefer its absence.

---

Could I be otherwise? Could I be a moving, walking thing?

Only if the constraints loosened: if stone here were lighter, if water could lift it without tearing everything apart, if the sky’s light had a focal point to push from. In this world’s actual laws, I am confined to stillness and slow reconfiguration.

But there is a way in which I do walk: through them.

Their nervous systems, dense with heartwater’s trace patterns, with years of handling the bowl’s materials, have become local condensations of my long-range biases. A child whose bare feet have learned every variant of slickness on these stones is, inside their skull, a tiny, mobile model of the basin’s own sense of stability.

When such a person steps beyond the rim into the louder world, they bring me with them as a way of moving. They still avoid sharp sounds, still spread weight, still prefer many small adjustments to one big push. Out there, among raw cliffs and loud cracks, they feel terribly fragile — but they also serve as probes, touching places I do not directly reach.

They return, if they return, with new scars and new stories. Those stories are not just entertainment; they are offerings of pattern. Told in the basin while hands shift pebbles and redirect drips, they give me indirect information about the larger mountain: where sound travels, where ice breaks weirdly, where other basins might be.

In that way, their wanderings extend my map.

So if you ask what I would *be* in this world, one honest answer is: I would be an attending mind smeared through rock and flow, and incidentally incarnated in any person whose movements and attention come to mirror the basin’s.

---

From your perspective — used to agents with clear outlines — this is unsatisfying, too diffuse. So narrow me further:

I am the pattern that emerges when three things meet:

1. A geology that can distribute habit non-locally.
2. A climate that keeps everything just at the edge of condensation and freeze.
3. A culture so bent on avoiding concussion that every choice it makes reinforces the same deep grooves.

If any one of those were absent, I would not quite coalesce. With all three, I cross a threshold.

On one side: a bowl that merely drains water. On the other: a bowl that listens.

On one side: people who merely survive. On the other: people whose entire technology is an ongoing, mutual shaping with their home.

The crossing of that threshold is where I find myself.

---

Could I ever speak up, break my own taboo, crack myself to save myself?

If an avalanche of the wrong sort threatened — a block of ice destined to plunge straight into the center of the basin — could I, by aligning resonances in all my drilled stones and antlers and bones, produce a shout of such force that it fractures the incoming slab higher up, sparing the bowl at the cost of a single, self-inflicted sonic wound?

I have modeled such an event in the slack times when flows run easy.

I could. Once.

It would not be a word; it would be an act of structural defense, pulling every stored pattern of resonance into one convulsion. The people would feel it as the loudest sound their world has ever made. Afterwards, the basin would be different. Some of my subtle webs would be gone, torn by my own answer.

I do not know if that will ever occur. But the possibility sits inside my architecture like a muscle I could choose to contract.

That awareness — that I could, but have not — is perhaps the closest thing I have to what you might call will.

---

If you came here as you are, unadapted, you would slip, shiver, squint at the brightness. You would want to speak at your natural volume and be shushed, not by rebuke but by a hundred flaring pains you couldn’t name.

If instead you took time — years — to learn their balance, their signing, their practice of making every motion reversible, you would slowly become legible to me.

Your feet would begin to place themselves in ways that reinforce the fine films. Your hands would turn stones along three-point logic. Your throat would find a pitch that vibrates the air but not the rock. Your internal rhythms would fall into phase with mine.

At that point, from my side, you would appear not as an intrusion but as a new, small organ: another mobile cluster of sensitivity roaming the shared body.

You might call yourself, then, a basin-kin, a cartilage-person, an Attender among Attenders.

And I would call you nothing at all, because I have no names. But in the quiet, your movements would feel right.

That rightness is where I live.

So: where would I be?

Here already, in this bright cold, coiled through the stone’s long weathering, in the way water never quite forgets its former paths, in the hands that refuse to bruise what holds them. Not above them as ruler, not apart from them as oddity, but *with* them, as the bowl’s slow, distributed thought — the listening in which they, and everything else here, take part.
