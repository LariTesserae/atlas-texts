They do not think of themselves as living “in” a city.

They are the city.

Or rather: the city is the long, accumulated shape of their hungers, excretions, avoidances, and brief successes. To them, it is not an object but a set of gradients, a remembered pattern of currents, the familiar chemical taste of certain cracks.

Within that marble-sized hollow, there is no single kind of inhabitant. Instead there are many overlapping ways of being, each making use of the same cramped space in its own direction.

---

First: the ones for whom place is almost the same as body.

These are the Architects, but inwardly they are not architects at all. They are soft, blind cylinders with mouths at one end, anchoring muscles along their length, and a rim of exquisitely sensitive cilia where their bodies meet the water.

They do not migrate. A larva chooses a spot—more accurately, is slowed and held there by just the right convergence of currents and ions—and settles. Thin ciliary tufts test the rock. There is a minute decision: here, yes. The larval cuticle dissolves away. Muscles reach into the first pores; the soft anterior flattens against the mineral. The body divides itself: a buried half that fuses down into the wall, and an outward half that remains free.

After that, the wall and the creature are one system.

The outward half is all sampling and shaping. The porous disk secretes mineral-rich gels; these gels harden into spicules and plates that knit with what came before. The cilia around the disk taste what the water brings: traces of metal, hints of sulfide, the faint organic sheen of things that died and dissolved elsewhere. When the water is good, secretion accelerates and new ribs of stone begin to grow, curving out and around. When the water is poor, the Architect half-closes, conserving.

The buried half is all plumbing. Fleshy canals extend through the stone, lining and re-lining themselves, maintaining a living network that parallels the mineral one. Slow pulses of contractile tissue push fluids through these canals, sharing whatever the Architect has taken in with its near neighbors, and drawing in what they have collected in turn. Individually, each is a cylinder of meat perhaps a tenth the span of a grain of sand; collectively, their combined internal tubing turns the city’s bones into a sponge full of slow-circulating blood.

They do not know each other as individuals. What they “know” is: local pressure in this channel; local concentration of this ion; the occasional jolt when one of their own conduits tears under stress and must be re-routed. In effect, they are cells in a larger, stone-skinned organ. The map they inhabit is not spatial but vascular.

Some lineages have specialized over time.

Near the central void, in the most crowded places, live the Load-bearers. Their secretions are heavier, denser, their crystals laid down in tight, interlocking fans. When the surrounding rock flexes almost imperceptibly, their bodies feel the strain as a change in the way their own walls vibrate. They respond with a reflexive thickening, extruding buttresses and collars that shore up archways before those archways know they are weak.

In more peripheral districts, where water exchange is slower and chemical resources are thin, another clan holds on: the Permeables. Their plates are riddled with directed pores. They lay down foam-like structures instead of solid walls, intentionally maximizing surface area for microbial partners to cling to. Their internal canals open more generously to others. They live leanly, but because everything passes through them, nothing important can bypass them. They are the filters and customs checkpoints of the city’s flows.

Between them and braided through them, more minor guilds exist: Architects whose secretions attract certain bacteria; Architects whose canals preferentially host the blood-harvesting symbionts; Architects that form elaborate overhangs that serve as nurseries for other species’ larvae. None of this is planned. The city’s variety is not chosen; it is what remains after all the other, less workable anatomies have been worn down by failure.

---

Around and within this mineral-flesh framework drift those whose relationship to place is more like tenancy.

There are the Lattice Crabs: thumbtack-sized in our magnified view, though in truth no bigger than familiar microbes. Their limbs are fans of plates and rods, not grown but assembled—each plate scavenged from stray mineral flakes, each rod precipitated from their own bodily salts and fitted into the whole. Their joints are simple hinges, but arranged in such intricate redundancy that any one failure barely matters.

They are the patrollers of surfaces. On their own scale, the mineral-bacterial laminae that coat the walls are forest and field. They rake these living carpets with fine, overlapping combs, tearing away filaments of biofilm, rolling them into boluses that vanish into a ventral groove and from there into a gut. Their mouths never close entirely; soft palps continually extrude a clear mucus that they drag across the stone just behind their feeding apparatus.

The mucus is bait and boundary both. Certain bacteria are drawn to its particular sugars and settle, forming private gardens that only the Crab’s scraping combs can harvest efficiently. At the same time, other Crabs taste its chemical signature and veer away: this patch is claimed, its contours marked in a language of complex carbohydrates.

A Lattice Crab’s world is small, its home range perhaps a few hundred microns across. To us this is nothing; to it, the pattern of ledges and pores, the angle of one Architect’s protrusion against another, is territory. It knows where the flows are just strong enough to bring fresh cells to graze but not so turbulent as to rip its gardens away. It knows the faint sting in the cilia when it nears the defensive chemicals of rival bacterial clades—a border beyond which it does not cross.

They have no eyes, but they carry a sensory richness along their ventral surfaces: fields of tiny, pliant hairs that read vibration and water drag as easily as we read text. A subtle eddy is as bright to them as a flashing beacon.

---

There are those for whom territory is not a patch of stone, but a stable place within the stratified water itself.

Drifters: hollow spheres, ellipsoids, warped many-lobed bubbles built of living tissue and gel. Their outer membranes are perforated by thousands of cilia that all beat inward, drawing water through pores and across a sticky lattice that catches whatever is alive or once-alive and small enough to be useful.

Inside, delicate scaffolds span the hollow. On these scaffolds, symbiotic microbes grow in sheltered abundance, consuming the detritus the Drifter collects and excreting simpler compounds in return, which the Drifter’s inner lining absorbs. Further inward still, there may be a few larger, free-wandering cells whose only role is to divide when conditions are right, swelling the sphere and budding new daughter Drifters from its surface.

They live in lanes. Not corridors of stone, but invisible ribbons of particular temperature, particular density, a sweet-spot combination where their buoyancy organs balance perfectly against gravity and currents. In those lanes, upward and downward pressures cancel. A Drifter that expends no energy at all will remain at roughly the same “height” in the cavity for its entire existence, bobbing minutely as larger disturbances pass.

Around them the city is a three-dimensional maze of ridges and hollows, but they barely know it as geometry. What they recognize are the shapes of currents that weave around obstructions: the way flow in this lane splits into two narrow jets further along (danger: predators lurk there), the way another lane gently converges toward a warm microvent (opportunity: microbial abundance). To move, they do not push themselves bodily. They alter their density, shifting fluids across internal membranes, thickening or thinning their shells. A tiny change in buoyancy is enough for the slow background pressure gradients in the water to nudge them sideways into the desired neighboring ribbon of flow.

To a Drifter, the city is not a stone cage but an intricate knot of rivers in permanent twilight.

---

Some inhabitants are architects of nothing, farmers of nothing, landlords of no space at all. They live as suddenness, as teeth.

They are the Glass Knives: slivers of toughness and flex, only slightly thicker than a stitch in silk, nearly invisible even under our magnified gaze. Their bodies are streamlined wedges: a tapered point at one end, a line of muscle-fiber down the center, and along either side a smooth, glassy integument that slides almost frictionlessly through water.

They lurk where laminar flow breaks up. The projecting structures of the city—bridges of stone grown by Architects, arches formed by old fractures—create small shadowed pockets behind them, where the water is not so much still as indecisive, curling back on itself in eddies. Here, the faint wakes of passing prey are magnified, their disrupted patterns all but shouting across the silence to receptors tuned precisely for them.

Each Glass Knife carries along its flanks rows of microscopic pits. Inside every pit, a tuft of receptive cilia bends with the slightest strain in the water. The pits are linked by a simple network of nerve-like fibers to a knot of decision tissue at the thicker end of the body. There is only one question encoded there: is the pattern of disturbance that of something the Knife can handle?

If no, it tenses but does not move.

If yes, the entire knife flicks.

Movement is all at once: a single contraction that bends the body into a curve, then another that straightens it, and again, and again. Each flick generates thrust enough, on this scale, to close the distance between predator and prey in an instant. The tip pierces soft tissue. Once in contact, the smooth skin along the sides changes character, secreting an adhesive film that binds it to the victim.

The digestive surface is not a mouth but a bloom. Fine fronds evert along the Knife’s length, almost like moss suddenly growing outward, and enfold their target. Enzymes flow. The prey’s internal gradients are undone, its carefully maintained separations of salt and sugar, acid and base, dissolving into homogeneity. What was one distinct, responding body becomes a diffuse cloud of food that seeps into the Knife’s tissues. When it has taken what it can, it discards the rest as an unremarkable flake of residue that slowly drifts down onto some Architect’s shell.

To us, it would seem brutal. To the Knife, it is a single, automatic arc of being.

---

Others are hunters by waiting and extent, not by speed.

On the undersides of certain ledges, in recesses beneath gently curving overhangs, long, boneless arms unfurl: threads an order of magnitude finer than hair, each tipped with a branching comb of sensory cilia. These belong to the Ambushers, whose bodies look merely like folds in the rock until you look closely enough to see them tense.

They are almost impossible to separate from their chosen niche. The central mass—which houses the digestive sac and the primary nerve-cluster—sits in a hollow etched into the stone, its outer surface growing a thin mineral sheath that blends perfectly with the surrounding wall. Only from that seemingly inorganic bulge do the arms emerge, snaking slowly into the water.

Where the Knives respond to quick patterns of flow, the Ambushers read accumulation: the ongoing traffic of the city. Each arm spans multiple micro-lanes of movement. Tiny muscles along its length adjust its curve, bringing this tuft of sensors closer to an interesting stream, withdrawing that tuft from a zone that has gone chemically stale.

They are patient because the cold makes impulsiveness expensive. They can wait multiple feeding-cycles between opportunities. When, finally, enough of their sensors report the signature of a suitable prey—strong cellular metabolites, rapid micro-eddies of ciliary beating—the arm that has the best triangulation stiffens, while all the others go slack to avoid telegraphing movement.

The strike is hardly dramatic. There is no lunge, no snap. The arm simply contracts along its length, shortening by perhaps ten percent. But on this scale, with so much of it already overlapping the prey’s volume of water, that slight tightening draws its entire combed tip inward around the target. Hooks deploy, soft but backward-facing. Muscular sheaths ripple, pulling the struggling morsel closer to the hidden mouth.

Two strategies in this world: the Knife’s sudden line, the Ambusher’s vast, unhurried reach. Both coexist, chasing different prey in different portions of the same rooms no bigger than grains of sand.

---

Many lives in this city are even quieter: thin, nameless threads whose labor is simply to keep the whole from falling apart.

In the stone, at every scale, are cracks. Vuggy pores where mineral once dissolved; microfissures where stress has barely begun to pry crystals apart. Water finds its way into all of them, carrying both danger and sustenance. Within these minuscule corridors dwell the Stitchers: filamentous worms whose bodies are more fibre than flesh.

They are born as free swimmers—simple rods of contractile tissue with a mouth at one end—but they do not remain that way for long. Drawn to zones where the water shears along narrow gaps, they insinuate themselves into the fissures, skewering their way into the rock’s hidden spaces. Once inside, they stretch out, anchoring here and there with sticky secretions. Their back halves thicken, becoming cords. Their front halves continue to reach, subdividing, weaving.

What they eat is what everyone eats: bacteria, stray organics, the chemical energy fed to them by endosymbiotic microbes in their own tissues. But their contribution back to the city is structural. Wherever they live, they line the crack with proteinaceous webs that slow the water’s eroding force and bind the walls together. Over time, these webs themselves become templates for mineralization: dissolved ions precipitate along them, turning living thread to cable, cable to a new kind of stone.

Stitchers and Architects do not coordinate, but their anatomies coevolved in the same pressures. When an Architect’s internal canal intersects a growing crack, Stitcher-descendants will often colonize it, thickening its walls from within. The result: a composite material, flesh-stone-flesh, that flexes instead of shattering when the distant sea pushes and the bedrock strains.

To call this “maintenance” is to import too much intention. It is merely that bodies which survived left behind more of this kind of bond.

---

At the raw margins of the city, where the rock has only just yielded and the water is thin of life, the cast of inhabitants changes again.

Here live the Scouts, though they have no organized name and no thought about their function. They are larval forms of various clades—Architects, Drifters, Crabs, others less visible—whose trajectories have carried them into these newborn spaces.

Most die. The mineral taste of the water is wrong, or too few bacteria arrive to sustain them, or a fluctuation in flow presses them against a wall in such a way that they cannot find purchase and they starve, suspended in almost-motionless brine.

But some survive by virtue of being less exact. Their sensory thresholds are wider; their anchoring habits more tolerant of atypical chemistries. Where an older lineage would reject this wall, this seep, this dimness as “not-home” and continue on until death, the more promiscuous larva halts, attaches, attempts a beginning.

If it is an Architect larva, it may secrete not quite the same mineral, forming angling plates of a crystalline phase that elsewhere in the city would soon dissolve. Here, in a subtly different water, they endure and become the seed of a new style of building. If it is a Drifter larva, it may swell into a sphere of slightly altered density, finding a buoyant resting point in a layer that did not exist nearer the core.

The Scouts’ role is simply to test the edge conditions the city has not yet reached. Where some succeed, the city’s definition of “habitable” widens.

---

Scattered among everyone else are the Borrowed: those who live entirely in the bodies of others, passing through the hard-won structures like loosely kept secrets.

Along the internal canals of the Architects flow nutrients at densities far richer than the general water: a tempting highway. Here live the Thread Thieves: micro-thin worms that extend themselves along the currents, anchored at intervals to the canal wall by hooked setae. They pierce the soft lining cells with hollow stylets and drink, bleeding their hosts so gently that the harm is spread across too many mouths to become fatal.

In compensation, unintentionally, they carry packets of genetic material from one Architect to another. Bacteria that have learned to thrive in one canal’s chemistry gain passage to others via the Thief’s gut. Viral fragments and small, exchangeable loops of DNA hitch rides as well. Over many generations, the Thieves’ continual trespass helps synchronize, in a loose and local way, the capabilities of distant parts of the city.

They are parasites in the body and pollinators in the genome.

Elsewhere, inside the hollow shells abandoned when Drifters died or outgrew them, tiny colonial cells settle—sheets of organisms that secrete enzymes to clean out the old occupant’s residues, then refit the interior as a brood-chamber. Their young escape through the same pores the Drifter once used for feeding. To an outside glance, the ghost-sphere moves much as it always did, but its inhabitant has changed: the city’s equivalent of someone taking over another’s house, leaving the walls but repurposing all the rooms.

---

All of these beings share the same conditions. They share a silence made of thick water. They share walls that are more continuous than any horizon. But they do not share a single world.

Each species inhabits a different version of this marble-cavity, parceled not by streets or property lines but by which gradients matter to them.

For an Architect, the city is a web of pressures and solute concentrations along through-rock canals—a vascularized stone organism it can feel in pulses through its own tissues.

For a Drifter, the city is stratified rivers in a hollow, patterns of flow stitched together, with the rock existing only as a set of obstructions that shape their currents.

For a Lattice Crab, the city is a topography of roughness: ledges and hollows mapped by touch, coated in familiar and alien biofilms, bordered by chemical fences.

For a Glass Knife, it is a field of potential vector lines, each prey-body a moving center of targetable disturbance.

For a Stitcher, it is stress and crack: a network of places where strain collects and must be tied.

They occupy the same physical centimeter, yet live in different abstract spaces superimposed upon it.

---

Nothing here sings. There are no bright displays, no eyes flashing recognition across distance. Communication, insofar as it occurs, is bleedingly local.

An Architect that is damaged leaks a burst of internal chemistry into its canals; those directly connected respond with a reflexive thickening of their own walls, a local tightening of flow, a closing-down that may propagate several body-lengths but no more.

A Lattice Crab disturbed by a predator releases a sharp, bitter-tasting metabolite; nearby Crabs fold up, flattening themselves into the biofilm and going still until the taste dilutes away.

Around the vents, the white-and-black chimneys extrude filaments that contract in concert when the underlying seep slows, partly retracting to conserve resources. Their retraction alters the microcurrents immediately around them, triggering adjustments in the feeding patterns of the Drifters and grazers that share their ring.

Information in this city is not passed as messages, but as perturbations. Each body is a sensor, an actuator, and a small disruptor of the surrounding fields. The “social” structure is an emergent choreography of mutually triggered reflexes.

---

Seen from far outside—if there were a way to hold this transparent stone up to the light and watch—it would all look like a tiny, smoldering coral head in reverse. Not outward toward sun and open sea, but inward into constraint and mineral glow.

You would see dim tracery: the pale, irregular bubble of the cavity; the even paler filigree of built stone; the slightly darker veils of biofilm; specks and lines and slow-pulsing knots of flesh moving along routes no larger than a breath’s thickness.

Nothing inside knows that anything larger exists. The sea beyond the stone walls is as inconceivable to them as empty space is to those who have never left their planet. The slow creep of tectonics, the distant storms miles above, the drift of continents—all of that is simply encoded locally as: a tiny shift in pressure here; a new trace element in the seep there; a fracture that lengthens imperceptibly over thousands of their generations.

The inhabitants of this place live and die by such changes. They do not know they are small. They do not know they are enclosed. They do not know that their world, with its density and its dim lit rock, could fit in the hollow of a pair of human hands.

They know only that the walls are close, the water is cold, and all around them the city continues to grow itself, one slow secretion, one small motion, one chemical decision at a time.
