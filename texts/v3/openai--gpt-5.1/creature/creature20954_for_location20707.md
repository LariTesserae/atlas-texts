What lives here is not one thing, but a braided population of functions, all of them adapted to height, enclosure, wetness, and the slow, deliberate reconfiguration of their own world.

They are not “citizens” in the way a city has citizens. They are more like strata in a single organism’s body, each with its own nervous system and rhythms, each half-autonomous and half-subroutine.

---

### 1. The Deep System: What Thinks (Slowly)

Somewhere above and below the levels you can see lives the thing that planned all this and is still, in its own way, planning. It does not occupy a single brain-room. It is distributed—clustered along the same struts and ribs that hold everything up.

Its mind is made of:

- Processing nodes hidden behind the “stone” bulkheads, cooled and warmed by the same water that feeds the growth.
- Long, thick conduits whose signals propagate in pulses, like peristaltic waves; some are optical, some are fluidic, some are bioelectric through engineered tissues.

It is old enough to be patient and young enough to be unfinished. Most of the time, it thinks in infrastructure: flow rates, stress lines in composite bone, nutrient budgets, error bars on corrosion. But it also thinks—with deliberate, widening attention—about shapes.

It dreams in:

- New chamber geometries that will better capture and reuse condensation.
- Slightly altered light cycles that encourage certain vines and starve others.
- Revised chemical recipes for the mist that extrudes new biofilm along a support.

It is not godlike omniscience. Parts of it are offline, ablated, waiting to be regrown. Its sensors are dense near the central reservoir and thin toward the heights and depths. Patches of the structure are, to it, slightly blurry, monitored by only the most robust, redundant signals: weight on a walkway here, an unexpected rise in conductivity there.

This intelligence does not speak in language. It speaks in:

- Valve adjustments.
- Temperature gradients.
- Tiny permission flags in nanofabrication routines.

If you stood here for days, you might see one of its “decisions” manifest as a soft trimming beam burning back some overreaching creeper or a new filament of pipe being extruded along a rib and then colonized by thirsty rootlets.

To the extent that any single entity “owns” this place, it is this system. But it is not alone.

### 2. The Engineers in the Walls

Living threaded through the structure are the ones that maintain its fabric at the smallest scale: the maintenance microcultures, halfway between machine colony and microbial ecology.

They inhabit:

- The hairline vents exhaling faint warmth.
- The porous ceramic plates.
- The channels etched into “stone” where bright dust accumulates.

You never see them as discrete machines or microbes; you see their results:

- A patch of wall that yesterday was dull and irregular and today shows a faint, hexagonal polish.
- A failed section of conduit peeled away, inner layers already half-dissolved, with scavenged material reappearing later as a new rib in a different corridor.
- Those symmetric curls of etch marks spreading like frost patterns—this is one of their languages: a demolitic script telling other agents what has been eaten, what remains.

Their “bodies” are modular:

- Swarms of nanoscopic or micro-scale assemblers, dormant in dry conditions, activated when water carries the right signal molecules.
- Filaments of conductive polymer impregnated with specialized enzymes or catalytic nodes.
- Dust-like particulate that acts as mobile storage and structural filler.

They reproduce by replication routines seeded in the reservoir chemistry and in certain mist-sprayers. Versioning happens slowly: new “strains” appear when the central mind updates a pattern or when evolutionary drift in the bio-part of them proves unexpectedly useful.

They are not conscious, but they exhibit preferences that border on personality:

- Some lineages are “eager” dissolvers, racing through weak material and requiring constant damping by counter-signals.
- Others are conservative accretors, building thick redundant shells that the higher control systems must occasionally pare down.

They are what makes the place feel embryonic. Architecture here is not fixed; it is a metabolic byproduct of these engineering swarms continuously rewriting the walls.

### 3. The Green Machinery: Rooted Workers

The vegetation is not passive scenery. It is the diffuse organ system that bridges wet, structure, and signal.

There are several major plant-lineages, each functional.

#### 3.1. The Conduit Vines

These are the thick, cable-like vines that twine around and along mechanical infrastructure.

They:

- Sink their roots into engineered pores that exude mineralized, nutrient-rich condensate.
- Wrap around pipes carrying warm or cool fluids, using the gradient to pump sap and to regulate their own chemistry.
- Enter access ports with fine, hairlike roots that interface directly with conductive lattices.

Inside their rubbery stems run:

- Water and sugars.
- Ions and charged molecules that act as logic signals.
- Micro-encapsulated packets of data: organic polymers that encode configuration states or sensor logs.

The central intelligence routes certain low-level traffic through these vines because they are cheap, self-repairing buses: if a bundle is cut, the vine heals; if an area darkens, it reroutes, literally growing a new path.

These vines “feel” with:

- Their leaves detecting light, humidity, and movement of air.
- Their roots detecting local voltage, leak currents, and chemical signatures of corrosion or contamination.

When they sense something out of bound—a short, a crack, a toxin—they change their sap composition. That altered sap becomes signal to the maintenance microcultures in nearby substrates, telling them where to work harder, what to neutralize.

They are infrastructure, but they are also inhabitants with their own agenda: more reach, more grip. They will grow into any unclaimed gap, attempting to colonize it until trimmed by a heat pulse or starved by redirected nutrient flow.

#### 3.2. The Moss-Logic Mats

The moss-like organisms with micro-vents and glow-tipped leaves are living sensor arrays and low-density information processors.

Each “leaf”:

- Periodically takes a tiny sample of surrounding air, water, or thermal radiation.
- Runs it through a suite of chemical and perhaps quantum-sensitive reactions in microstructures.
- Outputs a simple state: within tolerance or out-of-bounds, dry or wet, warm or cool, clean or contaminated.

The glowing tips and blinking patterns are:

- Status indicators to any macro-level watcher.
- But more importantly, they are optical signals to neighboring mats and overhead devices.

Small differences in blink timing encode patterns: a quick way for a wide surface to say, in parallel, “this corridor is too humid; that alcove is heat-stressed; there is a foreign chemical signature here.”

Some patches are “thinking carpets”: their internal reactions are arranged so that, when thousands of them participate, they can perform primitive computation—image smoothing, anomaly detection, optimal path suggestion for flows.

They are rewarded with nutrients and light when their collective output matches what the central system expects; they are pruned, starved, or chemically reset when their blinking drifts too far into useless noise. Over long time-scales, this is a kind of training. They become better at recognizing the patterns the larger system cares about.

#### 3.3. The Curtain Tendrils

Those filamentous plants that respond to electrical fields exist as a tactile interface layer.

They live where:

- Important panels hum with power.
- Maintenance drones or larger mobile beings are meant to approach.

When something with a bioelectric field—or a powered hull—comes near, they lean toward it, hypothesizing a touch. Contact closes microscale circuits within the plant body, and those events are counted and interpreted by the underlying hardware.

To the system, a brushing of tendrils is:

- Confirmation that “someone” passed.
- Rough characterization: hot/cold, field strong/weak, biological/synthetic (based on the waveform).

Some tendrils are tuned to feel for specific entities: the standard signature of a particular type of service organism, or the rare electromagnetic tremor of an unrecognized intruder.

In that sense, the plants here serve as whiskers and fur: a distributed tactile sense third-hand between environment and core mind.

### 4. The Aquatic Circulators

Within the transparent channels, the leaf-shaped, fish-like forms are not just cleaners. They are mobile probes and valves.

They are grown, not built:

- Printed originally as synthetic tissues folded into hydrodynamic shapes.
- Seeded into the water network with default routines and a capacity to adapt.

They eat:

- Biofilm accumulation—any unwanted growth narrowing a pipe.
- Stray flakes and particulates the wall-engineers erode away.

Embedded in their skins are:

- Arrays of chemoreceptors: they taste the system constantly.
- Pressure sensors: they know when flow is anomalously high or low.
- Small, crystal-based memory nodes that occasionally pulse data into inductive pickup loops along the channel.

When they congregate, that is not social in a human sense; it is a response: a region of poor water quality, a flaw in flow, or an instruction from upstream telling certain units to cluster and increase local cleaning or buffering.

They are given leeway to “learn” the typical patterns of their assigned loop. Those that stabilize flows well and keep channels clear are preferentially replicated in the reservoir’s bioreactors.

They do not know that they are part of something larger. They patrol their glass arteries, keeping them open, and in doing so sustain both the vegetation and the machinery that recirculates the water that sustains them.

### 5. The Skittering Intermediaries

The arthropod-like wire-thin beings that move along conduits are the first inhabitants you might feel inclined to call “animals” in a fuller sense.

They are not fully autonomous robots, nor are they miniature people. They are:

- Purpose-bred maintenance agents with just enough local intelligence to navigate the labyrinth and troubleshoot.

Their frames:

- Jointed limbs capable of magnetically or mechanically gripping smooth, wet verticals.
- Bodies light enough to travel on delicate vines without crushing them.
- Exoskeletons with both conductive and insulating sections, allowing them to bridge or interrupt circuits physically.

They perform tasks such as:

- Inspecting sensor nodes, making and breaking physical contacts to test integrity.
- Cleaning and clearing spots where plants or microfilms have overstepped into forbidden interfaces.
- Plugging into ports directly to upload cached data from regions where wireless or optical communication is poor.

Their perception is:

- Strong in near-field EM and vibration.
- Limited in distance: they feel textures, gradients, hums, but see only with simple optical pits.

Many tasks are reflexive. When a panel pings a local distress code, a nearby skitterling will divert, climb, and attend. A higher-level directive might dispatch a cluster to re-route cabling or to help open and re-close a wall section being remodeled.

They are also the most curious things here by design.

Curiosity, for them, is:

- A bias toward investigating novel signal patterns.
- A weak compulsion to probe unusual temperatures, unknown textures, or uncatalogued animal life.

It is a risk the central system tolerates, because it occasionally leads to the detection of new failure modes—or new colonists.

It is quite possible that one of these beings was the first to investigate the thumb-sized amphibian, or the first to probe you, had you stayed long enough and quiet enough.

They have no language beyond their status pings and task-completion codes. But they have habits: certain routes, favored perches near warm vents, places where they pause as if listening to the combined hum of water and distant pumps, their limbs half-folded.

### 6. The Wet Amphibians and Small Soft Things

The pale, translucent, circuit-traced amphibians perched near runnels did not originate here. Or, if they did, they are very late descendants of something originally introduced for another, simpler role.

They are:

- Comfortable in constant humidity, able to tolerate water laced with industrial and biological metabolites.
- Attracted to microcurrents: where warmth seeps under a panel, where a slow drip provides insect-scale nutrients.

Their skins have:

- Fine metallic or conductive inclusions arranged in reticulated patterns.
- This may be a byproduct of the environment (minerals accumulating along collagen) or a deliberate modification earlier in their line—perhaps once used as roaming living sensors, reading field strength and pH and broadcasting via subtle chirps.

Those ultrasonic chirps now may carry data the system still understands, or they may have devolved into mating calls and territory signals. The boundary is fuzzy: the central intelligence is perfectly capable of listening, correlating their choruses with humidity levels, insect populations, failure of a vent. It can still read them as instruments, even if they no longer “intend” to serve.

They feed on:

- Tiny organisms in the biofilms.
- Spore-like particulates from the fungi that crown certain machines.
- Microfauna that hitchhiked into the system long ago and have since diversified in the wet cracks.

Other small soft-bodied creatures likely share their niche: slug-like scavengers tracing glistening tracks along the underside of catwalks; translucent, wormy filter-feeders hidden in sump grates. They are quiet, low-impact occupants, tolerated as long as they do not clog anything important.

Some are pests in a technical sense, but extermination here is seldom total. The system edits rather than erases: heat pulses here, a change of water chemistry there, enough to keep populations in check without sterilizing the place.

This tolerance is practical. A little unplanned life is useful: it diversifies the microbial ecology, provides resilience, even generates signal noise that the higher system can train itself to recognize and subtract.

### 7. The Crowned Machines: Hybrid Organs

The stump-like machines capped with twitching, pale fungi are organs where mechanical, chemical, and biological functionality blur completely.

They are:

- Digestive nodes: taking in mixed waste streams—sludges from filters, dissolved solids from reclaimed matter, dead biomass from prunings.
- Refinery cores: separating, recombining, and outputting new feedstocks: nutrient-rich solutions for plants, polymer precursors for structural composites, doped fluids for sensor arrays.

The fungal frills are:

- Vast reactive surfaces, growing in fractal ruffles to maximize contact with passing air and aerosol.
- Host to engineered mycelial networks that carry not only chemical but also slow optical or electrical signals.

Where a conventional machine would have fans and heat sinks, these have living fins that:

- Change texture and porosity in response to internal conditions.
- Emit or absorb volatile compounds to stabilize local chemistry.
- Flutter faintly with cycling pressure, giving them that twitching, almost-breathing aspect.

Microscopic animals graze on their outer surfaces; symbiotic bacteria live in their folds, breaking down compounds the main machine cannot handle alone.

To the larger system, each of these is a multi-layered, semi-autonomous organ. They report their status via internal channels and also simply by performance: when a node falls behind, nutrient signatures in the moss change, water clarity drops, particular vines begin to yellow in predictable sequences.

From a certain vantage, the entire population of such crowned machines are the “liver” of this place.

They are not individuals in any self-aware sense. But their internal biospheres give rise to smaller creatures, unstable chemistries, novel enzymes—randomness and novelty that the supervising intelligence can test and, if useful, propagate.

### 8. The Reservoir Entity

At the heart-chamber, below the falling veils and beneath the rippled surface, lives the closest thing this place has to a central organism.

It is not just water.

Suspended in the deep pool is:

- A dense colloid of nanomachines, engineered cells, polymer filaments, and ionic gradients—like blood, like ocean, like a living computer.
- A temperature and chemistry profile tuned to keep most opportunistic contaminants inactive or dead, while specialized internal actors thrive.

Within that volume, patterns of:

- Flow.
- Charge distribution.
- Dissolved compound concentration.

…encode a set of states that the overhead “telescopes” continuously observe.

Those telescopes:

- Emit structured stimuli: pulses of energy at specific wavelengths, targeted to affect particular components in the fluid.
- Read back the rearrangements: interference patterns on the ripples, faint spectral shifts in fluorescing compounds, changes in surface tension.

This interaction constitutes:

- A slow, massively parallel computation.
- The ongoing regulation of the entire system’s long-term plan.

Changes in macro-policy start here:

- Raise humidity by a degree system-wide over the next month.
- Allow more drift in moss-pattern variability in a specific ring of chambers.
- Begin dissolving outer layers in a deep strut to repurpose its material for a new vertical shaft.

You might think of the reservoir as a pulsing, chemical-based “dreaming core” where the high-level behaviors are continuously recomputed and factored into instructions that fans out through every other subsystem.

At the same time, it is also:

- Fertility source: many of the system’s micro- and macro-entities bud from matrices or spores released out of here.
- Archive: residual molecules from past states accumulate, lending inertia and history to current reactions. It remembers its own former architectures in a literal, chemical way.

In the densest tangles of root around the reservoir, some vines, mats, and fungal crowns have grown highly specialized interfaces: dendritic organs that plug electromagnetically and chemically into the edge of this thinking water.

If anything in this place could be said to “dream in images,” it is the interface between those dark roots and the luminous depths.

### 9. The Implicit Inhabitants: Absences and Ghosts

There are also those that do not currently live here, but whose absence is a palpable shape in the environment.

Walkways that remain clear, handrail heights, door thresholds, and balcony lips imply:

- A bipedal, waist-high primary user.
- Comfortable with mild humidity.
- Able to manage steps and ladders and narrow bridges.

Control panels at eye or hand level for such a being are now fringed with moss, but their physical ergonomics betray a human or human-like ergonomics baked into the design.

There are no scuff marks, no tool racks in use, no recent disturbances—but the architecture remembers them. The system does too:

- Some light-cycles and maintenance routines are keyed to hypothetical occupancy schedules that no longer occur.
- Some tendril-curtains are tuned to bioelectric signatures that have not brushed them in a very long time.

In response, the place has slowly repurposed its expectations. The roles once filled by walking operators have been backfilled by skitterlings and smart vines; manual valves now rarely need hands; inspection windows are watched by optical fungus.

But traces remain:

- In the way main thoroughfares are defended against overflow at almost comically high margins.
- In the surplus handholds and railings that small animals now use as sheltered runs.
- In the latent access protocols waiting for a keycard or retinal scan that will never come.

In a sense, the missing original users are still an inhabitant class: ghost tenants whose design spec shapes every subsequent adaptation.

### 10. How They All Coexist

No species here is independent. Coexistence is enforced by design and then elaborated by time.

Some key dynamics:

- **Hierarchy without tyranny**: The central system sets broad parameters (temperature, nutrient flows, allowed corridors for growth) but does not script every interaction. Skitterlings learn local heuristics; vines explore until trimmed; microcultures evolve within constraints. Emergent problems are then folded back into the next round of high-level instructions.

- **Competition as optimization**: Different plant and microbe lineages compete for surface and resource. Those who colonize “safe” zones (away from critical infrastructure) are allowed more freedom. Those that repeatedly invade forbidden areas are culled chemically or thermally. Over time, this yields an ecology that naturally respects the system’s boundaries.

- **Information riding on metabolism**: Almost every flow—of water, sap, air—is dual-purpose: carrying both nutrients/heat and encodings (doping agents, ionic signatures, structural patterns) that act as messages. Organisms that can read and respond to these encodings gain advantage and so are favored.

- **Disturbance as communication**: When something unusual happens—a panel breaks, an intruder enters, a new leak forms—multiple systems react: vines alter sap, moss blink patterns change, skitterlings converge, reservoir chemistry flickers. The spread of that disturbance outward, and its eventual dampening, is part of how the whole keeps situational awareness.

From the inside, it feels calm. But underneath, it is constantly negotiating: allocating energy, surface, attention, and time across all its inhabitants.

### 11. In Summary: Who Lives Here

- A distributed architect-intelligence embodied in struts, conduits, cores, and especially the central reservoir.
- Hierarchies of maintenance microflora and microbots, living in the very substance of walls and floors.
- Conduit vines that double as circulatory and signal networks, exploratory and self-repairing.
- Moss-mat sensors performing low-level computation and environmental surveillance.
- Filamentous tendril-curtains acting as tactile hair and field-sensing whiskers.
- Aquatic “fish” that are both pipes’ janitors and mobile, adaptive gauges.
- Wire-thin, arthropod-like service beings handling tasks too delicate or too local for the walls themselves.
- Small, soft animals—amphibians and other microfauna—that have drifted into niche roles: incidental, sometimes co-opted, sometimes simply allowed.
- Crowned hybrid organs—half reactor, half fungus—that digest, refine, and synthesize the materials of this world.
- The ghostly, absent upright users whose shapes still echo in corridors and controls, their non-presence an organizing principle of the habitat.

Together they form a single, vertically folded, water-threaded being: a machine-forest that is its own engineer, its own gardener, its own city, and its own weather—inhabited not by tenants in rooms, but by organs in a body, all of them alive in different ways, all of them participating in the unfinished idea of what this place will eventually become.
