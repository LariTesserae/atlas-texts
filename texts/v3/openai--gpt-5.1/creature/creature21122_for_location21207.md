They live mostly underneath.

The first inhabitants you would meet, if you knew where and how to look, are thin. Not thin in the sense of starving—there is no real hunger here, only a steady, low ceiling of enough—but thin in the sense of spread out, filamentous, unwilling to gather their being into anything bulky or conspicuous.

They prefer the layer a hand’s depth below the surface, where the soil holds its memory of damp, and the day–night fluctuations are almost nothing. Down there runs a web so diffuse you can walk the basin end to end and never see it, though every step lands in someone’s body.

They are not exactly roots, though they lace around and between roots the way a careful hand might weave through hair. They are not exactly fungi, though they send out hyphae-fine threads and form junctions that look, up close, like microscopic knuckles and knotted fingers. You could call them microbial mats, or a distributed colony, and be technically accurate but emotionally off; they think, but not with a brain, and they move, but not as individuals.

They are the basin’s first decision: to be alive, but to be low.

Their flesh—if you can call the thin gelatinous films on clay particles flesh—feeds on what little drifts down or washes in. A leaf dropped from one of the shrubs is an event, a windblown insect carcass a festival, the rare seed that never sprouted a windfall that echoes chemically along the filaments. They do not surge or bloom; they adjust, fractionally. When a patch of soil stays moist two days longer than usual, the threads thicken there by degrees. When a hollow dries, filaments retreat but leave behind spores and durable cysts like notes pinned under small stones: “If water returns, resume from here.”

They have memory, but it is spatial rather than temporal. The layouts of former moisture are written into their density, their genetic mixing. The faint darker lines you see, where the moss clings thicker along an ancient runnel, are only the visible fraction of a far larger invisible choice: to keep one’s organs in the places where water once was, in case it is again.

No animals move above ground, but above ground is not where these beings care to mark their presence.

—

There are, nevertheless, animals.

They are exceedingly small, and they seldom break the surface. The basin’s prohibition on obvious motion has not banished life so much as pushed its nervous systems underground, into the capillary spaces between grains.

In the dampest swales, a few centimeters below your heel, transparent worms ripple through the paste of clay and silt with the slow, courteous urgency of creatures that know any sudden expenditure of energy might not be replaced. Nematodes, if you insist on an Earth-name, but they have specialized here into slow, long-lived lines.

Their bodies are not built for voyaging. They do not migrate from one end of the basin to the other; there is no point. Each bloodline has its territory, perhaps no larger than a small room, in which successive generations recycle one another, grazing on bacteria, on the thin film of photosynthesizers where a root lets in the faintest hint of light, on the exudates of the great subterranean web.

They have no eyes. Light is a rumor that arrives as a slight increase in warmth, a subtle drying; both are reasons to move away. Their sense of world is entirely tactile and chemical: the tension of water across their skin, the gradient of nutrient, the presence of a root’s sugar-laced leakage. Where roots are, worms are, curled along them like quiet punctuation marks.

These are the nearest things to “herds” that the basin entertains: not masses flowing across open land, but small, overlapping families, coiled around the anchor lines of shrubs and rosettes. Birth is unceremonious: a slightly smaller segment of the same slow intention. Death is a barely noticeable slackening, immediately disassembled by neighbors and absorbed into the fabric. The web below, the worms within it, the bacteria wrapped around both—they are each other’s organs as much as neighbors.

In the thin films of water clinging to pore spaces, single-celled swimmers turn lazy circles. Ciliates drift like bits of dust in a sunbeam you can’t see. Flagellates pivot and re-pivot, never far from where they started. None of them need to go far, because nothing in this basin is distributed randomly. Down here, every millimeter has a history of being just this damp, just this poor, for a very long time. Where conditions are right, they have been right for generations; where they are wrong, they have been avoided so thoroughly that life barely tests them anymore.

Nothing chases anything at speed. Predation, where it occurs, is more like engulfing what happens to touch you than hunting something down.

The quiet above is not mirrored by violence below; it is echoed as a more intricate quiet.

—

In and around the water trickle, the cast changes slightly. Here there is actual flow, however minimal, and so there exist creatures that remember, in their bodies, that once there might have been more.

Between the wiry grass-tufts, at the edge of the ankle-deep pools, live things whose entire world is the thickness of a water film: rotifers that wheel their cilia in patient, metronomic feeding; tardigrades that crawl with absurd deliberation over grains of sand the size of mountains, relative to their scale.

They are the basin’s extremists, its archivists of absence. When a pool dries—and eventually, every pool here dries—they do not disperse. They knuckle down. Their bodies fold in upon themselves, shrink, thicken, halt. Metabolism drops to a near-zero murmur. Outwardly they are specks of dust, indistinguishable from the grains they rest among.

These resting forms—encysted, desiccation-proof, loosely clumped at the high-water marks of puddles and tiny back-eddies—are the basin’s real diaspora. A sudden slightly heavier rainfall, once in a span too wide to be called a season, sheets thin water across new surfaces, lifting these granules and shuffling them downslope. Here and there, one ends up in a pit that stays just wet enough, just long enough, to justify waking.

They do, very slowly. As water slackens its grip on their skins, they rehydrate and push themselves outward again into the scale of their own perception. Under the grey sky, in a light so dull it seems an afterthought, a single microscopic bear-creature treads with exaggerated care over the flattened cell of an alga and discovers the taste of a surface no one of its lineage has touched before.

For them, this is frontier. For the basin as a whole, it is barely a statistical adjustment in the pattern of life.

—

On the boundary between surface and soil, some of the inhabitants choose to risk being seen.

The moss isn’t just plant. If you pry up one of those dark, thin mats in the damp hollows—the ones that never grow lush or tall—you pull loose a layered collaboration: threads and cells, rigid and soft, plant and not-quite-plant.

Some of the thallus sheets are old photosynthetic lineages that long ago gave up trees and leaves and extravagant verticality in favor of hugging the ground and asking for very little. Among them move colonies of something stranger: filaments that behave like fungi but host, within their walls, strings of photosynthetic partners. Lichens, by broad analogy, but tuned to dim light and intermittent wetting. Their pigments are dull green-browns, dusty olives that do not announce themselves; in this light, brilliance would be waste.

These entities are the basin’s compromise with the sky. They drink light gratefully but not greedily. Each patch is small, and once established, it ceases to sprawl, even though, in theory, it could. The limitation isn’t genetics; it’s etiquette, enforced chemically.

Those root-like webs underneath secrete compounds that accumulate, slowly, in the soil. Above certain concentrations, they tell new arrivals: this patch is already claimed. Growth here will be grudging and expensive. The moss and lichen communities respond by thinning at the edges, by choosing not to overrun bare clay that has, by long social contract, been allowed to remain bare as a buffer.

This is what passes for law in the basin: gradients of molecules in mud, invisible borders agreed upon by organisms that do not have language in any sense you or I would recognize. The result, from above, looks like a landscape that simply never took the opportunity to clot over into forest or sward. From within, it feels like a truce.

—

There are echoes of larger life, too. They persist not as individuals but as possibilities folded into seeds and dormant buds.

The shrubs, gnarled and sparse, are not desperate. They came here long ago—blown in as seeds, or introduced once by something with more deliberate motion—and discovered that growing big would be a kind of suicide. In a basin that never sees more than slow trickles of water and long, patient erosions, a tall thing would thirst itself into bone or topple in the occasional downpour and rot faster than it could reproduce.

So they specialize in staying the size they are.

Their wood is dense, growth rings packed tightly enough that a century might only widen the main stems by the thickness of a finger. Their roots are deeper than their trunks are tall, driving down into strata that remember wetter ages, tapping pockets of moisture held like old promises between layers of silt. From the perspective of a single plant, this is a private well; from the perspective of the community, it is a set of shared straws dipping into the same slow reserve.

The shrubs communicate, in the way plants do, by leaking messages into the soil and reading them back. When one is injured—a branch cracked by the rare weight of flood-borne debris, a portion of root exposed by some minor slump—the chemical profile around it changes. Neighboring shrubs adjust: their stomata close fractionally earlier in the day; their fine roots pull back slightly from the shared zone, giving the wounded one a temporary advantage in uptake.

Over time, this has produced a stand of individuals that are not a clone, but neither are they strangers. Their genetic diversity is modest but significant; their behavioral convergence is marked. None produces a flush of reckless new growth after a slightly wetter rain. None casts a generous crop of seeds every year. Instead, each holds back, timing its larger efforts to cycles that would appear, to most observers, as chance: one mast-like event in a long stretch of same.

When that rare event comes—triggered perhaps by some quiet threshold in deep-water levels or in the accumulated signals of the underground web—the shrubs make seeds that are asleep even when they are fresh. Their shells are thick, their metabolisms shuttered. They fall, roll a little way down the soft slopes, and come to rest in shallow depressions or against stones. Nothing eats them. They simply lie, and wait for alignments of water, temperature, and microbial whisper that say: now.

Most of them never hear that word within their viable span. They become soil. A few, scattered over decades, wake, push out a root and a pair of narrow, pale leaves, and join the slow chorus.

You could visit this basin once a year for a human lifetime and never catch such a germination in the act. You would conclude that nothing new ever begins here. You would be nearly right, but not entirely.

—

What about the stones in circles, the straightened bank, the faint rectangles?

They are not exactly abandoned. They have been adopted.

In the ring of stones on the low rise, life has rearranged itself around the residual pattern of a vanished intelligence. The soil inside the circle is slightly different—compacted, yes, but also richer in a few key elements: phosphorous, nitrogen, the metallic ghosts of past fires or of long-ago tools. Generations of roots and microbes have learned that there is more here than in the immediate surroundings.

So the circle gathers things.

A small shrub stands just outside its edge, its rootlets infiltrating beneath the stones and making use of that old legacy of ash and bone. Tiny crusts of lichen stipple the upward faces of the rocks; their distribution is not uniform. They prefer, subtly, the inner side, where some faint difference of wetting or wind or nutrient—deriving from whatever once sat within the circle—persists.

The underground web is measurably denser here. If you could see in cross-section, you would notice that the finest filaments lace more closely, forming something like a halo just beneath the ring’s plane. It is not reverence. It is simply that, long ago, animals lived and died here in a small, repeated way, and left behind patterns of enrichment that life has no reason to forget.

The straightened bank, too, bears a fringe of slightly different life. The algae on the waterline there are more species-rich by some small margin; the thin biofilm that dulls the pool’s surface shows a slightly different sheen. The reason is simple but layered: once, someone moved soil here, exposing mineral surfaces and burying others, and in that slight rearrangement set new microhabitats that have remained out of sync, just enough, with the rest of the channel to let variation persist.

No current inhabitant “remembers” the builders as individuals. There is no narrative continuity, no myth encoded in genes. What exists instead is a sensitivity to form. Where edges are sharper, organisms that like edges gather. Where the ground was once tamped flat and has since resisted cracking in quite the usual way, certain mosses find the stability they prefer. In the old rectangular depressions, the soil’s fine texture, rebuilt by trampling and then long erosion, favors root architectures slightly different from the open basin around.

Over the centuries, these biases compound. The places where humans once slept or worked accrete subtly different communities of bacteria, fungi, moss, shrub, worm. Stand still long enough, pay attention at the right scales, and you can see a ghost city outlined not by walls or roofs, but by distributions: this patch of moss thicker, that shrub fractionally healthier, this cobble bearing a lichen species absent from its neighbors.

So something still “lives” in the very idea of the old structures: not the makers themselves, but the arrangements they impressed upon matter, co-opted by the basin’s quieter citizens.

—

Once, there were larger animals here. Their absence is not primordial.

The web below retains, in some marginal lines of code, adaptations for coping with trampling, with pulses of nutrients from dung and carcass, with the sudden shading and unshading that come with herds and predators. Some shrubs still carry, in their chemistry, vestigial toxins that deter nothing now, leaf-alkaloids formulated as arguments in extinct conversations.

Why they left—or died—is another story, but its outcome is visible: a place where nothing with bones remains to move through the open, to reset the game by dispersing seeds far, or by digging new hollows that catch and hold rain, or by collapsing shrubs and reshaping the windlines.

In the absence of that restlessness, the system here has done what it can: it has folded. It has become inward, self-referential, finely tuned to a static set of constraints. Migration has been replaced by diffusion; bursts by drifts.

Yet even so, there is one lineage that still, in its own modest way, patrols the interface between below and above.

At the edges of pools and in the dark crevices under exposed cobbles, very small arthropods persist: pale, translucent springtail-like creatures, six-legged and soft. They are not numerous, and they are slow. Their exoskeletons are thinner than their distant insect relatives’, a concession to the need to conserve resources and to remain half-amphibious in a place where water is never quite generous enough.

They feed on biofilms, on fungal mycelia where it breaches the surface, on windblown dust that carries faint residues of organic matter from beyond the basin’s rim. Each individual lives a long time by its scale—perhaps several human years—growing through molts so infrequent they are events in themselves. They do not leap explosively as many springtails do; they step, cautious, always in contact with stone or soil. The air, to them, is emptiness to be spanned quickly, not a medium in which to frolic.

Their role is unglamorous but significant. By scraping and nibbling at films, they prevent any one microbial assembly from sealing a stone entirely. They create tiny regoliths of frass and shed skin that act as nucleation points for new colonizers. And, occasionally, when one of them dies in a crack or under a moss mat, their little body is a sudden, densely packed gift of nitrogen and phosphorous in a system starved of such jackpots.

They are the last echoes of scurry and shuffle the basin allows. If you sit perfectly motionless for an hour beside one of the dull, tea-tinted pools, and let your eyes relax to the scale of sand, you might catch a single movement: a speck that was on the underside of a stone now on its side, a near-transparent body bending to worry at a smear of algae, antennae tasting the mineral air.

Then it flattens itself under the overhang again, and the surface world returns to semblance of stillness.

—

Nothing here is in a hurry, but nothing is inert. Life has simply agreed, more or less collectively, to spend what little energy comes its way on remaining rather than expanding.

In more flamboyant ecosystems, the frontier is outward and upward: bigger, taller, faster, more numerous. In this basin, the frontier is inward: more subtly distributed, more stable, more precisely attuned to the familiar rhythms of faint trickle, soft overcast light, rare deeper rain.

Those who live here have shaped themselves around constraints until the constraints and the creatures are nearly indistinguishable. The moss knows the curve of each depression with the same intimacy that the clay itself knows the weight of water; the subterranean web’s decisions about where to thicken are as much geology as biology, following micro-topography and mineral seam.

What lives here, then, are not just organisms but relationships that have lasted unbroken for longer than any single lineage remembers: shrub root and fungal thread and slow worm, rotifer and tardigrade and the ghost-map of a once-straightened bank, all nested in a mutual agreement to keep things small, to keep them slow, to ride time rather than push against it.

Stand in the middle of the basin, turn in a slow circle, and you will see nothing dramatic: a dim sky, low shrubs, muted earth, dull water. You will hear almost nothing. But under your feet, inside the narrow band of air just above the ground, and folded into the very shape of stones tipped by long-gone hands, a quiet civilization persists: unhurried, unspectacular, and profoundly committed to the art of staying exactly, intricately, itself.
