They have no need for silence, because they have never known it.

Whatever counts as “people” here are built inside the roar. Their soft tissues are tuned around it the way bones are tuned around gravity. To them, quiet would be a catastrophic pressure drop, like the air being ripped out of the world. Sound is not a channel of information; it is the background constant that makes information possible elsewhere.

They are not the most numerous creatures, but they are the pattern-makers. The low terraces and structural infill, the subtle thickening of parapets and re-cutting of stairs, the shafts rimmed with warning-lips: those are theirs. You can trace their presence not by monuments or grand forms, but by wherever the mineral-plain has been persuaded to become layout instead of happenstance.

They are not tall. The tallest of them, fully extended, might meet the chin of an average human, and they spend most of their lives not fully extended. Their profiles are compact, folded, made to fit within arm’s reach of floor and wall at all times.

Their bodies run along the same design logic as everything else here: compressed, heavy-boned, built to negotiate vertical surfaces as readily as horizontal ones, and to be in contact with something—wall, rail, other bodies—more often than free-standing.

Imagine a torso like a rounded wedge, front-to-back longer than it is high, clad in a dense, close-growing pelage of flexible, moisture-absorbing filaments. From this core, four principal limbs extend, but not in the exact human cross pattern; instead the forepair sits slightly higher and closer together than the rear, giving them a persistent forward lean and an easy transition between walking on four limbs and rearing up on the rear pair for manipulation or vantage.

Each limb ends not in a single, hand-like structure, but in a spreading fan of sub-limbs: three main digits and a smaller opposing pad, each further tipped with clusters of short, gripping tendrils. The tendrils flex independently, adhering by a combination of micro-hooks and viscous secretion. On raw stone, they rasp; on patina-coated surfaces, they simply stick, like a practiced touch to a familiar skin. Their gait is a continual three-point contact, body always triangulated between grips.

Their heads are blunt and forward-facing, but the face is strangely muted. Where you would expect eyes, there are only small, dark pits recessed beneath heavy ridges, hooded by thick folds of skin and cilia that can close completely. Vision exists, but as a near-field sense—enough to discriminate edges, moving masses, and the glow of patina, not distant vistas.

Instead, the front of the face is all surface: a wide, slightly convex plane of soft, porous skin crowded with microfolds, cilia, and nodular organs. Scents, humidity, chemical traces laid down by touch—these collect and are parsed here. They lead with their faces to walls, posts, ledges, pressing that soft plane against surfaces the way a hand might slide along a banister. Information is something they smear themselves across.

The mouth is a slit low on the underside of the head, hidden until they choose to open it. It is ringed not with lips but with short, muscular, finger-like papillae that manipulate food, scrape films, and tease living matter off substrates. When they feed, they look as though they are trying to merge with what they are eating: mouth pressed flush to stone, head and upper body flattened, limbs braced to either side while internal mechanisms rasp and sieve.

Their eyes, small and deeply set, are attuned to differences in luminance rather than detail. They see gradients and glow more sharply than shape. The faint pulses from the blister-patina stand out to them like lit signage in an otherwise dim city: lines, nodes, and shifting intensities across every surface.

Inside their skulls, a series of small, fluid-filled chambers and bony loops serves as an elaborate inertial and vibratory organ. Where humans have an inner ear, they have an inner web. The endless low tremor of the ground and the roar in the air are mapped into a continuous, multi-dimensional sense of “how the world is currently humming.” Deviations, however minute, ring through this system like a discordant note. They don’t hear the roar; they feel its pattern.

The world’s other animals read surface chemicals and familiar paths. These beings read the deep-thrum as well, and this is what sets them apart. The shafts that drop into the unseen machinery below are theirs because they can feel the quality of the roar there, its slight differences in timbre and amplitude, the way it couples through stone. They are the ones who decided where it was safe to cut.

Their skin is not naked under the filament coat. Beneath the fur-like outer layer is a second, denser integument, almost leathery, laced with a network of shallow channels filled by a slow-circulating fluid high in ions and micro-organisms. This layer engages directly with the living patina and the glows. When they lean against a coated wall and pause, the patina pulses faintly in response, and their internal channels register that pulse.

In effect, they have grown a sensory system that uses the patina as an externalized nervous extension. Those translucent blisters are not “their” tissue, but neither are they entirely separate. The patina is an ecology they have fostered and learned to read. Through it, signals pass along walls and floors at the speed of slow light. A patch brightens in one corridor; those touching it feel the change and respond, or ignore it, depending on its pattern.

They did not invent the patina, but over very long spans of unchanging conditions, their bodies and behaviors and the blister-sheets have coevolved into something like a mutual communication substrate. They cultivate it inadvertently: by seeding it with micro-spores in their skin secretions, by avoiding scraping it off key channels, by building in ways that keep surfaces broad and continuous.

Their young are born small, wet, and sensorially blind in the usual ways. The first sense that opens is the vibratory one: they press against parent bodies and feel the parent’s internal hum integrated with the ground’s. Then comes the chemical surface-sense: the first exploration of wall and floor. Vision, such as it is, and their limited vocal range emerge last, almost as afterthoughts.

For the first part of their lives, they rarely climb more than a body-length from the basins and low inner terraces where they are kept. These “low wombs” of the city are flat, gently domed spaces whose patina layer is particularly thick and luminous, bathing the young in a constant, slow pulse of communal signals. What we might call “language” begins for them as patterns of touch and glow and vibratory tone shared in these places.

Because little changes here, their culture is not oriented around novelty but around refinement. They do not ask, “What will happen?” but, “What is the exact pattern of what always happens, and how are we properly placed within it?”

Social structure is therefore an architecture of positions as much as of relations.

They divide their world not into ages or seasons but into layers of depth and closeness to key features.

At the lowest social layer are the Broadwalkers: those who spend most of their time on the wide, mid-height terraces, among the swarms of smaller animals, managing the flows of bodies and the maintenance of accessible structures. They are numerous, and their lives are spent in dense contact with kin and neighbors. Their realm is what a visitor would see most readily: the crowded corridors, the low archways, the constant brush of fur and skin against stone.

Above them, both physically and in status, are the Rim-keepers and Vent-sentinels. They dwell near the shaft edges and the steepest drops, where the roar is fractionally louder and the vibration quality subtly different. Their work is to feel—constantly, collectively—for alterations in the hum from below. They are the ones you see lingering at shaft rims, forelimbs dipped briefly into the up-welling breath, then withdrawing.

They are trained, over lifetimes, to distinguish the million tiny fluctuations of the deep machinery from the rare, dangerous shifts that might signal a systemic change. In a world where nothing seems to vary, their task is to attend to the almost-variations. Their authority comes from this attention. When they alter a route, close a passage, thicken a parapet, it is because the hum told them something.

More central, but less visible, are the Deep-sense lineages.

They do not live down the shafts—they are not capable of surviving in whatever environment lies directly under the settlement—but they dwell in the closest, darkest recesses: in narrow clefts near weeping seams, in chambers sunk as low as stone stability allows. Their bodies are subtly different: limbs shorter and more powerful, eyes even more reduced, vibratory webs in their skulls more elaborate.

They spend hours at a time pressed full-length against patina-rich, tremor-prone surfaces, bodies slack, as though asleep, while their inner webs map changes. They have learned to parse long-term drift: the very slow thickening of one harmonic, the minute thinning of another. Where Rim-keepers hear for immediate danger, the Deep-sense are the historians and prognosticators. Yet “history” for them is not events; it is record of the hum’s shape over long spans.

They do not remember great floods or collapses; instead they remember when a low, constant undertone brightened by a tiny fraction over several generations, and how the weeping walls responded with a shift in their seep volumes, and how the population’s routes subtly re-mapped to keep the equilibrium. These are epics to them: sagas of minute reconfiguration, not of upheaval.

There is no singular ruler, no temple or palace, because there is nothing here to centralize around.

Authority is distributed in networks that map directly onto the architecture. A cluster of platforms served by the same weeping seam and the same shaft-rim will tend to become a social unit, bound together by shared hum-pattern and water source. Within it, the Deep-sense individuals attached to that seam and the Rim-keepers attached to that shaft wield guiding voices.

Disputes, when they occur, are almost always over location: who may dwell on which level relative to a seam; who may adjust which wall; whether a new passage may be bored between terraces or a vent widened. Since everything is saturated and nothing is empty wilderness, to build is to rebalance.

Their tools are famously patient.

A chisel or adze of sharpened, imported stone lasts for generations, its edge renewed on the grooved flatstones until it is worn down to a stub. Every significant slab, slit, or hole you see is the work of countless repeated strikes, scrapes, and pries, each one a tiny notch of decision encoded into the city.

They have no written script in any recognizable sense; they have the grooves and holes and patterns of alterations themselves. Where we might inscribe marks onto surfaces, they inscribe by changing the surfaces. A gradually thickened buttress records the long consensus of many individuals agreeing that this lintel must be supported. A recess shaved back to widen passage in the same canonical way as a hundred others signals that the route has been officially promoted from minor to major.

You might be tempted to call this engineering without explicit mathematics—an embodied calculus of load and flow—but they would not distinguish between “practical” and “ritual” modifications. Each adjustment is a social and sensory event. A new support stone is not just to hold up a sagging beam; it is also to modulate how that beam’s vibration couples into the wall, and thus into the nearby patina, and thus into the local hum. Every stone you place changes the story of that place by a fraction.

Their reproductive lives mesh with this slow building.

They breed infrequently, not because they are constrained by resources in any obvious seasonal way, but because they are constrained by position in the layout. New juveniles require not just physical volume but a niche in the web of vibrations and scents and patina glows. Until a gap opens—through a death, a rare relocation, or a deliberate reconfiguration of terraces—adults will delay reproduction.

This is not a conscious policy; it is embodied. Their endocrine systems are modulated by the density of certain chemical and vibrational cues that reflect local saturation. A crowded hum, dense with overlapping signatures, slows fertility. A slightly thinner one accelerates it. They do not count offspring; they feel whether there is “space” for one.

Because of the constants of their environment, they never developed pyrotechnic technology. Fire here, if it is even possible, would quickly suffocate in the thick, moist air and leave soot in a world where nothing else leaves stains. Instead, their manipulation of energy is almost entirely mechanical and hydraulic.

They build levers and weighted beams that respond to tiny changes in flow. Some of the hollow pillars you see, facet-cut and standing in rows, are actually springs and counterweights, flexing minutely under the slight expansions and contractions of the ground. Cords run from within them to adjustable plates and shutters in narrow gaps, which in turn modulate how air or fluid moves up and down certain conduits. By this, they can tune the local character of the roar, or dampen dangerous resonances.

To an outside eye, these look like crude stone posts. To them, they are instruments: not to create music but to keep the great underlying note in safe range. Their “music” is in fact the act of preventing new notes.

The patina’s glow, too, is tuned. In some places it is scraped away in narrow, clean lines, creating channels of darkness along a luminous wall: a kind of negative writing that stands out against the diffuse glow. In other areas it is encouraged to thicken at nodal points by smearing the wall with secretions that favor its growth. Certain pulse patterns—short-long-short, or slow, rolling waves—have come to stand for shared concepts: danger, abundance, gathering, accepted alteration, forbidden alteration.

Children play by touching patches of patina and watching their own faint pulses travel along a wall and return, modifying them with pressure and timing. In doing so, they practice the same intuitions their elders use to shape routes and walls.

You might look for something like art and at first see nothing: no figurative carving, no marks detached from direct utility. But the way they cut grip-grooves along steps, the way they shape the curves of parapet tops to fit the resting weight of their forelimbs, the way they space and angle scaffold-like beams under a sagging lintel—there is aesthetic preference there.

Given many mechanically adequate options, they converge on certain forms. A step riser tends toward a curve that rocks the foot just so under the always-present tremor. A beam support is chamfered at a specific angle that feels right to a resting shoulder. Over generations, these preferences accumulate into a style: blunt, yes, but with a tactile grace.

Prey and predator do exist in their world, but not in the cinematic way of chases and pounces across open fields.

Some of the smalls are domesticated commensals. Those furred, four-limbed runners that move in streams along regular routes: they are not pets in any sentimental sense, but they have been incorporated into the system. Their sense of smell is even finer over short ranges; they scout for subtle chemical shifts around weeping seams that might presage blockage or contamination, returning along familiar paths to bump against the limbs of their larger neighbors with specific patterns of touch.

In exchange, they have guaranteed access to rich feeding surfaces, and perhaps even to specialized secretion points on the bodies of their keepers. Over time, their wild forebears may have been replaced entirely by lines that thrive in this cooperative niche. They are like tongues and whiskers extended into crevices where the people themselves cannot spend all their attention.

The plate-backed drinkers at the weeping walls are tolerated but not controlled. Their role in the ecology is filtration and redistribution: by pressing against the damp stone and drawing fluid, then moving on, they likely exude concentrated excretions somewhere else, seeding chemoactive matter back into the upper layers of the substrate where the patina can feed. To the people, they are both resource and hazard. Their slow trails can destabilize patina layers or leave slicks where footing becomes treacherous.

There are predators, but they operate in narrow niches: small, fast-bodied creatures that move at the edges of corridors, picking off juveniles or infirm animals—rare moments of violence in a generally ordered flow. The people have made the architecture unfriendly to such incursions, with abrupt level changes, blind angles, and choke points. Their own young are kept in the hum-dense inner spaces where nothing can approach without broadcasting its vibrational signature.

If you search for rituals of death, you won’t find graveyards or ossuaries.

Dead bodies here are porous sacks of bound minerals and rare organics in a world where nothing much rots. To leave them on the surface would mean a gradual dessication and an unpleasant disruption in local chemical balance. They are instead deliberately disassembled.

When one of them dies, the closest kin and co-residents gather in a low, patina-lit space. The body is laid along a particular axis relative to a nearby seam—aligned, not with the heavens, which they cannot see, but with the flow of deep mist or fluid they sense in the stone. Over hours, using specially kept, finely honed tools, they divide the carcass into components.

Dense bone is cleaned and retained. It will become tools, supports, ceremonial grips on certain keystones. Sinew and cord can be dried and braided into bindings. Rich, soft tissues are carefully rendered down, then fed in measured amounts into specific patina-rich hollows, a concentrated offering to the communal skin. The remaining mass is not buried deep but introduced deliberately into the warm, chemoactive layers just below the surface in zones maintained for such integration.

There is sadness here, but it is intimate and spatial, not historical. The loss is felt as a change in the local hum-pattern: a familiar personal vibration gone. For a while, the nearby patina pulses those “missing” rhythms faintly, echoing what was. Over time, that echo attenuates, dissolved into the general glow.

They do not imagine afterlives in other realms; their spiritual vocabulary, insofar as they have one, is about distribution. A being that dies is not gone elsewhere; it is thinner here, thicker there, its former internal coherences diluted into the substrate and the patina, its bones and tools persisting in the structure. Eternity is not a new state; it is the same state, endlessly recirculated.

They have myths, but they are myths of arrangement, not origin.

Ask one of them (if you could) how all this began, and they would likely have no answer in the sense a human might want. They do not conceive of a time when the roar was absent or when the basin lacked its terraces. Their oldest epic traces not a creation event but a particular re-routing of flows:

The Time When a Great Wall Was Slightly Moved.

In that story, a seam that had wept a little less over many lifetimes finally thinned past a threshold, and the neighboring terraces’ patina dimmed by a barely perceptible amount. The Deep-sense of that district felt the long fade and, in response, a deliberative process unfolded: platforms were shaved back, a shaft’s rim expanded, a new pillar installed.

The myth praises the forebears who recognized the slow change early enough to act in a way that prevented any abrupt collapse. Its heroes are not warriors or founders but feelers and adjusters. Its great drama is a multi-generational consensus to shift the line of a wall by a small but crucial distance.

Other stories are about crossings: secret passages between two hum-basins that were once separate; a narrow ledge cut where before there had been a cliff; a bond formed between districts that used to be strangers. To them, the world is not space dotted with points; it is a network of adjacency and access. To add a connection is an act of profound narrative weight.

If there is any concept of “gods,” they would be understood as the great, slowly varying patterns in the roar itself. Not personified, not imagined as beings with will, but as vast, impersonal asymmetries:

The Deep Thrum Beneath All Thrum.  
The Slight Rise and Fall at the Edge of the Basin.  
The Constant Pressure in the Warm Air Above.

These are like aspects more than entities, recognized as sources of constraint. You do not pray to them; you attend to them. Ritual is attentiveness formalized: timed sessions of stillness pressed against certain walls, sequences of patina touches, communal adjustments of a series of tensioned cords on a run of pillars to damp a new resonance.

Their children learn rituals the way they learn stair-heights: by repeated, bodily practice, not by doctrine. At a certain age, a juvenile is brought to a shaft rim for the first time and allowed to dip its own forelimbs into the upwelling draft. The immediate, overwhelming sensation of the deep roar touching their personal vibratory web is a sort of initiation: a direct encounter with scale.

From then on, they know that the endless hum inside their own bones is matched, and dwarfed, by a great hum beyond them.

If you were to step into their environment, your human senses would be overloaded and underinformed at once. Your ears would ring with the roar, your eyes—accustomed to sharp shadows—would struggle with the flat, omnidirectional light, your nose would find only a bland mineral damp.

They, conversely, would find you terrifying in your blindness.

You would stride through spaces they read as dense with signals, unaware that the patina under your palm brightened sharply at your touch, that the slight local tremor of your weight against a parapet registered to them as a bizarre, unpatterned vibration. Your voice, pitched above and away from the roar, would fall into a band they scarcely use, perhaps startling them like an ultrasound spasm.

They would cluster at a cautious distance, pressed low to the surfaces, forelimbs anchored, bodies trembling with the effort of parsing you. Some might edge forward, fans of sub-limbs extended, to taste the air currents ruffled by your movement. Their first conceptual slot for you would not be “person,” but “mobile anomaly in the hum”: an unpredictable, nonrecurring pattern.

If you tried to speak with gestures or marks, they would notice primarily where you changed the layout. A scratched symbol on a wall would be insignificant compared to the fact of a scratch at all: a tiny cut through patina that altered its continuous glow. Your “message” would be that an uncontrolled actor had disrupted the communal skin.

To communicate with them, you would have to learn to modulate the world itself as they do: to tap certain stones with specific rhythms, to press your palm and hold until the patina around your hand brightened and then dimmed in a shared, recognizable pattern. You would learn, gradually, that “hello” here is to touch, wait, and withdraw in a particular cadence, leaving a fading afterimage of your presence on the wall.

They live in a “perpetual present,” but it is not an empty stillness. It is fullness held within tight tolerances.

Every part of their existence is about maintaining that fullness without tipping. Their architecture is a frozen flow; their society, an equilibrium of positions; their tools, mediators between deep, blind forces and the safe, trodden surfaces they inhabit.

Nothing here is ornamental in a human sense, but nothing is purely utilitarian either, because what is “useful” to them includes the stability of meaning. A parapet carved just so; a groove worn in exactly that curve; a patina pulse repeated with ceremonial precision—these preserve not only structures but the shared sense that the world is recognizably itself.

And under it all, the roar continues: the single, unbroken note that they have never heard as separate, which is simply the condition of being.

They live not beside that roar but through it. Their bones are tuned to its pitch, their myths to its faint variations, their entire city a set of shaped surfaces pressed delicately against its vast, unseen source.
